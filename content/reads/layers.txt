@@ L0
value: Layer 0 is where computer science becomes something you can install, configure, pay for and get paged about. Kafka, Spark, PostgreSQL, Kubernetes, PyTorch, Claude, an H100: each one is a bundle of dozens of ideas from the layers below, chosen, combined and polished so that you do not have to think about most of them. That is the whole value of this layer. A product packages decisions. When you start a Kafka cluster you are accepting, without writing a line, a specific answer to how data is laid out on disk, how replicas agree, how consumers track their position and how the network is used. When you call an LLM API you are using tokenization, attention, a KV cache, quantized weights and a GPU cluster, and you see none of it.
This packaging is what lets one engineer do what once took a research lab. In 2004, building a reliable distributed log meant writing your own replication protocol. Today it means choosing between Kafka, Pulsar, Redpanda and Kinesis. The products also create a shared vocabulary. A team that says 'we'll land it in a bronze Delta table and serve it from Pinot' is communicating a whole architecture in one sentence.
how: Every product on this layer is a specific mix of the patterns on Layer 1. Kafka is a distributed commit log plus publish–subscribe plus a coordination service. Databricks is a lakehouse plus distributed SQL plus a catalog. Elasticsearch is a search engine plus vector search plus a common backend for log analytics. Reading a product this way is the most useful skill this map can teach: instead of memorizing features, you ask 'which patterns does this combine, and which trade-offs did it pick inside each pattern?'
The lower layers add value to this one in a very concrete way. Layer 2 techniques decide the product's performance profile: Kafka is fast because of sequential writes, batching and zero-copy; Pinot is fast because of columnar segments, dictionary encoding and star-tree indexes. Layer 3 foundations decide what is even possible: the page cache, TCP, virtual memory and the memory hierarchy are why those techniques work. Layer 4 maths decides the limits: queueing theory says what happens to latency at 90% utilization, probability says how often a bloom filter lies, information theory says how far compression can go. Layer 5 physics sets the hard floor: the speed of light in fibre, the erase-before-write rule of flash, the heat a GPU must dump.
Many products also stand directly on other products. Kafka runs on the JVM; Kafka Streams keeps state in RocksDB; Databricks is Spark plus Delta plus Unity Catalog; Elasticsearch is a distributed shell around Lucene. In the map these appear as 'uses' links inside Layer 0.
intuition: Think of Layer 0 as the shelf of finished tools in a workshop. A good carpenter does not forge a new saw for each job, but a great one knows how saws are made, so she can tell when a cheap one will bind, which tooth pattern suits which wood, and when no saw on the shelf fits and something new must be built. That is why it pays to walk down the stack: products change every few years, while the ideas underneath change every few decades. Kafka will be replaced one day; the append-only log will not.
A second intuition: products are opinions. Delta Lake and Iceberg solve the same problem with different opinions about metadata. Pinot and Elasticsearch both serve analytics with different opinions about indexing. When two products look similar, the useful question is 'which lower-layer trade-off did each one choose?' The map answers that by showing their links downward.
friction: The cost of packaging is that the hidden decisions still leak. Kafka hides the log, until a consumer group rebalances during a deploy and lag spikes. Spark hides the shuffle, until one skewed key makes one task run for an hour. Kubernetes hides machines, until a noisy neighbor eats the memory bandwidth. The 'law of leaky abstractions' says every non-trivial abstraction leaks somewhere, and Layer 0 is where you feel it, usually at 2 a.m.
There is also churn and lock-in. Licenses change (Elasticsearch, Redis and Terraform all changed licenses, and forks appeared: OpenSearch, Valkey, OpenTofu). Managed services hide even more, which is convenient until you need to explain a bill or a latency spike. Finally, versions move fast: Kafka removed ZooKeeper in 4.0, Spark removed Mesos support in 4.0, and AI model names change monthly. This map records status as of October 2026; treat it as a dated snapshot and check the project pages before relying on a version.

@@ L1
value: Layer 1 is the layer of patterns: the shapes that keep showing up when people build real systems. A distributed commit log, a lakehouse, an OLAP engine, a coordination service, a search engine, a container orchestrator, a transformer LLM, an agent loop. None of these is a single product, and none is a single technique. Each is a recipe that combines several Layer 2 techniques to deliver one kind of value: durable streams, cheap analytics on files, fast aggregations, agreement on metadata, relevance-ranked retrieval, self-healing deployments, fluent text, tool-using automation.
Patterns are the right level for design conversations. When you design a system, you rarely start by choosing Raft versus Paxos. You start by saying 'we need a durable, replayable log between ingestion and processing, a lakehouse for history, and a real-time OLAP store for dashboards'. That sentence is made entirely of Layer 1 nouns. Once you know the pattern, you can choose a product from Layer 0, and you know which Layer 2 techniques you will need to tune.
how: Each pattern is held up by a small set of techniques. A distributed commit log needs a write-ahead log, partitioning, replication, idempotent producers and batching. A lakehouse needs optimistic concurrency, MVCC snapshots, columnar files and min/max statistics. An OLAP engine needs columnar storage, encoding, vectorized execution and bitmap indexes. A transformer LLM needs tokenization, embeddings, attention, a KV cache and post-training with reinforcement learning. In the map, open any Layer 1 node and the next column lists exactly these supports, each with a one-line reason.
Patterns also lean sideways on each other. Stream processing needs a commit log underneath to replay from. RAG needs vector search. Agents need LLMs. Event sourcing and CQRS need a log. Lakehouses evolved from data lakes and absorbed the warehouse. These sideways links are shown as 'Related' in the panel.
The deeper layers explain why each pattern exists at all. OLAP is columnar because of how CPU caches move 64-byte lines (Layer 3) and because similar values compress close to their entropy (Layer 4). Consensus services exist because of impossibility results like FLP and the overlap of majorities (Layers 3–4). Deep learning works because the chain rule makes gradients cheap to compute and GPUs make matrix multiplication cheap to run.
intuition: Patterns are like building types in architecture: house, bridge, warehouse, stadium. Each type solves a recurring problem in a recognizable way, and once you know the type you can 'read' any instance of it quickly. A senior engineer looking at an unfamiliar system mostly does pattern-matching: 'oh, this is a log with compaction acting as a table', 'this is a scatter–gather search over shards', 'this is a reconciliation loop'. The map helps you build that library deliberately.
Another intuition: most patterns are answers to a tension. Logs answer 'how do many systems see the same history?'. Lakehouses answer 'how do we get warehouse guarantees at data-lake prices?'. Orchestrators answer 'how do we run a thousand services without hand-placing them?'. Agents answer 'how do we turn a model that talks into a system that does?'. Knowing the tension tells you when the pattern is the wrong choice.
friction: Patterns get applied by fashion as much as by need. Microservices, Lambda architecture, data mesh and agents have all been adopted where a simpler design would do. Every pattern carries costs that its marketing omits: microservices add network failure to every call; lakehouses add small-file and compaction problems; stream processing adds event-time complexity; agents add non-determinism and new security risks.
Patterns also blur. Kafka now has queues; Pinot does upserts; Postgres does vectors; warehouses read lake files. That is healthy, but it makes 'which pattern is this?' a harder question, and teams sometimes pick a product for its main pattern and then push it far outside its comfort zone. The practical defence is to know each pattern's supporting techniques, so you can see when a product's design no longer fits your load.

@@ L2
value: Layer 2 is the toolbox of mechanisms: replication, partitioning, consensus, the write-ahead log, LSM trees, B-trees, MVCC, columnar storage, bloom filters, query optimization, shuffles, attention, gradient descent, KV caches. These are the moves that experienced engineers reach for again and again, across products and decades. They are where most of the real engineering trade-offs live, and they are what system-design interviews at Staff+ level actually probe.
The value of this layer is reuse with understanding. The write-ahead log appears in PostgreSQL, Kafka, RocksDB, etcd, Delta Lake's transaction log and even in how Temporal records workflow history. Partitioning appears in Kafka topics, Spark shuffles, Cassandra rings and Pinot segments. Once you understand a technique deeply, you understand a slice of dozens of products at once, and you can predict their behavior under load.
how: Each technique turns a Layer 3 fact into a useful property. Sequential disk access is far faster than random access (a Layer 3 fact about storage devices), so the write-ahead log and the LSM tree turn random updates into sequential appends. Memory moves in 64-byte cache lines (Layer 3), so columnar storage and vectorized execution keep useful data packed into those lines. Networks partition and messages get lost (Layer 3 theory), so replication and consensus are designed around majorities and terms. GPUs are fast at matrix multiplication (Layer 3 architecture), so attention is written as matrix products.
Layer 4 maths gives each technique its guarantees and costs. Quorums work because any two majorities overlap (combinatorics). Bloom filters have a predictable false-positive rate (probability). HyperLogLog estimates distinct counts from the statistics of random hashes. Gradient descent works because the chain rule makes gradients cheap. Compression cannot beat entropy (information theory). Without the maths, a technique is a recipe; with it, you know exactly when it will fail.
intuition: Most Layer 2 techniques are clever ways of paying a cost at a better time or in a better place. A write-ahead log pays for durability with one sequential write now, instead of many random writes. An LSM tree pays for writes later, during compaction. MVCC pays with extra storage to avoid making readers wait. Caches pay with memory to avoid recomputation. Batching pays with a little latency to save a lot of overhead. Speculative execution pays with duplicate work to cut tail latency. When you meet a new technique, ask: what cost does it move, from where, to where?
A second intuition: many techniques are the same idea in different clothes. PagedAttention in vLLM is virtual memory for the KV cache. Log compaction in Kafka is LSM compaction for topics. A CRDT merge is a lattice join. Kubernetes' reconciliation loop is a thermostat. Noticing these echoes is a large part of first-principles thinking.
friction: Every technique has a failure mode that someone learned the hard way. Replication lag makes reads stale. Consensus slows down when one node has a slow disk. LSM compaction steals I/O at the worst moment. MVCC needs garbage collection (Postgres VACUUM). Retries without jitter cause retry storms. Exactly-once is really 'effectively once' and only inside one system. Attention costs grow with the square of context length. The friction is rarely in the idea; it is in the edge cases, tuning knobs and interactions between techniques. That is why the panel for each technique lists both what it is built on and what is built on it: you need both directions to debug.

@@ L3
value: Layer 3 holds the classic subjects of a computer science degree: networks, operating systems, computer architecture, database theory, algorithms and data structures, compilers and programming languages, theory of computation and cryptography. They are the 'physics of software': the stable rules that every technique above has to obey. They change slowly. TCP is from 1974, virtual memory from 1962, the relational model from 1970, B-trees from 1972. And they explain almost every performance surprise you will ever see in production.
The value of this layer is that it turns mysteries into mechanisms. Why is Kafka fast? The page cache and sequential I/O. Why did latency jump when we moved a service to another region? TCP's slow start and round-trip time. Why is this Spark job slow even though CPU is idle? Shuffle over the network and spills to disk. Why does a Postgres replica lag? WAL replay is single-threaded. Each answer is a Layer 3 concept.
how: Each subject provides abstractions that the techniques above build on. The OS gives processes, virtual memory, the page cache, file systems and system calls. Networks give reliable byte streams (TCP), names (DNS), and secure channels (TLS). Architecture gives pipelines, caches, SIMD and GPUs. Database theory gives the relational model, transactions, isolation levels and recovery. Algorithms give hashing, trees, sorting and graph search. Compilers give parsing, type systems, JIT compilation and garbage collection. Theory gives Turing machines, computability and complexity. Cryptography gives hash functions, encryption and signatures.
Each of these in turn rests on mathematics (Layer 4): routing on graph theory, TCP on control and queueing theory, hashing on number theory and the birthday bound, databases on set theory and logic, type systems on lambda calculus, cryptography on number theory and finite fields. And computer architecture rests directly on physics (Layer 5): digital logic is made of transistors; memory latency is set by wire length and the speed of light; flash wears out because of quantum tunneling.
intuition: A useful picture is that each Layer 3 subject is a contract between two worlds. The OS is a contract between programs and hardware: 'you may pretend you have the whole machine'. TCP is a contract between applications and an unreliable network: 'you may pretend the wire never drops bytes'. SQL is a contract between people and storage: 'say what you want, not how to get it'. A compiler is a contract between human-readable code and machine instructions. Every one of these contracts is a beautiful illusion, and learning Layer 3 is learning how each illusion is maintained and where it breaks.
Another intuition: the numbers matter more than the definitions. Knowing that an L1 cache hit takes about 1 ns, DRAM about 100 ns, an SSD read about 100 µs and a cross-continent round trip about 100 ms is the single most useful piece of Layer 3 knowledge for a systems designer. Those four orders of magnitude explain why most of the techniques on Layer 2 exist.
friction: The illusions leak in specific, learnable ways. Virtual memory leaks as page faults and TLB misses. TCP leaks as head-of-line blocking and slow start. The page cache leaks when fsync is skipped and a power cut loses 'written' data. Isolation levels leak as anomalies like write skew under snapshot isolation. Garbage collection leaks as pauses. Floating point leaks as rounding errors. The friction here is educational: almost every hard production bug is a Layer 3 illusion breaking, and engineers who know these subjects debug much faster.
There is also a teaching problem: these subjects are usually taught separately, in different semesters, with toy examples. The point of this map is to connect them upward to real systems, so that 'virtual memory' is not only an exam topic but also 'why vLLM can serve twice as many users'.

@@ L4
value: Layer 4 is mathematics: logic, set theory, graphs, number theory, linear algebra, calculus, optimization, probability, statistics, information theory, queueing and control theory, lambda calculus and type theory. It is the layer that turns engineering rules of thumb into guarantees. Without it, 'add a bloom filter' is folklore; with it, you can compute that 10 bits per key give about a 1% false-positive rate. Without it, 'don't run disks at 95%' is advice; with it, you can see from queueing theory that waiting time grows like 1/(1−utilization) and explodes near 100%.
This layer is also where the biggest ideas in modern computing actually live. Deep learning is linear algebra plus calculus plus probability. Databases are set theory and logic. Cryptography is number theory. Compression is information theory. Consensus correctness is combinatorics and logic. Recommendation and vector search are geometry. If you want first-principles thinking, this is the layer where 'first principles' becomes precise.
how: Layer 4 adds value upward by providing three things: models, bounds and proofs. Models describe a system simply enough to reason about it: a queue, a Markov chain, a graph, a vector space. Bounds tell you what is impossible or how good things can get: entropy bounds compression, the birthday bound limits hash sizes, Amdahl's law limits parallel speed-up, the FLP result limits consensus. Proofs tell you an algorithm is correct: induction for recursion and Raft's safety, serializability theory for transactions, convergence for gradient descent in convex cases.
Underneath, Layer 5 gives mathematics its roots and its physical meaning. Formal logic comes from Aristotle, Boole and Frege; Gödel and Turing showed its limits. Linear algebra is also the language of quantum mechanics; calculus is the language of Maxwell's equations. Information theory turned out to be the same mathematics as thermodynamic entropy, and Landauer showed that erasing a bit has a real energy cost. This is why the map continues below mathematics: the maths is not arbitrary; it describes the physical world computers live in.
intuition: Mathematics is compression of experience. A queueing formula compresses thousands of observations about traffic jams, supermarket lines and overloaded servers into one line. The chain rule compresses 'how does a change here ripple through there?' into a multiplication. Probability compresses 'what usually happens' into a distribution. When an engineer 'has intuition' about a system, they often have an informal version of exactly these models in their head. Learning the formal version makes that intuition portable and checkable.
A second intuition: a small number of mathematical ideas carry most of computer science. If you deeply understand vectors and matrices, probability and expectation, logarithms and exponentials, graphs, and the idea of a proof by induction, you can follow almost any paper in systems or machine learning.
friction: Mathematics is precise about its models, but the models are simplifications. Queueing models assume arrival patterns that real traffic does not follow; real load is bursty and heavy-tailed. Convergence proofs for gradient descent assume convexity, which deep networks do not have, yet training works anyway, and the theory is still catching up. Complexity theory talks about large n, while real systems live at specific n where constants matter. Learning theory still cannot fully explain why huge models generalize.
There is also a human friction: mathematics is often taught without the systems that motivate it. This map tries to fix that by always showing what each idea is used for above it. If a formula feels abstract, follow its 'Used by' links upward until you reach a product you know.

@@ L5
value: Layer 5 is where computer science touches the rest of reality: physics, chemistry, biology, philosophy and linguistics. It answers the questions that the other layers take for granted. Why can a piece of silicon act as a switch? Why is memory far away slow? Why does flash wear out? Why does computing make heat? What can be computed at all? Where did logic come from? Why did we think neural networks might work? This layer is the bedrock of first-principles thinking: when you reach it, you can no longer say 'because the library does it this way'.
The value is perspective. Knowing that light covers about 20 cm per nanosecond in a wire and about 5 microseconds per kilometre in fibre makes some designs obviously impossible (a strongly consistent database across continents with 1 ms writes) and others obviously sensible (keeping hot data close to the CPU). Knowing that erasing a bit costs energy explains why data centres are now limited by power, not floor space. Knowing Gödel and Turing explains why no tool can find every bug, and why sandboxing AI agents is a necessity, not a nicety.
how: Layer 5 supports the layers above in three ways. First, physics and chemistry build the hardware. Quantum mechanics explains band gaps; doped silicon forms p–n junctions; a MOSFET is a voltage-controlled switch; lithography prints billions of them; copper wires connect them; capacitors store DRAM bits; tunneling writes and erases flash; Maxwell's equations govern every signal on every wire and fibre. Moore's law was the economic engine of this, and the end of Dennard scaling around 2006 is why we have multicore CPUs and GPUs.
Second, philosophy and logic built the theory. Aristotle's syllogisms, Leibniz's dream of calculating reasoning, Boole's algebra of logic, Frege's predicate logic, Hilbert's question 'can mathematics be decided mechanically?', and Gödel's and Turing's answer: no. The Church–Turing thesis gave us the definition of computation itself. Shannon took Boole's logic into circuits and turned information into something measurable.
Third, biology and linguistics gave inspiration. McCulloch and Pitts modeled neurons as logic gates in 1943. Hubel and Wiesel's visual cortex research inspired convolutional networks. Dopamine reward-prediction errors match temporal-difference learning. Evolution inspired genetic algorithms. Firth's 'you know a word by the company it keeps' became word embeddings. Chomsky's hierarchy classifies the languages that parsers and regexes accept. In the map these biological links are marked as analogies, because modern systems borrow the idea, not the mechanism.
intuition: Everything above is an arrangement of electrons, photons and logic. That sounds obvious, but it is useful. When a system seems to violate common sense (a cross-region database 'losing' a write, a laptop slowing down when hot, a model generalizing from examples), the explanation usually ends here: signals take time to travel and there is no global 'now'; heat must leave the chip; and induction from examples is a philosophical leap that we have learned to make reliable with statistics.
friction: The analogies from biology are the weakest links in the whole map. Deep networks are not brains; 'neurons' in a transformer share little mechanism with real neurons. Treat biological inspiration as a source of ideas, not of explanations. Physics, on the other hand, is unforgiving but increasingly expensive to work with: transistors are approaching atomic scale, leakage through tunneling grows, and each new lithography step costs billions. Philosophy's questions (can machines think? what makes a belief justified? what should AI be allowed to do?) are becoming practical engineering and policy questions, and they are still open.
