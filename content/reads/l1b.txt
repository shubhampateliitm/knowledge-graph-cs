@@ mlops
value: MLOps applies software-engineering discipline to machine learning: reproducible training, versioned data and models, automated deployment, and monitoring of models in production. Most ML projects fail not at modeling but at getting reliable models into production and keeping them there.
how: Pipelines (orchestrated DAGs) prepare data and train models; experiment tracking (MLflow, Weights & Biases) records parameters and metrics; a model registry versions models and approvals; CI/CD deploys them to batch scoring or online serving; monitoring tracks prediction quality, data drift and latency; feature stores keep training and serving features consistent.
intuition: Treat a model like a product release, not a notebook result.
friction: ML has hidden technical debt: data dependencies, feedback loops and configuration sprawl. Labels arrive late, so quality monitoring lags. Tool sprawl and unclear ownership between data scientists and engineers.
ref: paper | Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems | Sculley et al., NeurIPS 2015 |
ref: book | Designing Machine Learning Systems | Chip Huyen, 2022 |

@@ featurestore
value: A feature store manages the inputs (features) that models use, so the same definitions and values are available for training (historical, point-in-time correct) and for serving (fresh, low latency). It prevents training–serving skew and lets teams reuse features.
how: Feature pipelines compute features from batch and streaming sources; an offline store keeps history for point-in-time joins ('what did we know at the moment of each training example?'); an online store (Redis, DynamoDB, Cassandra) serves the latest values by entity key; a registry describes features, owners and lineage. Uber's Michelangelo popularized the idea; Feast, Tecton and Databricks provide implementations.
intuition: A shared pantry with labeled ingredients, stocked identically for the test kitchen and the restaurant.
friction: Keeping offline and online consistent, handling backfills and late data, and the cost of streaming features. Many teams discover they only need a few well-managed tables.
ref: blog | Meet Michelangelo: Uber's Machine Learning Platform | Hermann & Del Balso, 2017 |
ref: book | Designing Machine Learning Systems, ch. 'Feature Engineering' | Chip Huyen, 2022 |

@@ ml
value: Machine learning lets computers learn behavior from examples instead of explicit rules. Spam filters, fraud detection, ad click prediction, recommendations, search ranking, demand forecasting and many other systems use it. The value is handling problems where the rules are too many, too subtle or too changing to write by hand.
how: Supervised learning fits a function from inputs to labeled outputs by minimizing a loss on training data; unsupervised learning finds structure (clusters, embeddings) without labels; reinforcement learning learns actions from rewards. Models range from linear models and decision trees (and gradient-boosted trees) to neural networks. Generalization is checked on held-out data; regularization and validation control overfitting.
intuition: Show many examples with answers, and let the system find the pattern that predicts the answers.
friction: Models are only as good as their data: biased, stale or leaked data produces bad models. Correlation is not causation. Models degrade when the world changes (drift). Explaining decisions and ensuring fairness are hard.
ref: book | An Introduction to Statistical Learning, 2nd ed. | James, Witten, Hastie & Tibshirani, 2021 | https://www.statlearning.com/
ref: book | Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning | Christopher Bishop, 2006 |
ref: course | Stanford CS229 Machine Learning | Andrew Ng | https://cs229.stanford.edu/

@@ dl
value: Deep learning trains neural networks with many layers end to end, letting them learn features directly from raw data (pixels, audio, text) instead of relying on hand-designed features. Since AlexNet won ImageNet in 2012, it has transformed vision, speech, translation, and now language and generation.
how: A network stacks layers of linear transformations (matrix multiplications, convolutions, attention) and non-linear activations. Training uses backpropagation to compute gradients and stochastic gradient descent (often Adam) to update millions to trillions of parameters. Normalization, residual connections and careful initialization make deep stacks trainable; GPUs and parallel training make it fast enough; large datasets make it generalize.
intuition: Each layer learns slightly more abstract features: edges, then shapes, then objects; or characters, then words, then meaning.
friction: Data and compute hungry; hard to interpret; sensitive to hyperparameters; theory lags practice (why do huge models generalize?).
ref: book | Deep Learning | Goodfellow, Bengio & Courville, 2016 | https://www.deeplearningbook.org/
ref: book | Dive into Deep Learning | Zhang, Lipton, Li & Smola | https://d2l.ai/
ref: paper | ImageNet Classification with Deep Convolutional Neural Networks | Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton, NeurIPS 2012 |

@@ llmserving
value: LLM serving runs large models for many concurrent users at acceptable cost and latency. Because generating each token requires reading all model weights and a growing KV cache from GPU memory, serving is a memory-bandwidth and memory-capacity problem as much as a compute problem.
how: Inference has two phases: prefill (process the prompt in parallel, compute-bound) and decode (generate tokens one at a time, memory-bound). Serving engines (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM, TGI) batch many requests continuously, manage the KV cache in pages, reuse cached prefixes, quantize weights and caches, use speculative decoding, split models across GPUs (tensor and pipeline parallelism), and sometimes separate prefill and decode onto different GPUs ('disaggregated serving').
intuition: Each step must stream the whole model through the GPU; batching lets many users share that stream.
friction: Trade-offs between throughput, time-to-first-token and per-token latency; long contexts explode memory; tail latency under load; cost per token dominates product economics.
ref: paper | Efficient Memory Management for LLM Serving with PagedAttention | Kwon et al., SOSP 2023 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180
ref: paper | Orca: A Distributed Serving System for Transformer-Based Generative Models | Yu et al., OSDI 2022 |
ref: blog | LLM Inference Performance Engineering: Best Practices | Databricks, 2023 |

@@ rag
value: Retrieval-augmented generation gives an LLM relevant, up-to-date or private information at question time by retrieving documents and placing them in the prompt. It reduces hallucinations, allows citations, and avoids retraining the model on every new document.
how: Documents are split into chunks, embedded into vectors and indexed (vector database, often combined with keyword BM25 search). At query time, the question is embedded, the nearest chunks are retrieved (and often re-ranked by a cross-encoder), and the model answers using them. Variants include query rewriting, multi-hop retrieval, graph-based retrieval and agentic retrieval where the model decides what to search.
intuition: An open-book exam: the model is smart but should look things up rather than guess.
friction: Retrieval quality decides answer quality; chunking choices matter; long contexts dilute attention; access control must be enforced at retrieval; evaluating RAG end to end is hard.
ref: paper | Retrieval-Augmented Generation for Knowledge-Intensive NLP Tasks | Lewis et al., NeurIPS 2020 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401
ref: paper | Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts | Liu et al., 2023 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.03172

@@ rl
value: Reinforcement learning learns to act by trial and error: an agent takes actions in an environment, receives rewards, and improves its policy to collect more reward. It mastered Go (AlphaGo), Atari and robotics tasks, and today it is central to post-training LLMs (RLHF, reasoning models).
how: Problems are modeled as Markov decision processes. Value-based methods (Q-learning, DQN) learn the expected future reward of actions; policy-gradient methods (REINFORCE, PPO, GRPO) adjust the policy directly to make rewarded actions more likely; actor–critic methods combine them. Temporal-difference learning updates estimates from the difference between predicted and observed reward.
intuition: Learning to ride a bicycle: no one gives you the right answer at each moment, only the consequence (staying up or falling).
friction: Sample-inefficient; reward design is hard and models exploit loopholes ('reward hacking'); training can be unstable; real-world exploration is expensive or unsafe.
ref: book | Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, 2nd ed. | Sutton & Barto, 2018 | http://incompleteideas.net/book/the-book-2nd.html
ref: paper | Proximal Policy Optimization Algorithms | Schulman et al., 2017 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1707.06347

@@ recsys
value: Recommender systems decide what each user sees: products, videos, posts, ads, songs. They drive a large share of engagement and revenue at companies like Amazon, Netflix, YouTube and TikTok, and are close cousins of ad targeting and audience building.
how: Classic approaches use collaborative filtering (users who liked X also liked Y), matrix factorization (users and items as vectors whose dot product predicts affinity) and content features. Modern systems use two stages: candidate retrieval (two-tower neural networks producing user and item embeddings, searched with approximate nearest neighbours) and ranking (deep models over many features predicting clicks, watch time or conversions), plus re-ranking for diversity and business rules. Sequential and transformer-based models use the user's recent history.
intuition: Find people like you and things like what you liked, then sort by what you are most likely to want right now.
friction: Feedback loops and popularity bias; cold start for new users and items; offline metrics often disagree with online A/B results; privacy constraints limit signals.
ref: paper | Matrix Factorization Techniques for Recommender Systems | Koren, Bell & Volinsky, IEEE Computer 2009 |
ref: paper | Deep Neural Networks for YouTube Recommendations | Covington, Adams & Sargin, RecSys 2016 |
ref: book | Recommender Systems Handbook, 3rd ed. | Ricci, Rokach & Shapira, 2022 |

@@ cv
value: Computer vision teaches machines to understand images and video: classify, detect and segment objects, read text, track motion, recognize faces and generate images. It powers medical imaging, self-driving, quality inspection, photo search and content recognition.
how: Convolutional neural networks (LeNet, AlexNet, ResNet) learn hierarchies of local features with shared filters; vision transformers (ViT, 2020) split images into patches treated like tokens; contrastive models (CLIP) align images with text; segmentation (Segment Anything) and detection models (YOLO family) target specific tasks; diffusion models generate images.
intuition: Learn the visual vocabulary from data: edges, textures, parts, objects.
friction: Needs large labeled datasets or clever self-supervision; distribution shift (lighting, cameras) breaks models; bias in face and person recognition; compute-heavy video processing.
ref: paper | Deep Residual Learning for Image Recognition | He et al., CVPR 2016 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1512.03385
ref: paper | An Image is Worth 16x16 Words: Transformers for Image Recognition at Scale | Dosovitskiy et al., 2020 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.11929
ref: course | Stanford CS231n: Deep Learning for Computer Vision | Stanford | https://cs231n.stanford.edu/

@@ nlp
value: Natural language processing lets machines read, understand and generate human language: search, translation, summarization, sentiment, chatbots, information extraction. LLMs have absorbed most of the field into one general approach.
how: Text is tokenized and embedded; models moved from rules and statistical models (n-grams, HMMs, CRFs) to word embeddings (word2vec, 2013), recurrent networks with attention, and transformers (BERT for understanding, GPT for generation). Fine-tuning and prompting adapt pretrained models to tasks.
intuition: Language is patterns of usage; learn those patterns from enough text and you capture much of meaning.
friction: Ambiguity, low-resource languages, factuality and bias; evaluation of generated text is hard.
ref: book | Speech and Language Processing, 3rd ed. draft | Jurafsky & Martin | https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/
ref: paper | BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers | Devlin et al., 2018 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1810.04805

@@ diffusion
value: Diffusion models generate images, video, audio and even molecules by learning to reverse a gradual noising process. Since 2020 they have produced the most realistic generated images (Stable Diffusion, DALL·E, Midjourney, Imagen) and video (Sora, Veo).
how: Training: take real data, add a random amount of Gaussian noise, and train a network to predict the noise (or the clean data). Generation: start from pure noise and repeatedly apply the network to remove a bit of noise, optionally guided by a text embedding (classifier-free guidance). Latent diffusion runs this in a compressed latent space; diffusion transformers replace U-Nets; flow matching and distillation reduce the number of steps.
intuition: Sculpting from static: each step removes a little noise until a picture emerges.
friction: Many network evaluations per sample (slow without distillation); control over composition and text rendering; copyright and consent concerns about training data.
ref: paper | Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models | Ho, Jain & Abbeel, 2020 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.11239
ref: blog | What are Diffusion Models? | Lilian Weng, 2021 | https://lilianweng.github.io/posts/2021-07-11-diffusion-models/

@@ vectorsearch
value: Vector search finds items whose embeddings are closest to a query embedding, enabling search by meaning: 'shoes for running on trails' finds relevant products even without exact words. It underlies RAG, semantic search, recommendations, deduplication and image search.
how: Items are embedded by a model into vectors; an approximate nearest-neighbour index (HNSW graphs, IVF clustering, product quantization, DiskANN) answers top-k queries quickly with small accuracy loss; filters and hybrid scoring combine metadata and keyword relevance. Implementations range from libraries (FAISS) to databases (pgvector, Milvus, Pinecone, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch).
intuition: Place every item as a point in a 'meaning space'; search becomes 'what is near here?'
friction: Quality depends on the embedding model; recall versus speed versus memory trade-offs; filtered search and updates complicate indexes; high-dimensional geometry defies intuition.
ref: paper | Billion-scale similarity search with GPUs (FAISS) | Johnson, Douze & Jégou, 2017 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1702.08734
ref: paper | Efficient and robust approximate nearest neighbor search using HNSW graphs | Malkov & Yashunin, 2016 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.09320

@@ gpucomp
value: GPU computing uses thousands of simple cores to run the same operation on many data elements at once. Originally for graphics, GPUs became the engines of deep learning, scientific simulation and increasingly data analytics, because these workloads are dominated by parallel arithmetic, especially matrix multiplication.
how: A GPU has many streaming multiprocessors, each running many threads in warps that execute in lockstep, with a hierarchy of registers, shared memory, caches and high-bandwidth memory. Programming models (CUDA, ROCm/HIP, Triton, Metal) express kernels over grids of threads. Tensor cores add dedicated matrix-math units. Performance depends on keeping cores fed: coalesced memory access, data reuse in fast memory, and large batches.
intuition: A CPU is a few expert chefs; a GPU is a thousand line cooks who all chop at the same time.
friction: Data movement between CPU and GPU and within GPU memory is often the bottleneck; branchy or irregular code runs poorly; supply, cost, power and vendor lock-in.
ref: book | Programming Massively Parallel Processors, 4th ed. | Hwu, Kirk & El Hajj, 2022 |
ref: blog | Making Deep Learning Go Brrrr From First Principles | Horace He, 2022 | https://horace.io/brrr_intro.html

@@ hpc
value: High-performance computing techniques let thousands of processors work on one problem. For AI, the key operations are collectives, especially all-reduce, which combine data (such as gradients) across all accelerators in a training job.
how: Collective libraries (MPI, NCCL, RCCL) implement all-reduce, all-gather, reduce-scatter and broadcast using ring and tree algorithms over high-speed links (NVLink, InfiniBand, RoCE). Training combines data parallelism (split the batch), tensor parallelism (split matrices), pipeline parallelism (split layers) and expert parallelism (split mixture-of-experts), arranged to match network topology.
intuition: A relay team that passes partial sums around a ring so everyone ends up with the total.
friction: Communication overhead grows with scale; a single slow or failed GPU stalls a whole job; checkpointing and failure recovery at tens of thousands of GPUs are major engineering problems.
ref: paper | Megatron-LM: Training Multi-Billion Parameter Language Models Using Model Parallelism | Shoeybi et al., 2019 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.08053
ref: paper | ZeRO: Memory Optimizations Toward Training Trillion Parameter Models | Rajbhandari et al., 2019 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.02054

@@ cpucomp
value: General-purpose CPUs run any program and are designed to make a single thread as fast as possible, plus a moderate number of threads in parallel. Operating systems, databases, application servers and most code run on them.
how: Deep pipelines, superscalar out-of-order execution, branch prediction, large multi-level caches, SIMD units, and many cores with coherent shared memory. Performance per watt now drives design, with heterogeneous cores (performance plus efficiency).
intuition: A few very smart workers who can handle any task and guess what you will need next.
friction: Single-thread speed gains slowed after Dennard scaling ended (around 2006); memory latency dominates many workloads; speculative execution enabled attacks like Spectre.
ref: book | Computer Architecture: A Quantitative Approach, 6th ed. | Hennessy & Patterson, 2017 |
ref: paper | A New Golden Age for Computer Architecture | Hennessy & Patterson, CACM 2019 |

@@ webarch
value: Web architecture is the client–server model of the internet: browsers and apps talk to servers over HTTP, find them via DNS, secure the channel with TLS, and receive HTML, JSON or streams. It is the most widely used distributed system in the world.
how: Requests travel through DNS, CDNs, load balancers and gateways to application servers, which call databases and caches. Rendering may happen on the server (SSR), in the browser (SPA) or both. REST's constraints (statelessness, cacheable responses, uniform interface) shaped how APIs are designed.
intuition: Every page is a conversation of short requests and responses, with caches remembering common answers along the way.
friction: Latency from many round trips; state management across stateless requests; security (XSS, CSRF, injection); performance on slow devices.
ref: paper | Architectural Styles and the Design of Network-based Software Architectures (REST) | Roy Fielding, PhD thesis 2000 | https://ics.uci.edu/~fielding/pubs/dissertation/top.htm
ref: book | High Performance Browser Networking | Ilya Grigorik | https://hpbn.co/

@@ vcs
value: Version control tracks every change to code (and increasingly data, models and infrastructure) so teams can collaborate, review, roll back and understand history. It is the backbone of software engineering and CI/CD.
how: Modern distributed systems (Git, Mercurial) store snapshots as content-addressed objects in a directed acyclic graph of commits, with branches as movable pointers; merges reconcile divergent histories. Platforms (GitHub, GitLab) add pull requests, code review and automation. Data versioning tools (lakeFS, Delta and Iceberg time travel) bring similar ideas to data.
intuition: An undo history for a whole team, with labeled save points.
friction: Merge conflicts, large binaries and monorepo scale; history is only as useful as commit discipline.
ref: book | Pro Git, 2nd ed. | Chacon & Straub | https://git-scm.com/book/en/v2

@@ nosql
value: Dynamo-style distributed key-value and wide-column stores (DynamoDB, Cassandra, ScyllaDB, Riak) scale writes horizontally and stay available during failures by relaxing consistency. They power shopping carts, messaging, IoT and time-series workloads at massive scale.
how: Consistent hashing places keys on a ring; each key is replicated to several nodes; reads and writes use tunable quorums; version vectors or timestamps detect conflicts; hinted handoff, read repair and Merkle-tree anti-entropy restore consistency; LSM trees give fast writes.
intuition: Always say yes to a write somewhere, and reconcile copies later.
friction: Application-level conflict handling; limited queries (design tables around access patterns); eventual consistency surprises; operational repair work.
ref: paper | Dynamo: Amazon's Highly Available Key-value Store | DeCandia et al., SOSP 2007 | https://www.allthingsdistributed.com/files/amazon-dynamo-sosp2007.pdf

@@ newsql
value: Distributed SQL ('NewSQL') databases offer SQL and serializable ACID transactions while scaling horizontally across machines and regions: Spanner, CockroachDB, TiDB, YugabyteDB, FoundationDB-based systems. They aim to remove the old trade-off between relational correctness and NoSQL scale.
how: Data is split into ranges, each replicated with Raft or Paxos; transactions use MVCC with timestamps from careful clocks (TrueTime, hybrid logical clocks) and distributed commit protocols; SQL layers plan queries across ranges.
intuition: Many small replicated databases stitched together into one logical database.
friction: Write latency includes consensus round trips; cross-region transactions are slow; contention causes retries; operational complexity is higher than a single-node database.
ref: paper | Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database | Corbett et al., OSDI 2012 |
ref: paper | What's Really New with NewSQL? | Pavlo & Aslett, SIGMOD Record 2016 |

@@ graphdb
value: Graph databases store entities and relationships directly, making queries about connections (paths, neighborhoods, communities) natural and fast: fraud rings, social networks, knowledge graphs, identity graphs, network topology.
how: Property graphs (Neo4j, Neptune, TigerGraph) store nodes and edges with properties and traverse pointers; RDF stores use triples and SPARQL. Query languages include Cypher and the new ISO standard GQL (2024). Graph analytics engines run algorithms like PageRank and connected components over whole graphs.
intuition: When the relationships are the data, store them as first-class citizens.
friction: Distributing large graphs across machines is hard because traversals cross partitions; many 'graph' problems are solved fine with relational joins or batch graph processing.
ref: book | Graph Databases, 2nd ed. | Robinson, Webber & Eifrem, 2015 |

@@ storageengine
value: The storage engine is the part of a database that decides how data is laid out on disk and in memory, and how it is read, written and recovered. The same database idea can perform very differently with different engines (B-tree versus LSM).
how: Engines combine an index structure (B+tree in InnoDB, WiredTiger, SQLite; LSM in RocksDB, Pebble, WiredTiger's LSM mode), a buffer or block cache, a write-ahead log, concurrency control, compression and background maintenance (compaction or page reorganization). Embedded engines like RocksDB are reused inside many systems.
intuition: The engine is the warehouse layout; the database is the business running on top.
friction: The read–write–space trade-off (RUM) means no engine is best for every workload; tuning knobs are numerous.
ref: book | Database Internals | Alex Petrov, 2019 |
ref: paper | Designing Access Methods: The RUM Conjecture | Athanassoulis et al., EDBT 2016 |

@@ langruntime
value: Language runtimes and compilers turn the code people write into instructions machines execute, and manage memory, threads and errors along the way. Their design decides much of a system's performance, safety and developer experience: compare Spark on the JVM, Photon in C++, Polars in Rust and pandas in Python.
how: Ahead-of-time compilers (GCC, Clang/LLVM, rustc, Go) parse, type-check, optimize and generate machine code before running. Virtual machines (JVM, CPython, V8, CLR) load bytecode, interpret it, and JIT-compile hot code with runtime profiling. Memory is managed manually, by ownership rules, by reference counting, or by tracing garbage collectors.
intuition: A translator between human ideas and machine steps, who also cleans up after the program.
friction: Trade-offs between startup time, peak speed, memory safety and simplicity; GC pauses; interoperability costs between runtimes (for example Python UDFs in Spark).
ref: book | Crafting Interpreters | Robert Nystrom, 2021 | https://craftinginterpreters.com/
ref: book | Engineering a Compiler, 3rd ed. | Cooper & Torczon, 2022 |

@@ dimmodel
value: Dimensional modeling (Ralph Kimball, 1996) organizes analytical data into facts (measurable events: impressions, orders, payments) and dimensions (descriptive context: date, campaign, device, customer). The resulting star schemas are easy for people to understand, easy for BI tools to query, and fast for columnar engines. It remains the most widely used way to model warehouse and lakehouse gold layers.
how: Declare the grain of each fact table (one row per impression per device, for example), choose dimensions, and identify numeric facts. Dimensions are denormalized (all attributes in one wide table). Slowly changing dimensions (SCD Type 2) keep history by adding new rows with validity dates. Conformed dimensions are shared across fact tables so metrics can be combined. Surrogate keys decouple from source systems.
intuition: Every business question is 'measure something, sliced by some context'. Store measures in the middle and context around them, like a star.
friction: Requires upfront agreement on grain and definitions; SCD handling and late-arriving facts are tricky; very wide denormalized tables or 'one big table' designs compete with it in columnar systems; it does not by itself solve metric consistency (semantic layers try to).
ref: book | The Data Warehouse Toolkit, 3rd ed. | Kimball & Ross, 2013 |
ref: docs | Kimball dimensional modeling techniques | Kimball Group | https://www.kimballgroup.com/data-warehouse-business-intelligence-resources/kimball-techniques/dimensional-modeling-techniques/

@@ datamesh
value: Data mesh (Zhamak Dehghani, 2019) is an organizational pattern: domain teams own and publish their data as products, with clear owners, documentation, quality guarantees and contracts, on a self-serve platform, under federated governance. Data contracts make the promises explicit and testable. It addresses central data teams becoming bottlenecks.
how: Each domain exposes data products (tables, streams, APIs) with schemas, SLAs and semantics; a data contract defines schema, quality checks, freshness and compatibility rules, enforced in CI and at runtime; a platform team provides storage, catalog, access control and tooling; governance sets global standards (identity, privacy, interoperability).
intuition: Treat data like an API that other teams depend on, owned by the people who understand it best.
friction: Requires organizational change and domain teams with data skills; risk of duplicated effort and inconsistent definitions; tooling for contracts is young.
ref: blog | Data Mesh Principles and Logical Architecture | Zhamak Dehghani, 2020 | https://martinfowler.com/articles/data-mesh-principles.html
ref: book | Data Contracts | Andrew Jones, 2023 |

@@ multitenancy
value: Multi-tenancy lets many customers (or internal teams) share one system safely, which lowers cost and simplifies operations for SaaS products and shared data platforms.
how: Isolation can be physical (separate clusters or databases per tenant), logical (shared database with tenant ID on every row, separate schemas) or hybrid ('silo' for big tenants, 'pool' for small ones). Fairness needs per-tenant quotas, rate limits and scheduling; security needs row-level access control and per-tenant encryption keys; noisy-neighbor protection uses resource isolation (cgroups, separate queues, partitioning by tenant).
intuition: An apartment building: shared structure, private units, and rules so one neighbor's party doesn't keep everyone awake.
friction: A bug can leak data across tenants; one heavy tenant can degrade others; per-tenant customization and migrations get complicated; cost attribution needs careful metering.
ref: blog | SaaS Tenant Isolation Strategies | AWS whitepaper, 2020 |
ref: blog | Multi-tenant architecture guidance | Microsoft Azure Architecture Center | https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/architecture/guide/multitenant/overview

@@ durableexec
value: Durable execution makes long-running processes reliable without hand-written state machines: a workflow can wait for days, call flaky services, and survive crashes and deploys, resuming exactly where it was. Temporal, AWS Step Functions, Azure Durable Functions, Restate and newer orchestrators provide it.
how: The engine records each step's inputs and results in a durable log. If a worker fails, the workflow function is re-run from the start, but completed steps return their recorded results instead of executing again (deterministic replay), so the code reaches the same point and continues. Side-effecting steps are retried with idempotency; long sagas undo earlier steps with compensating actions.
intuition: Save the game at every checkpoint, so a crash only costs you the last move.
friction: Workflow code must be deterministic; versioning in-flight workflows is tricky; the engine is a critical dependency.
ref: docs | Temporal documentation: workflows and durable execution | Temporal | https://docs.temporal.io/
ref: paper | Sagas | Garcia-Molina & Salem, SIGMOD 1987 |

@@ cicd
value: Continuous integration and continuous delivery build, test and deploy every change automatically in small increments. It is the single strongest predictor of software delivery performance in the DORA research, and the foundation for safe, frequent releases.
how: On every commit, a pipeline compiles code, runs unit, integration and end-to-end tests, scans for vulnerabilities, builds artifacts, and deploys through environments using strategies like blue-green, canary and feature flags, with automatic rollback on failure. Infrastructure and data pipelines follow the same pattern.
intuition: Many small, tested steps are safer than one big leap.
friction: Flaky tests erode trust; slow pipelines discourage small commits; testing data and ML pipelines is harder than testing code; distributed systems need deeper tests (simulation, chaos engineering).
ref: book | Accelerate | Forsgren, Humble & Kim, 2018 |
ref: book | Continuous Delivery | Humble & Farley, 2010 |

@@ blockchain
value: Blockchains maintain a shared, tamper-evident ledger among participants who do not trust each other and may not even know each other, without a central operator. They enable cryptocurrencies, programmable financial contracts and some supply-chain and identity uses.
how: Transactions are batched into blocks; each block contains a hash of the previous block and a Merkle root of its transactions, so altering history changes every later hash. Consensus among untrusted nodes uses proof of work (costly hashing), proof of stake (collateral plus Byzantine voting), or classic BFT protocols for permissioned chains. Smart contracts are programs executed identically by every node.
intuition: A public notebook copied by everyone, where tearing out a page is obvious and adding a page costs real effort.
friction: Low throughput and high latency compared with centralized databases; energy use (proof of work); smart-contract security bugs; most enterprise problems do not need trustless consensus.
ref: paper | Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System | Satoshi Nakamoto, 2008 | https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf
ref: book | Bitcoin and Cryptocurrency Technologies | Narayanan et al., 2016 |

@@ mediastream
value: Video streaming and codecs make it possible to watch high-quality video over ordinary internet connections. Video is most of internet traffic, and on CTV it is the product itself. Compression shrinks raw video by two to three orders of magnitude; adaptive streaming keeps playback smooth as networks change.
how: Codecs exploit spatial redundancy (neighboring pixels are similar: transform coding with the DCT), temporal redundancy (consecutive frames are similar: motion compensation), and perceptual limits (the eye barely sees fine color detail or some high frequencies: chroma subsampling and quantization). Streams are packaged into short segments at several bitrates (HLS, DASH), cached by CDNs, and players switch quality with adaptive bitrate algorithms. Ads are inserted client-side or server-side (SSAI).
intuition: Send only what changes, only as precisely as the eye can tell, in pieces the network can handle right now.
friction: Encoding high-quality ladders is compute-expensive; low latency for live events is hard; device fragmentation on smart TVs; DRM and ad-insertion complexity.
ref: book | Video Demystified, 5th ed. | Keith Jack, 2007 |
ref: blog | Per-Title Encode Optimization | Netflix Technology Blog, 2015 |

@@ contentid
value: Content recognition identifies media from its signal, even when it is noisy, compressed, cropped or overlaid. It powers music identification (Shazam), copyright detection (YouTube Content ID), broadcast monitoring, ad verification and TV viewership measurement (ACR).
how: Extract features that survive distortion (audio spectrogram peaks, video frame signatures, perceptual hashes), turn them into compact hashes, and index reference content so a query fingerprint can be matched by hash lookups and then verified by checking that many matches line up consistently in time.
intuition: Recognize a face from a few distinctive features, not from every pixel.
friction: Robustness versus false positives; scaling indexes to all live channels and huge catalogs; latency for real-time matching; privacy and consent.
ref: paper | An Industrial-Strength Audio Search Algorithm | Avery Wang, ISMIR 2003 |
ref: paper | A Highly Robust Audio Fingerprinting System | Haitsma & Kalker, ISMIR 2002 |

@@ adtech
value: Programmatic advertising buys and sells ad impressions automatically, one at a time, in real time. It funds much of the free internet and is increasingly central to connected TV. The value is matching each impression to the advertiser who values it most, using data about context and audience.
how: Publishers (supply) connect through supply-side platforms (SSPs) to ad exchanges; advertisers (demand) bid through demand-side platforms (DSPs). When an impression is available, the exchange sends OpenRTB bid requests; DSPs predict value (click, conversion, view probability) with ML models and bid within about 100 ms; an auction picks the winner; pixels and logs record impressions and conversions for billing, attribution and optimization. Budget pacing, frequency capping and fraud filtering run alongside.
intuition: A worldwide auction house that sells a single ad view millions of times per second.
friction: Enormous scale and latency constraints; fraud and supply-chain opacity; privacy regulation and the loss of third-party cookies and mobile IDs; measurement and attribution disputes.
ref: spec | OpenRTB specification | IAB Tech Lab | https://iabtechlab.com/standards/openrtb/
ref: paper | Real-Time Bidding for Online Advertising: Measurement and Analysis | Yuan, Wang & Zhao, 2013 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1306.6542

@@ identity
value: Identity resolution links the many identifiers that belong to the same person or household (cookies, device IDs, emails, IP-based TV identifiers, login IDs) into one profile, and audience building groups profiles into segments ('sports viewers', 'new car intenders'). It is how advertisers reach the right people across devices and measure results.
how: Deterministic matching uses shared keys (a hashed email seen on two devices); probabilistic matching uses signals like IP address, location and timing with statistical models. Links form an identity graph; connected components define households or people. Audiences are built from behavior (viewing, browsing, purchases) with rules, models or lookalike expansion, under consent and privacy constraints.
intuition: Connect the dots between devices, then group people by what they do.
friction: Privacy regulation and consent; probabilistic links have errors that compound; IDs churn as cookies expire and devices change; clean rooms and privacy-enhancing technologies change how data can be joined.
ref: book | Programmatic Advertising | Busch (ed.), 2016 |
ref: blog | What is an identity graph? | IAB Tech Lab resources | https://iabtechlab.com/

@@ experimentation
value: Online controlled experiments (A/B tests) are the gold standard for deciding whether a change (a feature, a model, an ad strategy) actually improves outcomes. Companies like Microsoft, Google, Booking and Netflix run thousands per year.
how: Randomly assign users (or devices, households, geographies) to control and treatment; run long enough to collect samples; compare metrics with statistical tests and confidence intervals; guard against pitfalls with sample-ratio checks, pre-registered metrics, variance reduction (CUPED) and corrections for multiple comparisons. Ad lift studies use the same logic with holdout groups.
intuition: The only way to know whether X causes Y is to change X at random and watch Y.
friction: Peeking at results inflates false positives; network effects and interference between users break independence; long-term effects differ from short-term; small effects need huge samples.
ref: book | Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments | Kohavi, Tang & Xu, 2020 |
ref: paper | Improving the Sensitivity of Online Controlled Experiments by Utilizing Pre-Experiment Data (CUPED) | Deng et al., WSDM 2013 |

@@ reasoningmodels
value: Reasoning models (OpenAI's o1 in 2024, DeepSeek-R1, Claude with extended thinking, Gemini thinking models and successors) spend extra computation at inference time thinking through a problem step by step before answering. They improved results dramatically on maths, coding and multi-step tasks, and showed a new scaling axis: test-time compute.
how: Models are trained with reinforcement learning on problems with checkable answers (maths, code with tests), rewarding correct final results; they learn to produce long chains of thought that explore, verify and backtrack. At inference, more thinking tokens, sampling several solutions and choosing by verification or voting further improve accuracy.
intuition: Give the model scratch paper and time, and train it to use them well.
friction: Higher latency and cost per answer; chains of thought are not always faithful explanations; gains are largest where answers can be verified automatically.
ref: paper | Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in Large Language Models | Wei et al., 2022 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2201.11903
ref: paper | DeepSeek-R1: Incentivizing Reasoning Capability in LLMs via RL | DeepSeek-AI, 2025 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948

@@ multimodal
value: Multimodal models understand and generate across text, images, audio and video in one system: describe a photo, read a chart, answer questions about a video, transcribe and translate speech. They make AI useful for the many tasks that are not purely text.
how: Each modality is encoded into tokens or embeddings (image patches through a vision encoder, audio frames through an audio encoder) and processed by a shared transformer, sometimes trained natively on mixed data from the start. Contrastive pretraining (CLIP) aligns images and text in one embedding space; generation uses diffusion or autoregressive decoders.
intuition: Translate every sense into the same internal language.
friction: Much larger compute and data needs; evaluation is harder; video is especially expensive in tokens; hallucinations about visual details.
ref: paper | Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision (CLIP) | Radford et al., 2021 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.00020
ref: paper | Flamingo: a Visual Language Model for Few-Shot Learning | Alayrac et al., 2022 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.14198
