@@ quantum
value: Quantum mechanics is the set of rules that describe matter at the scale of atoms and electrons. Its central claim is simple to state and strange to accept: a particle is described by a wave (the wavefunction), the wave tells you only the probabilities of measurement results, and when a particle is confined, its allowed energies come in discrete steps, not in a smooth range. Classical physics, with its balls and fields, gets all three points wrong at small scales.
Every device a software engineer depends on rests on these rules. A transistor works because electrons in a silicon crystal can occupy only certain energy bands (the band gap). Flash memory works because electrons can tunnel through a thin insulator. A laser, an LED, a photodiode in a fibre link, and an atomic clock all work because atoms and solids absorb and emit light only at exact energies. Hard-disk read heads use electron spin. No classical theory can explain why silicon is a semiconductor, copper is a metal and glass is an insulator. Quantum mechanics explains all three from the same equation.
For the layers above, quantum mechanics gives a small number of ideas that keep returning. Energy levels are discrete when a particle is confined, which leads to band theory (bandgap) and to the exact frequencies used by atomic clocks. Waves can leak into places a classical particle cannot go, which gives tunnelling. Electron spin gives magnetism. The state of a system is a vector and measurements are matrices, which is why linear algebra (L4) is the language of the theory, and the Born rule makes the outcomes probabilistic, which is why probability (L4) is its second language. Quantum computing is what you get when you treat these rules as a computing resource instead of a nuisance.
terms: Wavefunction ψ — a complex-valued function (or vector) that holds everything that can be known about a quantum system; it changes smoothly in time by the Schrödinger equation.
Born rule — the probability of finding a particle at a place is the square of the size of its wavefunction there, |ψ|².
Quantum number — an integer (or half-integer) that labels one allowed state, for example the energy level n of an electron in an atom.
Planck's constant h — the basic unit linking energy and frequency: E = hf, with h ≈ 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.
de Broglie wavelength — the wavelength of a particle, λ = h / p, where p is its momentum; it tells you when wave effects matter.
Superposition — a state that is a weighted sum of other states; a measurement picks one outcome with probability set by the weights.
Pauli exclusion principle — two electrons cannot occupy exactly the same quantum state; this is why atoms have shells and solids have filled bands.
Spin — an intrinsic two-valued property of the electron (up or down), with no classical equivalent; it carries a magnetic moment.
Electron-volt (eV) — the energy gained by one electron across 1 volt, about 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; the natural unit for atomic and solid-state energies.
history: By 1900 classical physics had a few stubborn failures. Heated objects glow with a spectrum that classical theory predicted would be infinite at short wavelengths. In 1900 Max Planck fixed the formula by assuming that light of frequency f is exchanged only in packets of energy hf. He saw it as a mathematical trick. In 1905 Albert Einstein took it seriously: to explain the photoelectric effect (light kicks electrons out of a metal only if the frequency is high enough, whatever the brightness), he proposed that light itself comes in quanta.
In 1913 Niels Bohr explained the sharp colours of hydrogen by allowing only certain orbits with energies of −13.6 eV / n². In 1924 Louis de Broglie suggested that matter also has a wavelength, and electron diffraction experiments (Davisson and Germer, 1927) confirmed it. Then came two formulations in two years. Werner Heisenberg, with Born and Jordan, wrote matrix mechanics in 1925. Erwin Schrödinger wrote his wave equation in 1926. Soon after, Schrödinger and others showed that the two are the same theory. Max Born gave the probability reading of the wavefunction in 1926, Heisenberg stated the uncertainty principle in 1927, and Wolfgang Pauli had stated the exclusion principle in 1925. Paul Dirac (1928) combined the theory with special relativity and predicted antimatter.
The step that matters for computing came a few years later. Felix Bloch (1928) showed how electrons move in a periodic crystal, and Alan Wilson (1931) used band theory to explain metals, insulators and semiconductors. Those papers led directly to the transistor at Bell Labs in 1947, to integrated circuits and to lasers (1960). Later milestones are density functional theory (Hohenberg, Kohn and Sham, 1964 to 1965), which lets computers predict material properties, and the idea of quantum computation (Feynman 1981, Deutsch 1985, Shor 1994).
how: The core of the theory is the Schrödinger equation: iħ ∂ψ/∂t = Hψ. Here ψ is the wavefunction, H is the Hamiltonian (an operator that stands for the total energy: kinetic plus potential), and ħ is h divided by 2π. Between measurements, the state evolves smoothly and deterministically, like a wave. The solutions that do not change shape in time are the energy eigenstates, which satisfy Hψ = Eψ. This is an eigenvalue problem. The allowed energies E are the eigenvalues, and this is where "quantised" energy comes from: it is not a new rule, it is what boundary conditions do to a wave, in the same way that a guitar string fixed at both ends can ring only at certain pitches. Here linear algebra (L4) is not a tool added afterwards; the states are vectors, observables are matrices, and the allowed measurement results are eigenvalues.
Measurement is where probability (L4) enters. If a state is a sum of eigenstates, ψ = Σ cₙ ψₙ, then measuring the energy gives the value Eₙ with probability |cₙ|², and afterwards the state is ψₙ. The coefficients cₙ are complex numbers, so they can add or cancel before you square them. This interference of amplitudes, not randomness alone, is what makes quantum behaviour different from a classical coin flip. In the double-slit experiment, single electrons arrive one at a time yet build up a fringe pattern, because the amplitude through each slit adds before squaring.
Confinement gives discrete energies. For an electron in a one-dimensional box of width L, the only allowed wavelengths fit a whole number of half-waves in the box, so E_n = n²h² / (8mL²). Narrower boxes give larger spacing. For the hydrogen atom, the Coulomb potential gives E_n = −13.6 eV / n². Real atoms have many electrons, and here the Pauli exclusion principle matters: each state holds at most one electron per spin direction, so electrons fill levels from the bottom up. This produces the shell structure of the periodic table, and therefore all of chemistry.
Now bring many atoms together. In a crystal, the electron sees a repeating potential. Bloch's theorem says the allowed states are waves modulated by the lattice period, labelled by a crystal momentum k. The sharp atomic levels spread into bands of closely spaced states, with forbidden gaps between them. The Pauli principle then decides everything: if the highest occupied band is partly filled, electrons can move easily (a metal); if a band is full and the next is empty, there is a gap (an insulator or a semiconductor). Band theory and the band gap are the direct descendants of this paragraph.
Temperature enters through statistics. Electrons are fermions, so at thermal equilibrium the chance that a state of energy E is occupied is given by the Fermi–Dirac function, 1 / (e^((E − μ)/kT) + 1), where μ is the chemical potential (the Fermi level). At absolute zero it is a sharp step: everything below μ is full and everything above is empty. At 300 K the step is blurred over a few kT (about 26 meV each), and only electrons within that thin window can respond to a small voltage. In a semiconductor the Fermi level sits inside the gap, far from both bands, so the tail of this function gives a very small number of free carriers (about 10¹⁰ per cm³ in pure silicon against about 5 × 10²² atoms per cm³). Doping moves the Fermi level and changes that number by many orders of magnitude, which is the whole business of making transistors.
Observables that cannot be measured together are another structural feature. Position and momentum are matrices that do not commute (xp − px = iħ), so no state can have a sharp value of both. The uncertainty principle is just this fact written as an inequality. The same non-commuting structure holds for the three spin components, and it is exactly what a qubit uses.
Two more consequences matter for engineers. The uncertainty principle, Δx · Δp ≥ ħ/2, says that squeezing an electron into a small space forces a large spread of momentum, and therefore of energy. This sets a floor on how small a confined device can be before its energy levels move. And because the wavefunction decays inside a barrier but does not drop to zero, a thin barrier can be crossed (tunnelling). Spin, finally, is an extra two-valued degree of freedom that gives each electron a small magnetic moment and, through exchange interaction between electrons, is the source of ferromagnetism.

example: Take an electron confined in a box 1 nanometre wide, a crude model of an electron trapped in a nanostructure. The formula is E_n = n²h² / (8mL²). Use h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, m = 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg and L = 1 × 10⁻⁹ m. First h² = 4.39 × 10⁻⁶⁷. Then 8mL² = 8 × 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ × 10⁻¹⁸ = 7.29 × 10⁻⁴⁸. The ratio is 6.02 × 10⁻²⁰ J. Dividing by 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J/eV gives E₁ ≈ 0.376 eV.
The other levels follow by n²: E₂ = 4 × 0.376 = 1.50 eV, E₃ = 3.38 eV, E₄ = 6.02 eV. If the electron drops from n = 2 to n = 1, it releases 1.50 − 0.376 = 1.13 eV. The matching photon has wavelength λ = 1240 eV·nm / 1.13 eV ≈ 1,100 nm, which is infrared light. Now halve the box to 0.5 nm. All energies grow by 4, so the same jump releases 4.5 eV, an ultraviolet photon of about 275 nm. This is the principle behind quantum dots, whose colour is set by their size.
Compare this with room temperature. Thermal energy is kT ≈ 0.0259 eV at 300 K. The gap between the first two levels (1.13 eV) is about 44 times kT, so the electron is frozen in the lowest state and thermal jostling cannot excite it. Quantum effects are visible here. Now try a 100 nm box: the spacing falls by 10,000 (since 1/L²) to about 0.0001 eV, far below kT, and the levels blur into a continuum. The classical model works again. This simple calculation tells you the rule of thumb: quantum behaviour matters when the spacing between levels is larger than kT, or when the size is close to the electron's wavelength (a few nanometres in silicon).
Finally, a check on the leakage story. A barrier 1 nm thick with a height of 3 eV, seen by an electron, gives a decay constant κ = √(2m·V)/ħ = √(2 × 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ × 3 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹) / 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ ≈ 8.9 × 10⁹ per metre. The transmission is about e^(−2κd) = e^(−17.8) ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁸. Make the barrier 0.5 nm and the transmission becomes about e^(−8.9) ≈ 1.4 × 10⁻⁴, roughly 7,000 times larger. Halving the thickness multiplied the leakage by thousands. This is why thin gate oxides leaked so heavily.
intuition: At small sizes an electron behaves like a ripple in a pond that must fit its container, not like a marble that can sit anywhere.
Once you accept that a particle is a wave, quantisation stops being mysterious. Waves in a closed space can only exist as patterns that fit: a whole number of bumps. Each pattern has its own energy, so the energies are a list, not a continuum. Probability follows because a wave is spread out, so "where is the particle?" has no single answer until something forces a result. The mathematics is the mathematics of vibrations, with one extra rule (the Born rule) that turns wave size into the chance of a click on a detector. Large objects look classical because their wavelengths are absurdly short and their levels are packed so tightly that nobody can see the steps.
mistakes: People often think "quantum" means "small and random, so nothing can be predicted". In fact the wavefunction evolves by an exact, deterministic equation. The randomness appears only in measurement outcomes, and even then the probabilities are predicted to many decimal places. Atomic clocks and chip design depend on that precision.
People often think a particle is in "both states at once" in a way that is simply a lack of knowledge. In fact superposition is not ignorance. The amplitudes interfere, which a classical probability mixture cannot do. In the double-slit experiment, blocking one slit makes some places brighter that were dark with both slits open. Ignorance cannot give this effect.
People often think quantum effects are limited to the tiny and the cold. In fact they decide properties of everyday solids at room temperature: the colour of copper, the hardness of a diamond, the fact that your chair does not collapse (Pauli exclusion). What is hard is seeing coherent quantum behaviour in large systems, because interaction with the environment (decoherence) destroys it quickly.
People often think the observer must be a conscious mind. In fact "measurement" means any interaction that records the result in the environment, such as a photon hitting a detector or a collision with a gas molecule. How exactly the single outcome appears is still debated (Copenhagen, many-worlds and other readings), but all readings predict the same experimental numbers, so engineers can ignore the dispute.
tradeoffs: Choose a classical or semi-classical model when the device is large compared with the electron wavelength (roughly above 10 to 20 nm in silicon) and the energy-level spacing is below kT (about 26 meV at 300 K). The drift-diffusion model with Ohm's law handles billions of electrons cheaply and is what circuit simulators like SPICE use. You give up all effects that depend on wavelength: tunnelling, quantum confinement and ballistic transport. Use it for wires, large MOSFETs and board-level power.
Choose quantum-corrected models for devices with dimensions of 5 to 10 nm. Practical tools add a quantum correction to a classical simulation (density-gradient models, or Schrödinger-Poisson solvers for the thin channel), and the cost is moderate. For nanosheet transistors with channels only a few nanometres thick, confinement shifts the threshold voltage and cannot be ignored. You pay in simulation time and in more fitted parameters.
Choose full quantum transport (non-equilibrium Green's function methods) or first-principles density functional theory (DFT) when you need to predict a new material, an interface or a molecule from scratch. DFT scales roughly with the cube of the number of electrons, so it handles hundreds to a few thousand atoms on a cluster, not a whole chip. It is also approximate in a known way: common functionals underestimate semiconductor band gaps, often by 30 to 50 percent for silicon-like materials. You give up speed and sometimes accuracy (DFT) for a model with no fitted parameters.
Choose the effective-mass approximation when you want band structure information with simple formulas. It treats an electron in a crystal as a free particle with a different mass (about 0.26 mₑ for conduction in silicon, in the transport direction, and different along other axes). It is accurate close to band edges and fails at high energies or in strongly strained, very thin layers. You give up the detailed band shape in return for formulas that fit on a page.
Choose the quantum model for system-level questions only when you truly need quantum effects as a resource, as in quantum computing. In that case, the approximations above no longer help: simulating n qubits exactly on a classical machine needs 2ⁿ complex amplitudes, so about 50 qubits already needs petabytes. Classical simulation becomes the bottleneck itself.
friction: The theory is spectacularly accurate (the electron's magnetic moment is predicted to about ten digits), but it is hard to use. Exact solutions exist only for a few systems, such as hydrogen and the box. Anything with many interacting electrons needs approximations, and the cost of an exact solution grows exponentially with the number of particles. This is the same reason quantum computers are interesting: classical machines cannot follow the full state.
The meaning of the theory is still disputed. The measurement problem (why a single outcome appears) has several rival interpretations that cannot be distinguished by experiment today. Quantum theory also does not fit cleanly with gravity; a full quantum theory of gravity is still an open problem, though this does not matter for any chip.
For engineers, the practical pain is that quantum effects arrive as problems when devices shrink. Tunnelling raises leakage current in thin oxides, random dopant atoms change a tiny transistor's threshold, and the uncertainty principle together with thermal noise limits how precisely a few electrons can be controlled. In quantum hardware, the same physics causes decoherence: a qubit loses its state in microseconds to milliseconds because stray fields and heat disturb it. Making devices that use quantum effects (flash cells, lasers, qubits) and making devices that suffer from them (logic at 2 nm) are two sides of the same discipline.
practice: Most engineers will never solve the Schrödinger equation, so the useful skill is to know which quantum idea explains which device, and to know the rule of thumb for when it matters. Compare size to wavelength: electrons in silicon have a thermal de Broglie wavelength of roughly 4 to 8 nm at 300 K, so below about 10 nm expect confinement and tunnelling. Compare level spacing to kT: if spacing is much larger than 26 meV, only the lowest states are used. Compare photon energy with the gap: wavelength in nm ≈ 1240 / energy in eV, so silicon's 1.12 eV gap corresponds to about 1,100 nm, which is why silicon photodetectors stop working beyond that and why fibre systems at 1,310 and 1,550 nm use germanium or InGaAs detectors.
To study the subject, start with the Feynman Lectures Vol. III (chapters 1 to 3 on amplitudes and 8 on the Schrödinger equation) for intuition, then a modern textbook such as Griffiths for the mathematics. If your interest is computing, go next to band theory, then to the p-n junction and the transistor, and then to quantum computing, where Nielsen and Chuang start from the same postulates stated in the language of linear algebra. Keep a small set of numbers in your head: 1 eV = 11,600 K, kT = 26 meV at room temperature, 1,240 eV·nm for photons, and h = 4.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV·s.

@@ bandgap
value: In a crystal, electrons cannot have just any energy. Their allowed energies form wide ranges called bands, separated by ranges where no electron state exists. The band gap is the width of the forbidden range between the top of the last filled band (the valence band) and the bottom of the first empty band (the conduction band). It is the energy a bound electron needs to become a free, current-carrying one. Silicon's gap is about 1.12 eV at room temperature.
The size of the gap decides what a material is. With no gap (metals) current always flows. With a very large gap, such as diamond at about 5.5 eV or glass at 8 eV or more, almost no electron can be freed and the material is an insulator. In between sits the semiconductor. At 1.12 eV, silicon at room temperature has only about 10¹⁰ free electrons per cm³, against about 5 × 10²² atoms per cm³, so it nearly insulates when pure. A small amount of doping, an electric field or light can change that number by factors of a million. This controllability, not any special conductivity, is why silicon is the base of digital logic: a transistor needs a material that can be switched from "almost an insulator" to "a decent conductor" with a small voltage.
The idea stands on quantum mechanics (L5). Quantum mechanics says electrons are waves, that waves in a periodic lattice form allowed and forbidden energy ranges, and that the Pauli exclusion principle fills the allowed states from the bottom, one electron per spin. Band theory is what you get when you apply those three facts to a crystal. For the layers above, the gap sets the height of the barrier in a p–n junction, the colour of an LED, the wavelength a photodetector can see, the voltage a power device can block, and the leakage current of a chip.
terms: Valence band — the highest energy band that is full of electrons at absolute zero; its electrons are bound in chemical bonds.
Conduction band — the next band above; electrons placed here can move freely and carry current.
Band gap Eg — the energy difference between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band, in electron-volts.
Hole — the empty place left in the valence band when an electron leaves; it behaves like a positive charge carrier.
Fermi level — the energy at which a state has a 50% chance of being occupied; its position in the gap says how many electrons and holes there are.
Direct and indirect gap — in a direct gap material, the lowest conduction state sits at the same crystal momentum as the highest valence state, so light can move an electron across; in an indirect material (silicon) a lattice vibration must also help.
Intrinsic carrier density nᵢ — the number of free electrons (equal to the number of holes) per cm³ in a pure semiconductor at a given temperature.
Effective mass — the mass a carrier appears to have in response to a force while it moves through the crystal; it packs the lattice's effect into one number.
history: The first step was Felix Bloch's 1928 theorem: in a periodic potential, electron states are plane waves multiplied by a function with the period of the lattice. Rudolf Peierls and Léon Brillouin worked out in the early 1930s how a weak lattice potential opens gaps in the energy spectrum. In 1931 Alan Wilson, building on this, gave the first clear account of why some solids are metals, some insulators and some semiconductors: it is a matter of whether bands are partly filled, full, or full with a small gap above. Wilson's paper also said that a semiconductor's conductivity should grow with temperature, which matched experiment.
For a while, band theory was a tool for explaining. The transistor changed that. John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley at Bell Labs built the first transistor in 1947 using germanium, whose gap is 0.66 eV. Germanium was easier to purify at the time, but its small gap meant that thermal carriers became a serious problem above about 75 degrees Celsius. Silicon, with its 1.12 eV gap, kept leakage far lower at the same temperature, and silicon dioxide, which grows as a stable insulating layer on silicon, made manufacturing practical. Gordon Teal at Texas Instruments made the first silicon transistor in 1954, and silicon took over in the late 1950s.
Later decades widened the menu of gaps. Direct-gap compound semiconductors such as GaAs were used for lasers and LEDs from the 1960s. Shuji Nakamura, Isamu Akasaki and Hiroshi Amano built efficient blue LEDs from GaN in the early 1990s, which opened the way to white LED lighting (Nobel Prize in Physics, 2014). Wide-gap SiC and GaN power devices became commercial in the 2010s. Today the field uses band structures computed with density functional theory and its corrections, to design alloys and heterostructures (layers with different gaps) on purpose, an approach known as band-gap engineering.
how: Start with one atom. It has sharp energy levels. Bring N identical atoms together to form a crystal. Their outer-electron wavefunctions overlap, and the Pauli exclusion principle (from quantum mechanics) forbids N electrons from sharing one level, so each atomic level splits into N slightly different levels. For N around 10²³ these are packed so closely that they form a continuous band. The width of a band depends on how strongly neighbouring atoms overlap, typically a few eV. This picture is called tight-binding. A second view starts from nearly free electrons: an electron wave moving through the lattice is scattered when its wavelength matches twice the lattice spacing (Bragg reflection). Waves at that wavelength form standing waves, with two possible energies depending on whether the charge piles up on the ions or between them. The energy difference between them is the gap.
Each band is labelled by crystal momentum k. A plot of energy against k is the band structure. Silicon's valence band has its top at k = 0 (the Γ point). Its conduction band has its bottom near 85% of the way to the zone edge in the [100] direction. Because the minimum and the maximum are at different k, silicon has an indirect gap. A photon carries energy but almost no momentum, so it cannot move an electron from the valence maximum to the conduction minimum alone. A phonon (a lattice vibration) must provide the missing momentum at the same moment. That three-body event is rare, which makes silicon a weak emitter of light and a weak, thick absorber. GaAs has both extremes at k = 0, so a photon can move an electron straight across: it is a direct gap, and an excellent light source.
Which bands are filled decides the electrical behaviour. In a metal the highest band is partly filled, so a small electric field can shift electrons to nearby empty states. In an insulator or semiconductor, the valence band is completely full. A full band carries no net current, because for every electron moving right another moves left and there is no empty state to move into. Current requires an electron in the conduction band (or a hole in the valence band). At temperature T, the probability that a state at energy E is occupied is the Fermi–Dirac function, whose tail beyond the gap gives nᵢ ∝ T^(3/2) · exp(−Eg / 2kT). The factor 2 in the exponent arises because the Fermi level sits mid-gap in a pure material, so each carrier needs only Eg/2 of "distance" in thermal terms. This single formula explains why a semiconductor's resistance falls as it heats (unlike a metal), and why leakage in chips rises steeply with temperature.
The gap alone is not what makes a material useful; the ability to move the Fermi level is. Adding a donor atom, such as phosphorus in silicon, puts an extra electron into a state about 45 meV below the conduction band. Since kT at room temperature is 26 meV, nearly all donors are ionised, so the electron density becomes the doping density (say 10¹⁶ cm⁻³), about a million times the intrinsic value. An acceptor such as boron does the opposite and makes holes. This is the content of the doping concept, and it is possible only because the gap is small enough for dopant states to sit within thermal reach of a band edge. Where p-type and n-type regions meet, the difference in Fermi levels produces the built-in potential of a p–n junction, which is on the order of 0.7 V for silicon, a fraction of its 1.12 eV gap.
The gap also fixes optical behaviour. A photon with energy below Eg passes through (silicon is transparent beyond about 1,100 nm). A photon with energy above Eg is absorbed and creates an electron–hole pair, which is how solar cells and image sensors work. When an electron falls back across the gap and gives up its energy as a photon, the colour is λ(nm) ≈ 1240 / Eg(eV). This is why a red LED is made of AlGaInP (Eg about 1.9 eV) and a blue LED is made of InGaN (about 2.7 eV).

example: Compare germanium and silicon at room temperature using nᵢ ∝ T^(3/2) · exp(−Eg / 2kT). At 300 K, kT = 0.0259 eV. For silicon, Eg / 2kT = 1.12 / 0.0518 = 21.6, so the exponential is e^(−21.6), about 4 × 10⁻¹⁰. For germanium, 0.66 / 0.0518 = 12.7, so the exponential is e^(−12.7), about 3 × 10⁻⁶. The ratio of the exponentials is about 7,000. The real ratio of nᵢ is smaller, about 2,400 (germanium 2 × 10¹³ against silicon 1 × 10¹⁰ per cm³), because the prefactors differ. The direction and the order of magnitude are right: a gap smaller by only 0.46 eV gives thousands of times more thermal carriers. That is why germanium transistors leaked badly when warm.
Now heat silicon from 300 K to 400 K (about 127 degrees Celsius, a hot chip). The prefactor T^(3/2) grows by (400/300)^1.5 = 1.54. The exponent changes from 21.6 to 1.12 / (2 × 0.03447) = 16.2, so the exponential grows by e^(5.4) ≈ 220. The total is roughly 340 times more intrinsic carriers (the measured value is a few hundred times, because the gap itself shrinks slightly with temperature). Silicon at 400 K has nᵢ of order 10¹² cm⁻³, still far below typical dopant densities of 10¹⁵ to 10¹⁹ cm⁻³, so the doped regions still work. But at about 150 degrees Celsius and above, in lightly doped regions, intrinsic carriers start to compete with the dopants. That sets the upper temperature limit of silicon devices at roughly 150 to 200 degrees Celsius. SiC, whose gap is three times wider, has nᵢ many orders of magnitude lower at the same temperature and works above 300 degrees Celsius.
A light example: a photodetector for fibre at 1,550 nm. The photon energy is 1240 / 1550 = 0.80 eV, below silicon's gap, so silicon cannot detect it. Germanium (0.66 eV) absorbs it, and so does InGaAs (0.75 eV, matched to InP substrates). Engineers therefore use InGaAs or Ge-on-Si photodiodes for telecom light, and silicon for visible light and for the 850 nm light used in short links (1240 / 850 = 1.46 eV, above the gap).
intuition: The gap is a step in the floor: electrons at the bottom can only climb it when something pays the exact energy, so the material is nearly an insulator that you can unlock on demand.
The idea is natural because it follows directly from waves in a repeating structure. A wave that fits the lattice can pass, and a wave that does not fit is reflected, which leaves a band of forbidden energies. Filling those allowed states with a fixed number of electrons then forces a clear question: is the last band full or not? A full band has no free states to move into, so it cannot conduct. Only a small gap lets you pay the "step" cheaply with heat, light or a dopant atom, which is what makes a semiconductor tunable instead of simply bad or good.
mistakes: People often think the band gap is a physical hole or a spatial gap in the crystal. In fact it is a gap in energy, not in space. Electrons and the lattice occupy the same places; there are just no wave solutions with energies inside the gap.
People often think a bigger gap is always better. In fact, it depends on the job. A wide gap (GaN, SiC) blocks high voltage and survives heat, but needs more energy to switch carriers and is harder to dope. A solar cell works best near 1.3 to 1.4 eV (the Shockley–Queisser optimum, about 33% efficiency), because a gap that is too wide wastes the low-energy photons and one that is too small wastes the excess energy of high-energy photons as heat. Silicon at 1.12 eV is close to that optimum, which is one reason it is a good solar material too.
People often think silicon is used because it conducts well, or because it has the best gap for everything. In fact it is used for a mix of reasons. It is abundant (sand), its oxide is a stable, high-quality insulator that can be grown on its surface, and it can be purified to extreme levels. It is a poor light emitter (indirect gap), and its electron mobility is lower than GaAs. It won on manufacturing, not on physics.
People often think a semiconductor "stops conducting" below its gap energy as a temperature rule. In fact conduction always depends on the thermal tail, a smooth exponential, not a threshold. At 300 K a few electrons are always across the gap. That tail is the leakage you see in chips.
tradeoffs: Choose silicon for logic, memory and most sensors, where you need low cost, a native oxide, mature tools and a gap that keeps leakage low at 85 to 125 degrees Celsius junction temperatures. You give up efficient light emission (indirect gap) and the highest carrier speed. Choose a compound direct-gap material (GaAs, InP, InGaAs, GaN) when light is the product: lasers and LEDs need a direct gap, as do high-speed optical modulators. You pay for it with wafers that cost many times more per area, are smaller, and are more fragile.
Choose a wide-gap material (SiC at about 3.3 eV, GaN at 3.4 eV) for power electronics above roughly 600 V or where junction temperatures exceed 175 degrees Celsius. The breakdown electric field is about ten times higher than silicon's, so a device can be thinner for the same voltage, with lower resistance and lower switching loss, and chargers and EV inverters can be smaller. You give up price (SiC wafers are several times the cost of silicon), defect density, and dopant activation at low temperature. Choose silicon for 600 V and below only where cost wins and efficiency is secondary.
Choose a small gap material (Ge, InSb, HgCdTe) for infrared detection, where photons carry less than 1 eV, and accept that the detector must be cooled to reduce thermal carriers (many infrared imagers run at 77 K). You pay in cooling power and system size.
Choose the calculation method by the question. The effective-mass and k·p models give band edges near a point cheaply and are good for device simulation within about 0.5 eV of the edge. Tight-binding is a fast fit for nanostructures with thousands of atoms. Density functional theory with common functionals (LDA, PBE) predicts the band shape well but underestimates gaps, often by 30 to 50 percent; hybrid functionals and the GW method fix most of this at a cost of one to three orders of magnitude more computation. If you need a number, measure it or use a corrected method; if you need a trend across many candidate materials, cheap DFT is usually enough.
friction: Silicon's indirect gap is a permanent obstacle for putting light sources on a chip. That is why silicon photonics uses lasers made of III–V materials bonded or attached next to the silicon, and why integrating them remains a cost and yield problem. The gap also varies with temperature (silicon's falls by roughly 0.25 meV per kelvin), strain (used on purpose to speed up transistors) and, in thin layers, quantum confinement, so "1.12 eV" is a typical value, not a constant.
The gap fixes a floor on switching energy and on leakage. Chips need a supply voltage well above the thermal voltage (26 mV), and the subthreshold slope of a MOSFET cannot be steeper than about 60 mV per decade of current at room temperature, because the carrier energy distribution has that exponential tail. That limits how far supply voltage can fall, and so limits power. Heat creates carriers: leakage current doubles for roughly every 10 degrees Celsius in some regimes, which feeds thermal runaway if cooling is poor.
For wide-gap devices, the friction is practical. Defects at interfaces trap charge and shift thresholds; GaN transistors suffer from current collapse after high-voltage stress; SiC gate oxides are less reliable than those on silicon. For new materials, such as the two-dimensional semiconductor MoS₂ (gap about 1.8 eV for a single layer), the main problem is not the gap but making large, defect-free, well-contacted layers on a production line.
practice: Learn to read a gap as three numbers: the wavelength it matches (1240 / Eg in nm), the temperature at which its intrinsic carriers become a problem, and the field it can withstand. Use them for quick sanity checks. A photodiode for 1,310 or 1,550 nm needs a gap below 0.95 eV, so Ge or InGaAs. An LED of 450 nm needs about 2.75 eV, so GaN-based alloys. A charger that runs at 65 W in a small case may benefit from GaN, because its lower switching loss allows higher frequency and smaller magnetics.
For chips, remember that leakage is a thermal-tail effect. Server operators see it as power that rises with temperature even at idle, so good cooling saves electricity twice: once for the fans, and again for leakage. When reading a process node's data sheet, the threshold voltage options (high-Vt, standard, low-Vt) are in effect a choice about where on this exponential tail you sit: low-Vt cells are fast and leak maybe ten times more.
To study further, work through Pierret's Semiconductor Device Fundamentals chapters 1 to 3 for bands, carriers and doping, and Kittel or Ashcroft and Mermin for the solid-state physics. Free tools help too: nanoHUB hosts band structure and device simulators that you can run in a browser. Do the example calculation by hand for Si and Ge once, and the exponential dependence will stay with you.

@@ pnjunction
value: A p–n junction is the boundary inside one crystal where p-type silicon (with extra mobile holes) meets n-type silicon (with extra mobile electrons). At this boundary the charge carriers diffuse across, leave behind fixed charged atoms, and build an internal electric field. The result is a region with almost no mobile carriers (the depletion region) and a potential step, about 0.7 V in silicon, that opposes further flow. An external voltage can lower or raise this step, so the junction conducts easily in one direction and hardly at all in the other. It is a voltage-controlled one-way valve, made without any moving part.
Almost every semiconductor device contains one or more of these junctions. The diode is a single junction. A bipolar transistor is two junctions back to back. In a MOSFET, the source and drain are n-type regions in a p-type body (or the reverse), and both form p–n junctions with the body, which must stay reverse-biased for the transistor to work. A solar cell or photodiode is a junction that turns absorbed light into current. An LED or a semiconductor laser is a junction operated in forward bias so that recombination emits light. Even the leakage that drains your phone battery in standby is, in large part, junction leakage.
Two lower-layer concepts make it work. Band theory (bandgap) says electrons and holes exist and that the gap of 1.12 eV keeps them rare in pure material; the built-in potential of the junction is a fraction of that gap and can never exceed it. Doping (the concept "Silicon & doping") supplies the mobile carriers on each side in a controlled amount, from about 10¹⁵ to 10²⁰ per cm³; the doping levels decide the width and the built-in potential of the junction. The junction itself is what lets the layer above, the transistor, switch current with a voltage.
terms: p-type — silicon doped with acceptor atoms (such as boron) so that mobile holes outnumber electrons.
n-type — silicon doped with donor atoms (such as phosphorus or arsenic) so that mobile electrons outnumber holes.
Depletion region — the layer around the junction that has lost its mobile carriers and holds only fixed ionised dopant atoms; its width is typically 0.1 to 1 micrometre.
Built-in potential Vbi — the voltage step across the junction at zero bias, set by doping and temperature; about 0.6 to 0.8 V in silicon.
Forward and reverse bias — an external voltage that lowers the barrier (p side positive, forward) or raises it (n side positive, reverse).
Diffusion current and drift current — the flow of carriers from high to low concentration, and the flow driven by the electric field; at zero bias they cancel exactly.
Minority carrier — the less common carrier on one side, for example electrons on the p side; injected minority carriers carry the forward current.
Thermal voltage VT — kT/q, about 25.85 mV at 300 K; the natural voltage scale of every junction equation.
Saturation current Is — the tiny reverse current of an ideal junction, often between 10⁻¹⁵ and 10⁻¹² A for small silicon diodes.
history: The p–n junction was found by accident. In February 1940 at Bell Labs, Russell Ohl was studying a silicon sample with a crack that gave odd electrical readings, and he noticed that the current changed when light fell on it. The crack divided regions with different impurity content: one side was richer in one type of impurity, the other in another. Ohl called the regions p and n, and the junction between them was the first one made, and it produced the first silicon solar-cell effect. Bell Labs could not yet explain it in detail, but it showed that a built-in barrier could exist inside a single crystal.
The theory followed. In 1949 William Shockley published "The Theory of p–n Junctions in Semiconductors and p–n Junction Transistors" (Bell System Technical Journal), which gave the ideal diode equation and predicted the junction transistor. Shockley's equation, I = Is (e^(V/VT) − 1), is still the starting point of every textbook. The bipolar junction transistor was demonstrated in 1951 (Shockley, Sparks and Teal) by growing a crystal with two junctions. Leo Esaki observed tunnelling in a heavily doped germanium junction in 1957 (Nobel Prize in Physics, 1973). In 1954 Chapin, Fuller and Pearson at Bell Labs announced the first practical silicon solar cell, with about 6% efficiency.
Light-emitting junctions came next: the first visible-light LED (red, GaAsP) by Nick Holonyak in 1962, and the first diode lasers in 1962. Planar processing (Jean Hoerni, 1959) formed junctions by diffusing dopants through windows in an oxide layer, and this made the integrated circuit possible. Today junctions are made by ion implantation, which fires dopant ions into the wafer at controlled energy, followed by a short anneal. Modern transistor junctions are only a few nanometres deep and must stay sharp over distances of a few nanometres, which has become one of the hardest parts of manufacturing.
how: Join a p-type and an n-type region. On the n side, electrons are plentiful (say 10¹⁶ per cm³) and on the p side they are very rare (about 10⁴ per cm³). Random thermal motion pushes electrons from the n side into the p side, and holes the other way. This is diffusion, the same process that spreads a drop of ink in water. When an electron leaves the n side, it leaves behind a positively charged donor atom that cannot move, because it is fixed in the crystal. Likewise, holes leaving the p side expose negative acceptor atoms. These fixed charges build up a layer of positive charge on the n side and negative charge on the p side, which makes an electric field pointing from n to p. The field pushes electrons back toward n and holes back toward p (drift). Diffusion grows weaker as the layer grows and drift stronger, until they cancel. That equilibrium defines the depletion region and the built-in potential Vbi = VT ln(Na·Nd / nᵢ²), where Na and Nd are the doping densities and nᵢ is the intrinsic carrier density from band theory. The band gap sets nᵢ, and so bounds Vbi below Eg/q.
In terms of energy bands, the Fermi level must be flat across the whole device at equilibrium. The p side's bands sit higher (its Fermi level is near the valence band) and the n side's sit lower, so the bands bend smoothly in the depletion region by q·Vbi. An electron on the n side that wants to go to the p side must climb this hill. Very few have the energy, and the number falls off as e^(−qV/kT). This exponential is where all the junction behaviour comes from.
Now apply a voltage V. In forward bias (positive on p) the external field opposes the built-in one, the hill shrinks to q(Vbi − V), and exponentially more carriers can cross. Electrons cross into the p side and become minority carriers there; they diffuse onwards and recombine over a diffusion length (microns to hundreds of microns). The total current is Shockley's equation I = Is (e^(V/VT) − 1). For V above about 4 VT (100 mV) the "−1" can be ignored, and each increase of 59.5 mV (VT ln 10) multiplies the current by 10. In reverse bias (n side positive) the hill grows to q(Vbi + |V|), the depletion region widens as √(Vbi + |V|), and the current saturates at −Is, tiny because only minority carriers generated near the edge can be swept across. Real diodes add generation current in the depletion region (growing with its width, so reverse leakage creeps up with voltage), series resistance (which bends the forward curve at high current) and, at high reverse voltage, breakdown.
Two breakdown mechanisms exist. In avalanche breakdown (typically above about 7 V for moderately doped silicon), a carrier gains enough energy from the field to knock more carriers loose, and these in turn do the same: a chain reaction. In Zener breakdown (below about 5 V, heavily doped junctions with a very thin depletion region) electrons tunnel straight across the gap, which links this concept to quantum tunnelling. Both are used on purpose, for voltage references and surge protection, and both are harmful when unplanned.
The depletion region also behaves as a capacitor: two layers of fixed charge separated by a width W, so Cj = εA/W. Because W grows with reverse bias, the capacitance falls with voltage, which is useful for tuning circuits (varactor diodes) and limiting for speed. Light and heat add carriers: a photon of energy above Eg creates an electron–hole pair; if it is created in or near the depletion region, the field separates the pair before it can recombine. This is the photocurrent of a solar cell or a photodetector, flowing from n to p in reverse direction. In forward bias the opposite happens: injected electrons and holes meet and recombine. In a direct-gap material the energy leaves as a photon (an LED); in silicon it leaves as heat.

example: Take a symmetric silicon junction with Na = Nd = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ at 300 K, with nᵢ = 10¹⁰ cm⁻³ and VT = 25.85 mV. The built-in potential is Vbi = VT ln(Na·Nd / nᵢ²) = 0.02585 × ln(10³² / 10²⁰) = 0.02585 × ln(10¹²) = 0.02585 × 27.63 ≈ 0.714 V. This is close to the 0.6 to 0.7 V "diode drop" you see in practice, and comfortably below 1.12 V (the band gap).
Now the depletion width at zero bias, W = √( 2 εs Vbi / q × (1/Na + 1/Nd) ). For silicon, εs ≈ 11.7 × 8.854 × 10⁻¹⁴ F/cm ≈ 1.04 × 10⁻¹² F/cm and q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. The first part is 2 × 1.04 × 10⁻¹² × 0.714 / 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 9.3 × 10⁶. The bracket is 2 × 10⁻¹⁶ cm³. The product is 1.85 × 10⁻⁹ cm², and W ≈ 4.3 × 10⁻⁵ cm = 0.43 micrometres. With a reverse bias of 5 V, Vbi + V = 5.71 V and W grows by √(5.71 / 0.714) ≈ 2.8 times, to about 1.2 micrometres. For a junction of area 100 µm × 100 µm = 10⁻⁴ cm², the zero-bias capacitance is Cj = εA/W = 1.04 × 10⁻¹² × 10⁻⁴ / 4.3 × 10⁻⁵ ≈ 2.4 pF, which falls to about 0.9 pF at 5 V reverse.
For the current, assume Is = 10⁻¹⁴ A (a small diode). At V = 0.5 V: I = 10⁻¹⁴ × e^(0.5 / 0.02585) = 10⁻¹⁴ × e^(19.34) ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ × 2.5 × 10⁸ ≈ 2.5 µA. At 0.6 V: e^(23.2) ≈ 1.2 × 10¹⁰, so I ≈ 0.12 mA. At 0.7 V: e^(27.08) ≈ 5.8 × 10¹¹, so I ≈ 5.8 mA. Each 0.1 V adds a factor of about 48, and so 0.6 V to 0.7 V appears as the "turn-on" voltage even though the curve is smooth. In reverse at −1 V the current is about −10⁻¹⁴ A, a factor of 10¹¹ below the forward current at 0.7 V. Real devices leak much more (nanoamps) because of generation in the depletion region and surface effects, but the rectification ratio is still above 10⁶.
As a last check, use the junction as a photodetector. A photodiode of area 0.01 cm² under 1 mW/cm² of 850 nm light receives 10 µW. At 850 nm, a photon has 1.46 eV, so there are 10⁻⁵ / (1.46 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹) ≈ 4.3 × 10¹³ photons per second. If 80% create collected pairs, the photocurrent is 0.8 × 4.3 × 10¹³ × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 5.5 µA, a responsivity of about 0.55 A/W. This level of signal is easily amplified, and it is how a short-reach optical receiver starts.
intuition: Two crowds are separated by a gate that closes itself: the first people to cross leave a charge behind that pushes everyone else back, and a small outside push can reopen it in one direction only.
The junction is natural because diffusion makes the first move and electrostatics makes the correction. Carriers spread out because spreading is statistically likely. But carriers carry charge, and charge builds a field that opposes more spreading. The system settles where the two effects balance, with a built-in voltage that is just large enough to stop net flow. An outside voltage tilts this balance. Because carrier numbers depend exponentially on energy (e^(−E/kT)), a small tilt of tens of millivolts changes the current by factors of ten, which is why a tiny signal can control a large current, and why transistors can amplify.
mistakes: People often think a p–n junction is two pieces of silicon pressed or soldered together. In fact it is a single continuous crystal in which only the dopant atoms change. A mechanical contact between two separate pieces would have oxide and defects in between and would not work as a junction.
People often think the depletion region is empty because the "electrons moved away", so it is an insulator with nothing in it. In fact it is full of fixed charged atoms. The absence of mobile carriers makes it resistive, and the fixed charge is what creates the field. That field is also what sweeps photo-generated carriers out, which is the reason it is the active region of a solar cell.
People often think the 0.7 V diode drop is a fixed threshold, like a switch. In fact the current rises smoothly and exponentially. 0.7 V is the voltage where a typical small diode carries a few milliamps; a diode with a larger area or a larger Is reaches the same current at 0.5 V, and a power diode at 100 A may need 1 V or more. The drop also falls by about 2 mV for each degree of warming.
People often think reverse bias gives zero current, so the diode is an ideal one-way gate. In fact there is a small, rising leakage current, and above the breakdown voltage the junction conducts strongly in reverse. In a memory cell or a DRAM storage node, that small leakage is exactly what loses the stored charge and forces refresh.
tradeoffs: Choose a silicon p–n diode when you need low reverse leakage (picoamps to nanoamps), high reverse voltage (hundreds of volts) and moderate switching speed. Choose a Schottky diode (a metal–semiconductor junction) when you need a lower forward drop (about 0.3 V instead of 0.7 V) and very fast switching, because it stores no minority carriers. You give up reverse leakage (Schottky diodes leak microamps or more, rising quickly with temperature) and, in silicon, a lower voltage rating, around 100 V in common parts. Silicon carbide Schottky diodes push the rating to 600 to 1,700 V and are used in power converters.
Choose a plain p–n photodiode for low-cost, moderate-speed detection in the visible range. Choose a p–i–n photodiode (with a wide undoped layer between p and n) when you need speed and sensitivity at long wavelengths: the wide intrinsic region absorbs more light and lowers capacitance, so 10 to 50 GHz fibre receivers use it. Choose an avalanche photodiode (APD) when the signal is so weak that amplifier noise dominates: the avalanche multiplication gives a gain of 10 to 100. You give up a high bias voltage (tens to hundreds of volts), extra noise from the random multiplication, and tight temperature control.
Choose the ideal Shockley equation for hand analysis and for the range from about 0.3 V up to a few mA. Choose a compact model with series resistance, recombination (ideality factor n between 1 and 2) and breakdown, such as the model in SPICE, when you design circuits with large currents or low voltages. Choose numerical device simulation (TCAD, solving the Poisson and continuity equations) for scaled devices below about 100 nm, where the depletion approximation (abrupt edges) is wrong because the doping changes over distances similar to the depletion width. Each step costs more setup and calibration.
Choose a Zener-type (heavily doped) junction for a stable reference below about 5 V and avalanche-type for higher voltages. Their temperature behaviour is opposite: Zener breakdown voltage falls with temperature, avalanche breakdown voltage rises, and near 5 to 6 V the two cancel and the temperature coefficient is close to zero. That is why precision references use diodes near 5.6 V. You give up tight tolerance on cheap parts, and you must manage power dissipation.
friction: Reverse leakage is the most visible problem in computing. Every source and drain junction leaks a little, and the total over billions of transistors adds up to a significant share of chip power. The leakage grows with temperature (it follows nᵢ², about doubling every 10 to 15 degrees Celsius in the generation-dominated regime), so a hot chip leaks more, which heats it further.
Junction capacitance limits speed. Each junction must be charged and discharged, and the charge stored by minority carriers in a forward-biased junction must be removed before the diode can block, which takes nanoseconds in a normal silicon diode. This reverse-recovery time causes switching loss and noise in power converters, which is a reason to choose Schottky or SiC parts.
At small sizes, a sharp junction is difficult to make. Dopant atoms diffuse during heating, so the transition between p and n spreads out; at the 3 nm class, the number of dopant atoms in a channel is small enough that their random positions change each transistor's threshold. Forming very shallow, highly doped, low-resistance junctions without damaging the crystal is a central problem for process engineers, and one reason for new structures such as FinFET and gate-all-around nanosheets. Finally, junctions are poor light sources in silicon, since the indirect gap makes recombination mostly heat.
practice: When you read a datasheet for a diode, look at four numbers: forward voltage at the working current, reverse leakage at the working voltage and temperature, reverse breakdown voltage, and junction capacitance or reverse-recovery time. They map directly to the physics above: barrier height, thermal generation, field strength, and depletion width.
In power and thermal design, remember that the forward drop falls about 2 mV per degree Celsius at constant current, which can cause current hogging when diodes are paralleled; use a ballast resistor or devices from the same batch with positive temperature coefficient behaviour (MOSFET-based rectifiers) for paralleling. For ESD and surge protection, use TVS diodes, which are avalanche junctions sized for the energy you expect. For reading sensors, run photodiodes at zero or small reverse bias for low noise and at larger reverse bias for speed. For servers, treat leakage as a cost that depends on temperature: improving the cooling setpoint by 10 degrees may cut idle power measurably.
To study the topic, work through Pierret's chapters on the p–n junction electrostatics and the ideal diode, then simulate a diode in a free circuit simulator such as ngspice, and try the free device simulators on nanoHUB. Redo the example by hand with different doping levels; seeing how Vbi and W change with Na and Nd gives you the feel that lets you reason about transistors, which are built from the same junction.

@@ tunneling
value: Quantum tunnelling is the effect by which a particle crosses a barrier that is higher than its energy. Classically, a ball without enough energy to climb a hill stays behind it. A quantum particle is a wave, and a wave does not stop sharply at a wall: inside the barrier it dies away exponentially, and if the barrier is thin enough, a small part of the wave is still alive on the other side. The result is a real probability of finding the particle there. That probability falls by roughly a factor of ten for every 0.2 nm of extra thickness in a typical oxide, so tunnelling is negligible for a thick wall and dominant for a wall a few atoms thick.
This is a wanted effect and an unwanted effect at the same time, and both matter to computing. Flash memory (every SSD, phone and USB stick) stores bits by forcing electrons through a thin oxide with a high voltage, and keeping them there for years because the oxide is a good barrier when no voltage is applied. The same effect forces designers to limit how thin transistor insulators can be, since once the gate oxide reached about 1.2 nm (a few atoms) around 2003, the tunnelling leakage through the gate became a significant fraction of chip power. It also appears in DRAM and logic as a part of leakage, in the Zener diodes and tunnel diodes of the p–n junction family, and in the scanning tunnelling microscope that lets us see single atoms.
The concept stands on quantum mechanics (L5): the wavefunction and the Schrödinger equation give the exponential decay in a barrier and the formula for the leak probability. Above it, tunnelling explains why flash wears out (the storage devices and flash translation layer concepts), why DRAM must refresh, and why oxide engineering (high-k dielectrics) was necessary.
terms: Potential barrier — a region where a particle's potential energy is higher than its total energy, such as an insulating oxide between two conductors.
Evanescent wave — the exponentially decaying form the wavefunction takes inside a barrier; it carries no current by itself.
Decay constant κ — κ = √(2m(V − E)) / ħ; the wavefunction falls as e^(−κx) inside the barrier.
Transmission probability T — the chance that a particle arriving at the barrier gets through; for thick barriers T ≈ e^(−2κd).
Direct tunnelling — electrons cross the full thickness of a very thin oxide (below about 3 nm) at low field.
Fowler–Nordheim (FN) tunnelling — at high field the barrier becomes triangular and thin; electrons tunnel through its narrow top. The current density grows roughly as E² · e^(−B/E).
Floating gate — an electrically isolated conductor (polysilicon) inside a flash cell that holds charge; the stored charge shifts the cell's threshold voltage.
Charge-trap layer — an insulating layer (silicon nitride) that traps charge in localised states; used in 3D NAND instead of a conducting floating gate.
Gate leakage — current that tunnels through a transistor's gate insulator, even when the transistor is off.
history: Tunnelling emerged in 1927 and 1928 from the new quantum mechanics. Friedrich Hund noticed it in calculations on molecules in 1927. George Gamow, and independently Ronald Gurney and Edward Condon, used it in 1928 to explain alpha decay: an alpha particle inside a nucleus has too little energy to leave, yet it does, with a half-life that depends exponentially on the barrier. That was a first quantitative success, since half-lives that range over more than twenty orders of magnitude followed from small changes in the alpha particle's energy. In the same year Ralph Fowler and Lothar Nordheim derived the field emission of electrons from metals.
For the next decades, tunnelling stayed in the physics books. In 1957 Leo Esaki built a heavily doped germanium diode and found a region with falling current as voltage rose: electrons were tunnelling across the thin p–n junction (Nobel Prize 1973, shared with Ivar Giaever and Brian Josephson). Giaever (1960) showed tunnelling between superconductors, and Josephson (1962) predicted the effect now used in superconducting quantum bits. Heinrich Rohrer and Gerd Binnig built the scanning tunnelling microscope at IBM Zurich in 1981, which measures the tunnelling current between a sharp tip and a surface; it can resolve single atoms (Nobel Prize 1986).
Flash memory is the commercial story. Fujio Masuoka at Toshiba invented flash in the early 1980s, with NOR flash announced in 1984 and NAND flash in 1987. Cells were written and erased by Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling through a tunnel oxide of about 8 to 10 nm. In logic, the problem came from the opposite side. Scaling of the gate oxide followed Moore's law until Intel's 90 nm generation, with a gate oxide of about 1.2 nm, where gate leakage rose so much that scaling had to stop. Hafnium-based high-k gate insulators with metal gates, introduced by Intel at 45 nm in 2007 (and by others soon after), solved the problem for a time. Floating-gate NAND was replaced by charge-trap, vertically stacked "3D NAND" from about 2013 (Samsung's V-NAND), because planar cells ran out of room.
how: Begin with a particle of energy E meeting a barrier of height V > E and width d. Inside the barrier, the Schrödinger equation becomes −(ħ²/2m) ψ'' = (E − V) ψ, with E − V negative. In the free region it gave oscillating solutions (waves); here it gives real exponentials. So ψ(x) ∝ e^(−κx) with κ = √(2m(V − E)) / ħ. The wave neither stops nor continues; it decays. The Born rule says the probability density |ψ|² then decays as e^(−2κx). If the barrier ends before the wave has vanished, the wave continues on the other side as a travelling wave with a smaller amplitude. The probability that the particle gets through is T ≈ e^(−2κd) for a thick, high barrier. This is the WKB (Wentzel–Kramers–Brillouin) approximation. For a barrier whose height varies with position, you replace κd by the integral of κ(x) over the barrier.
Three features follow from the formula. First, T depends exponentially on thickness d, so a change of a fraction of a nanometre changes the leakage by orders of magnitude. Second, T depends on the square root of the barrier height (V − E), but also exponentially, so lowering the barrier or raising the electron's energy matters a great deal. Third, T depends on the square root of mass, so light particles tunnel much more easily than heavy ones. An electron tunnels easily across 1 to 3 nm; a proton can do it only across much smaller distances, and a macroscopic object essentially never does.
In a flash cell, this effect is controlled by a voltage. A flash transistor has a control gate on top and, below it, a floating gate (or a charge-trap layer) separated from the silicon channel by a thin tunnel oxide, and from the control gate by another insulating layer. To program (write) the cell, a high voltage (of the order of 15 to 20 V in classic NAND) is applied to the control gate. Part of it appears across the tunnel oxide, which gives a field of about 10 MV/cm. At that field the oxide's conduction-band profile tilts into a triangle and the barrier an electron sees becomes only a few nanometres wide: FN tunnelling. Electrons flow from the channel through the oxide into the floating gate and stay there. When the voltage is removed, the barrier returns to its full thickness (about 3.1 eV high for SiO₂ against silicon, and 8 to 10 nm wide), so T is almost zero, and the charge stays for years. Stored electrons raise the cell's threshold voltage; reading applies an intermediate voltage and sees whether the cell conducts. Erasing reverses the field and pulls the electrons back out. Multi-level cells store 2, 3 or 4 bits (MLC, TLC, QLC) by distinguishing 4, 8 or 16 charge levels, which requires more precise charge control.
The damage mechanism is also tunnelling. Each program or erase pushes a large number of electrons through the oxide; some are trapped in defects, and some break bonds and create new defects. Trapped charge shifts the threshold voltage and defects form leakage paths that lose stored charge faster. After many cycles the cell can no longer separate its levels, which defines endurance: roughly 100,000 cycles for SLC cells, a few thousand for TLC and several hundred to about a thousand for QLC (the exact figures depend on the generation and vendor). The flash translation layer (L3) responds with wear levelling, spreading writes so that no block wears out first, and with error-correcting codes that absorb the growing error rate.
In logic, the gate leakage comes from direct tunnelling, with the same formula. With SiO₂ thinner than about 2 nm, the oxide is only seven to eight atoms thick, and electrons from the channel tunnel into the gate whether the transistor is on or off. Replacing it with hafnium oxide (relative permittivity about 20 against 3.9 for SiO₂) lets the physical layer be several times thicker for the same capacitance, which cuts T by many orders of magnitude. This is the subject of the oxides and high-k dielectrics concept. Other forms of tunnelling in a modern chip include band-to-band tunnelling at the drain edge, which causes gate-induced drain leakage (GIDL), and the Zener tunnelling in heavily doped junctions. For DRAM, the small capacitor loses charge through several leakage paths, including junction and GIDL leakage related to tunnelling, so each row must be refreshed within the retention time (the usual refresh window is 64 ms).

example: Estimate how fast leakage changes with the thickness of a gate oxide. Use SiO₂ against silicon: barrier height V − E ≈ 3.1 eV, and an electron effective mass in the oxide of about 0.5 mₑ. Then κ = √(2 × 0.5 × 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ × 3.1 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹) / 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴. The inside of the root is 4.52 × 10⁻⁴⁹, its root is 6.73 × 10⁻²⁵, and κ ≈ 6.4 × 10⁹ per metre (6.4 per nm). The transmission is T ≈ e^(−2κd) = e^(−12.8 d), with d in nm.
For d = 3 nm: T ≈ e^(−38.4) ≈ 2 × 10⁻¹⁷. For d = 2 nm: e^(−25.6) ≈ 7 × 10⁻¹². For d = 1.2 nm: e^(−15.4) ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁷. From 3 nm to 1.2 nm, a change of 1.8 nm raised the transmission by 10¹⁰. Each 0.18 nm (ln 10 / 12.8) multiplies T by 10. This matches the practical rule that gate leakage increases about ten times for each 0.2 nm of thinning. Of course, the real current also depends on how many electrons strike the barrier per second and on the field, so this is a trend and not a prediction.
Now compare with a high-k layer. Hafnium oxide has a permittivity of about 20 against 3.9 for SiO₂, so a layer of 5 nm HfO₂ has the same capacitance as 5 × 3.9 / 20 ≈ 1 nm of SiO₂. The barrier of HfO₂ is lower (about 1.5 eV) and the mass is a bit smaller, so κ is about 4 per nm instead of 6.4. Then T ≈ e^(−2 × 4 × 5) = e^(−40) ≈ 4 × 10⁻¹⁸ against the 2 × 10⁻⁷ of a 1.2 nm SiO₂ layer: a factor of 10¹⁰ less in this simple model. Intel reported more than ten times lower gate leakage for its 45 nm high-k process than for its 65 nm SiO₂ process, much less than this simple model gives, since interface layers, defects and trap-assisted tunnelling matter, but the direction and the reason are exactly this.
For flash, estimate the field. A cell with a 9 nm tunnel oxide, a coupling ratio of 0.6 (the share of the control-gate voltage that appears on the floating gate) and 16 V on the control gate has about 9.6 V on the floating gate. With the channel at 0 V, the field across the tunnel oxide is about 9.6 V / 9 nm ≈ 1.07 V/nm = 10.7 MV/cm. This is in the FN regime, where the electron sees a triangular barrier only a few nanometres wide, and current flows. At rest, with only a small voltage left on the floating gate, the field is a tiny fraction of that (well below 1 MV/cm) and T is minuscule, so the charge stays for years. Changing the voltage turns the same oxide from a wall into a gate.
intuition: A wave hitting a thick wall dies inside it, but a thin wall lets some of the wave through, and the thinner the wall, the more gets through.
Think of the sound of a loud party heard from the next room. A thick wall mutes it completely, a thin wall lets a muffled version through, and the loss rises smoothly with thickness. A quantum particle behaves in the same way: its wavefunction cannot end abruptly at a wall, because that would need infinite energy in the mathematics, so it decays smoothly. The exponential is what gives tunnelling its engineering character: the effect is absent at 5 nm and overwhelming at 1 nm, with no gentle middle ground. That is why a chip designer meets it as a cliff, not as a slope.
mistakes: People often think the electron "digs through" the barrier or borrows energy for a short time. In fact the electron does not pass through the barrier region with negative kinetic energy as a particle would; the wavefunction extends into the barrier, and the particle is only ever found on one side or the other. Energy is conserved: it has the same energy before and after.
People often think tunnelling is faster than light because there is no time spent in the barrier. In fact the question of "tunnelling time" is subtle and debated, but no signal or information travels faster than light. The leading edge of a wave packet still obeys relativity.
People often think leakage from tunnelling is a defect that better manufacturing can remove. In fact it is a basic property of thin barriers. You can only reduce it by using thicker barriers, higher barriers or different materials, such as high-k oxides, which keep the capacitance while adding thickness.
People often think flash wears out because electrons are "used up" or because the stored charge slowly leaks. In fact flash wears because each tunnelling cycle damages the oxide, creating traps and leakage paths. A cell that is never rewritten holds data for years; a cell rewritten thousands of times can fail within months at the same temperature.
tradeoffs: Choose direct-tunnelling analysis (the simple exponential formula) for oxides thinner than about 3 nm and low fields, and the Fowler–Nordheim formula for thicker oxides at high field, above about 5 MV/cm. Use a more exact method, such as the transfer-matrix or NEGF approach, when the barrier is a few atomic layers, when you have a stack of materials, or when resonant states matter. The WKB formula is cheap and shows trends, but it can be wrong by orders of magnitude in absolute terms, since it ignores band structure, image-charge lowering and trap-assisted tunnelling.
Choose floating-gate flash cells when you want the proven, well-understood technology with a good control of charge and a thick tunnel oxide for retention; choose charge-trap cells (3D NAND today) when you need to stack many layers. Charge-trap storage uses a thin nitride layer instead of a conducting plate, so it can be formed in a vertical channel hole with 200 or more layers. You give up some retention (charge in traps can drift) and some process simplicity; you gain bits per area, and because a damaged spot affects only a local trap rather than the whole plate, better tolerance to oxide defects.
Choose a thick tunnel oxide when retention and endurance matter more than speed and voltage: enterprise SSDs and 10-year archival parts. Choose a thinner oxide when you want low programming voltage and speed, and accept fewer cycles and weaker retention. Choose SLC mode (1 bit per cell) when you need endurance or write speed; use TLC or QLC for capacity, where the wider spread of charge levels drives more ECC and more wear. Controllers often use a small SLC cache in front of TLC or QLC for this reason.
Choose a high-k dielectric with a metal gate for logic below 45 nm, trading process complexity and interface quality for orders of magnitude less gate leakage at the same capacitance. Do not rely on SiO₂ alone below about 1.5 nm. For standby-critical products, choose also higher-threshold transistors and power gating, which reduce subthreshold leakage but not tunnelling. If you want to exploit tunnelling in logic, such as tunnel field-effect transistors (TFETs) that could switch more steeply than 60 mV per decade, accept that the on-current has been low in real devices so far, and that no TFET has reached mainstream production.
friction: Tunnelling forces a hard limit on shrinking insulators. Below about 1 nm of SiO₂-equivalent thickness, no material is a good enough barrier, which is why modern transistors rely on a full set of tricks (high-k, strain, FinFET, gate-all-around) instead of a thinner oxide. At the very small channel lengths of recent nodes, source-to-drain tunnelling through the channel barrier itself is also a leakage path, an effect that grows as gate lengths approach 5 nm.
For storage, the cost is endurance. A TLC NAND cell survives a few thousand cycles and a QLC one fewer. Retention worsens as cells wear and with temperature, because a damaged oxide leaks more and thermal energy helps electrons escape (a rule used in industry is that retention drops by a factor of about 2 for each 10 to 15 degrees of extra temperature, following an Arrhenius law). Controllers hide this with ECC, read-retry, wear levelling and over-provisioning, and these add latency, write amplification and design complexity.
Tunnelling is also hard to model well. Real oxides have defects, so trap-assisted tunnelling, stress-induced leakage and soft breakdown happen at fields where the clean theory says nothing should. Predicting lifetime therefore needs accelerated stress tests and statistics, not only equations. And for devices that want tunnelling as a feature, such as tunnel FETs and resonant tunnelling diodes, getting a strong enough current at low voltage with a variation-tolerant design has been unsolved for two decades.
practice: Use the exponential rule to reason about reliability and design questions. A change of 0.2 nm in an oxide or 10% in field can change leakage or wear by a factor of ten. When someone says a new flash generation has "the same endurance with a thinner oxide", ask what changed, because physics says endurance usually falls.
For storage systems, treat P/E cycles as a budget. Check a drive's rated endurance (drive writes per day, or terabytes written) against your write rate and your write amplification, which is often 2 to 5 for random small writes and near 1 for large sequential writes. Reduce amplification by aligning writes to the erase block size, batching small writes, leaving 10 to 28 percent free space (over-provisioning), and avoiding in-place overwrite patterns. Monitor SMART attributes such as media wearout indicator and percentage used. For databases with heavy writes, pick drives with higher endurance (or SLC and pseudo-SLC modes) and not just higher capacity. For power budgets of idle servers, remember that chip leakage (partly tunnelling, mostly subthreshold) is the reason idle power is not zero, and that it rises with temperature.
To study the subject, read the barrier and tunnelling problem in Griffiths' Introduction to Quantum Mechanics (section 8.2 covers the WKB tunnelling formula), then the flash chapters of Bez et al. For hands-on practice, redo the numbers in the example for a different barrier height and see how quickly the transmission changes.

@@ thermo
value: Thermodynamics is the science of energy, heat and the direction in which processes run. Two laws matter most for computing. The first law says energy is conserved, so every watt a chip draws from the wall leaves as a watt of heat. The second law says that entropy, a measure of how many microscopic arrangements match what we see, never decreases in an isolated system. Because of it, heat flows from hot to cold by itself, and moving it the other way (cooling) costs work.
For a software engineer, this is the reason hardware looks the way it does. Since about 2005, clock speed has stopped near 3 to 5 GHz because a chip cannot remove more than about 100 W per cm² from its surface at a safe temperature. A single modern AI accelerator draws 700 to 1,000 W or more, and a full rack of them draws roughly 100 to 130 kW, which air cannot carry away without enormous fans, so racks move to liquid cooling. Data centres are built next to cheap power and cold water, and they are now judged by two numbers: energy per useful operation, and the fraction of facility power that does not reach the servers (power usage effectiveness, PUE).
Thermodynamics is a base layer: it does not stand on other concepts in this map. It supplies tools to the layers above. Statistical entropy, S = k ln W, is the same counting idea as combinatorics (L4) and as Shannon's entropy in information theory (L4). The heat equation links to differential equations (L4). Thermal motion produces Brownian motion (stochastic processes, L4) and noise in every resistor (thermal noise, L5). The entropy of erasing a bit gives Landauer's principle. Heat limits explain the end of Dennard scaling in Moore's law, and the power and degradation behaviour of lithium-ion batteries.
terms: Energy — the capacity to do work or produce heat; measured in joules (1 W = 1 J per second).
Heat and work — two ways energy crosses a boundary: heat is energy moved because of a temperature difference; work is energy moved by an organised force.
Temperature — a measure of the average thermal energy of each degree of freedom; kT (Boltzmann's constant times absolute temperature) is about 4.14 × 10⁻²¹ J at 300 K.
Entropy S — a measure of how many microscopic arrangements (microstates) are consistent with the macroscopic state: S = k ln W.
Second law — the entropy of an isolated system does not decrease; heat does not flow from cold to hot without work.
Carnot efficiency — the maximum fraction of heat that any engine can turn into work between a hot and a cold reservoir: 1 − T_cold / T_hot.
Dynamic power — the power used to charge and discharge capacitances in switching circuits: P = α C V² f.
Thermal resistance R_θ — the temperature rise per watt between a chip and its surroundings, in K/W; ΔT = P × R_θ.
Power usage effectiveness (PUE) — total facility energy divided by the energy delivered to IT equipment; 1.0 is the ideal.
history: Thermodynamics began as engineering. Steam engines worked in the 1700s before anyone knew why. In 1824 Sadi Carnot, a French engineer, asked how much work a heat engine can extract from heat, and he showed that it depends only on the temperatures of the hot and cold sides. In the 1840s James Joule measured the mechanical equivalent of heat by stirring water with a paddle, which established that heat is a form of energy and that total energy is conserved (the first law, with contributions from Mayer and Helmholtz). In 1850 Rudolf Clausius and William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) stated the second law, and in 1865 Clausius named entropy.
The next step was statistical. James Clerk Maxwell (1860s) and Ludwig Boltzmann explained gases as huge crowds of molecules in random motion. Boltzmann's 1877 work linked entropy to the number of microstates, S = k ln W, which is carved on his gravestone. Josiah Willard Gibbs (1870s to 1900s) completed the statistical framework and its use in chemistry. This made the second law a statement about probability: a process that lowers entropy is not forbidden, only astronomically unlikely for large systems.
The links to computing came in the 20th century. Claude Shannon (1948) defined the entropy of a message with the same formula as Gibbs, H = −Σ p log p; John von Neumann is said to have suggested the name. Rolf Landauer (1961) and Charles Bennett (1973) tied information erasure to heat. For hardware, Robert Dennard and colleagues at IBM described in 1974 how a transistor's power could stay proportional to its area as it shrank, so chips got faster and kept the same power density. That ended when voltage could not fall further because of leakage, around 2005 to 2006. Intel cancelled its planned 4 GHz Pentium 4 successor in 2004 and the industry turned to multicore chips. Since then, "dark silicon" (parts of a chip that cannot be powered at once) and data-centre cooling have driven design.
how: The first law, ΔU = Q − W, says that a system's internal energy changes by the heat added minus the work done. For a chip in steady state, electrical power in equals heat out, since it does no mechanical work and stores no energy. Almost every watt becomes heat inside a square centimetre of silicon, and has to travel through the package, the heat spreader, the heat sink and then to the air or liquid. Heat moves by conduction (Fourier's law: heat flux q = −k ∇T, with k about 150 W/m·K for silicon and 400 for copper), convection (a moving fluid carries heat away, with a rate set by flow and temperature rise), and radiation (small at these temperatures). Each stage has a thermal resistance, they add in series, and the chip's junction temperature is T_junction = T_ambient + P × R_θ,total. Silicon devices must stay below about 85 to 105 degrees Celsius, so the product P × R_θ is capped. That is the root of the "power wall".
Chip power has two parts. Dynamic power, P = α C V² f, is the energy used to charge the wire and gate capacitances C to voltage V, for the fraction α of nodes that switch in each of f cycles per second. Each switching event burns about ½ C V² as heat in the transistor resistance, and the other ½ C V² is stored and then dumped on discharge; this does not depend on how fast or slowly the transistor charges the capacitor, which is surprising at first. Static power is the leakage through off transistors (subthreshold current, and tunnelling through gates and junctions, as covered in the tunnelling and p–n junction concepts), and it grows exponentially with temperature and with lowered threshold voltage. Because frequency needs voltage (roughly f ∝ V in the useful range), power scales as about V³, so a small cut in voltage and frequency saves a lot of power. This is the basis for dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS), and for why spreading a task over many slower cores uses less power than a single fast core.
Entropy is the second law's tool. Boltzmann's counting says that the macroscopic state with the most microstates is overwhelmingly the most likely, and a system left alone moves to it. For heat, the number of ways to share energy between two bodies is largest when they have the same temperature, so heat flows from hot to cold, never the reverse. A cooling system is a heat pump: it uses work W to move heat Q from cold to hot. The best possible ratio is the Carnot coefficient of performance, Q / W = T_cold / (T_hot − T_cold). Cooling a chip at 330 K (57 degrees Celsius) to an outside air temperature of 300 K gives a limit of 330 / 30 = 11 units of heat per unit of work, and real systems reach a fraction of it. When the outside air is colder than the allowed chip temperature, no pump is needed at all (free cooling), and this is why many data centres are in cold climates.
This connects to the rest of the map. Information theory (L4) uses the same entropy formula as Gibbs, and Landauer's principle turns the equality into a cost: erasing one bit releases at least kT ln 2 of heat. Diffusion and Brownian motion (stochastic processes, L4) arise from the random thermal motion that entropy counts, and the heat equation ∂T/∂t = α ∇²T (differential equations, L4) describes how temperature spreads in a chip, which tools such as HotSpot solve numerically. Johnson–Nyquist noise in a resistor, v² = 4kTRΔf, is thermal motion seen as voltage, and sets the floor for every receiver. Combinatorics (L4) supplies the counting behind W.
At data-centre scale, the same chain repeats. IT power turns into heat; chilled water or air takes it to a chiller or cooling tower; the tower rejects heat by evaporating water (about 2.4 MJ per kg, so each kWh of heat evaporates roughly 1.5 kg of water). PUE records what fraction of power goes to cooling and distribution instead of computing. Best hyperscale sites report annual PUE of about 1.1; typical industry averages are around 1.5 to 1.6.

example: Start with a processor whose effective switched capacitance (α C, summed over the chip) is 50 nF, running at V = 0.8 V and f = 3 GHz. Dynamic power is 50 × 10⁻⁹ × 0.8² × 3 × 10⁹ = 50 × 10⁻⁹ × 0.64 × 3 × 10⁹ ≈ 96 W. Add leakage of 15 W, so 111 W in total, all of which becomes heat. If the chip area is 1.5 cm², the power density is 74 W/cm². With a total thermal resistance from junction to ambient of 0.4 K/W and an ambient of 35 degrees Celsius, the junction reaches 35 + 111 × 0.4 ≈ 79 degrees Celsius, which is within limits.
Now apply DVFS: lower voltage by 20% to 0.64 V and frequency by about 20% to 2.4 GHz. Dynamic power becomes 50 × 10⁻⁹ × 0.64² × 2.4 × 10⁹ = 50 × 10⁻⁹ × 0.4096 × 2.4 × 10⁹ ≈ 49 W, 51% of the original, for 80% of the speed. Leakage also falls with voltage, so total power drops to near half. The energy per operation falls to about 0.64 of the original (power 0.51 divided by speed 0.8). This is the arithmetic behind cutting cloud instance clock speeds to save power, and behind modern chips running at a lower "efficiency" point.
Next the rack. Suppose 72 accelerators in a rack draw 120 kW in total. With air, the heat capacity of air is about 1.2 kJ per cubic metre per kelvin, so with a 15 K temperature rise the rack needs 120,000 / (1,200 × 15) ≈ 6.7 m³/s of air. That is about 14,000 cubic feet per minute through one rack, a wind that is loud, hard to route and costly in fan power. With water (4.18 kJ per kg per kelvin) and a 10 K rise, the flow is 120,000 / (4,180 × 10) ≈ 2.9 kg/s, which is about 170 litres per minute, a flow that fits in a hose. Water's volumetric heat capacity is roughly 3,500 times that of air, which is the whole case for liquid cooling.
Finally the facility. The IT load of 120 kW with a PUE of 1.5 needs 180 kW at the meter, and with PUE 1.2 needs 144 kW. The difference is 36 kW. Over a year (8,760 hours) that is 36 × 8,760 ≈ 315,000 kWh. At $0.08 per kWh, it comes to about $25,000 per rack per year. Multiply by 10,000 racks and PUE is a $250 million a year question. intuition: Energy is a bank balance that never changes in total, and entropy is a tax that always rises, so every computation must pay in heat.
The second law is natural once you count. There are overwhelmingly more disordered arrangements of molecules than ordered ones, so a system wandering at random almost always ends in a disordered one. Heat spreading out is the same as probability spreading out. The same counting explains why cooling is work: pushing heat uphill means making a rare arrangement more likely, which needs an organised input of energy. A chip is a device that takes organised electrical energy and, in the end, turns it all into disordered thermal energy; useful computation is a by-product on the way.
mistakes: People often think a chip "uses" energy and so the energy is gone. In fact the first law says energy is conserved. Electrical energy goes in and the same amount comes out as heat; what is lost is its usefulness, not the energy itself. So a 1 kW server is a 1 kW heater.
People often think entropy means "disorder" in a loose, visual sense. In fact entropy is a count: the logarithm of the number of microscopic states that look the same macroscopically. A tidy crystal at high temperature may have more entropy than a messy-looking one at low temperature. Thinking in terms of counting is what links it to information theory.
People often think the second law forbids any decrease of entropy. In fact it forbids a decrease in an isolated system. A refrigerator lowers the entropy of its contents but raises the entropy of the room by more, and life, factories and computers all work this way. The statement is also statistical: a small system can briefly fluctuate to lower entropy, and experiments measure this.
People often think lower PUE means a data centre is greener or more efficient. In fact PUE only compares overhead to IT power. A site can have a PUE of 1.1 and still waste energy if its servers are idle, because PUE says nothing about useful work per watt. It also hides water use: evaporative cooling cuts electricity use and raises water use, which PUE does not show.
tradeoffs: Choose air cooling when rack power is below about 20 to 30 kW, you want standard hardware, and your site has cheap fans and space. Choose direct-to-chip liquid cooling (cold plates on CPUs and GPUs) once racks exceed about 40 to 50 kW; it removes 70 to 80 percent of the heat with water and leaves the rest to air. You give up simplicity: leak risk, new plumbing, coolant distribution units, and serviceability. Choose immersion cooling (servers in dielectric fluid) for the highest densities or for the lowest fan energy, accepting a heavier tank-based building design, a limited supplier ecosystem, and hardware compatibility checks.
Choose evaporative cooling when power is expensive and water is cheap: it can bring PUE to about 1.1 to 1.2. Choose dry coolers, or chillers, when water is scarce or priced highly, at the cost of higher electricity use (PUE 1.3 or more in warm climates). Many sites use hybrid systems, evaporating water only on the hottest days. You pick which scarce resource to spend, and a single PUE number cannot tell you whether you chose well; track water usage effectiveness (litres per kWh) as well.
Choose to run chips at lower voltage and frequency (DVFS, power capping) when throughput per watt or per rack matters more than single-thread speed, as in AI training clusters where the facility power is fixed. Power capping an accelerator by 20% often costs only 5 to 10 percent of throughput on memory-bound work. Choose maximum clocks when latency dominates and power is not constrained. You give up speed in one case and efficiency in the other.
For modelling, choose a lumped thermal model (one resistance and capacitance per layer, solved in microseconds) for control and firmware, where you need the average die temperature and its time constant of seconds. Choose a finite-element or CFD model when you need hotspots across the die (which can be 20 K above the average) or airflow across a rack, accepting hours of compute and a lot of calibration data. Choose equilibrium thermodynamics for bulk quantities such as efficiency limits, and statistical mechanics when you need fluctuations or small systems (a few molecules or a nanoscale transistor), where averages hide what happens.
friction: Power density limits what can be built. A chip's heat comes from a small area, and the path out is long: from the transistor layer through silicon, interface material, package and heat sink. Hotspots at the level of a few hundred microns can be much hotter than the average, so the usable power is set by the worst spot, not by the total. In 3D-stacked chips (HBM stacks above logic) the problem is worse because the heat from lower layers must pass through the upper ones.
Chips can no longer run all their transistors at full speed at once. With voltage nearly fixed (about 0.7 to 0.8 V) while transistor counts keep rising, the fraction that can switch at once within the power budget shrinks, which is the "dark silicon" problem. Designers answer with specialised accelerators that do one task at much lower energy per operation than a general core, which is a reason for the rise of GPUs, TPUs and fixed-function blocks.
At the facility level, grid connection is often the real constraint. A single large AI campus may need hundreds of megawatts, and utilities need years to deliver it. Water use is a public issue in dry regions. Heat reuse is limited by the second law: server waste heat is at 40 to 60 degrees Celsius, too cool for generating electricity efficiently (the Carnot limit is about 9 percent), though it suits district heating where a network exists.
practice: Keep a few conversion habits. One watt of IT power needs roughly PUE watts at the meter. A kilowatt-hour costs about $0.05 to $0.15 depending on region, so 1 kW running all year costs about $400 to $1,300. A 1 kW server in a 1.5 PUE building costs about 1.5 times that. In the cloud, you pay for this in the instance price; on premises you pay in rack power limits (typical colocation racks are rated at 5 to 15 kW, AI racks need 40 kW and more).
For performance work, measure energy, not just time. Tools such as Intel RAPL (CPU package power counters), NVIDIA's nvidia-smi or NVML (GPU power), and cloud carbon dashboards let you compute joules per request or per training step. Often a lower clock or a batched design cuts energy per task more than any code optimisation. For capacity planning, size clusters by power budget first (kW per rack, kW per hall) and count servers second. For reliability, remember that failure rates rise with temperature (a common rule of thumb is that rates double with every 10 degrees Celsius, based on the Arrhenius model), so running 10 degrees cooler is cheap insurance. If you run Kubernetes or Spark clusters, idle machines still draw 30 to 50 percent of peak power, so bin-packing and autoscaling reduce energy as well as cost.

@@ landauer
value: Landauer's principle says that erasing one bit of information, in a way that cannot be undone, must release at least k·T·ln 2 of heat into the surroundings. Here k is Boltzmann's constant (1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K) and T is the temperature of the environment. At room temperature (300 K) this is about 2.9 × 10⁻²¹ joules, or 0.018 electron-volts. The rule was stated by Rolf Landauer at IBM in 1961, and it ties together two things that look unrelated: the abstract idea of information and the physical idea of heat.
The number is tiny. A modern logic gate switching once spends 10⁴ to 10⁵ times more, and a DRAM cell refresh or write spends about a million times more. So Landauer's principle does not explain why your laptop is hot. What it does is set a floor that no technology can go under, and it answers a deep question: is there a minimum energy cost for computing? The answer is subtle. Computing steps that lose no information (reversible steps) have no minimum cost. Only throwing information away does. This gives engineers a clear picture of where energy must go in the limit, and points to the one escape route, reversible computing.
The principle stands on thermodynamics (L5). The second law says that the total entropy of the world cannot fall. Boltzmann's formula S = k ln W says that entropy counts the number of microscopic states, so a memory cell that is forced from "0 or 1" to "0" has halved its states and lowered its entropy by k ln 2. The second law then requires the environment to gain at least that much entropy, and heat Q delivered at temperature T carries entropy Q/T, so Q ≥ kT ln 2. Above, the idea feeds information theory (L4), where it links Shannon's bits to physical entropy, and the broader claim that information is physical.
terms: Bit erasure — resetting a memory element to a fixed standard state (such as 0) whatever its earlier value, so the earlier value is lost.
Logically irreversible operation — an operation whose output does not determine its input, for example AND (outputs 0 for three different inputs) or erasing a bit.
Logically reversible operation — an operation that maps each input to a different output, so the input can be recovered; examples are NOT and the Toffoli gate.
Boltzmann constant k — 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K; it converts temperature to energy per degree of freedom.
kT ln 2 — the Landauer bound per bit; about 2.87 × 10⁻²¹ J at 300 K.
Maxwell's demon — a thought experiment (1867) about a small being that sorts fast and slow molecules and seems to beat the second law.
Szilard engine — a one-molecule version of the demon (1929) that extracts kT ln 2 of work for each bit of information it measures.
Adiabatic (reversible) computing — circuits that change state slowly and recycle the energy of the signal instead of dumping it as heat each cycle.
history: The roots lie in Maxwell's demon, imagined by James Clerk Maxwell in 1867. A tiny being at a door between two gas chambers lets fast molecules go one way and slow ones the other, creating a temperature difference without work, which would break the second law. In 1929 Leo Szilard simplified it to a single molecule in a box: the demon measures which half the molecule is in, then uses a piston to extract kT ln 2 of work. Szilard argued that the measurement must cost at least as much, but could not say precisely why. Léon Brillouin (1951) argued that measurement costs energy because the demon needs light to see the molecule.
Rolf Landauer, working at IBM Research, shifted the focus in 1961 in "Irreversibility and Heat Generation in the Computing Process". He argued that measurement can in principle be free, and the unavoidable cost is the erasure of memory. Charles Bennett, also at IBM, completed the picture. In 1973 he showed that any computation can be done reversibly, by keeping a record of all intermediate results, copying the answer, and then running the computation backwards to uncompute the record. In 1982 he applied this to Szilard's demon: the demon's memory fills up with measurement results, and resetting it to continue the cycle costs exactly the work gained. This is the accepted resolution of the paradox. Edward Fredkin and Tommaso Toffoli (around 1982) designed reversible logic gates, and Feynman's 1985 "Quantum mechanical computers" built on the same ideas.
For decades, the bound was a theoretical argument. Experiments finally tested it. In 2012, Antoine Bérut, Artak Arakelyan, Sergio Ciliberto and colleagues at ENS Lyon published in Nature a test using a single silica bead about 2 micrometres across, held in a double-well optical trap that acted as a one-bit memory. They erased the bit by tilting the trap and found that the average heat released approached kT ln 2 as erasure was made slower. Later experiments used nanomagnets (2016), feedback traps (Jun, Gavrilov and Bechhoefer, 2014) and a single trapped ion (2018). Studies also showed the extra cost of fast erasure: finite-time cost rises as the erasure time shrinks.
how: Start with a memory cell holding one bit, 0 or 1, with the value unknown. We say the memory has two possible states, so the number of microstates is W = 2 and its entropy is S = k ln 2. Now erase: force the cell to 0 whatever it held. After erasure W = 1 and S = 0. The memory's entropy dropped by k ln 2. Thermodynamics (L5) lays down the rule: the second law says the total entropy of memory plus environment cannot decrease. So the environment's entropy must rise by at least k ln 2. The environment is a heat bath at temperature T, and its entropy rises by Q/T when it absorbs heat Q. Setting Q/T ≥ k ln 2 gives Q ≥ kT ln 2. That is the whole proof at the level of principle.
The key point is why only erasure costs energy. If an operation is logically reversible, every output comes from exactly one input, so no information disappears and no entropy has to be exported. Consider a NOT gate: 0 goes to 1 and 1 goes to 0. You can always run it backwards. Compare AND, which maps the inputs 00, 01 and 10 all to 0. Given the output 0, you cannot say which input it was. Information about the input was lost, and the physical state of the world has to account for it: the lost distinction must reappear as extra disorder in the environment, which is heat. In real hardware, a gate overwrites its output node, so the old charge is thrown away as heat.
The experiment shows the mechanism in a concrete form. A bead sits in an optical trap with two wells, left for 0 and right for 1, separated by a barrier. The bead moves randomly because of thermal motion, so which well it is in is the stored bit. To erase, the experimenters first lower the barrier, so the bead can wander across the whole trap and forget which well it came from. Then they tilt the trap so that only the "0" well remains attractive, and finally raise the barrier again. The bead ends in the 0 well whatever its start. By tracking the bead's position and the force from the trap, they compute the work done on it, which equals the heat released in the water around it. Averaged over many runs, that heat falls toward kT ln 2 when the whole cycle is slow (tens of seconds) and rises when it is fast.
Next, what a bound means. Landauer's argument is about the minimum, reached only by a slow, careful process, in which the system is always close to equilibrium with the bath. A fast erasure has to dissipate more, and the extra heat grows as the process is speeded up (it scales roughly as 1/τ for erasure time τ in the simplest models). A second point: the bound applies to erasing a bit that is, from the erasing device's viewpoint, equally likely 0 or 1. If the device knows the bit's value (for example it was copied elsewhere), then resetting it can be done at no cost by a reversible operation. That is why correlations matter, and it is also how Bennett's demon resolution works.
Bennett's reversible computation shows how to approach zero dissipation. A computation of T steps is done as three phases: run forward, saving all intermediate values (this needs memory growing with T); copy the final answer to an output register (a reversible copy to a clean register); then run the program in reverse to restore the clean initial state. No bit is erased except when you finally decide to discard the answer. The price is memory, or time. Bennett's pebbling strategies cut the memory to O(S log T) with a time overhead of about T^(1+ε). So the trade is: energy against time and space.
Real circuits are very far from the bound for four reasons. First, a CMOS gate charges and discharges a capacitance C through a supply voltage V and spends about ½CV² each time, which for 1 fF at 0.7 V is 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ J, about 85,000 times kT ln 2. Second, reliable digital logic needs the signal energy to be much larger than thermal noise: an error probability of about e^(−E/kT) means that for an error rate of 10⁻¹⁵ the energy per switch must be about 35 kT, and designers keep margins above that. Third, wires, clocks and memory movement dominate, not the logic itself. Fourth, leakage (see the tunnelling and p–n junction concepts) burns energy while doing nothing. The Landauer limit is therefore not a design target for today's chips; it becomes relevant if a device ever approaches tens of kT per operation.

example: Calculate the bound. k = 1.380649 × 10⁻²³ J/K and T = 300 K, so kT = 4.14 × 10⁻²¹ J. Multiply by ln 2 = 0.6931 to get 2.87 × 10⁻²¹ J. In electron-volts, divide by 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹: 0.0179 eV, or about 18 meV. At 4 K (liquid helium) the bound is 75 times lower, 3.8 × 10⁻²³ J, but a refrigerator needs about T_hot/T_cold − 1 ≈ 74 more units of work per unit of heat removed at the Carnot limit, so cooling does not remove the cost from the whole system; it only moves it.
Compare with a real gate. Take a logic node of capacitance 1 fF (10⁻¹⁵ F, typical for a gate plus its local wire) switched at V = 0.7 V. The energy per switching event is ½CV² = 0.5 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 0.49 = 2.45 × 10⁻¹⁶ J. Divide by the bound: 2.45 × 10⁻¹⁶ / 2.87 × 10⁻²¹ ≈ 85,000. A DRAM cell with 10 fF and 1 V stores ½CV² = 5 × 10⁻¹⁵ J, which is about 1.7 million times the bound. For an AI accelerator doing 1,000 trillion operations per second at 700 W, one operation costs 7 × 10⁻¹³ J (0.7 picojoules) and that is 2.4 × 10⁸ times the cost of erasing one bit; since each operation involves tens to hundreds of bit-level operations, the per-bit gap is still 10⁵ to 10⁶.
Now flip the question: how many bit erasures could 100 W pay for at the limit? 100 / 2.87 × 10⁻²¹ ≈ 3.5 × 10²² per second. A chip with 10¹⁰ transistors switching at 3 GHz with an activity of 0.1 has about 3 × 10¹⁸ switching events per second. So even if every switching event were a full bit erasure, the limit would allow about 10,000 times more. This tells you that fundamental physics is not the current bottleneck for efficiency; engineering is. Once you also count the practical floor of about 35 kT per reliable switch, which is about 50 times the bare bound, the usable margin shrinks to a few hundred times. That is a long way off, but it is not unlimited.
Last, a reliability check. A switch with energy E has thermal error probability about e^(−E/kT). For E = kT ln 2 (the bare bound) the error probability is e^(−0.69) = 50%, a coin flip. For E = 10 kT it is 4.5 × 10⁻⁵. For E = 40 kT it is 4 × 10⁻¹⁸. Therefore a practical memory or gate cannot operate at the Landauer limit. It has to run at least 30 to 40 times above it, so the reachable floor is nearer 10⁻¹⁹ J per switch than 10⁻²¹ J.
intuition: Forgetting costs heat because it squeezes many possible pasts into one, and the extra possibilities must go somewhere, into the disorder of the surroundings.
Think of a room with a thousand unsorted papers. Sorting them into one pile is easy to do, but the information about the old order is gone, and in the physical world nothing disappears: the lost information must be written somewhere, as tiny extra motion of atoms nearby, which is heat. The principle says only erasing is expensive. Moving, copying and reversible calculation can in principle be free, because nothing is forgotten.
mistakes: People often think computing must use energy in proportion to the amount of calculation. In fact the only unavoidable cost is for logically irreversible steps, specifically erasure. Bennett showed that any computation can be done reversibly in principle. The costs engineers pay today come from charging wires and leaking transistors, not from this bound.
People often think Landauer's limit explains why chips get hot. In fact current chips are 10⁴ to 10⁶ times above the limit, so it does not explain their heat. The heat comes from ½CV² switching losses and leakage. The limit matters as a long-range floor and as a clean statement of the link between information and entropy.
People often think the bound has been proven only on paper. In fact several experiments measured it in small systems: the 2012 Lyon experiment on a colloidal bead, then nanomagnets (2016) and others. They test the bound for slow erasure. They did not build a computer that runs at the limit.
People often think reversible computing makes computing free. In fact reversible circuits must still drive clocks, correct errors (error correction needs fresh, erased bits, so it dumps entropy), and run slowly so that friction-like losses are small. The energy per operation falls roughly as the operation time grows, so you gain efficiency at the price of speed, and extra circuit area.
tradeoffs: Choose conventional irreversible CMOS when throughput and cost per operation matter, which covers almost every product today. It is simple, fast, and cheap, and you pay ½CV² for every switch. Choose adiabatic (charge-recovery) logic only where energy per operation dominates and speed is flexible, such as ultra-low-power sensors or special research chips. Energy per operation scales roughly as (RC / τ) × CV², so running 10 times slower can cut switching energy by about 10 times in the ideal case. You give up speed, need multi-phase power clocks, use more transistors, and the savings shrink once leakage and clock-generator losses are counted.
Choose to treat Landauer's bound as a design target only for very low-energy devices (below about 10⁻¹⁸ J per operation, as in some superconducting logic and nanomagnet logic proposals). For ordinary chips, treat it as a guide to which operations are fundamentally wasteful. Where the model fails: it applies to equilibrium-like, slow processes and ignores the cost of reliability; the realistic floor is tens of kT, not 0.69 kT.
Choose recomputation or reversible pebbling when memory is cheap but energy and heat are costly, as in a cryogenic or space system. Choose erasure and overwrite (the normal approach) when memory is cheap and energy is not the constraint. The reversible approach holds all intermediate states, so memory grows, and Bennett's tricks cut it to O(S log T) with about T^(1+ε) time; you give up simplicity, and compilers and algorithms need rewriting. This is an active research area (companies such as Vaire Computing work on it), with no mainstream product yet.
Choose the thermodynamic model (Landauer) over the information-theoretic model (Shannon) when the question is about heat, energy and physical devices. Choose Shannon when the question is about codes, compression and channel capacity, where physical cost does not appear. The two meet in the formula: H bits of entropy equal k ln 2 × H of thermodynamic entropy. Use a stochastic thermodynamics model (fluctuation theorems) for single nanoscale devices, where the heat per erasure is a random variable with a distribution, not a fixed number.
friction: The bound is far below practice, so it gives little direct guidance for today's design. The gap is largest in memory and wires: moving a bit across a chip costs much more than computing on it, and moving it from DRAM costs hundreds of times more than a floating-point operation. These costs are set by capacitance and distance, not by Landauer.
Reversible computing has failed to reach production for 50 years, for sound reasons. Circuits must run slowly, which conflicts with a market that wants speed. Resonant clock circuits must store and return energy efficiently, with high-quality inductors or other resonators, and any loss in them is a loss that never goes away. Every error-correction step, including those of a reversible circuit, needs to dump entropy by resetting bits, so some erasure always returns. And software is written for irreversible machines.
There is also a conceptual debate. Some physicists (John Norton and others) argue that the principle is derived from assumptions that already contain the second law, so it does not independently resolve Maxwell's demon; most researchers accept the Landauer–Bennett account. A second subtlety is the role of the observer: whether a bit is "erased" depends on what correlations exist with other systems, which makes the cost depend on knowledge, not on the physical state alone. Quantum versions of the bound exist and also involve entanglement. All of these are active in the field of stochastic and quantum thermodynamics.
practice: As an engineer you will not design to Landauer's limit, but you can use the idea in three ways. First, as a sanity check on claims: a device that claims femtojoules per operation is at least 10⁵ above the limit, so room for improvement exists; a device that claims energies below kT ln 2 per irreversible bit operation is wrong. Second, as a way to think about waste: the largest energy costs in computing come from moving and refreshing data, not from the arithmetic, so reducing data movement (caching, batching, compression, keeping data near compute) is the practical sibling of the principle.
Third, as a lens on algorithm design: in-place overwriting and discarding of intermediate state are what the bound counts, while keeping results in memory costs no Landauer energy but costs capacity and refresh. Trade-offs of recompute against store are the same ones Bennett analysed. For estimating future limits, remember the numbers: 2.9 × 10⁻²¹ J per bit at 300 K, about 35 kT (10⁻¹⁹ J) for a reliable switch, about 10⁻¹⁶ J for today's logic gate, and about 10⁻¹³ J (0.1 pJ) for a 64-bit floating-point operation at advanced nodes, which is about thirty million times the bound for a single bit.

For the wider story, see the book Maxwell's Demon 2 (Leff and Rex), and Sagawa's work on the thermodynamics of information.

value: Magnetism is a force between moving electric charges, and in materials it comes mainly from electron spin. In a ferromagnet such as iron or cobalt, the tiny magnetic moments of many electrons line up and stay lined up, so a region of the material has a stable north-south direction even with no current. That stable direction is a memory: two directions can stand for 0 and 1. A hard disk is a spinning plate coated with billions of tiny magnetic grains, and a head that writes a bit by setting the direction of a patch of grains, and reads it by sensing the field above them.
This matters for anyone who runs data systems. Most of the world's stored bytes sit on hard disks and tape, because magnetic storage is several times cheaper per terabyte than flash (recently about three to five times) and holds data for years without power. Large cloud object stores, backup tiers, video archives and data lakes are mostly spinning disks. Hard disks are also the reason for the shape of many database and file system designs: a seek costs milliseconds, sequential reads are fast, and so log-structured and batch designs win. Magnetism also gives transformers and inductors in power supplies, motors in fans and disk spindles, and newer non-volatile memories (MRAM).
The concept stands on two lower ones. Quantum mechanics (L5) supplies the source of the effect: electron spin, and the exchange interaction, a consequence of the Pauli exclusion principle, which makes neighbouring spins prefer to line up. Without it, classical physics cannot explain any permanent magnet at all (the Bohr–van Leeuwen theorem proves this). Electromagnetism (Maxwell, L5) supplies the tools: currents make magnetic fields (Ampère's law), changing fields make voltages (Faraday's law), and these two laws are how a write head's coil sets a bit and how an inductive sensor or transformer works.
terms: Magnetic moment — a small bar-magnet strength carried by an electron (its spin) or a loop of current; the unit for an electron is the Bohr magneton, 9.27 × 10⁻²⁴ J/T.
Ferromagnet — a material in which neighbouring moments align spontaneously below a critical temperature (iron, cobalt, nickel and their alloys).
Exchange interaction — a quantum effect, due to the Pauli principle, that lowers the energy when neighbouring spins are parallel (in a ferromagnet); it is far stronger than the direct magnetic force between moments.
Curie temperature — the temperature above which thermal motion destroys the alignment; 1,043 K for iron, and about 750 K for the FePt alloy used in new disks.
Domain — a region in which all moments point the same way; domain walls separate regions that point differently.
Coercivity — the reverse field needed to flip a magnetised material; high coercivity means a stable bit that is hard to write.
Magnetic anisotropy Ku — the energy density that makes a grain prefer one axis; it holds a grain's direction against thermal noise.
Magnetoresistance (GMR, TMR) — a change in electrical resistance that depends on whether two magnetic layers are aligned; this is how a read head senses a bit.
Superparamagnetic limit — the grain size below which thermal noise flips a grain's direction spontaneously, so it forgets its bit.
history: Magnets were known in antiquity, and Maxwell's equations (1860s) unified electricity and magnetism. Pierre Weiss proposed in 1907 that a ferromagnet contains domains, each fully magnetised, which explains why an iron bar can be unmagnetised overall. The reason for alignment was understood only with quantum mechanics: Werner Heisenberg showed in 1928 that the exchange interaction can align spins.
Magnetic recording started early. Valdemar Poulsen recorded sound on steel wire in 1898, and magnetic tape was used for audio in the 1930s and for computer data from 1951 (UNIVAC I). IBM's 350 disk storage unit (RAMAC, 1956) held about 5 MB on fifty 24-inch platters, and cost about $3,000 per month to lease. Densities grew by about 40 to 100 percent a year for decades, driven by smaller heads and better media. Inductive heads, which read and write with the same coil, gave way to separate designs: in 1990 IBM introduced magnetoresistive read heads. Albert Fert and Peter Grünberg discovered giant magnetoresistance (GMR) in 1988 (Nobel Prize 2007), and IBM shipped GMR heads in 1997. Tunnelling magnetoresistance (TMR) heads, based on electrons tunnelling through a thin oxide between two magnets, followed in the mid-2000s.
The next problems were physical limits. Around 2000, designers worried that grains would become too small to hold their direction at room temperature (the superparamagnetic limit). Perpendicular recording, with grains magnetised up or down relative to the disk instead of along the track, reached products in 2005 to 2006 and allowed denser grains. Later steps were shingled recording (about 2013 to 2014), which overlaps tracks like roof tiles, helium-filled drives for lower drag, and heat-assisted magnetic recording (HAMR), where a laser warms a tiny spot during writing; HAMR drives of 30 TB and more began shipping to cloud customers around 2024 to 2025. Spin-torque magnetic memory (STT-MRAM) became a commercial embedded memory around 2018 to 2019.
how: Start with the electron. Besides its charge, it has spin, which makes it a tiny magnet. In most materials the moments of different electrons point in random directions or cancel in pairs (Pauli exclusion fills states with one up and one down). In iron, cobalt and nickel, the outer d-shell electrons are only partly paired, leaving net moments, and the exchange interaction makes neighbouring atoms prefer the same spin direction. It is a quantum effect of the Pauli principle: parallel spins keep electrons apart in space, which lowers their electrostatic repulsion. The energy saving is of the order of 0.1 eV per atom pair, equivalent to temperatures of ~1,000 K, which is why iron remains magnetic until 1,043 K. Above the Curie temperature, thermal agitation wins and the order disappears.
If exchange were all, an iron bar would always be a single giant magnet. It is not, because every magnetised region produces a stray field that costs energy. The material lowers its total energy by splitting into domains whose fields close back on one another, separated by domain walls a few tens of nanometres thick. In a storage medium, the designers do the opposite: they make the material from tiny grains, 6 to 10 nm across, separated by a non-magnetic oxide, so that each grain is a single domain. A bit is written as a patch of roughly 5 to 20 grains all magnetised in the same direction, about 10 to 20 nm long along the track and 50 to 80 nm across it. A pattern of up and down patches along a track forms the data, and the boundaries between patches (transitions) are what the read head detects.
Writing uses electromagnetism. The write head is a small electromagnet: a coil carrying current I produces a field in a soft magnetic core (Ampère's law), and the core concentrates it into a gap a few tens of nanometres wide that hovers just over the platter. The field, up to about 2 tesla for the best pole materials, must exceed the coercivity of the grains so that they flip to match. Reversing the current reverses the field. Reading uses magnetoresistance: the sensor is a stack of two thin magnetic layers separated by a spacer. One layer is pinned in a fixed direction and the other is free to turn with the field from the disk. With TMR, electrons tunnel through a spacer about 1 nm of MgO, and the resistance is lower when the two layers are parallel than when they are antiparallel, by 100 percent or more. A small current through the sensor turns the stray field of a transition into a voltage change.
The head flies on a thin film of air, between about 1 and 3 nm above the surface, held there by the air flow created by the spinning platter (7,200 rpm is a common speed, with the surface moving at tens of metres per second). The actuator is a voice coil motor, an electromagnet in a magnetic field, that positions the head over a track. Tracks are about 50 to 80 nm apart, so servo information written in the track tells the head where it is, and a feedback loop keeps it within a few nanometres.
Stability sets the limits. A grain flips by thermal activation across an energy barrier of Ku·V, where Ku is its anisotropy energy density and V its volume. The chance that it flips is governed by the Arrhenius law: the time to flip is τ = τ₀ · exp(Ku·V / kT), with τ₀ about 10⁻⁹ s. The ratio Ku·V / kT is the thermal stability factor, and drives usually need about 60 or more for ten-year retention of a full bit with many grains. To store data at higher density you want smaller grains, which means smaller V, so you need higher Ku to compensate. But higher Ku means higher coercivity and the write head's field (limited to about 2.4 T by the physics of the pole material) cannot flip the grains. This is the trilemma of magnetic recording: signal-to-noise (more grains per bit), thermal stability (large Ku·V), and writability (field available). HAMR breaks the trilemma by using a material with very high Ku (FePt, ~7 × 10⁶ J/m³, about 20 times that of the older alloys), and heating a spot of 30 to 50 nm to near its Curie temperature for a nanosecond with a laser and a near-field transducer. Near the Curie temperature the coercivity drops, the head can write, and then the spot cools in a few nanoseconds and freezes.

example: First the timing of a 7,200 rpm disk. One revolution takes 60 / 7,200 = 8.33 ms, so the average rotational wait for a random sector is half of that, 4.17 ms. Add an average seek of about 4 ms to move the head, and a random 4 KB read takes about 8.2 ms, or about 120 random reads per second. A fast sequential read on the outer track reaches about 270 MB/s, so one revolution reads 270 MB/s × 8.33 ms ≈ 2.25 MB. Reading 2.25 MB sequentially costs 8.33 ms, while reading the same data as 550 random 4 KB blocks costs 550 × 8.2 ms ≈ 4.5 seconds: a 540 times difference. This is why disk-based systems batch, sort and append.
Now the physics of one grain. Take a cylindrical grain of diameter 8 nm and height 15 nm: V = π × (4 × 10⁻⁹)² × 15 × 10⁻⁹ = 7.5 × 10⁻²⁵ m³. With Ku = 3.5 × 10⁵ J/m³ (typical for a CoCrPt alloy), Ku·V = 2.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. At 300 K, kT = 4.14 × 10⁻²¹ J, so the stability factor is 2.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 4.14 × 10⁻²¹ ≈ 64. The flip time is 10⁻⁹ × e^64 ≈ 10⁻⁹ × 6 × 10²⁷ = 6 × 10¹⁸ seconds, far beyond ten years (3 × 10⁸ s). Now shrink the diameter to 5 nm to double the density: V falls by (5/8)² = 0.39 and the factor drops to 25. The flip time becomes 10⁻⁹ × e^25 ≈ 72 seconds. Data written to such a disk would vanish in about a minute. Shrinking by 37 percent in diameter turned a stable bit into a forgetful one, because the time depends exponentially on the stability factor.
The cure: use FePt with Ku = 7 × 10⁶ J/m³. A grain of diameter 5 nm and height 10 nm has V = π × (2.5 × 10⁻⁹)² × 10 × 10⁻⁹ = 2.0 × 10⁻²⁵ m³ and Ku·V = 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ J, so the stability factor is about 330, safe for geological times. But the anisotropy field of FePt is 2Ku / Ms ≈ 2 × 7 × 10⁶ / 1.1 × 10⁶ ≈ 13 T, well above the 2.4 T a head can deliver. So the drive must heat the grain: at about 600 to 700 K Ku has fallen enough for the head's field to flip it, and on cooling it becomes stable again. That is HAMR in numbers.
intuition: A disk is a field of tiny compass needles locked in place by their own stiffness; the head turns a patch of them up or down, and the stiffness keeps them from turning back on their own.
The idea is natural once you see two quantum facts together. Exchange makes neighbours agree, which creates a stable direction. A directional preference (anisotropy) makes that direction hard to change, which makes it a memory. Both are energy barriers, and a barrier in a world at temperature T is crossed with probability about e^(−barrier/kT). So the design problem is simply to make the barrier a few dozen kT high, while keeping it low enough to write. Every limit of magnetic storage is a statement about this single exponential.
mistakes: People often think a magnet is a material with a lot of "magnetic stuff" in it, separate from electricity. In fact a magnetic field is produced by moving charge, including the intrinsic spin of electrons, and the two are part of the same electromagnetic theory. The same physics gives you a coil, a transformer and a hard drive head.
People often think a hard drive writes bits by "burning" or marking the disk. In fact the head only changes the direction of magnetisation of grains, and does not change the material. That is why a disk can be rewritten billions of times without wearing, unlike flash, and why a strong external magnet or heat above the Curie temperature can erase it.
People often think shrinking bits is limited only by making a smaller head. In fact the limit is the stability of the grains (Ku·V / kT). Smaller bits need smaller grains, and below a certain size heat alone can flip them. This is why density growth slowed from about 40 percent per year to a few percent per year in the 2010s, and why new techniques such as HAMR are needed.
People often think SSDs have replaced hard disks. In fact SSDs have replaced disks for hot and warm data, but disks remain the main medium for capacity tiers because of price per terabyte. Tape remains cheaper still for archives, at a lower cost per terabyte than disk when drive and media costs are spread over a large library.
tradeoffs: Choose hard disks when you need a lot of capacity at the lowest cost per terabyte with moderate sequential throughput: object storage, data lakes, backups and video. Choose SSDs when you need random reads in microseconds (about 100 µs against about 8 ms, a factor of 80), tens of thousands to millions of IOPS per device, or low latency tails, accepting a higher cost per terabyte (roughly 3 to 5 times) and finite write endurance. Choose tape for cold archives you read rarely, when you can wait minutes for a robot to mount a cartridge, because cartridges (LTO-9 holds 18 TB native) need no power while stored and have a long shelf life; you give up random access and any quick retrieval.
Choose conventional magnetic recording (CMR) disks for workloads with random or in-place writes, such as databases and general file systems. Choose shingled drives (SMR) when data is written in large sequential chunks and rarely modified, such as backup, archive and object stores that you can fit to zones, for about 15 to 25 percent more capacity per platter. You give up the ability to overwrite in place: modifying one track requires rewriting the shingles after it, so random writes can be slow (tens of IOPS) unless the host manages zones.
Choose a simple stable-grain model (the Arrhenius thermal stability factor of 60 or more) when estimating whether a magnetic bit survives. Choose micromagnetic simulation (solving the Landau–Lifshitz–Gilbert equation on a mesh) when you need domain walls, switching dynamics or head fields, at much higher computing cost. Choose the Weiss mean-field model for a quick Curie temperature, and the Heisenberg model with Monte Carlo when you need critical behaviour. Each step adds accuracy and cost; for system design the thermal stability factor is usually enough.
Choose MRAM when you need non-volatile memory that is fast (nanoseconds), has nearly unlimited endurance (10¹² cycles and more), and low standby power, such as embedded memory in microcontrollers or caches that must survive power loss. Choose SRAM or DRAM for the highest density and speed at low cost today. MRAM cells are larger than DRAM cells, need relatively high write currents, and are more expensive per bit.
friction: Magnetic storage is tied to mechanics, and mechanics is slow. A seek takes milliseconds, a rotation takes 8 ms, and the head flies a few nanometres above a surface moving at tens of metres per second. Heads crash if dust or shock intrude, and drives are sensitive to vibration, an effect that matters in dense chassis where neighbouring drives shake each other. Failure rates of roughly 1 to 2 percent per year (the annual figure published by Backblaze for its fleet, which varies by model) are why large systems use replication or erasure coding.
Density growth has slowed. Capacity per drive rises by roughly 10 to 15 percent a year using more platters, helium, SMR and now HAMR, in place of the 40 percent per year of the 1990s and 2000s. Capacity grows faster than speed: a drive's sequential rate grows with the square root of the areal density, but its capacity grows with the density, so the time to read a whole drive (about 20 TB at 270 MB/s is over 20 hours) gets longer every year, and rebuild times after a failure get longer too. Random IOPS per drive have not changed in 20 years.
New technologies bring new problems. HAMR needs lasers and near-field transducers that survive repeated heating, and heat-resistant lubricants and overcoats. MRAM must balance retention, write current and switching speed, with variability at small sizes. Spintronics research is also hard to scale.
practice: When you design a storage tier, keep the physical numbers in mind: a 7,200 rpm disk gives about 100 to 150 random IOPS and about 250 MB/s sequential, a 20 TB drive takes more than 20 hours to read in full, and the specified rate of unrecoverable read errors is about one per 10¹⁴ bits for consumer drives and one per 10¹⁵ for enterprise drives. Use these to size erasure coding (more parity for larger drives), set rebuild bandwidth limits, and decide how many drives an object-store node should hold.
Design software for sequential access on disks: use large blocks (1 MB or more), append-only logs, batch writes, and LSM trees or columnar files (Parquet) with large row groups. Read-ahead and a few outstanding requests help disks, but too many random requests only add queueing. For data lakes on HDD-backed object storage, favour fewer, larger files over millions of small files. For tape, plan restore times and test them.
For SMR, use zone-aware software (the ZBC and ZAC command sets and Linux's zoned block support) or choose drive-managed SMR only for backup. Watch SMART data (reallocated sectors, pending sectors) and temperature, since failure rates rise with heat. To study further, read Chappert, Fert and Nguyen Van Dau on spin electronics, the chapters on disks in Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces (free online), and the Backblaze quarterly drive statistics for real failure data.

@@ photonics
value: Photonics is the science and engineering of using light (photons) to carry, process and sense information. In computing its most important use is the optical fibre: a strand of glass thinner than a hair that guides pulses of infrared light for tens of kilometres with only a small loss. Almost all long-distance internet traffic, every undersea cable and an increasing part of the links inside data centres travel as light, because glass loses far less signal than copper at high data rates. Modern fibre loses about 0.2 dB per kilometre, so after 80 km about 2.5 percent of the light is still there, and an amplifier can restore it without converting it to electricity.
Photonics also gives the layer above its lasers and detectors. A laser diode is the light source of a fibre link, of a Lidar sensor, of an optical mouse and of a Blu-ray drive. A photodiode (a p–n junction that turns absorbed photons into current) is the receiver in every link and every camera sensor. Photolithography uses light of 193 nm and 13.5 nm to print the patterns of chips. Inside AI data centres, the question of the 2020s is how far the optics can move toward the processor, because copper wires are running out of reach at 100 to 200 Gbit/s per lane.
Photonics stands on two lower concepts. Electromagnetism (Maxwell, L5) says that light is an electromagnetic wave. Its equations, with boundary conditions at the glass interfaces, give refraction, total internal reflection, and the guided modes of a fibre. Quantum mechanics (L5) says that light is exchanged in photons of energy hf, that atoms and semiconductors emit light at energies set by their level spacing, and that an incoming photon can stimulate the emission of an identical one, which is the principle of the laser. Detectors work by the reverse: a photon lifts an electron across a band gap.
terms: Photon — a quantum of light with energy E = hf = hc/λ; at 1,550 nm a photon carries 0.80 eV.
Refractive index n — the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in a material; about 1.45 for silica glass, so light in fibre travels at about 200,000 km/s (5 µs per km).
Total internal reflection — light hitting a boundary into a lower-index material at a shallow enough angle is fully reflected; it keeps light inside the fibre core.
Single-mode fibre — a fibre with a core of about 9 µm that supports one guided light pattern (mode); it avoids modal spreading and carries long-distance signals.
Attenuation (dB/km) — the logarithmic loss of power per kilometre; 3 dB means half the power is lost.
Chromatic dispersion — different wavelengths travel at slightly different speeds, so a pulse spreads out; about 17 ps per nm per km in standard fibre at 1,550 nm.
Stimulated emission — an excited atom, struck by a photon, emits a second photon with the same wavelength, phase and direction; this is the gain process of a laser.
Wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) — sending many wavelengths (colours) of light through one fibre at the same time, each carrying its own data.
Coherent detection — a receiver that mixes the incoming light with a local laser to recover both amplitude and phase, allowing high-order modulation.
history: The idea of guiding light is old (John Tyndall showed in 1870 that light follows a jet of water), but fibre was useless for communication because glass lost too much light: about 1,000 dB/km in the 1950s, so 90 percent of the light was gone after only 10 metres. Meanwhile, Albert Einstein described stimulated emission in 1917. Charles Townes built the maser (microwave version) in 1954, and Theodore Maiman built the first laser, using a ruby crystal, in 1960. Semiconductor diode lasers followed in 1962 (Hall at GE, Nathan at IBM, Holonyak at GE), and they became practical at room temperature with double heterostructures (Zhores Alferov and Herbert Kroemer, working separately, and Hayashi and Panish at Bell Labs in 1970).
In 1966 Charles Kao and George Hockham, at Standard Telecommunication Laboratories in England, showed that the loss was due to impurities and not to glass itself, and that pure silica could reach 20 dB/km, a level at which fibre links would work (Kao shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2009). In 1970 Robert Maurer, Donald Keck and Peter Schultz at Corning made a fibre with 17 dB/km. By 1979 losses had reached 0.2 dB/km at 1,550 nm, near the theoretical limit of silica set by Rayleigh scattering. The first transatlantic fibre cable, TAT-8, entered service in 1988.
The next breakthrough was amplification. Erbium-doped fibre amplifiers (EDFAs; Emmanuel Desurvire, David Payne and others, 1987) amplify light directly, over the 1,530 to 1,565 nm band, with no conversion to electricity, and they amplify all wavelengths together. That made WDM economical in the 1990s. Capacity per fibre grew from 2.5 Gbit/s to terabits per second. From the 2010s, coherent detection with digital signal processing allowed 100, 400 and 800 Gbit/s per wavelength. Silicon photonics, which builds optical waveguides, modulators and germanium detectors on silicon wafers in chip foundries, grew from the 2000s, and co-packaged optics (CPO) switches began to appear from 2024 to 2025.
how: A fibre has a glass core surrounded by a cladding with a slightly lower refractive index (for example 1.468 and 1.463). By Maxwell's equations, a light wave at the boundary is reflected totally if its angle to the boundary is larger than the critical angle, arcsin(n_clad / n_core) = arcsin(0.9966) ≈ 85.3 degrees. Light that goes nearly parallel to the axis therefore bounces back and forth and stays in the core. In a precise description, the core allows only certain patterns, called modes, each a solution of Maxwell's equations with the boundary conditions. If the core is small (about 9 µm) and the index step is small, only one mode exists at 1,310 or 1,550 nm: a single-mode fibre. In a multimode fibre (50 µm core), many modes travel at slightly different speeds, which spreads pulses and limits the distance to about 100 m at 100 Gbit/s but allows cheap sources.
A link has four blocks. A transmitter converts electrical bits into modulated light: either by switching a laser directly (for short links), or, for faster and longer links, by an external modulator (Mach–Zehnder or electro-absorption) that changes the amplitude, and for coherent links the phase and polarisation. The laser itself works by stimulated emission: a forward-biased p–n junction (see that concept) injects electrons and holes into a thin active layer of a direct-gap material such as InGaAsP; their recombination releases photons whose energy matches the gap (λ = 1240 / Eg in nm); mirrors on both ends make a cavity, and photons passing through stimulate more identical photons, so the output is coherent light at a single wavelength. The fibre carries the light and loses it slowly. A receiver converts light to current with a photodiode (germanium or InGaAs at 1,310 and 1,550 nm, because silicon is blind beyond 1,100 nm) and digitises it.
Two details explain why the ends work. A laser only amplifies light when more electrons sit in the upper energy state than the lower one (population inversion), which a diode achieves by pumping current through the junction. Below a threshold current (typically a few to 30 mA) spontaneous emission dominates and the output is weak and incoherent; above it, stimulated emission wins, the output rises steeply with current, and the light becomes coherent. Modulating the current above threshold changes the light output within tens of picoseconds. On the receiving side, a photodiode converts light to current with a responsivity R = ηq/(hf), about 0.9 A/W at 1,550 nm for a good InGaAs device, so −17 dBm (20 µW) of light gives about 18 µA, which a transimpedance amplifier turns into a voltage. The thermal noise of that amplifier, set by temperature (the thermal noise concept), is what ultimately defines the receiver sensitivity.
Three impairments set the distance. Attenuation is mostly Rayleigh scattering, which falls as 1/λ⁴, plus absorption by water (OH) at 1,383 nm and the infrared absorption edge of silica; the minimum is at 1,550 nm, 0.17 to 0.2 dB/km. Amplifiers every 60 to 100 km restore power but add noise (amplified spontaneous emission), so the signal-to-noise ratio falls with each span. Chromatic dispersion makes pulses spread by 17 ps/(nm·km) times the spectral width, while the fibre's nonlinearity (the refractive index changes with intensity) limits how much power can be launched; there is therefore an optimum launch power, around 0 to 3 dBm per channel, and the Shannon capacity of the fibre has a maximum. Modern coherent receivers correct dispersion in digital signal processing, which uses a lot of power but allows 1,000 km and more without optical dispersion compensation.
Capacity grows by multiplying three things: wavelengths (WDM, 40 to 100 or more channels), bits per symbol (the coherent QAM format, 2 to 6 bits per symbol per polarisation), and symbol rate (30 to 130 gigabaud). A modern long-haul wavelength carries 400 to 800 Gbit/s, and one fibre pair 20 to 40 Tbit/s over the C band. Subsea cables pack 8 to 24 fibre pairs, limited by the power available to the repeaters. In data centres, links are shorter and cheaper: multimode VCSEL transceivers up to about 100 m, single-mode transceivers for 500 m to 10 km, often with PAM4 modulation (4 amplitude levels, 2 bits per symbol) at 100 Gbit/s per lane, 8 lanes for an 800G module. Inside racks, copper cables carry signals up to about 1 to 2 metres at 100 Gbit/s per lane, and less at 200 Gbit/s per lane, which is why optics keep moving closer.

example: Do a link budget for one 80 km span of standard fibre at 1,550 nm. Loss is 0.2 dB/km × 80 km = 16 dB, plus 1 dB for connectors and splices, so 17 dB in all. A launch power of 0 dBm (1 mW) arrives at −17 dBm, which is 20 µW (since 10^(−1.7) = 0.02 mW). A good coherent receiver works at −20 dBm or better, so the margin is 3 dB. An EDFA with 17 dB of gain brings the signal back to 0 dBm. Ten spans make 800 km: ten amplifiers each add noise, and the optical signal-to-noise ratio falls by 10 log₁₀(10) = 10 dB compared with a single span. A single span might give an OSNR of about 35 dB (in 0.1 nm), so 10 spans leave about 25 dB, enough for a 16QAM channel at 400 Gbit/s over such a distance.
Now the dispersion limit of a simple link. A 25 gigabaud signal has a bandwidth of about 25 GHz. In wavelength terms, Δλ = λ² Δf / c = (1,550 × 10⁻⁹)² × 25 × 10⁹ / 3 × 10⁸ ≈ 0.2 nm. Over 10 km of standard fibre at 1,550 nm, the pulse spreads by D × L × Δλ = 17 × 10 × 0.2 = 34 ps. The symbol period is 40 ps, so the pulse spreads by almost a full symbol and neighbouring bits overlap badly. At 1,310 nm the dispersion of standard fibre is nearly zero, so for 10 km links at 25 to 100 Gbit/s per lane, designers use 1,310 nm lasers (the LR optics standard) even though the loss is 0.35 dB/km instead of 0.2, which does not matter for 10 km (3.5 dB). For 80 km or more at 1,550 nm, the answer is coherent detection with digital dispersion compensation instead.
Last, a cost-of-power calculation for a data-centre switch. An 800G pluggable optical module uses roughly 15 W, so 15 / 800 × 10⁹ = 19 pJ per bit. A switch with 64 such ports burns 64 × 15 = 960 W in its optics alone. Co-packaged optics aim for around 5 to 10 pJ/bit by removing the long electrical path between the switch chip and the module, which would bring the same 64 ports to 250 to 500 W. In a cluster with 10,000 switches, saving 500 W each is 5 MW, which is worth about $4 million a year at $0.09 per kWh before cooling is counted.
intuition: A fibre is a pipe with mirror walls, so light cannot leak out, and the glass is so clear that you could see through a window of it 100 metres thick.
Light is the best carrier of data for the same reason it is a poor carrier of power: it has a very high frequency (about 193 THz at 1,550 nm), so a modulated beam has huge bandwidth, and unlike electrical signals it does not lose energy by heating the wire as the data rate goes up. Copper loses more and more with frequency (skin effect and dielectric loss), while glass loses almost the same at 1 Gbit/s and at 1 Tbit/s. The cost is at the ends, where electricity must be converted to light and back, and that conversion is what makes distance the deciding factor.
mistakes: People often think light in a fibre moves at the speed of light in vacuum. In fact in glass with n ≈ 1.468 the speed is about 204,000 km/s, roughly 5 µs per km. This is why a round trip between New York and London over 5,600 km of cable takes at least 56 ms, and why microwave links, which travel through air at near vacuum speed, win some finance routes.
People often think more wavelengths or more fibres always mean more bandwidth with no limit. In fact the Shannon limit and fibre nonlinearity cap how much signal power can be used, so a single-mode fibre in the C band can carry on the order of 100 Tbit/s, not infinitely more. This is why operators add fibre pairs, use more bands (L, S) and use multi-core fibre in research.
People often think optical means "fast and free of loss". In fact every conversion between electrical and optical forms costs energy, area and latency (a pluggable module adds tens of nanoseconds, more with forward error correction), and lasers have limited lifetime and drift with temperature. Optics win on bandwidth times distance, not on every metric.
People often think a laser is just a bright light. In fact a laser is defined by stimulated emission and a cavity, which produce light that is coherent (single wavelength and phase). A bright LED emits many wavelengths in random phases and cannot be modulated as fast or focused as tightly, which is why fibre links use lasers, with LEDs only for short, slow links.
tradeoffs: Choose copper (passive direct-attach cables or PCB traces) when the distance is under about 2 metres at 100 Gbit/s per lane (shorter at 200G), as inside a rack, because it costs a fraction of the optics, uses no extra power and has no laser to fail. Choose active optical cables or transceivers beyond that: you pay about 10 to 20 pJ/bit and several times the price per port, but reach 100 m to 10 km with thin, light cables. As lane speeds go to 200 Gbit/s and beyond, the copper reach shrinks, and the crossing point moves toward the chip, which is the argument for co-packaged optics.
Choose multimode fibre with VCSEL lasers (850 nm) for links up to about 100 m within a data hall: the transceivers are cheap and tolerate loose alignment, but multimode fibre limits the distance and the bit rate per lane. Choose single-mode fibre with 1,310 nm lasers for 500 m to 10 km, with a higher transceiver cost but a fibre plant that will not need to be replaced for the next speed generation. Choose coherent optics beyond about 40 to 80 km, or for campus links with limited fibre, paying with a transceiver that uses more power (15 to 25 W for 400ZR) and DSP chips, but using the full phase and amplitude of the light to carry more bits per wavelength.
Choose pluggable modules when you want flexibility, multiple vendors and easy replacement of a failed laser; choose co-packaged optics (CPO) when switch power and port density are the limits, since the optical engine sits next to the switch ASIC and the electrical path is a few millimetres. You give up serviceability: a failed laser may mean replacing a switch, so vendors place the lasers in a replaceable external module (external laser source). Choose on-board optics as a middle step.
For modelling, choose ray optics when the structure is much larger than the wavelength (lenses, multimode fibre, free-space alignment), Maxwell's wave solutions (mode solvers, finite-difference time-domain simulation) when features are comparable to the wavelength (single-mode waveguides, silicon photonics, gratings), and quantum models (rate equations with photon numbers, or full quantum optics) for laser noise, single-photon detectors and quantum communication. Each step is more accurate but more costly to compute.
friction: The edges of the system are the hard part. Lasers are the least reliable component of a link: they age, drift with temperature (wavelength shifts by about 0.1 nm per degree for a DFB laser) and need temperature control; laser reliability is a main concern for module makers and for operators of large clusters. Silicon cannot emit light efficiently because of its indirect gap, so the lasers are made of III–V materials such as InP and bonded or attached, which adds cost and yield losses.
Coupling light between a chip and a fibre is a precision problem. A silicon waveguide is a few hundred nanometres wide, a fibre core is about 9,000 nm, and alignment tolerances are around a micron, which makes packaging expensive and the largest share of module cost. Thermal management is difficult in co-packaging: lasers dislike heat, and the switch ASIC next to them can reach 100 degrees Celsius.
Capacity growth is also approaching limits. Nonlinear effects and the amplifier bandwidth cap capacity per fibre, so growth now comes from spatial parallelism (more fibres and cables), which cuts the cost advantage. Optical switching (MEMS mirrors or similar circuit switches in Google's data centres) removes electrical conversion but needs slow reconfiguration (milliseconds), so it suits predictable, long-lived flows such as training traffic. All-optical computing and optical buffering remain research topics because photons do not interact easily, which is also why they make good messengers and poor logic gates.
practice: In capacity planning, use light speed in fibre, 5 µs per km, for latency budgets: a 100 km metro link adds 0.5 ms one way, and a cross-continent 4,000 km link adds 20 ms. Add 1 to 2 µs for each optical module pair in tight loops. When choosing optics, state reach and speed first (SR for 100 m, DR for 500 m, FR for 2 km, LR for 10 km), then check power per module, because 800G modules draw about 14 to 18 W and a fully loaded switch must be cooled for it.
Run link-budget checks like the one in the example: transmitter power minus fibre loss, connector loss (0.3 to 0.75 dB each) and margin must exceed the receiver sensitivity. Dirty connectors cause most field problems in data centres, so clean end faces and inspect them. Monitor digital optical monitoring (DOM) values on modules: transmit and receive power, temperature and bias current drift warn you of a failing laser days before the link drops. Keep the fibre plant single-mode where you can; it survives several speed generations, while multimode does not.
To study the subject, read Kao and Hockham's 1966 paper (short and clear), Agrawal's Fiber-Optic Communication Systems, and the Ethernet standards summaries for 400G and 800G. For silicon photonics and co-packaged optics, look at the Open Compute Project and OIF documents, and talks from the OFC conference.
quiz: Why does light stay inside an optical fibre? || The core has a slightly higher refractive index than the cladding, so light hitting the boundary at a shallow enough angle (above the critical angle, about 85 degrees for n of 1.468 and 1.463) is totally reflected. In wave terms, the core supports guided modes.
Why does optical fibre use 1,550 nm light for long distances, and 1,310 nm for 10 km links? || Loss is lowest at 1,550 nm (about 0.2 dB/km, set by Rayleigh scattering), which suits long spans with EDFAs. Dispersion of standard fibre is near zero at 1,310 nm, which suits short links that have no dispersion compensation, even though the loss is higher.
What does an erbium-doped fibre amplifier do that makes WDM practical? || It amplifies light in the 1,530 to 1,565 nm band directly through stimulated emission, with no conversion to electricity, and all wavelengths in the band are amplified at once, so one amplifier serves dozens of channels.
A 60 km span has 0.2 dB/km loss plus 1.5 dB of connector loss, and the launch power is +2 dBm. What power reaches the receiver? || Loss is 60 × 0.2 + 1.5 = 13.5 dB, so the received power is 2 − 13.5 = −11.5 dBm, about 71 µW.
A designer must connect two switches 30 m apart at 800G. When would you pick active copper, multimode optics, or single-mode optics? || At 30 m, passive copper does not reach; active copper or AECs reach a few metres, so use optics. Pick multimode VCSEL optics for the cheapest short link, if the fibre plant is multimode and the speed fits. Pick single-mode DR optics if you want one fibre plant that survives later speed upgrades and may need to cover more than 100 m.

@@ crystal
value: A quartz crystal oscillator is a thin slice of quartz that vibrates mechanically at one very stable frequency when an electric field is applied, and a small circuit that keeps it vibrating. It is the heartbeat of almost every digital device: the 25 MHz reference of a network card, the 32.768 kHz clock that keeps time in a phone while it is off, and the 24 or 100 MHz reference that a phase-locked loop multiplies up to the gigahertz clock of a processor. A typical crystal keeps its frequency to within 10 to 50 parts per million (ppm), which is far better than any electronic circuit built from resistors and capacitors could do.
The word that matters for a systems engineer is "drift". 20 ppm sounds tiny, but it is 20 microseconds of error per second, or 1.7 seconds per day. Two machines with their own crystals will therefore disagree about the time, and the disagreement grows without bound unless something corrects it. This is the physical reason that clock synchronisation (NTP, PTP, GPS) exists, why distributed databases cannot trust a wall-clock timestamp from another machine, and why Spanner's TrueTime reports time as an interval and assumes a worst-case local drift of 200 ppm.
The idea stands on electromagnetism (Maxwell, L5), in two ways. First, quartz is piezoelectric: an electric field applied across it creates a mechanical strain, and strain creates a voltage, a coupling between the electric field and the material that is described by the same electromagnetic theory. Second, near resonance the crystal behaves in a circuit as a series of an inductor, a capacitor and a resistor with an extremely high quality factor, and analysing it needs circuit theory and the feedback conditions of an oscillator. Above it, quartz clocks give hardware clocks and timers their ticks, and every software clock call is ultimately a count of those ticks.
terms: Piezoelectric effect — in certain crystals, mechanical stress produces an electric voltage, and an applied voltage produces mechanical strain.
Resonant frequency — the frequency at which a body vibrates most easily; for a crystal slice it is set by its thickness and cut.
Quality factor Q — how many cycles a resonator rings before its energy decays; a quartz crystal has Q of 10,000 to over 1,000,000, while an LC circuit has 50 to 500.
parts per million (ppm) — a fractional frequency error of 10⁻⁶; 1 ppm equals 86.4 ms per day.
Crystal cut — the angle at which the slice is cut from the quartz bar; the AT cut has a nearly flat temperature response near room temperature, and the tuning-fork cut is used at 32.768 kHz.
Pierce oscillator — the common circuit: one inverting amplifier, the crystal, and two small capacitors that together make the feedback loop oscillate at the crystal's frequency.
Phase-locked loop (PLL) — a circuit that locks a faster oscillator to a multiple of a reference frequency; it turns a 25 MHz crystal into a 3 GHz clock.
TCXO and OCXO — a temperature-compensated crystal oscillator corrects its frequency with a sensor and a circuit (about 0.5 to 2 ppm); an oven-controlled one keeps the crystal at a constant temperature in a small heated enclosure (about 0.001 to 0.1 ppm).
Jitter and phase noise — short-term random variation in the timing of clock edges, as opposed to long-term drift.
history: Pierre and Jacques Curie discovered piezoelectricity in 1880. For decades it was a curiosity. In World War I, Paul Langevin used quartz to generate and detect ultrasound for submarine detection. The step to timekeeping came from radio. Radio engineers needed transmitters that stayed on a fixed frequency, and in 1921 Walter Cady at Wesleyan University showed that a quartz crystal could control the frequency of an electronic oscillator. George Washington Pierce made the simple oscillator circuit that still bears his name in 1923.
In 1927 Warren Marrison and J. W. Horton at Bell Telephone Laboratories built the first quartz clock, which divided the crystal's frequency down to drive a clock face, and it outperformed the best pendulum clocks (Marrison, 1948 article). By the 1930s quartz clocks served as time standards at national laboratories, and they stayed the best clocks until atomic clocks appeared in the 1950s. Quartz wristwatches arrived in 1969 (Seiko Astron), using a 32,768 Hz tuning-fork crystal, and the same frequency is still used today because 32,768 is 2¹⁵: fifteen divide-by-two stages make exactly 1 Hz.
Industrial progress went into cuts and materials. The AT cut (Lack, Willard and Fair, 1934) has a cubic temperature curve with a very flat region near room temperature, and it became the standard cut for MHz frequencies. Synthetic quartz grown in autoclaves replaced natural quartz after World War II, which made crystals cheap enough for mass production. Temperature compensation and ovens followed, and from the 2000s MEMS resonators (silicon beams etched on a chip) appeared as a competitor for some uses, led by companies such as SiTime.
how: Quartz (silicon dioxide in its crystalline form) lacks a centre of symmetry, so when it is squeezed, the positive and negative charges in each unit cell shift relative to each other, and a voltage appears across the faces. The reverse also holds: a voltage across the faces makes the slice deform. Electromagnetism (Maxwell, L5) describes the electric half of this: the electrodes plated on the two faces form a capacitor, and the electric field inside the quartz couples to its strain. For an AT-cut slice, the field makes the two faces shear sideways against each other (thickness-shear mode).
A vibrating slice has a resonant frequency set by its thickness: f = N / t, where N is the frequency constant. For the AT cut N is about 1.66 MHz·mm, so a 10 MHz crystal is about 0.166 mm thick, thinner than a sheet of paper. The elastic stiffness of quartz and its density set the speed of sound inside, and they hardly change with temperature or time. That is where the stability comes from: the frequency depends on the geometry and on a physical constant of a very stable solid, not on the value of a resistor or a capacitor that ages.
In a circuit, the crystal looks like a series RLC branch (a motional inductance Lm, capacitance Cm and resistance Rm) in parallel with the electrode capacitance C0. The series resonance is at f = 1 / (2π √(Lm Cm)). In a real crystal Lm is huge (henries) and Cm tiny (femtofarads), which gives a Q of 100,000, so the resonance is very narrow. A narrow resonance means that any small change in the loop's phase is converted into a very small change in frequency, which is why the output frequency is so steady. An oscillator circuit, such as the Pierce circuit, needs to supply the small amount of energy lost in each cycle: an inverting amplifier plus two capacitors form a feedback loop with a total phase shift of 360 degrees at the crystal's frequency, with a gain slightly above one. Oscillation starts from thermal noise, grows over thousands of cycles, and settles when the amplifier saturates. Start-up takes about 1 to 10 ms for MHz crystals and 100 ms to 1 s for 32 kHz crystals.
Several effects move the frequency. Temperature is the largest: an AT cut follows a cubic curve with a swing of about ±10 to ±30 ppm from −40 to +85 degrees Celsius. A tuning-fork 32.768 kHz crystal follows a parabola, f/f₀ ≈ −0.034 ppm × (T − 25 °C)², so it runs slow when either hotter or colder than 25 degrees Celsius. Ageing adds about 1 to 5 ppm in the first year and about 1 ppm per year after that. The load capacitance and the drive level shift frequency by a few ppm, and tolerance at manufacture is ±10 to ±30 ppm. A TCXO senses temperature and adds a correction voltage to a varactor, reducing the variation to 0.5 to 2.5 ppm. An OCXO places the crystal in an oven kept at the crystal's "turnover" temperature (where the curve is flat), around 70 to 85 degrees Celsius, reaching 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻¹⁰, at the cost of watts of power and a warm-up time of minutes.
Computers use the crystal through frequency multiplication. A PLL contains a voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) running at, say, 3 GHz; a divider reduces it by a factor of 120; a phase detector compares the result with the 25 MHz crystal reference and corrects the VCO until they match. The output is 120 times the crystal frequency with the crystal's long-term accuracy, though with more short-term jitter. Within a server, there are several such references: for the CPU, memory, PCIe (a 100 MHz reference with ±300 ppm tolerance and optional spread-spectrum modulation of −0.5 percent to reduce electromagnetic interference), Ethernet (25 MHz, with ±100 ppm tolerance between link partners, absorbed by elastic buffers) and the real-time clock (32.768 kHz, running from a coin cell). The operating system reads the counter (such as the TSC or an HPET), scales it by a calibrated frequency to get a time, and uses NTP or PTP to correct the offset and to estimate the frequency error.

example: Take two servers, A and B, whose crystals are off by +20 ppm and −20 ppm from the nominal value. Their relative frequency error is 40 ppm. Each second, the clocks drift apart by 40 µs; each hour by 40 × 10⁻⁶ × 3,600 = 0.144 s; each day by 40 × 10⁻⁶ × 86,400 = 3.46 s. Suppose the application needs the clocks within 1 ms of each other. With no correction, the limit is reached after 1 ms / 40 ppm = 25 seconds. If a time-sync protocol only adjusts the offset every 1,000 seconds and does not learn the frequency error, the error grows to 40 ms between corrections. That is why NTP clients estimate the frequency error as well (they steer the rate, not only the offset), and why a good implementation can hold a few milliseconds over the internet and tens of microseconds with hardware timestamps on a LAN.
Now the temperature effect on a 32.768 kHz crystal, f/f₀ ≈ −0.034 ppm × (T − 25)². At 25 degrees Celsius the error is 0. At 45 degrees Celsius (a warm board), (20)² × 0.034 = 13.6 ppm slow, which is 1.18 seconds per day. At 60 degrees Celsius, (35)² × 0.034 = 41.7 ppm, or 3.6 seconds per day. At 0 degrees Celsius, (25)² × 0.034 = 21 ppm. If the same crystal is initially trimmed at 25 degrees Celsius, a phone left in a hot car can lose several seconds a day. A TCXO that corrects this to ±1 ppm limits the error to 86 ms per day.
Finally, the PLL arithmetic. A 25 MHz crystal with ±20 ppm tolerance gives a reference whose frequency may lie between 24.9995 and 25.0005 MHz. Multiplying by 120 yields a core clock of 3 GHz ± 60 kHz, which means the same ±20 ppm. A processor does not care about this error, but a real-time clock built by counting the processor's cycles would, so operating systems calibrate it. Ethernet is more demanding in a different way: two link partners with 25 MHz crystals 100 ppm apart (the allowed maximum) produce a byte rate difference of one byte in 10,000, which the receiver absorbs in an elastic buffer by occasionally inserting or deleting idle symbols between packets.
intuition: A tuning fork that is struck again and again by electronics, so it always rings at the same pitch, and a counter that treats each ring as one tick.
The reason it works is that a good resonator is picky: only a very narrow band of frequencies can build up in it, and its natural frequency is fixed by the mass and stiffness of a solid, which are very steady. An electronic circuit with a resistor and a capacitor has a time constant that depends on parts that vary by 1 to 5 percent and change with temperature. A crystal turns a vague electrical tolerance of percent into a mechanical tolerance of parts per million, a thousandfold improvement, using the stiffness of a rock.
mistakes: People often think a computer's clock is accurate because it counts the processor's own clock. In fact it counts ticks derived from a crystal that is off by about 10 to 100 ppm, and that error depends on temperature and age. Without synchronisation, a server's clock drifts by seconds per day, and servers in the same rack may differ because of their different temperatures.
People often think drift is a constant, so you can calibrate it once and be done. In fact it varies with temperature (a hot CPU heats the crystal near it), with ageing, and with supply voltage and load. Good time-sync software therefore estimates the drift continuously, and a calibration made at the factory only gets you part of the way.
People often think a quartz crystal is a very precise oscillator in the sense of short-term jitter, so it is good enough for any timing. In fact it is excellent in the short term and mediocre in the long term. For a high-speed serial link, jitter of picoseconds matters, and a clean crystal reference with a good PLL is needed. For a day-long measurement, drift matters, and a crystal needs correction from GPS or an atomic reference. Short-term stability and long-term accuracy are different properties.
People often think the wall-clock time can be used to order events across machines. In fact any pair of machines differs by an offset that is not known precisely and grows between corrections, and a correction can make time jump backwards. Ordering needs logical clocks, or physical clocks with explicit uncertainty bounds, such as TrueTime.
tradeoffs: Choose a plain crystal oscillator (XO) for most digital designs: it costs a few cents to a dollar, uses microwatts to milliwatts and gives ±10 to ±50 ppm. Choose a TCXO when you need 0.5 to 2.5 ppm over temperature, for example for GPS receivers, cellular modems or timestamping, for a few dollars and a bit more power. Choose an OCXO when you need 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻¹⁰ stability, such as telecom boundary clocks and time servers (grandmaster clocks), and accept 1 to 3 W, a warm-up of minutes and a cost of tens to hundreds of dollars. Choose a chip-scale atomic clock or a GPS-disciplined oscillator if you need holdover for hours or days without any external reference, and you will pay hundreds to thousands of dollars.
Choose a quartz crystal over a ceramic resonator or an on-chip RC oscillator when frequency accuracy matters (USB, Ethernet, radio, timekeeping): a ceramic resonator is about 0.1 to 0.5 percent accurate and an RC oscillator 1 to 5 percent, but both are cheaper and start in microseconds. Choose a MEMS oscillator when you need shock and vibration tolerance, fast start, small size or programmable frequencies; modern MEMS parts match TCXO stability and often have lower jitter sensitivity to mechanical stress, while quartz still has the edge in absolute phase noise and in many price points.
Choose software discipline according to the accuracy you need: NTP with a pool of internet servers for about 1 to 50 ms, NTP with local stratum-1 servers for about 100 µs to 1 ms, PTP with hardware timestamping in switches for 100 ns to 1 µs, and GPS or atomic receivers in the data centre for tens of nanoseconds. Each step needs more hardware and care. If you need only ordering and not absolute time, use logical or hybrid logical clocks, which need no hardware; if you need external consistency across data centres, use bounded-uncertainty clocks like TrueTime.
For modelling, use the simple RLC equivalent circuit (Butterworth–Van Dyke) for oscillator design and start-up analysis. Use finite-element piezoelectric models only when designing the crystal itself (cut, mounting, electrode shape), because they require material constants and give unneeded detail to a system designer.
friction: The crystal is sensitive to its environment in ways a datasheet can bury. Temperature gradients (not only temperature) cause frequency steps; a fan that switches on can shift the clock by parts per million within seconds. Mechanical shock and vibration shift frequency (acceleration sensitivity of 1 ppb per g is typical for good crystals), and microphonics add noise. Ageing changes frequency permanently. And activity dips: a rare but documented failure is the "frequency jump", where a tiny contaminant moves on the crystal surface, and the frequency changes by a few ppb in an instant. At low temperatures some crystals show a sudden step called an activity dip when a spurious mode crosses the main one.
Virtual machines add another layer. Guest operating systems read a virtualised counter, and when a VM is paused, migrated or starved of CPU time, its clock steps or lags. Cloud providers now offer hardware clocks (PTP devices exposed to guests) because the software clock is not good enough for some tasks. Time corrections cause trouble for software: a clock that jumps backwards makes timeouts, lease expiries and timestamps misbehave, so engineers use monotonic clocks (which never go backwards but also do not give calendar time) for measuring durations, and slew rather than step.
At the chip level, the slow part of the system is the crystal itself: it cannot be made on a standard CMOS process, so it is an external part with its own pins, board space and failure mode. Crystals fail by cracking from board flex or by being driven too hard. At frequencies above roughly 50 to 100 MHz, a fundamental-mode crystal is too thin to make, and designers use overtone crystals or PLLs instead.
practice: Treat every non-synchronised clock as wrong by about 100 ppm in the worst case (Google's design assumption is 200 ppm for servers). That means 8.6 seconds a day, or 8.6 ms over 100 seconds. Use this when you reason about lease durations, timeouts, and replication lag measured with timestamps from two machines. Sync clocks with chrony or ntpd (chrony is better for variable conditions and VMs), run at least four sources, and monitor offset and frequency error as metrics; alert when offset exceeds your budget, for example 10 ms.
Use CLOCK_MONOTONIC (or its equivalent) for durations and timeouts, wall-clock time only for display and cross-machine records, and design protocols so that correctness does not depend on tight clock agreement: use fencing tokens with leases, version numbers, and hybrid logical clocks for ordering. If you need microsecond accuracy, use PTP with hardware timestamping (the NIC must support it), and in cloud environments check whether the provider offers a PTP hardware clock. Log the clock source and the estimated error bound where precision matters.
For electronics, use crystals specified with the correct load capacitance for your circuit, keep traces short, place crystals away from heat sources and board flex points, and select TCXO parts for radio and timing functions. To study further, read Marrison's 1948 history, the NTP and PTP documentation, "There is No Now" by Justin Sheehy for the distributed-systems view, and the Spanner paper's TrueTime section.
quiz: Why does a quartz crystal keep a steadier frequency than an RC or LC circuit? || Its frequency is set by the thickness and the elastic constants of a solid that change very little, and its Q is 10,000 to 1,000,000 against hundreds for an LC circuit, so the resonance is extremely narrow and the oscillation locks onto it.
What does piezoelectricity do in the oscillator? || It couples electric field and mechanical strain. A voltage on the electrodes makes the slice flex, and the flexing produces a voltage, so the vibrating crystal acts as an electrical resonator with a very sharp frequency response.
Why is 32.768 kHz used for real-time clocks? || 32,768 = 2¹⁵, so a chain of 15 divide-by-two stages produces exactly 1 Hz. The frequency is also low enough for very low power operation in a small tuning-fork crystal.
Two servers have crystals of +25 ppm and −25 ppm and need to stay within 5 ms. How often must they be corrected if the offset is the only thing corrected? || The relative drift is 50 ppm, which is 50 µs per second. 5 ms / 50 µs per second = 100 seconds, so a correction is needed at least every 100 seconds (more often to leave a margin).
You need accurate timestamps across a data centre. How do you choose between NTP, PTP and GPS-disciplined oscillators? || NTP is cheap and gives milliseconds to hundreds of microseconds. PTP with hardware timestamps in NICs and switches gives sub-microsecond accuracy at the cost of compatible hardware and network design. GPS-disciplined oscillators give tens of nanoseconds as a source for PTP grandmasters, but need antennas, and are exposed to jamming, so use them as masters with a holdover clock.

@@ atomicclock
value: An atomic clock uses the natural frequency of an atom as its pendulum. Every caesium-133 atom in the universe has exactly the same energy gap between two particular states, and it absorbs microwave radiation at exactly 9,192,631,770 cycles per second. Since 1967 the SI second has been defined as that number of cycles. An atomic clock tunes a microwave source until the atoms respond most strongly, and then counts the cycles of the source. Because the frequency is set by quantum mechanics, not by the size of a manufactured part, two caesium clocks built in different countries agree to about one part in 10¹³ to 10¹⁶, many thousands of times better than the best quartz oscillator.
GPS puts this precision on the whole planet. Each GPS satellite carries several atomic clocks (rubidium and caesium) and broadcasts its time and its position. A receiver listens to at least four satellites and solves for its position and its clock error together. A civilian receiver then holds time to within a few tens of nanoseconds of UTC, anywhere under the open sky, for the price of a chip. Light travels 30 cm in a nanosecond, so a timing error of 100 ns equals a range error of 30 m. That is why GPS timing is the hidden source of time for cellphone base stations, power grids, financial exchanges and data-centre clocks, including the time masters behind Google Spanner's TrueTime.
The concept stands on quantum mechanics (L5). Quantum mechanics says that an atom's energy states are discrete and identical for all atoms of the same isotope, so the frequency f = ΔE / h of the transition between two states is a constant of nature. In caesium-133 the two states are the hyperfine levels, which arise from the interaction between the spin of the outer electron and the spin of the nucleus. Above this concept, the hardware clocks and timers that drive operating systems and databases use GPS and atomic references to discipline their quartz oscillators, which would otherwise drift apart.
terms: Hyperfine transition — a tiny energy change in an atom when the electron's spin flips relative to the nuclear spin; in caesium-133 it corresponds to 9.19 GHz.
Resonance — the frequency at which an atom absorbs radiation most strongly; the clock locks its oscillator to this peak.
Ramsey interrogation — a method that applies two short microwave pulses separated by a free-flight time, giving a narrower resonance line than a single long pulse.
Fractional frequency stability — the size of a clock's frequency error as a fraction of its frequency; 10⁻¹³ means 1 ns of error per 10,000 seconds.
Allan deviation — a measure of clock stability at an averaging time τ; it falls with τ for white noise, shows how long you should average, and reveals where drift starts.
UTC and TAI — International Atomic Time (TAI) is a continuous count of SI seconds from many clocks; Coordinated Universal Time (UTC) is TAI with leap seconds added to follow the Earth's rotation.
GNSS — a global navigation satellite system (GPS, Galileo, GLONASS, BeiDou); each constellation broadcasts time as well as position.
Holdover — the ability of a local clock to keep good time after its external reference (such as GPS) is lost.
PTP (IEEE 1588) — the Precision Time Protocol, which distributes time over a network with hardware timestamps to reach nanosecond to microsecond accuracy.
history: Isidor Rabi proposed in 1945 that the resonance of atoms in a magnetic field could be used to keep time. The ammonia clock of the US National Bureau of Standards (1949) was the first, but a caesium beam proved much better. Louis Essen and Jack Parry at the UK National Physical Laboratory built the first accurate caesium clock in 1955. Its measurement of the caesium frequency in terms of the astronomical second (with William Markowitz at the US Naval Observatory, 1955 to 1958) led the 13th General Conference on Weights and Measures to define the SI second in 1967 as 9,192,631,770 periods of the caesium-133 radiation.
Accuracy then improved by a factor of ten about every ten years. Norman Ramsey's method of separated oscillating fields (Nobel Prize 1989) narrowed the resonance. Laser cooling, developed by Steven Chu, Claude Cohen-Tannoudji and William Phillips (Nobel Prize 1997), slowed the atoms to microkelvin temperatures, and led to the caesium fountain clock: atoms are tossed up about a metre and fall back through the microwave cavity, giving about a second of interaction. NIST-F1 (1999) and later fountains reach uncertainties near 10⁻¹⁶. Optical clocks, which use transitions at hundreds of terahertz in strontium, ytterbium or aluminium ions, have reached about 10⁻¹⁸, and the standards community is preparing a redefinition of the second, with a decision expected around 2030.
GPS grew from the US Navy's Timation and the Air Force's 621B programmes. The first GPS satellite with an atomic clock launched in 1978, and the system was declared fully operational in 1995. The Soviet GLONASS, the European Galileo (service from 2016) and the Chinese BeiDou (global service from 2020) followed. On the network side, NTP (David Mills, 1985) spread time over the internet to milliseconds; IEEE 1588 PTP (2002) brought hardware timestamps for microseconds and below; and in 2012 Google's Spanner paper showed a global database that depends on GPS and atomic clocks in each data centre. In 2022 the General Conference decided that the leap second would be abandoned by or before 2035.
how: The clock has two parts: a quantum reference and a local oscillator. The reference is a cloud of caesium atoms. Caesium-133 has a single outer electron, and its ground state splits into two hyperfine levels (F = 3 and F = 4) because the electron spin and the nuclear spin can be parallel or opposite. The energy difference ΔE corresponds to a frequency f = ΔE / h = 9,192,631,770 Hz. Quantum mechanics (L5) guarantees that this gap is the same in every atom and does not depend on how the clock was made. A microwave field at exactly this frequency drives atoms from one level to the other; a field slightly off the frequency does so less efficiently.
In a caesium fountain, the sequence is: (1) a vapour of caesium is cooled by lasers to about 1 µK, so atoms move at a few centimetres per second; (2) the atoms are prepared in one hyperfine level and launched upward by about a metre; (3) on the way up and again on the way down they pass through a microwave cavity, which gives two pulses separated by roughly half a second (Ramsey's method); (4) a laser measures how many atoms changed level. The fraction that changed level depends on how close the microwave frequency is to resonance, and the resonance line is about 1 Hz wide. A servo loop adjusts a quartz or maser-based oscillator and a frequency synthesiser to keep the microwave frequency at the peak. The clock's output is the stable oscillator, steered by the atoms. Narrower resonance lines (higher quality factor, Q = f / linewidth ≈ 10¹⁰) give better stability, which is why optical clocks with linewidths of 1 Hz at 400 THz (Q about 10¹⁵) outperform microwave clocks.
Commercial devices are simpler. A rubidium-87 standard (6.8346 GHz) uses a small glass cell with vapour and a lamp. It has a stability around 10⁻¹¹ to 10⁻¹², drifts by about 10⁻¹¹ per month, and costs hundreds to a few thousand dollars. A commercial caesium beam standard reaches about 10⁻¹² to 10⁻¹³ with no calibration drift. A hydrogen maser (1.420 GHz) has the best short-term stability, about 10⁻¹⁵ at one day, for time laboratories. A chip-scale atomic clock (CSAC) is a rubidium or caesium cell with a laser and fits in about 17 cm³ at about 120 mW, with a stability of 10⁻¹⁰ and holdover that beats any quartz oscillator.
GPS distributes the time. About 31 satellites orbit at 20,200 km with a period of 12 sidereal hours; each broadcasts, on L1 (1,575.42 MHz) and other frequencies, a pseudo-random code with a chip length of 293 m, plus a message with its orbit parameters and clock correction. The receiver measures the arrival time of the code from each satellite, which, multiplied by the speed of light, gives a "pseudorange" to the satellite. Each pseudorange includes the receiver's clock error, which is the same for all satellites. With four unknowns (x, y, z and the receiver clock offset) the receiver needs four satellites and solves them together. The result is a position to a few metres and a time to a few tens of nanoseconds relative to UTC (the system is steered to the US Naval Observatory's UTC). Relativity matters: satellite clocks run fast by about 45.7 µs per day because they sit higher in Earth's gravity (general relativity), and slow by about 7.2 µs per day because they move fast (special relativity). The net is +38.5 µs per day, so the clocks are deliberately set to a frequency of 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz before launch.
Data centres use GPS in the chain: a grandmaster clock with a GPS receiver and a good local oscillator (OCXO or rubidium) supplies time to switches and servers over PTP; the NIC timestamps packets in hardware, and the servers' clock is disciplined (steered) to it, reaching tens to hundreds of nanoseconds. Spanner's TrueTime uses GPS receivers and atomic clocks as the time masters in each data centre, and clients poll them roughly every 30 seconds. Between polls, a client's clock is assumed to drift by up to 200 ppm, so the uncertainty ε follows a sawtooth from about 1 ms to 7 ms, and TrueTime returns an interval [earliest, latest]. Spanner makes a transaction wait out this uncertainty (commit wait) to guarantee a global order.

example: Start with the range arithmetic. Light travels c = 299,792,458 m/s, so 1 µs of timing error is about 300 m and 1 ns about 30 cm. A GPS receiver with a cheap quartz clock that is wrong by 1 ms would put every pseudorange off by 300 km. That is why the clock error is treated as a fourth unknown: all pseudoranges are wrong by the same amount, c·b, and four satellites can solve for it. Once solved, the receiver's time is the GPS time to a few tens of nanoseconds, even though its own clock is a plain crystal.
Now the relativity correction. A GPS satellite orbits at radius 26,560 km. Its speed is about 3.87 km/s, so special relativity slows its clock by about v²/(2c²) = (3,870)² / (2 × 9 × 10¹⁶) ≈ 8.3 × 10⁻¹¹ (about 7.2 µs per day). General relativity makes it run faster by the gravitational potential difference, ΔΦ/c² ≈ 5.3 × 10⁻¹⁰ (about 45.7 µs per day). The net rate difference is about 4.465 × 10⁻¹⁰, which is 38.6 µs per day. If uncorrected, the error in distance after one day would be 38.6 × 10⁻⁶ × 3 × 10⁸ ≈ 11.6 km, and it would grow every day. GPS works as a navigation system only because both effects are included, which is a daily test of relativity.
Holdover and drift. Suppose the GPS antenna is jammed for 24 hours and your grandmaster has to coast on its local oscillator. With an ordinary crystal at 10⁻⁶ stability, the error grows by 86 ms in a day. A good OCXO at 10⁻⁹ gives 86 µs. A rubidium standard at 10⁻¹¹ gives 0.86 µs, and a caesium clock at 10⁻¹² gives 86 ns. If your budget is 1 µs (typical PTP boundary budgets are far tighter), a rubidium standard keeps you within bounds for one day, an OCXO for about 17 minutes, and a quartz crystal for 1 second.
Finally, the TrueTime wait. With ε = 4 ms on average, a Spanner commit waits about 2ε = 8 ms for the timestamp to become safely in the past before the writes become visible. If the uncertainty can be cut to 100 µs (by using PTP with hardware timestamps and a nearby time master), the commit wait falls to 0.2 ms, 40 times shorter. This is why several newer systems invest in better clocks: for databases with external consistency, clock accuracy is directly commit latency.
intuition: Every caesium atom is an identical tuning fork made by nature, so counting its vibrations is the same everywhere in the world and for all time.
This is the cleanest answer to "what is a second". A pendulum or crystal depends on its dimensions, which vary between pieces and over time. An atom's energy levels are set by fundamental constants and the quantum rules, so every atom of that isotope has the same frequency. A clock based on atoms is therefore a standard that anyone can rebuild from the definition. GPS then solves the next problem, which is distribution: carry the time out from a few expensive clocks in labs and satellites to every receiver, using light-speed signals whose travel time can be computed.
mistakes: People often think GPS is mainly a positioning system and time is a by-product. In fact positioning is derived from time: the receiver measures signal travel times. The same signal carries accurate time, and many users (telecom, finance, power grids) use GPS only for time.
People often think atomic clocks are huge laboratory instruments. In fact the lab fountains are, but rubidium and caesium standards the size of a book are sold commercially, and chip-scale atomic clocks the size of a matchbox use 120 mW. Every GPS satellite carries several.
People often think an atomic clock never drifts or needs correction. In fact a good commercial caesium standard has a small but nonzero error, a rubidium standard drifts measurably, and real-world systems steer to UTC over time. In a data centre the weak links are usually the network path and the local oscillator, not the atomic source.
People often think UTC is a uniform count of seconds. In fact UTC occasionally inserts a leap second to stay close to the Earth's rotation (27 have been added since 1972), while GPS time and TAI never do. GPS time was 18 seconds ahead of UTC from 2017. Software that uses UTC for durations can see a minute of 61 seconds, which has caused outages; the leap second is due to be abandoned by 2035.
tradeoffs: Choose GPS as your time source when you have a clear view of the sky or an antenna run, and need nanosecond to hundred-nanosecond accuracy at a low cost. A GPS-disciplined OCXO costs a few hundred to a few thousand dollars and gives tens of nanoseconds. You accept dependence on a signal that is weak (about −130 dBm at the ground), can be jammed or spoofed, and needs rooftop antennas. Choose network time (NTP) when a millisecond is enough: no hardware, but accuracy depends on network asymmetry and load. Choose PTP with hardware timestamps and boundary clocks in switches when you need 100 ns to a few microseconds inside a data centre, at the cost of compatible NICs and switches and careful network design.
Choose a rubidium or caesium holdover oscillator behind GPS if a loss of reference would be expensive (financial exchanges, mobile networks, power utilities) and you need hours to days of acceptable time. Choose OCXO only when the outage is minutes or when a short loss is tolerable. Choose multiple constellations and multiple antennas to reduce the risk from one failing or being jammed. For the highest assurance, add an independent terrestrial time source (fibre-delivered time from a national laboratory, or eLoran), at greater cost.
Choose bounded-uncertainty time (TrueTime-style, each timestamp as an interval) when you need external consistency across data centres and can pay a commit wait of a few milliseconds; the cost is the hardware, the operations and a latency floor equal to the uncertainty. Choose hybrid logical clocks (used in CockroachDB and others) when you only have NTP-grade clocks: you get causality and approximate wall time, but weaker guarantees such as bounded clock-offset assumptions (a maximum skew like 500 ms), after which the system either refuses to serve or loses linearisability for some patterns. Choose logical clocks (Lamport, vector clocks) when you only need ordering and have no use for real time.
For describing a clock's quality, choose the Allan deviation, not just a ppm figure: it tells you the best averaging time (where it is lowest) and when drift dominates. A simple frequency-offset model (error = offset × time) is fine over minutes for a disciplined oscillator, but wrong for long holdover where ageing and temperature changes dominate.
friction: GNSS is a weak signal from a long way away. Jamming (cheap devices can block receivers within a radius of kilometres) and spoofing (fake signals that move a receiver's time and position) are documented in conflict zones and near ports, and they threaten infrastructure that relies on GPS alone. Antennas must be installed with cable runs and surge protection, and GPS signals are barely usable in the middle of a data centre, so every site has a rooftop antenna and a long distribution chain. Cable delay (about 5 ns per metre) must be calibrated.
Errors accumulate along the path. Each switch hop in PTP adds jitter unless it is a boundary or transparent clock; asymmetric paths (the forward delay differs from the return delay) cause a constant error that cannot be detected from the timestamps alone, since the protocol assumes symmetry. Operating systems add error between the NIC clock and the application clock, and virtual machines add more. At the sub-microsecond level, you need to look at the whole chain.
Time itself is messy. Leap seconds have caused outages and complicated software for fifty years (the smear approach used by Google and AWS spreads the extra second over 24 hours, but services that smear differently, or that step instead, disagree by up to a second during a leap). Different timescales (UTC, TAI, GPS time, local time with daylight saving) are easy to mix up. Finally, relativity makes "the same time" depend on altitude: clocks at different heights tick at different rates by about 10⁻¹⁶ per metre, which matters for optical clocks and not for data centres.
practice: Know your time budget before choosing a method. A few milliseconds: NTP (chrony) to a few good servers. Microseconds: PTP with hardware timestamping on NICs and switches that support it. Sub-microsecond or regulated finance (MiFID II requires UTC traceability within 100 µs for high-frequency trading): GPS-disciplined grandmaster with holdover. Define the uncertainty you will tolerate and expose it, as TrueTime does, rather than hiding it.
Operate time as a service. Monitor offset, frequency error, number of satellites, antenna status and holdover state, and alert when the offset exceeds the budget or when the grandmaster loses its reference. Use at least three or four time sources so that one wrong source can be outvoted, and avoid mixing leap-smearing and non-smearing servers in the same client configuration. Prefer monotonic clocks for measuring durations, and keep timestamps in UTC or TAI-based formats in storage. Consider the cloud provider's time service (AWS Time Sync, Google's NTP, Azure's PTP hardware clock) before building your own, since they provide microsecond-level accuracy in some instance types.
To study further, start with the NIST Time and Frequency Division pages, the Spanner paper (TrueTime section), and the IEEE 1588 overview. For the physics, read the Feynman Lectures on the two-state system, and the NIST fountain-clock papers.

value: A quantum computer stores information in qubits. A classical bit is 0 or 1. A qubit is described by two complex numbers (amplitudes), one for 0 and one for 1, and a register of n qubits is described by 2ⁿ amplitudes at once. Quantum gates change these amplitudes in a controlled way, and a measurement at the end returns one ordinary bit string, with probabilities given by the squared sizes of the amplitudes. The skill of quantum algorithm design is to arrange the gates so that amplitudes for wrong answers cancel each other (destructive interference) and amplitudes for right answers add up, so that the final measurement gives the right answer with high probability.
This does not mean a quantum computer tries all answers at once and picks the best. If you did that, a measurement would return a random answer. The known speed-ups are specific. Shor's algorithm (1994) finds the prime factors of an n-bit number in time roughly proportional to n³, where the best known classical method needs time that grows faster than any polynomial (about exp(n^(1/3)) for the number field sieve). Grover's algorithm (1996) searches N unsorted items in about √N steps, against N/2 on average. Simulating quantum systems (molecules, materials) is the application the founders of the field expected first. For most everyday computing there is no known quantum advantage.
The reason engineers care now is cryptography. Shor's algorithm would break RSA, Diffie–Hellman and elliptic-curve cryptography, which protect almost all internet traffic. Data recorded today could be decrypted later ("harvest now, decrypt later"). So the migration to post-quantum cryptography (lattice-based schemes such as ML-KEM, standardised by NIST in 2024) is already under way, even though no quantum computer can yet attack a real key. The concept stands on quantum mechanics (L5): superposition and interference give the amplitude arithmetic, entanglement gives correlations between qubits that have no classical counterpart, the Born rule gives the probabilities of the measurement, and decoherence, the loss of quantum behaviour through contact with the environment, is the main enemy of the hardware.
terms: Qubit — a two-level quantum system whose state is α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ with complex α and β and |α|² + |β|² = 1.
Superposition — a state that is a weighted sum of basis states; the weights are amplitudes, not probabilities.
Entanglement — a state of several qubits that cannot be written as a product of single-qubit states, such as (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2; measuring one qubit fixes the other's result.
Quantum gate — a reversible operation on qubits, written as a unitary matrix (for example Hadamard, CNOT); gates preserve total probability.
Interference — amplitudes of different computational paths add as complex numbers, so they can reinforce or cancel.
Decoherence — loss of the quantum state through interaction with the environment; it limits how long and how deep a computation can run.
Physical and logical qubit — a physical qubit is the noisy hardware element; a logical qubit is an error-protected qubit encoded in many physical ones.
Surface code — a leading error-correcting code that arranges qubits in a 2D grid and repeatedly measures local parity checks; its error rate falls exponentially with the code distance d if the physical error rate is below a threshold (about 1%).
BQP — the class of problems a quantum computer can solve in polynomial time with a small error probability; it is believed not to contain NP-complete problems.
history: Richard Feynman said in 1981 that simulating quantum physics with a classical computer seems to need exponential resources, and suggested building computers from quantum parts. David Deutsch (1985) defined the quantum Turing machine and showed that quantum computers could do some tasks faster. Deutsch and Jozsa (1992) and Bernstein and Vazirani (1993) gave problems with proven advantage, but of little practical interest. Daniel Simon (1994) found a problem with an exponential advantage, which inspired Peter Shor.
Shor's 1994 paper, "Algorithms for quantum computation: discrete logarithms and factoring", changed the field into a question of national security and cryptography. Lov Grover followed in 1996 with his search algorithm, and Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard and Vazirani (1997) proved it is optimal. The big doubt was noise: many physicists thought that quantum states are too fragile to compute with. Shor (1995) and Andrew Steane (1996) answered with quantum error-correcting codes, and the threshold theorem (Aharonov and Ben-Or; Kitaev; Knill, Laflamme and Zurek, 1996 to 1998) proved that if the error rate per operation is below a threshold, arbitrarily long computations are possible with only polylogarithmic overhead. Alexei Kitaev's surface code idea (1997, developed by Bravyi and Kitaev and others) gave a practical design.
Hardware followed: the first two-qubit gates with trapped ions (1995), superconducting qubits (Nakamura 1999; the transmon qubit, 2007), neutral atoms in optical tweezers (2000s). Google claimed "quantum supremacy" in 2019 with Sycamore (53 qubits) on a random-circuit sampling task of no practical use, which others partly matched with classical methods. In December 2024 Google's Willow chip (105 superconducting qubits) showed for the first time that error correction improves as the code grows: surface codes of distance 3, 5 and 7 cut the logical error rate by a factor of about 2.1 at each step. In 2025 Craig Gidney lowered the estimated resources for breaking RSA-2048 to under one million noisy qubits and less than a week of runtime, from about 20 million qubits in 2019. In August 2024 NIST published its first post-quantum standards (FIPS 203, 204 and 205).
how: Qubit states and gates are linear algebra (L4). A single qubit is a vector (α, β) in a two-dimensional complex space, and a gate is a 2 × 2 unitary matrix. The Hadamard gate H = (1/√2)[[1, 1], [1, −1]] turns |0⟩ into (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2, an equal superposition. Two qubits live in a four-dimensional space with amplitudes for |00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩ and |11⟩, and n qubits in a 2ⁿ-dimensional space. A CNOT gate flips the second qubit if the first is 1. Hadamard on the first qubit and then CNOT makes the Bell state (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2, in which the two qubits are entangled: the result of measuring either is random, but the two always agree. Probability (L4) enters at measurement: outcome x appears with probability |amplitude of x|², and the state collapses to x.
The power comes from interference. Apply H to |0⟩ and then H again. After the first H the amplitudes are (1/√2, 1/√2). The second H gives the amplitude of |1⟩ as (1/√2)(1/√2) + (1/√2)(−1/√2) = 0 and that of |0⟩ as 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. The two paths to |1⟩ cancel. A classical random coin flipped twice would give a random result; a quantum "coin" flipped twice returns to the start. A quantum algorithm is a sequence of gates in which, for every wrong answer, the paths to it cancel, and for the right answer they add up.
Shor's algorithm turns factoring N into period finding. Pick a random a coprime to N and consider f(x) = aˣ mod N, which repeats with some period r. If r is even and a^(r/2) is not −1 mod N, then gcd(a^(r/2) − 1, N) is a nontrivial factor. Finding r classically is as hard as factoring. The quantum part prepares a superposition of all x, computes f(x) into a second register, and applies the quantum Fourier transform (QFT) to the first. The QFT makes the amplitudes interfere so that only values near multiples of 2ⁿ/r survive, so a measurement gives a number from which r can be recovered (using continued fractions, from number theory). The cost is about n² to n³ gates for an n-bit number, mostly the modular exponentiation. For RSA-2048 this means thousands of logical qubits and billions of logical gates.
Grover's algorithm works on a function f that marks one item among N. Start in an equal superposition of all N items. Repeat: flip the sign of the marked item's amplitude (the oracle), then reflect all amplitudes about their average. Each repetition rotates the state vector by a small angle 2·arcsin(1/√N) toward the marked item, so after about (π/4)√N steps the marked item has probability near 1. The square root is optimal for unstructured search, so a quantum computer can never break a 256-bit symmetric key faster than about 2¹²⁸ steps. Quantum simulation uses the machine's own state to represent a quantum system's state: n qubits hold 2ⁿ amplitudes, and a simulated molecule evolves by gates built from its Hamiltonian.
Noise is the engineering problem. Physical gates have errors of about 0.1 to 0.5 percent per two-qubit gate on the best superconducting chips, and 0.1 percent or better on trapped ions, and a qubit decoheres in tens to hundreds of microseconds (superconducting) or seconds (ions). A useful algorithm needs 10⁹ to 10¹² operations, so the error per operation must be about 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻¹² and error correction is mandatory. The surface code stores one logical qubit in a d × d patch of data qubits plus a similar number of measurement qubits (2d² − 1 in all). Each round, the measurement qubits check parities of neighbouring data qubits without reading the data itself; a classical decoder interprets the pattern of changes (the syndrome) to find where errors happened, in real time. If the physical error rate p is below the threshold p_th (about 1 percent), the logical error per round falls roughly as (p/p_th)^((d+1)/2). This is what Willow showed: from distance 3 to 5 to 7 the logical error fell by a factor of 2.14 each time, and the best logical qubit reached an error of about 0.14 percent per cycle.

   logical error per round  ~  (p / p_th)^((d+1)/2),   p_th ~ 1%
     p = 0.3%, d = 3,5,7  ->  each step divides error by ~ 2 to 3
example: Factor N = 15 with Shor's method, to see what the quantum part gives. Choose a = 7 (coprime to 15). The sequence 7ˣ mod 15 for x = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 is 1, 7, 4, 13, 1. It returns to 1 at x = 4, so the period is r = 4. This is what the quantum computer finds, by measuring the QFT register: with 8 bits for x (256 values), the amplitudes concentrate on multiples of 256/4 = 64, so measurements return 0, 64, 128 or 192, each with probability one quarter, and from 64/256 = 1/4 the denominator 4 gives r. The period is even, and 7² = 49 ≡ 4 (mod 15), which is not −1 (that would be 14). Now compute gcd(4 − 1, 15) = 3 and gcd(4 + 1, 15) = 5. So 15 = 3 × 5. For a 2,048-bit N the same steps apply, but the modular exponentiation needs on the order of 10⁹ to 10¹⁰ logical Toffoli gates.
Memory for simulating qubits on a classical computer: each amplitude is a complex number of 8 bytes (single precision). With 30 qubits there are 2³⁰ amplitudes, which is 8.6 GB, fine on a laptop. With 40 qubits it is 8.8 TB, a large server cluster. With 50 qubits it is 9 PB, the top of today's supercomputers, and with 100 qubits it is 10¹⁰ times more than there are bytes on earth. That is why exact simulation is limited to about 45 to 50 qubits, though approximate methods (tensor networks) do better for circuits with low entanglement.
Resource estimate for error correction. Suppose physical error p = 0.1 percent and threshold 1 percent, so p/p_th = 0.1. For distance d = 11, the logical error per round is about 0.1^((11+1)/2) = 0.1⁶ = 10⁻⁶ (times a constant near 0.1, so about 10⁻⁷). The patch needs 2 × 11² − 1 = 241 physical qubits per logical qubit. A circuit with 10⁹ logical operations on 1,000 logical qubits, with error 10⁻⁷ per logical operation, would fail with near certainty, so you would need d = 17 or more (error about 10⁻¹⁰ per round per qubit) with 2 × 17² − 1 = 577 physical qubits each. For 1,400 logical qubits that is about 800,000 physical qubits, which is close to Gidney's estimate of under a million for RSA-2048 (a result that uses extra tricks to cut the cost further). Today's best devices have around 100 to a few thousand physical qubits, and a few tens at most of error-corrected logical qubits.
Finally, Grover versus a symmetric key. A 128-bit key has 2¹²⁸ ≈ 3.4 × 10³⁸ possibilities. Grover needs about (π/4) × 2⁶⁴ ≈ 1.4 × 10¹⁹ iterations. Each iteration is an AES evaluation done reversibly, with thousands of logical gates. Even at a hypothetical 1 MHz logical clock rate, 1.4 × 10¹⁹ iterations take 1.4 × 10¹³ seconds, about 460,000 years on one machine, and Grover does not parallelise well (k machines give only a √k speed-up). This is why guidance says AES-256 is enough against quantum attack, and the more serious threat is public-key cryptography, where Shor's advantage is exponential.
intuition: Instead of checking answers one by one, arrange waves so that the wrong answers cancel out and the right answer adds up.
Think of ripples from several stones in a pond. At some points the ripples add up and at others they cancel. A quantum algorithm launches many "ripples", the computational paths, and designs the gates so that the cancelling points are the wrong answers. The size of the state space (2ⁿ amplitudes) is what lets this work, but the skill is in the cancellation. Where the problem has structure that interference can use (periodicity for Shor, the quantum nature of the system for simulation), the speed-up can be large. Where it has no structure, the best possible is the square root.
mistakes: People often think a quantum computer tries all possibilities in parallel and so solves any hard problem quickly. In fact a measurement returns only one outcome, and without clever interference it is random. The known exponential speed-ups apply to a small set of structured problems; there is no known quantum algorithm that solves NP-complete problems in polynomial time, and most experts expect none.
People often think more qubits automatically means a more powerful computer. In fact the number of good, error-corrected operations is what counts. A chip with 1,000 noisy qubits and a 0.5 percent gate error can run only a few hundred gates before the result is noise. Hardware is compared by logical qubits and logical error rates, or at least by gate fidelity and connectivity, not by the raw qubit count.
People often think quantum computers will break all encryption soon. In fact, as of now no quantum computer has factored a number beyond toy sizes by Shor's algorithm, and the estimates for RSA-2048 are about a million good physical qubits, far beyond current machines. Symmetric ciphers and hashes are only weakened by Grover (the key size is effectively halved). But the migration is urgent because data lives long and replacing cryptography takes years.
People often think a quantum computer is a faster classical computer for general use. In fact it is a co-processor for specific problems. It is slow at ordinary I/O, cannot copy unknown quantum states (the no-cloning theorem), and is hard to program with branching and loops. Most of the work in any useful algorithm is still classical, including the decoders that correct errors in real time.
tradeoffs: Choose to migrate to post-quantum cryptography now if your data must stay secret for longer than the time until a quantum attack is possible, minus the time your migration takes (Mosca's inequality: if secrecy lifetime x plus migration time y exceeds the time z to a cryptographically relevant quantum computer, you are already late). For key exchange, deploy hybrid schemes (X25519 together with ML-KEM-768) which stay secure if either breaks; you give up larger keys and ciphertexts (an ML-KEM-768 public key is 1,184 bytes against 32 for X25519) and about 1 to 2 kB more per handshake. Choose to wait on signatures for short-lived data, where the risk is only that a forged signature can be created after the key is broken.
Choose the hardware platform by the bottleneck. Superconducting circuits (Google, IBM) have fast gates (about 20 to 100 ns), so error-correction cycles take about 1 µs, and they use chip fabrication techniques; but they need dilution refrigerators at about 15 mK, connectivity is local, and coherence is only about 100 µs. Trapped ions (Quantinuum, IonQ) have the best gate fidelities (above 99.9 percent) and all-to-all connectivity, but gates take 10 to 100 µs, so the cycle is 100 to 1,000 times slower, and scaling the trap is hard. Neutral atoms in optical tweezers (QuEra, Atom Computing, Pasqal) scale to thousands of qubits and allow reconfigurable connectivity, but have slow cycles (milliseconds) and atom loss. Photonic and spin-qubit platforms offer manufacturability at the cost of loss or control difficulty. No platform has won.
Choose the surface code for local-connectivity chips with a well-understood decoder and a 1 percent threshold; choose qLDPC (low-density parity-check) codes, such as IBM's bivariate bicycle "gross" code, when you can have longer-range connections: they need roughly ten times fewer physical qubits for the same protection, at the cost of harder hardware wiring and decoding. Choose error mitigation (extra sampling and post-processing) for experiments on today's noisy devices, but know that its sampling cost grows exponentially with circuit size; it cannot take you to large algorithms.
Choose a classical method whenever one exists: GPUs, tensor networks and Monte Carlo handle many problems that were once presented as quantum targets, and several claimed quantum advantages have been matched by improved classical algorithms. Consider a quantum approach only when the problem has a proven or well-argued asymptotic speed-up, a small input and output (since loading large data into amplitudes is costly), and a scale where classical simulation is out of reach (chemistry beyond about 50 strongly correlated orbitals, for example).
friction: Noise and scale are the two limits. A gate error of 0.1 to 0.5 percent is 10⁶ to 10⁹ times too high for a long algorithm, so error correction multiplies the qubit count by hundreds to thousands, and every logical operation costs many physical ones. A useful machine needs a million or more physical qubits with wiring, cooling, and control electronics: a dilution refrigerator can cool thousands of qubits but not millions without new architectures. Real-time decoding must process the syndrome of each error-correction round (about 1 µs for superconducting qubits) with a classical computer, a heavy engineering task.
Cryptographic timelines are uncertain in both directions. Resource estimates for RSA-2048 fell about twentyfold in six years, yet no one can say whether a relevant machine will exist in 8 years or 30. Quantum algorithms are also weaker than they sound: HHL gives an exponential gain only if data load for free, and "dequantised" classical algorithms removed several claimed advantages. The safe response is crypto-agility: the ability to change algorithms without rebuilding systems. Post-quantum algorithms are younger than RSA, so a flaw can still appear (the SIKE scheme was broken classically in 2022, on a laptop, after reaching the fourth round of the NIST process).
practice: For most engineers, the practical work is cryptographic. Inventory where you use RSA, ECDH and ECDSA: TLS, SSH, VPNs, code signing, certificates, JWT signing, stored encrypted data and hardware security modules. Prioritise long-lived confidentiality (backups, archives, health or financial data that must stay secret for 10 years or more), then signatures with long lifetimes (firmware, root certificates). Turn on hybrid post-quantum key exchange where your libraries and TLS terminators support it; major browsers and CDNs have enabled X25519 with ML-KEM by default in recent versions, so check that your servers and middleboxes handle the larger handshake messages. Use AES-256 and SHA-384 or larger where symmetric strength is a policy question. Build crypto-agility: avoid hard-coding algorithm identifiers and key sizes, and test certificate and handshake size limits.
To learn the computing side, start with Nielsen and Chuang or Preskill's free lecture notes. Run small circuits on a simulator (Qiskit, Cirq, or PennyLane) to see interference and entanglement; with 20 qubits a laptop is enough. When reading news, ask four questions: how many physical qubits, what two-qubit gate error, are the qubits error-corrected, and is the task one for which a classical computer has been shown to need exponentially more resources?
quiz: What does a Hadamard gate do, and why does applying it twice return the original state? || H turns |0⟩ into (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2. Applying it again makes the two paths to |1⟩ have opposite signs (+1/2 and −1/2), so they cancel, and the paths to |0⟩ add up. This is destructive and constructive interference.
How does Shor's algorithm use the quantum computer, and what does the classical part do? || The quantum part finds the period r of f(x) = aˣ mod N using a superposition, modular exponentiation and the quantum Fourier transform. The classical part turns r into factors with gcd(a^(r/2) ± 1, N) and repeats if the choice of a was unlucky.
Why are error-corrected qubits needed, and what did Google's Willow show? || Physical gate errors of about 0.1 to 0.5 percent are far too high for algorithms with billions of operations. Willow showed that, with physical errors below the surface-code threshold, increasing the code distance from 3 to 5 to 7 cut the logical error by about a factor of 2 each time, so adding qubits reduces errors.
Grover's algorithm searches 2⁶⁴ items. About how many iterations does it need, and what does this mean for AES-128? || About (π/4)√(2⁶⁴) = (π/4) × 2³² ≈ 3.4 × 10⁹ iterations for 2⁶⁴ items. For AES-128, with 2¹²⁸ keys, Grover needs about 2⁶⁴ iterations, which is impractical because each is a long reversible circuit and the search does not parallelise well. AES-256 is considered safe.
Your company holds customer records that must remain confidential for 15 years, and a migration to new cryptography takes 5 years. A relevant quantum computer may arrive in 10 to 20 years. What do you do? || By Mosca's inequality, 15 + 5 = 20 years may exceed the time to the machine, so start now: inventory, deploy hybrid key exchange (X25519 plus ML-KEM) to protect traffic from being recorded and decrypted later, and build crypto-agility. Waiting would risk the data.
