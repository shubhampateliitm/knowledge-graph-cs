@@ neurons
value: A neuron is a cell that receives many small electrical signals, adds them up, and sends its own signal when the sum is large enough. A synapse is the contact point between two neurons, and its strength can change with experience. These two facts together are the working basis of every brain: a signal-adding cell, and connections that learn. The human brain has about 86 billion neurons and roughly 100 trillion synapses, and it runs on about 20 watts, which is close to the power of a bright household light bulb.
For computing, neurons matter for two reasons. First, they are the origin of the artificial neuron: a unit that multiplies inputs by weights, adds them, and applies a threshold. Deep learning (L1) is built on that idea, and the idea that learning means changing connection strengths comes straight from synapses. Second, neurons are the example that shows how much the artificial version leaves out. A real neuron communicates with short spikes in time, uses chemistry at every connection, and does computation inside its own branches. Knowing what was kept and what was dropped tells you when a neural-network analogy is useful and when it is only a slogan.
This concept stands on electromagnetism (Maxwell, L5). A nerve signal is not a current in a wire. It is a wave of changing voltage across the cell membrane, driven by charged ions (sodium, potassium, calcium, chloride) moving through protein channels. The laws of electric fields and charge, and the physics of a leaky cable, give the equations that describe this wave. Several higher concepts build on neurons: the McCulloch–Pitts neuron (the first mathematical model), the visual cortex (circuits of neurons that detect edges), dopamine (a chemical signal that adjusts synapses), and human perception (vision and hearing as neural computation).
terms: Membrane potential — the voltage difference across the cell membrane; a resting neuron sits at about −70 millivolts (mV), inside relative to outside.
Action potential (spike) — a brief, fixed-size pulse of voltage, about 100 mV tall and 1 millisecond wide, that travels along the axon without getting weaker.
Dendrite and axon — dendrites are the branching inputs of a neuron; the axon is the single output cable that carries spikes to other cells.
Synapse — the junction where an axon terminal meets another cell; a spike causes release of neurotransmitter molecules that open ion channels on the receiving side.
Excitatory and inhibitory — an excitatory input pushes the membrane potential toward firing (glutamate is the main one); an inhibitory input pushes it away (GABA is the main one).
Threshold — the membrane potential, about −55 mV in many cells, above which a spike starts.
Synaptic plasticity — a lasting change in the strength of a synapse; long-term potentiation (LTP) makes it stronger, long-term depression (LTD) makes it weaker.
Hebbian learning — the rule that a synapse grows stronger when the cell before it and the cell after it are active together.
Firing rate — the number of spikes per second; many circuits carry information in this rate, others in exact spike times.
history: In the 1900s Santiago Ramón y Cajal argued, against Camillo Golgi, that the nervous system is made of separate cells that touch at gaps. This was the neuron doctrine, and both men shared the 1906 Nobel Prize. In 1952 Alan Hodgkin and Andrew Huxley recorded from the giant axon of the squid and wrote four coupled equations that reproduce the action potential from the movement of sodium and potassium ions. They received the Nobel Prize in 1963. This was the moment that the nerve signal became physics and chemistry that could be calculated.
In 1949 Donald Hebb published The Organization of Behavior. He proposed that when one cell repeatedly helps to fire another, some growth or change makes that link stronger. The sentence "cells that fire together wire together" is a later summary, not his wording. The physical proof came in 1973, when Timothy Bliss and Terje Lømo showed in the rabbit hippocampus that a brief high-frequency burst gives a synapse a boost lasting hours or longer (LTP). In 1997 Henry Markram and colleagues, and in 1998 Guo-Qiang Bi and Mu-Ming Poo, showed that timing matters at the scale of milliseconds. If the input spike comes a few milliseconds before the output spike, the synapse gets stronger. If it comes after, the synapse gets weaker. This is spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP).
Models followed at several levels of detail. Louis Lapicque proposed the integrate-and-fire neuron in 1907, long before the biology was understood. Hodgkin and Huxley gave the detailed version. In 2003 Eugene Izhikevich published a two-variable model that reproduces many firing patterns at low cost. In 2021 Beniaguev, Segev and London showed that a deep network of 5 to 8 layers is needed to imitate the input-output behaviour of a single detailed cortical pyramidal neuron. The simple "one neuron, one weighted sum" picture is therefore a choice of abstraction, not a fact about cells.
how: Start with the electrical structure. The membrane is a thin insulating layer of fat with a high concentration of potassium inside and sodium outside, kept that way by a pump (the sodium–potassium pump) that spends ATP, the cell's energy currency. At rest, potassium channels are partly open, and potassium leaks out until its electrical pull balances its concentration pull. That gives the resting potential near −70 mV. This is where electromagnetism (Maxwell, L5) enters: the Nernst equation (the balance of electric force and diffusion) sets the voltage for each ion, and the membrane behaves as a capacitor in parallel with several variable resistors, so its voltage changes by the ordinary laws of charge and current.
Inputs arrive at synapses. When a spike reaches an axon terminal, calcium enters, vesicles (small bags of neurotransmitter) fuse with the membrane, and the transmitter crosses a gap of about 20 nanometres. On the other side it opens channels. An excitatory synapse lets sodium in, which gives a small positive voltage step called an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP), typically a fraction of a millivolt to a few millivolts. An inhibitory synapse lets chloride in or potassium out, which gives a negative step. The step spreads along the dendrite, getting smaller with distance, and it decays with a time constant of roughly 10 to 20 milliseconds.
At the base of the axon, the cell sums these steps over space and time. If the sum lifts the potential above threshold (about 15 mV above rest), voltage-gated sodium channels open in a chain reaction. Sodium rushes in, the voltage shoots up to about +30 mV, then the sodium channels close and potassium channels open, and the voltage falls back below rest. The whole spike lasts about 1 ms, and then the cell cannot fire again for a short refractory period. The spike has the same size every time, so the neuron's output is all-or-nothing, and information is in when and how often spikes occur. Along the axon the spike regenerates itself at every point, so it does not fade. In myelinated axons the insulation forces the signal to jump between gaps, reaching speeds up to about 100 metres per second.
A simple model keeps only the core. In the leaky integrate-and-fire model, the voltage V decays toward rest like water leaking from a bucket, each input adds a jump, and when V crosses the threshold the cell emits a spike and resets. The leak is what makes timing matter: inputs that arrive close together add up, and inputs that are spread out leak away. This is the biological form of "weighted sum plus threshold", with the extra feature that time is built in.
Learning happens at the synapse. The receiving side has a receptor called NMDA that opens only when two conditions hold together: the transmitter is present (the input is active) and the cell is already depolarized (the output is active). This makes it a physical detector of "fire together". When it opens, calcium enters and triggers more receptors to be inserted into the synapse, which makes it stronger. A weaker, slower calcium signal does the opposite. This gives Hebb's rule in molecular form. Pure Hebbian growth is unstable, because strong synapses help to make the cell fire, which makes them stronger still. Real circuits control this with inhibition and with scaling rules that keep the total strength of a cell's inputs near a set level (homeostatic plasticity, a related idea that you meet in the homeostasis read).
The numbers explain the design. A spike costs a lot of energy, since every spike requires pumping ions back, but most neurons are silent most of the time; typical cortical cells fire at only a few spikes per second on average. The brain saves power by being sparse and event-driven, using energy only when something happens. This is the principle that neuromorphic chips such as Intel's Loihi and the SpiNNaker machine try to copy.

example: Use a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron with 1 ms steps, so you can follow it with a pen. Measure voltage in mV above rest, so rest is 0 and the threshold is 15. Each millisecond the voltage keeps 90% of its old value (a time constant near 10 ms) and then adds the inputs. Synapse A is excitatory and gives +5. Synapse B is excitatory and gives +9. After a spike, reset the voltage to 0.
Case 1: A and B fire in alternation. At t = 1, A arrives: V = 0.9 × 0 + 5 = 5. At t = 2, B arrives: V = 0.9 × 5 + 9 = 13.5. At t = 3, A arrives: V = 0.9 × 13.5 + 5 = 17.15, which is above 15, so the neuron spikes and resets to 0. Three inputs in three milliseconds were enough.
Case 2: only A fires, once every 5 ms. Between inputs the voltage is multiplied by 0.9⁵ = 0.59. In the long run V satisfies V = 0.59 V + 5, so V = 5 / 0.41 = 12.2 just after each input. That is below 15, so the neuron never fires, even though it receives the same input weight forever. The same total input produces no spike when it is spread out and a spike when it is packed together. That is the difference between coincidence detection and counting.
Case 3: add an inhibitory synapse that gives −3 at the same moment as each B input. Now the sequence is 5, then 0.9 × 5 + 6 = 10.5, then 0.9 × 10.5 + 5 = 14.45, which is just under 15, and the neuron fires only at t = 4 (V = 0.9 × 14.45 + 6 = 19.0). One inhibitory input delayed the spike by one millisecond. Inhibition changes the timing, and with enough weight it blocks firing completely.
Now learning. Suppose A has weight 5 and fires 4 ms before the output spike, and the STDP rule changes the weight by +10% when the input comes within 10 ms before the spike. Then A's weight becomes 5.5. Next time, one A input contributes more and the cell needs fewer coincident inputs to fire. A synapse that fires 4 ms after the output spike instead would lose 10%. In this way the cell slowly learns to trust inputs that predict its own firing.
intuition: A voting cell that counts shouts only if they come close together in time, and that learns over weeks whose voices to trust.
A neuron is natural once you ask what a cell can do with only chemistry and voltage. It cannot run a program, so it does the cheapest useful operation: add many weak signals and respond if the total is large. Adding many noisy inputs also averages out noise, so the cell is reliable even when each synapse is not. Putting learning in the connections, not in the cells, is also natural. A new experience can then change behaviour by changing a few weights, while the cells and their wiring stay the same. The all-or-nothing spike is the same engineering choice as a digital signal in a long cable: it is expensive to send, but it does not degrade over a metre.
mistakes: People often think a neuron is just a weighted sum with a threshold, like the unit in a neural network. In fact that describes a simplification. Real neurons have time-dependent leaks, dendrites that do non-linear processing in separate branches, thousands of synapse types, and spikes whose timing carries information. The deep-network result of Beniaguev and colleagues (2021) found that one detailed pyramidal cell needed a 5 to 8 layer network to copy it.
People often think the brain runs on electricity as a computer does. In fact the signal moves by ions through membrane channels, at speeds of about 1 to 100 m/s, far slower than electrons in a wire. The brain is slow per step and works through massive parallelism: about 10¹¹ cells acting at once.
People often think "cells that fire together wire together" is the whole of learning. In fact Hebbian growth alone is unstable and cannot say which of many correlated inputs caused a result. Real learning also needs normalisation, inhibition, a third signal (such as dopamine, which marks reward) and timing rules like STDP. Whether the brain uses anything like backpropagation is an open research question.
People often think glial cells outnumber neurons ten to one. In fact the human brain has about 86 billion neurons and about 85 billion non-neuronal cells, so the two are roughly equal. This count, by Azevedo and colleagues (2009), replaced the older figure.
tradeoffs: Choose a rate-based unit (weighted sum plus a smooth non-linearity such as ReLU) when you want to train large networks by gradient descent on GPUs. It is cheap, differentiable and works for tasks like image and language modelling. You give up spike timing, energy sparsity and any claim of biological realism. Choose the leaky integrate-and-fire neuron when time matters (coincidence detection, sequences, event cameras) or when you design neuromorphic hardware. It costs only a few arithmetic operations per millisecond, but its spike is not differentiable, so training needs tricks such as surrogate gradients.
Choose the Hodgkin–Huxley model, or a multi-compartment model with real dendrite geometry, when the question is about channels, drugs or the shape of a single cell. By the estimate of Izhikevich (2004), a Hodgkin–Huxley neuron needs about 1,200 floating-point operations per millisecond, against about 5 for integrate-and-fire and about 13 for his own two-variable model. That is a cost of 100 to 200 times for detail you may not need. A network of 10⁵ detailed cells is a supercomputer job.
Choose the Izhikevich two-variable model when you need many firing patterns (bursting, adaptation) in a large simulated network: it is the middle point. Choose a mean-field or population model, which tracks the firing rate of a whole group, when the circuit has thousands of similar cells and you care about averages such as oscillations. You give up individual spikes.
Where the models break: all the point-neuron models above treat the cell as one voltage. They fail when the answer depends on where on the dendrite inputs land, as in direction selectivity in the retina. And all of them break for plasticity if you use only the pairwise STDP rule, because real plasticity depends on firing rate, voltage and neuromodulators together.
friction: Nobody can yet describe a whole brain circuit at synapse level except in a few small animals. The roundworm C. elegans has 302 neurons and a fully known wiring diagram since 1986, but even that does not give a working model of its behaviour, because the strengths and chemical modulation of the connections are missing. In 2024 the FlyWire project published the wiring of the whole adult fruit fly brain, about 140,000 neurons and 50 million synapses. A human brain is roughly 600,000 times larger in neurons.
Energy is a second limit. The brain is about 2% of body mass and uses about 20% of resting energy. Most of that goes to restoring ion gradients after spikes and synaptic events. A modern GPU running a large language model uses hundreds of watts, and clusters use megawatts. Neuromorphic hardware has shown large energy savings on sparse, event-driven tasks, but on mainstream benchmarks it has not beaten GPUs, mostly because there is no good method to train spiking networks at large scale.
Finally, credit assignment is unsolved. Backpropagation needs exact error signals sent backward through the same weights, and no known circuit does that. Research proposals (feedback alignment, predictive coding, local learning rules with dopamine-like signals) explain parts of the picture and none is accepted as the brain's method. Plasticity is also hard to measure in a living brain, since you cannot read thousands of synapse weights over time.
practice: An engineer meets neurons in three places. First, in choosing a neural-network design. Know that the weighted-sum unit is a loose abstraction, and do not use biological arguments to justify engineering choices (such as "the brain does it, so this loss function must work"). Check them with experiments. Second, in event-driven and sparse computation: event cameras, spiking networks and neuromorphic chips such as Loihi use the same principle as the cell, which is "compute only when something changes". If your data is a stream of rare events, this is a real design option. Third, in reading neuroscience-inspired papers on memory, attention and learning rules: ask which of the properties above (timing, sparsity, local learning, neuromodulation) the paper actually uses.
To study this well, work through the cable and membrane chapters of Kandel's Principles of Neural Science, then read Gerstner, Kistler, Naud and Paninski, Neuronal Dynamics, which is free online and has exercises on integrate-and-fire neurons. Code a leaky integrate-and-fire neuron in 20 lines, and plot its voltage and spike rate against a constant input current (the f–I curve). Then add a Hebbian or STDP weight update and watch the instability yourself; it teaches more than any description. For data on real neurons, the Allen Institute publishes recordings and models openly.

value: The McCulloch–Pitts neuron is a very small model of a nerve cell: it receives yes/no inputs, adds them, and outputs "yes" only if the sum reaches a threshold. Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts published it in 1943 and proved something surprising. If you connect enough of these units, the network can compute any logical function that a circuit of AND, OR and NOT gates can compute. The brain, they argued, can be seen as a machine for logic.
The paper made three bridges at once. It connected biology to mathematics, because a neuron became a precise object that you can write equations for. It connected neurons to logic, because the units implement Boolean gates. And it connected both to the theory of computation, because McCulloch and Pitts also argued that networks with loops and an unlimited memory tape are as powerful as Turing's machines. John von Neumann used their neuron symbols in his 1945 report on the EDVAC computer design, so the paper influenced the architecture of digital computers as well as AI.
For the layer above, deep learning (L1), this paper is the source of the standard artificial neuron: a weighted sum of inputs followed by a threshold or non-linearity. Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) added the missing piece, learning the weights, and every modern network is a stack of descendants of that unit with smooth non-linearities. This read stands on two concepts. Neurons (L5) provide the all-or-nothing firing that the model copies. Boole's Laws of Thought (L5) provide the algebra of 0 and 1 in which the units are shown to be complete.
terms: Threshold logic unit — a unit that outputs 1 if the weighted sum of its inputs is at least a threshold θ, and 0 otherwise.
Excitatory and inhibitory input — an input that pushes toward firing, and an input that pushes away from it; in the 1943 paper one active inhibitory input blocks firing completely.
Boolean function — a rule that maps n bits to one bit, such as AND, OR, NOT or XOR; there are 2^(2^n) of them for n inputs.
Linearly separable — a Boolean function is linearly separable if a straight line (a plane, in more inputs) can split the inputs that give 1 from those that give 0; a single threshold unit computes exactly these.
Functional completeness — a set of gates is complete if every Boolean function can be built from them; AND, OR and NOT are complete.
Finite automaton — a machine with a fixed number of states that reads input one symbol at a time; networks without external memory are equivalent to these.
Perceptron — Rosenblatt's learning version of the unit, with weights adjusted by a simple error-correcting rule.
Synchronous time step — in the model, all units update together in discrete ticks, and a signal takes exactly one tick to cross one unit.
history: Warren McCulloch was a neurophysiologist and psychiatrist, and Walter Pitts was a self-taught logician of about 20 who had read Russell and Whitehead's Principia Mathematica as a teenager. They met in Chicago in 1941–1942 and worked together. Their paper, "A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity", appeared in the Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics in 1943. It used the notation of Carnap's logic and the "all-or-none" law of nerve firing then known from physiology. Alan Turing had defined his machines in 1936, so the idea that thinking might be computing was in the air.
The paper was read first by logicians, engineers and early cyberneticists. Von Neumann borrowed its neuron diagrams in 1945. Stephen Kleene in 1951 (published 1956) proved the precise result: networks of such units compute exactly the "regular events" recognized by finite automata. In 1949 Donald Hebb added the idea that synapse strengths change with activity. In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt introduced the perceptron at Cornell, with the Mark I Perceptron hardware (a 400-pixel camera and motor-driven weights) built a few years later. He proved, and Novikoff proved formally in 1962, that the perceptron learning rule finds a solution in finite steps whenever one exists.
Then came the setback. In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert's book Perceptrons gave a careful analysis of what single-layer units cannot do, including XOR and connectedness. It was widely read as a reason to stop working on neural networks, and funding dropped in what is called the first AI winter. The remedy was already known in outline, since multi-layer networks can compute XOR, but training them needed backpropagation, popularized by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams in 1986. McCulloch and Pitts themselves had a sad later story: Pitts broke with the group around Norbert Wiener in 1952 and withdrew from research, and he died in 1969.
how: A McCulloch–Pitts unit has n binary inputs x₁ … x_n, each 0 or 1, and one binary output. Time moves in discrete ticks. The unit adds up the inputs with weights, and if the sum is at least the threshold θ, the output is 1 at the next tick. Otherwise it is 0. In the original paper all excitatory inputs count +1 and an inhibitory input is absolute: if any inhibitory input is active, the unit is silent. Later textbook versions allow any real weights w_i, giving output = 1 if w₁x₁ + … + w_nx_n ≥ θ. These two versions can compute the same functions, so the weighted form is usually taught.
The paper states its physical assumptions clearly, and each one comes from the neuron facts. The activity of a neuron is all-or-none, so the output is a bit. A fixed number of excitatory synapses must be active within a short window for the cell to fire, so there is a threshold. The only significant delay is the synaptic delay, so one unit costs one tick. An active inhibitory synapse prevents firing absolutely. And the structure of the network does not change with time, so the weights are fixed. The neuron concept (L5) supplies exactly these five facts, and the whole theory is the consequence of treating them as exact. Because the idealization is exact, it can be reasoned about with logic: the state of the network at tick t is a statement in propositional logic about the state at tick t − 1.
Boole's algebra supplies the target. Boole showed in 1854 that logic can be written as arithmetic on 0 and 1: NOT x is 1 − x, x AND y is x·y, and x OR y is x + y − xy. A threshold unit gives the same results directly. For AND, use weights 1, 1 and θ = 2: the sum reaches 2 only when both inputs are 1. For OR, use θ = 1: any active input is enough. For NOT, use one inhibitory input and a constant "always on" excitatory input with θ = 1: the unit fires unless the input is on. Since AND, OR and NOT are functionally complete (every truth table can be written as an OR of ANDs, Boole's expansion), any Boolean function is a network of at most two layers of such units plus negations.
The units are not powerful alone. A single unit with n inputs computes only linearly separable functions. For n = 2, 14 of the 16 Boolean functions qualify, and only XOR and XNOR do not. For n = 3, it is 104 of 256. For n = 4, it is 1,882 of 65,536, which is under 3%. As n grows the fraction goes to nearly zero. So the power comes from layers: a hidden layer computes simple features, and an output layer combines them.
Time and loops add memory. Each unit delays the signal by one tick, so a layered network of depth d has a latency of d ticks. A unit that feeds its output back to itself with weight 1 and threshold 1 keeps firing after the input disappears: it stores one bit. An inhibitory input can reset it. With such loops, the network is a finite state machine with a number of states up to 2^(number of units). This is what Kleene proved to be exactly the regular languages. With a tape of unlimited length attached, McCulloch and Pitts argued, the system equals a Turing machine.
What the model leaves out is also a part of the mechanism. It has no learning: the weights and thresholds are chosen by hand. It treats every spike as the same event, without graded signals, rates or spike timing. All units tick together, which real neurons do not. Replacing the hard threshold with a smooth function such as a sigmoid or ReLU, and adding an automatic way to adjust the weights, gives the unit of modern deep learning. The step from this read to deep learning is therefore two changes: smooth non-linearity and learning by gradient.

example: Build XOR, the function that is 1 when exactly one of x and y is 1, and show why one unit cannot do it. A single unit with weights w₁, w₂ and threshold θ must satisfy four conditions. For (0,0) the output is 0, so 0 < θ. For (1,0) and (0,1) it is 1, so w₁ ≥ θ and w₂ ≥ θ. For (1,1) it is 0, so w₁ + w₂ < θ. But w₁ + w₂ ≥ 2θ, and since θ > 0, 2θ > θ, which contradicts w₁ + w₂ < θ. There is no solution. This is the geometric fact that a line cannot separate (0,1) and (1,0) from (0,0) and (1,1).
Now use three units. Unit A is OR: weights 1, 1, θ = 1. Unit B is AND: weights 1, 1, θ = 2. The output unit C has weight +1 from A, weight −1 from B, and θ = 1. Check the four inputs. For (0,0): A = 0, B = 0, sum into C = 0, so C = 0. For (1,0): A = 1, B = 0, sum = 1 ≥ 1, so C = 1. For (0,1): the same, C = 1. For (1,1): A = 1, B = 1, sum = 1 − 1 = 0 < 1, so C = 0. This is XOR. The answer appears two ticks after the inputs, because there are two layers.
Next, a bigger case: parity of n bits, which is 1 if an odd number of bits are 1. With weighted threshold units a two-layer network is enough. Make n hidden units, where unit k fires if at least k inputs are on (weights all 1, θ = k). If m inputs are on, hidden units 1 to m fire. The output unit takes the alternating sum h₁ − h₂ + h₃ − …, with threshold 1. For m = 3 this is 1 − 1 + 1 = 1, so odd parity gives 1. For m = 4 it is 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 = 0. So n + 1 units compute parity of n bits at depth 2. A circuit with only AND and OR gates and constant depth needs a size that grows faster than any polynomial in n (Furst, Saxe and Sipser 1984; Håstad 1986). Threshold units are much more economical for counting-like functions.
intuition: A neuron as a tiny committee that votes yes only if enough members say yes, and a brain as a large wiring diagram of such committees.
The model works because logic is built from very little. Any truth table is an OR of ANDs, and a threshold unit is the cheapest device that does AND, OR and NOT depending on one number. So once you accept that a neuron adds and compares, it is natural that networks of neurons can compute anything finite. The limit is also natural. One unit draws a single straight line through the input space, and most interesting functions need many lines, so you need layers. The history of neural networks is a repeated discovery of this fact: depth gives expressive power, and the open question is how to find the weights.
mistakes: People often think McCulloch and Pitts described how real neurons work. In fact they described an idealization chosen to make a proof possible. They knew the all-or-none law of firing, but they dropped timing, graded potentials and chemistry. Their goal was a logical argument about what a nervous system could compute, not a simulation of one.
People often think the 1943 model includes learning. In fact the weights and thresholds are fixed by the designer. Learning came in with Hebb (1949) as an idea and with Rosenblatt (1958) as an algorithm.
People often think Minsky and Papert proved that neural networks are useless. In fact they proved limits for single-layer perceptrons, for example that one unit cannot compute XOR or parity of all inputs with small weights. They discussed multi-layer networks as an open problem. The damage came from the way the result was read, and from the lack of a practical training method at that time.
People often think that a network of McCulloch–Pitts units is the same as a Turing machine. In fact a finite network is only a finite automaton, which has no unbounded memory. To reach Turing power you need an unlimited tape or an unlimited number of units, which no real network has. The same limit applies to a modern neural network of fixed size.
tradeoffs: Choose the hard-threshold unit when you want an exact, checkable logical object: proofs about circuit complexity, formal verification of small networks, binary and ternary neural networks for tiny hardware, or hand-designed logic. Its outputs are exact and cheap, but it has zero gradient almost everywhere, so you cannot train it by gradient descent. Choose a smooth unit (sigmoid, tanh, ReLU) when you need to learn weights from data. You give up exact binary behaviour and gain differentiability. For networks of any real size, this is nearly always the right choice.
Choose a single unit with a learning rule (perceptron, or logistic regression) when the problem is close to linearly separable and you want a convex, fast, interpretable model. With n = 4 inputs, only about 3% of Boolean functions are separable, but real data with many features is often close to separable, and a linear model is hard to beat on cost. Choose depth when the function has a compositional structure, such as parity or image features. Parity with n inputs needs n + 1 weighted threshold units at depth 2, but a single-layer network cannot do it, and a constant-depth AND/OR circuit needs a super-polynomial size. Depth buys exponential savings for some functions, and the price is that training is harder.
Choose a model as a finite automaton (a McCulloch–Pitts network with loops) when your task is a regular pattern over a stream: protocol parsing, simple alarms. It runs in constant memory. Choose a more powerful model (a stack, a counter or a full Turing-style memory) when you need to match nested structure such as balanced brackets. The 1943 model breaks down exactly there: a finite network cannot count without bound. Real recurrent networks are finite, but with real-valued states they can approximate counting for a limited length, which is why they struggle on very long nested inputs.
friction: The model is binary, synchronous and static. Real neurons are graded, asynchronous and plastic. The paper's use of logic also hides a point: a truth table of n inputs has 2^n rows, so building an arbitrary function as an OR of ANDs can need an exponential number of units. Existence of a network is not the same as a practical network.
The perceptron convergence theorem guarantees a solution only when the data are linearly separable, and when they are not, the simple rule keeps cycling. Training multi-layer networks of threshold units is hard because the output does not change smoothly with the weights. This difficulty (the credit-assignment problem for hard thresholds) is why backpropagation required smooth units, and why binary neural networks today are trained with approximations such as the straight-through estimator, which pretends the threshold is the identity when computing gradients.
Another open point is how much the biological story matters. Modern deep learning has mostly moved away from the neuron picture: transformers are built from matrix multiplications, normalisation and attention, and few of their parts have a neural counterpart. The McCulloch–Pitts idea remains useful as the smallest building block and as a way to see what depth and non-linearity buy. It is a good teaching model and a poor biological one.
practice: Treat the McCulloch–Pitts neuron as a design lens, not a product. When you read about a neural network, ask three questions: what function does one unit compute (a half-space), what do the layers add (compositions of half-spaces), and where do the non-linearities sit. A ReLU network with one hidden layer partitions the input into regions bounded by hyperplanes; with depth the number of regions can grow exponentially. This view helps you reason about capacity without equations.
Where an engineer meets threshold logic directly: binarized and quantized networks for edge devices (1-bit weights and activations, with XNOR and popcount replacing multiply-accumulate); decision-making rules in feature flags and alerting thresholds, which are single threshold units on a weighted sum; and digital design, where threshold gates are an alternative to AND/OR logic in some low-power circuits.
To study it, read the original paper (it is hard to follow because of its logical notation, so read a summary first), then the opening chapters of Minsky and Papert's Perceptrons and Kleene's result in a textbook on automata. Then code three things in an evening: hand-build AND, OR and XOR with threshold units; implement the perceptron rule and watch it fail to converge on XOR; add one hidden layer trained by gradient descent with a sigmoid and watch it succeed. These three exercises show why the field moved from hard thresholds to smooth ones.
quiz: Which Boolean gates can one McCulloch–Pitts unit compute, and which common one can it not? || It can compute AND, OR and NOT (and any linearly separable function). It cannot compute XOR or XNOR, because no single weighted threshold separates the two classes.
Why can a two-layer network of threshold units compute any Boolean function? || Every truth table can be written as an OR of ANDs (Boole's expansion). The hidden layer computes the ANDs (one per row with output 1, using negated inputs where needed), and the output unit ORs them.
How can n + 1 threshold units compute the parity of n bits at depth 2? || Hidden unit k fires if at least k inputs are on. If m inputs are on, units 1 to m fire. The output takes the alternating sum h₁ − h₂ + h₃ − …, which is 1 for odd m and 0 for even m, with threshold 1.
For 4 inputs there are 65,536 Boolean functions and 1,882 are linearly separable. What does this say about using one unit? || Only about 2.9% of the functions can be computed by one unit. One unit is enough only when the problem has a nearly linear structure, which is why hidden layers are needed for general functions.
You need to recognize balanced brackets in a stream. Would you use a finite McCulloch–Pitts network? || No. Balanced brackets need an unbounded counter, and a finite network is a finite automaton that can only count up to a fixed limit. Use a stack or a counter, or accept a depth limit.

@@ visualcortex
value: In 1959 David Hubel and Torsten Wiesel recorded from single neurons in the visual cortex of a cat and found that the cells do not respond to spots of light but to edges. A given cell fires for a bar at one orientation, in one place in the visual field, and stays quiet for other orientations. Further studies showed a ladder of cells: simple cells that need an edge at an exact position, complex cells that respond to the same edge anywhere in a small region, and higher areas that respond to corners, textures, shapes and finally whole objects and faces. The brain does not see a picture; it builds a description in stages.
This finding gave computer vision its basic design. The convolutional neural network (convolution, L2) is the engineering version of the idea: one small detector is repeated at every position (like a simple cell at every place), the results are pooled over nearby positions (like a complex cell), and the stages are stacked so that early layers find edges and later layers find parts and objects. Without this staged design, image models would need far more parameters and far more data. For a 224 × 224 image, a fully connected first layer that maps pixels to an equal number of units has about 2.5 billion weights. A 3 × 3 convolution has 9 weights, shared across the whole image.
This read stands on neurons (L5). The receptive field of a cortical cell is nothing more than the pattern of synaptic inputs it adds up: a cell that sums inputs from a row of aligned cells becomes an edge detector. The weighted-sum-and-threshold behaviour of neurons is the entire mechanism. What the visual cortex adds is the wiring plan: which cells connect to which, and in what order. That plan is the part that engineers copied, and it is where the evidence of this read comes from.
terms: Receptive field — the region of the visual field (and the stimulus pattern within it) that changes a neuron's firing; the cell is blind to everything outside.
Simple cell — a V1 cell with separate excitatory and inhibitory zones in its receptive field; it responds to an edge or bar of a specific orientation at a specific position and contrast polarity.
Complex cell — a V1 cell that responds to the same oriented edge anywhere within a larger region and for either contrast polarity.
Orientation column — a vertical strip of cortex whose cells all prefer about the same edge orientation; neighbouring strips prefer slightly different angles.
Retinotopy — the orderly map by which neighbouring points in the visual field activate neighbouring points on the cortex.
Center-surround cell — a retinal or LGN cell that fires when a spot of light falls on the centre of its field and is inhibited by light on the surround (or the reverse); it detects local contrast.
V1, V2, V4, IT — the stages of the ventral ("what") pathway: primary visual cortex, then V2, V4 and inferotemporal cortex, where cells respond to complex shapes and objects.
Feedforward and feedback — signals that go up the hierarchy toward complex features, and signals that come back down; the visual cortex has many more of the second kind than most models include.
history: In 1953 Stephen Kuffler had shown that retinal ganglion cells in the cat respond to small spots of light with an opposing centre and surround. Hubel and Wiesel, working in Kuffler's laboratory at Johns Hopkins and later at Harvard, tried to find what cells in the cortex respond to. For weeks in 1958–1959 they could get no response from a cortical cell to any spot. Then, while changing a slide in the projector, the edge of the glass slide moved across the screen, and the cell fired strongly. The cell cared about the edge and its direction, not about light. They published the first results in 1959 and the major paper, "Receptive fields, binocular interaction and functional architecture in the cat's visual cortex", in 1962. In 1968 they repeated and extended the work in the monkey.
They proposed a hierarchy: cells in the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) with centre-surround fields, lined up in a row, would give a simple cell with an elongated edge detector; several simple cells with the same orientation and different positions would give a complex cell. They also found orientation columns and ocular dominance stripes, and showed that the cortex is organized in repeating modules of roughly one square millimetre. Hubel and Wiesel shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (with Roger Sperry, who was honoured for other work).
The computational side followed quickly. Kunihiko Fukushima's Neocognitron (1980) was built directly from the simple and complex cell idea, with alternating S-layers and C-layers, and learned without supervision. Yann LeCun and colleagues at Bell Labs trained a similar structure with backpropagation to read handwritten zip codes in 1989, and the LeNet-5 paper (1998) made it a standard. Riesenhuber and Poggio's HMAX model (1999) used a max operation as the model of complex cells. In 2012 AlexNet won ImageNet with a large convolutional network, and in 2014 Yamins, DiCarlo and colleagues showed that networks optimized only for object recognition predicted the firing of neurons in monkey area IT better than earlier brain models.
how: Begin at the eye. The retina converts light to electrical signals in about 126 million photoreceptors, then compresses them to roughly 1 million ganglion-cell fibres per eye. Already in the retina, centre-surround cells compute local contrast: a ganglion cell with an ON centre fires when a small spot is brighter than its surroundings. This means that uniform light produces almost no signal, and only changes are sent. The signal travels through the optic nerve to the LGN in the thalamus, which keeps the same centre-surround code, and then to primary visual cortex (V1) at the back of the head. V1 contains roughly 140 million neurons in each hemisphere.
In V1, a simple cell receives input from several LGN cells whose centres lie in a line. If the centres are ON-centre and are aligned along a vertical line, the cell's receptive field is a narrow vertical bright strip, flanked by dark strips. A vertical edge in exactly the right place fires many of its inputs together, and the cell crosses its threshold. A horizontal edge activates only one of its inputs at a time, so the sum stays low. A small shift of the edge moves it into an inhibitory zone, so the response collapses. This is a direct use of the neuron concept: orientation selectivity is not a new ability but the result of adding up aligned inputs. A good mathematical description of these receptive fields is a Gabor function: a sinusoid multiplied by a Gaussian window. Olshausen and Field (1996) showed that if you ask for a sparse code of natural image patches, the learned filters come out as Gabor-like edge detectors, which suggests that the cortex learns the statistics of natural scenes.
A complex cell pools several simple cells that share an orientation but differ in position (and in contrast polarity). It fires if any one of them fires. The effect is invariance to small shifts: the same edge anywhere in the area drives the cell. Models describe this as a MAX operation or as the sum of squared outputs of two simple cells with filters a quarter-cycle apart (the energy model). The price is a loss of position precision, which is why the next stages must get information on where things are from elsewhere.
The hierarchy repeats. V2 cells respond to combinations of edges such as corners and texture boundaries. V4 cells respond to curvature and simple shapes. Cells in inferotemporal cortex (IT) respond to object parts, faces and whole objects, with large receptive fields covering many degrees of vision. Receptive field size grows at every stage, from a fraction of a degree in V1 to tens of degrees in IT, and the stimulus complexity grows together with it. This is exactly the structure of a convolutional network: convolution (the same detector at every position, with shared weights), then a non-linearity and pooling (position tolerance), then repetition. The speed matters too. People can detect an animal in a briefly shown photo with a brain response that appears about 150 ms after the image (Thorpe and colleagues, 1996), which is only a few synaptic steps for each stage, so a first guess is mostly feedforward.
What does the real system have that the models lack? Feedback connections are very numerous, and only about 5 to 10% of the synapses on LGN relay cells come from the retina; most come from cortex and brainstem. Feedback adjusts attention, fills in occluded parts, and helps when the image is hard. Recent work (Kar and DiCarlo, 2019) found that recurrent processing is needed for the hardest images, so the pure feedforward CNN is a first-pass model, not the whole system.

example: Use one row of pixels where 0 is dark and 1 is bright. A simple cell uses the weights −1, 0, +1 on three neighbouring pixels: it subtracts the left pixel from the right pixel. That is an edge detector for "dark to bright, left to right". Take the row p = [0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1], numbered from 0 to 7. At each centre position i the response is r(i) = p(i+1) − p(i−1). So r(1) = p2 − p0 = 0; r(2) = p3 − p1 = 1; r(3) = p4 − p2 = 1; r(4) = p5 − p3 = 0. A simple cell centred at position 2 fires with strength 1. A copy of that cell centred at position 5 gives r(5) = p6 − p4 = 0 and stays quiet.
Now shift the edge two pixels to the right: p = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 1, 1, 1]. The cell at position 2 now gives p3 − p1 = 0 and goes quiet, while the cell at position 5 now gives p6 − p4 = 1 − 0 = 1. The simple cells are not shift invariant; each one looks at one place.
Build a complex cell that takes the maximum over the simple cells at positions 1 to 6. For the first row the maximum is 1. For the shifted row it is also 1. The complex cell gives the same answer even though the edge moved by two pixels. This is the same as max-pooling over a window.
Polarity: take the reversed edge p = [1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]. The same detector gives r(2) = p3 − p1 = −1. If you rectify (keep only positive values), this simple cell stays silent. A second simple cell with weights +1, 0, −1 responds with +1. If the complex cell takes the maximum over both types of simple cell, it responds to the edge in either polarity. This is what V1 complex cells do.
Orientation: use a 3 × 3 patch with rows [0,0,0], [0,0,0], [1,1,1] (a horizontal edge) and the vertical-edge detector that applies −1, 0, +1 along each row and sums over the three rows. Each row gives (right − left): 0 − 0 = 0, 0 − 0 = 0, and 1 − 1 = 0. The total is 0: the vertical detector ignores the horizontal edge. Finally, count parameters. A 3 × 3 kernel has 9 weights. With 64 such kernels in the first CNN layer there are 576 weights, applied to all 50,176 positions of a 224 × 224 image. A fully connected layer with 50,176 inputs and 50,176 outputs would have 2.5 billion weights.
intuition: Vision is built like an assembly line: each stage finds simple pieces that the next stage combines into larger parts, and a small number of detector designs is repeated everywhere.
The staged design is natural for two reasons. First, images have local structure: nearby pixels are related, so a small detector is useful anywhere. This is also why the same detector can be repeated at every position, saving wiring and saving learning. Second, the world is compositional: objects are made of parts, parts are made of contours, and contours are made of edges. A system that reuses the same edge detectors for every object needs far fewer cells than one that stores a separate template for every object. Evolution and learning in cortex found the same solution that image statistics suggest.
mistakes: People often think a CNN is a copy of the visual cortex. In fact it borrows three ideas (local receptive fields, shared detectors, pooling), and omits many facts: the cortex has strong feedback and lateral connections, uses spikes, learns without labels and does not share weights exactly across positions. Backpropagation has no known biological form.
People often think the visual cortex works like a camera, forming a picture. In fact cells respond to edges and local contrast, and uniform areas produce almost no signal. We perceive uniform surfaces because the brain infers them from the boundaries.
People often think complex cells are simply higher-level versions of simple cells with larger fields. In fact the key difference is invariance: complex cells tolerate shifts in position and contrast polarity inside their field. Later work shows that real V1 is more varied than a clean two-class division, with a continuum between simple and complex, but the pooling idea remains the useful part.
People often think the hierarchy ends in "grandmother cells", each encoding one object. In fact object identity in IT is spread across populations of cells, and a simple linear readout from a few hundred cells gives good recognition (Hung, Kreiman, Poggio and DiCarlo, 2005). A single cell is usually tuned to a family of shapes, not to one object.
tradeoffs: Choose a convolutional network when the data has local, repeated structure and you have limited data or compute: images, audio spectrograms, genome sequences. The built-in assumption of locality and translation equivariance cuts the parameter count by orders of magnitude, as in the 9 weights versus 2.5 billion example above. You give up flexibility: it handles long-range relations between distant parts poorly, because information must travel through many layers.
Choose a vision transformer (ViT) when you have a very large training set (hundreds of millions of images or more) and want global interactions from the first layer. It has weaker built-in assumptions, so it needs more data to learn what the cortex gets from architecture, but it scales better with data and compute. With small data, a CNN or a hybrid usually wins. Choose a hybrid (convolutional stem, attention later) when you want local efficiency at low levels and global context at high levels, which is also similar to how the brain has local connections early and wide feedback later.
Choose the Gabor / linear–nonlinear model of V1 when you are doing neuroscience or building a compact front-end for a classical vision pipeline: it is simple and explains the response of V1 well for simple stimuli. It breaks down for natural scenes and beyond V1, where context, attention and feedback change the response, and the cell is not a fixed filter. Choose a deep network trained on recognition (Yamins and DiCarlo) when the goal is to predict responses in V4 and IT: deep CNNs are currently the best quantitative models of those areas. Their predictions still fall short of the noise ceiling and degrade for out-of-distribution images.
Choose to add recurrence or feedback when images are cluttered, occluded or ambiguous, or when you want robustness. You pay with latency and a harder training problem. The feedforward model is a good first approximation for the first 100 to 150 ms of vision and a poor one after that.
friction: Biological plausibility is the main open problem. Convolutional networks get the shape of the representation right but not the learning rule or the temporal dynamics. The brain learns from a few examples and from unlabelled video, while a CNN needs millions of labelled images. Another sign of the gap is adversarial examples: tiny changes to pixels that fool a network but not a person. Humans are shape-biased in recognition, while ImageNet-trained CNNs lean on texture (Geirhos and colleagues, 2019), though training on more varied data narrows that gap.
There are also gaps in the neuroscience. The classical simple and complex cell picture was worked out mostly with bars and gratings under anaesthesia. Responses to natural movies are more varied and depend on context, eye movements and attention. The wiring diagram of V1 is still being mapped; large electron-microscopy datasets in mouse visual cortex now allow connections to be counted directly, and they show more recurrent loops than the feedforward story suggests. Also, mice and cats see differently from primates, so which results transfer is a standing question.
For engineers, a practical friction is that the analogy invites wrong confidence. A model that matches IT responses may still fail under a shift of lighting or camera, and "brain-like" is not a quality guarantee.
practice: Use the visual cortex as a source of design questions. When you build or debug a vision model, ask what the first layer learned (it should look like oriented edges and colour blobs; if not, the data or the optimization has a problem), what invariance each pooling or stride step gives, and how large the receptive field of the last layer is. A 3 × 3 layer stack with stride 1 has receptive field 2L + 1 after L layers, so a network that must see a whole object needs depth or downsampling. Visualize filters and activations in the first layers; they are the cheapest sanity check.
For data pipelines in ad tech and streaming, the same facts explain why simple augmentations (small shifts, crops, flips) work: they imitate the invariances that complex cells give for free, and they fail when an invariance is wrong (flipping text, for example). To study the topic, read Hubel's book Eye, Brain and Vision (free online from Harvard), then the Hubel and Wiesel 1962 paper, the CS231n notes on convolutional networks, and Yamins and DiCarlo's 2014 and 2016 papers. A good exercise is to train a small CNN on MNIST or CIFAR-10 and plot its first-layer filters, then compare with Gabor filters.

An edge of the matching orientation excites all of them together and crosses the threshold, while other orientations excite only one or two.

@@ dopamine
value: Dopamine is a chemical released by a small group of neurons deep in the midbrain. For many years it was called the "pleasure chemical", but recordings from single dopamine neurons in the 1990s showed something more precise. These cells do not report reward. They report surprise about reward: they fire when the outcome is better than expected, stay at their baseline rate when it is as expected, and go silent when it is worse. This is called a reward prediction error (RPE).
The same quantity appears in a learning algorithm that computer scientists had invented independently. In temporal-difference (TD) learning, an agent keeps an estimate of how much future reward to expect in each situation and corrects it by the error between what it expected and what it got. The match between the recorded neuron firing and the computed error is so close that it is one of the clearest cases in science where an algorithm and a brain mechanism describe the same thing. For engineers, it explains why TD learning works as a biological idea too, and it gives a vocabulary (value, error, discount, credit assignment) for talking about motivation and habit.
This read stands on two concepts. Neurons (L5) provide the hardware: dopamine neurons send long axons that release the chemical across large areas of the striatum and cortex, where it changes how synapses are strengthened. Markov decision processes and Bellman equations (L4) provide the mathematics: the value of a state equals the immediate reward plus the discounted value of the next state, and the TD error is the amount by which this equation fails to hold on a single observed step. The result is the clearest bridge between the biology of learning and reinforcement learning (L1).
terms: Reward prediction error (RPE) — the difference between the reward received and the reward expected; positive for a better-than-expected outcome, negative for a worse one.
Temporal-difference (TD) error — δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s): the reward just received plus the discounted value of the next state, minus the value that was predicted for the current state.
Value function — V(s), the expected total future reward from state s; it is what the learner tries to estimate.
Discount factor γ — a number between 0 and 1 that makes rewards sooner worth more than rewards later.
Phasic and tonic firing — tonic is the slow baseline rate of 1 to 5 spikes per second; phasic is a short burst of about 100 to 200 ms that carries the signal.
Conditioned stimulus (CS) — a cue, such as a light or tone, that comes to predict a reward after learning.
Striatum — the main input area of the basal ganglia, where dopamine acts on synapses from cortex; it is involved in action selection and habit.
Three-factor rule — a learning rule in which a synapse changes only if the input activity, the output activity and a third signal (dopamine) are all present.
history: In the 1970s and 1980s psychologists had a rule, from Robert Rescorla and Allan Wagner (1972), that explained why a cue gains strength only when it is surprising. Rich Sutton and Andrew Barto developed it into learning algorithms, and Sutton's 1988 paper "Learning to Predict by the Methods of Temporal Differences" gave TD learning its modern form. In 1992 Gerald Tesauro's TD-Gammon learned to play backgammon at a very high level by playing against itself with a TD rule. At the same time, in Fribourg, Switzerland, Wolfram Schultz recorded from dopamine neurons in awake monkeys. In the early 1990s he found that the cells fired when a monkey received juice, but after training the response moved to the cue that predicted the juice.
Read Montague, Peter Dayan and Terrence Sejnowski realized in 1996 that these results were exactly what a TD error would do, and Schultz, Dayan and Montague published "A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward" in Science in 1997. A decade later, Bayer and Glimcher (2005) showed that the firing rate scaled with the size of the error. Reynolds, Hyland and Wickens (2001) showed in rats that reward-linked dopamine release gates the strengthening of cortical inputs to the striatum, which supports the three-factor rule. In 2020 Dabney and colleagues at DeepMind and Harvard reported in Nature that different dopamine neurons act as pessimists and optimists, together coding the whole distribution of possible rewards, as in distributional reinforcement learning, an idea they had developed first as an algorithm. Berridge and Robinson (1998) argued from a different line of evidence that dopamine is better described as "wanting" than as learning, and the debate continues.
how: Dopamine neurons sit in two small clusters in the midbrain, the ventral tegmental area (VTA) and the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), together only about half a million cells in a human brain of 86 billion neurons. Their axons spread widely, so each neuron contacts thousands of targets. This is a broadcast system: one shared message goes to many learners. Neurons (L5) explain why this is possible. Spikes are short and all-or-nothing, so a burst of a few spikes is a clean, brief signal that all recipients receive nearly at the same time.
The experiment has three stages. First, an untrained monkey gets a drop of juice at an unpredicted time. Dopamine neurons burst: the reward was a surprise, δ > 0. Second, a light is shown 1 second before each juice drop. At first the pattern is unchanged, but after many trials the burst at the juice vanishes and a burst appears at the light. Third, after training, the light is shown and no juice comes. At the time the juice was due, the firing rate falls below baseline: δ < 0. In all three the neurons report the error, and the sign and size match the prediction of the TD rule.
Now the algorithm. The learner holds a value estimate V(s) for each state, where a state could be "cue is on, 0.5 s have passed". At each step it observes a reward r and the next state s′ and computes δ = r + γV(s′) − V(s). Then it updates V(s) ← V(s) + αδ with a learning rate α. The Bellman equation (L4) states that the true value satisfies V(s) = E[r + γV(s′)], so the average TD error is zero when V is correct. The TD error is therefore a measure of how wrong the current predictions are, and it can be computed from local information at each time step, with no need to wait for the end of the trial.
This explains why the burst moves backward in time. The cue arrives at an unpredictable time, so there is no earlier state that predicts it. When the cue state first gets a positive value (because it is followed by a rewarded state), the arrival of the cue itself produces a positive error: V(cue) − V(before) > 0. At the same time the reward is now predicted, so at the reward time r − V(state) shrinks to zero. This backward travel of the prediction is called credit assignment through bootstrapping: value propagates one step backward with each trial, from the reward toward earlier cues. The learning of the earliest predictors needs no memory of the whole history, only the value stored in the next state.
How does the brain use the signal? The error is sent to the striatum, where it plays two roles that actor–critic models describe. As the teaching signal for the critic, it updates value estimates in the cortical–striatal synapses. As the teaching signal for the actor, it makes recent actions more likely if δ > 0 and less likely if δ < 0. Because dopamine acts as a third factor, a synapse that was active just before the burst (carrying the "eligibility" of an action or a cue) is strengthened, while those active before a dip are weakened. This is a physical form of the update rule αδ.
The same structure appears in machine learning. TD-Gammon, DQN (2015) and AlphaGo used the TD error or a close relative to train a value function. In the language-model setting, PPO-based RLHF trains a value network with a TD-style loss and uses generalized advantage estimation, which is built from TD errors. So the same idea, at much larger scale, trains game-playing agents and parts of language-model training.

example: Build the smallest case. A trial has three steps: the cue C appears (at an unpredictable time), then a delay state D, then the reward 1 arrives at the end of D. Set γ = 1 and α = 0.5, start with V(C) = V(D) = 0, and keep the value of the waiting period before the cue at 0 (the cue time is random, so nothing earlier predicts it). There are three prediction errors per trial: δ_cue = V(C) − 0, δ_mid = V(D) − V(C), and δ_reward = 1 − V(D).
Trial 1: δ_cue = 0, δ_mid = 0, δ_reward = 1 − 0 = 1. Update V(D) = 0 + 0.5 × 1 = 0.5. The burst is at the reward.
Trial 2: δ_cue = 0. δ_mid = 0.5 − 0 = 0.5, so V(C) becomes 0.25. δ_reward = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5, so V(D) becomes 0.75. The reward burst has halved, and value has moved one step back.
Trial 3: δ_cue = V(C) = 0.25, a first small burst at the cue. δ_mid = 0.75 − 0.25 = 0.5, so V(C) becomes 0.5. δ_reward = 1 − 0.75 = 0.25, so V(D) becomes 0.875.
Trial 5: δ_cue is about 0.69 and δ_reward about 0.06. By trial 10 δ_cue is about 0.98 and δ_reward is 0.002. The burst has moved from the reward to the cue, as in Schultz's recordings. Now omit the reward on a trial. δ_reward = 0 − V(D) = −0.999, so the neuron's rate dips as strongly as it rose at the beginning. If the reward is 2 instead of 1, δ_reward = 2 − 1 = +1: a larger-than-expected reward gives a burst even when the cue is fully learned.
Real data are noisier. The burst is about 100 to 200 ms long, at 20 to 30 spikes per second above a 3 spike-per-second baseline. Because a baseline of 3 spikes per second leaves little room to go lower, the negative error has to be coded by a small drop in rate, which is why the dip is harder to measure. Distributional models explain a part of this: neurons with different "optimism" scale the positive and negative errors unequally, so together they code more than a single average.
intuition: Learning happens when reality surprises you, and the size and direction of the surprise tell you how to correct the forecast.
A learner with a forecast needs only one number to improve it: the gap between what happened and what it expected. If the gap is zero, nothing needs to change, so the system saves effort and avoids unlearning what works. If the gap is large, it should learn fast. A single signal, broadcast to all synapses, can do this because every synapse already knows whether it contributed to the recent activity. This is also why drugs that mimic or block dopamine have such large effects on motivation and on habit: they corrupt the teaching signal used by a whole learning system.
mistakes: People often think dopamine is the pleasure chemical. In fact dopamine neurons respond to the surprise in a reward, not to its enjoyment. A fully expected reward produces no burst, while an unexpected one produces a large one. Liking (the pleasure) seems to rely on other systems, such as opioid and endocannabinoid signals in small hot spots, and Berridge's work separates "wanting" from "liking".
People often think the dopamine-TD match is exact and complete. In fact it is a strong model and not a proof. Dopamine signals also change with movement, novelty, aversive events and effort, and in some tasks dopamine rises slowly as an animal approaches a reward (a ramp), which a simple TD model does not predict (Hamid and colleagues, 2016, argued that the ramp reflects value, while others explain it by state uncertainty). Populations of dopamine neurons are also not identical; some groups respond more to aversive or salient events.
People often think the error signal tells the brain what to do. In fact it is a scalar teaching signal. It says "better" or "worse", not which action or which cue was responsible. Which synapses are changed depends on local activity (the eligibility trace), which carries the "what".
People often think more dopamine always means more learning or happiness. In fact the learning signal needs contrast against a baseline. Raising dopamine all the time, as stimulants do, removes the contrast, and loss of dopamine neurons in Parkinson's disease damages movement and motivation, not only reward.
tradeoffs: Choose the TD / reward-prediction-error model when you want a quantitative account of how cues acquire value over trials and how errors drive learning, as in conditioning experiments and in designing RL agents. It gives specific predictions of timing and sign. It breaks down when the signal also carries motivation, movement or salience, and for slow ramps. For those, use a richer model with state uncertainty or with a separate motivational term.
Choose the older Rescorla–Wagner model when only trial-level results matter (which cue gets credit across trials, blocking), because it is simpler and has one equation. You give up within-trial timing, which is where the TD model shows its advantage: the burst moving from reward to cue within a learned delay.
Choose model-free TD for fast, habitual decisions in a stable environment: it needs no model of the world, and stores only values. Choose model-based methods (planning with a learned model) when the environment changes or when a goal changes quickly: they adapt immediately but cost far more computation. The brain seems to use both (Daw and colleagues, 2005), and the dopamine RPE is the main evidence for the model-free part.
In machine learning, choose TD(0) with a bootstrapped value when you need low variance and online updates. It is biased when the value estimate is wrong. Choose Monte Carlo returns when the episodes are short and you want unbiased estimates, at the cost of high variance. The usual compromise is TD(λ) or generalized advantage estimation, with λ near 0.95. Some recent LLM training methods (such as GRPO) drop the learned value function and use group-based baselines instead, trading the critic's bias and memory cost for more samples per prompt.
Choose a scalar value when the average reward is all you need, and a distributional one when risk matters (for example when the cost of bad outcomes is much larger than the benefit of good ones) or when you want richer representations. Distributional agents such as C51 (Bellemare and colleagues, 2017) improved results on Atari, and the dopamine data suggest the brain does something similar.
friction: The theory has weak points on the biology side. Dopamine neurons are diverse in their projections and responses, so "the dopamine signal" is not a single thing. Measurements in the striatum show release patterns that depend on local circuits and can differ from the firing of the cells that supply them. The ramp, the response to aversive events, and the role of dopamine in movement (as seen in Parkinson's disease) are not simply explained. The TD error also needs a good state representation and a clock, because it must know "what time since the cue it is". How the brain builds these is open.
On the algorithm side, TD with function approximation can diverge when combined with off-policy learning and bootstrapping (the "deadly triad" of Sutton and Barto), which is why stable deep RL needed target networks and replay buffers. Credit assignment over long delays is hard for both systems: a bonus a week later is poorly linked to the action that caused it, and this is a limit for brains and for agents.
There is also an application risk. Systems that optimize an engagement reward can exploit the same learning machinery in people, producing habits that are strong and not necessarily helpful. The structure of variable, surprising rewards is exactly what produces large errors. This is a design concern for recommendation and ad products more than a scientific limit.
practice: When you design or debug a reinforcement-learning system, use the dopamine picture as a check on the learning signal. Plot the TD error over training: it should shrink and move earlier in the episode as the value function improves. If it stays large and noisy, the value function is not learning (check the learning rate, the reward scale and the state features). If it goes to zero too early, the critic may have collapsed or the rewards may be too sparse.
Normalize rewards or advantages, since the size of the error sets the size of every update. Choose γ to match the real horizon: with γ = 0.99 the effective horizon is about 100 steps. Use eligibility traces or λ-returns for delayed rewards. For recommender and bidding systems that use bandit or RL methods, remember that the learner is shaped by what it is rewarded for; sparse and delayed outcomes (a purchase a week after an ad) need careful credit assignment or a proxy reward, and proxy rewards can be gamed.
To study this, read Sutton and Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, chapter 15 on neuroscience (free online). Then read Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997), which is short, and the Dabney et al. (2020) paper. Implement the example above with a table of 20 time steps and watch the error move. Wolfram Schultz's review articles give the experimental background, and Berridge's work on wanting and liking gives the main alternative view.

@@ evolution
value: Evolution by natural selection explains how complex, well-fitted living things arise without a designer. It needs only three conditions: members of a population differ from each other, some of these differences are passed to offspring, and some differences make an individual more likely to leave offspring. If all three hold, the population changes over generations toward forms that reproduce better in their environment. No plan, no goal and no foresight are needed. The same loop of vary, select and copy has produced bacteria that survive antibiotics, wings, eyes and the immune system.
For computing, evolution is the model of an optimizer that uses no gradient and no model of the problem. It only needs a way to copy things with small random changes and a way to measure how well each copy does. This is why it is the basis of genetic algorithms and evolution strategies (L2), why it is the main example for the idea of optimization (L4) without a designer, and why evolutionary game theory (L4) can predict which strategies survive in a population. It is also a useful mental model for product development: ship variants, measure, keep what wins, repeat. But it also teaches a warning: selection optimizes what is measured, not what the designer hoped for.
This read stands on DNA as information (L5). Evolution needs a heredity system that copies accurately but not perfectly. DNA gives both: a copying process with an error rate near one change in 100 million letters per generation in humans, which keeps useful designs stable, and rare mistakes (mutations), which supply new variation. Without a digital, copyable code, offspring would blend the traits of their parents and variation would be lost. Evolution in turn stands under immune systems, homeostasis, genetic algorithms, and the optimization and game-theory ideas that borrow from it.
terms: Variation — differences between individuals in a population, caused by mutation and by the shuffling of genes in sexual reproduction.
Heritability — the share of the differences in a trait that is passed from parents to offspring through genes.
Fitness — how many offspring (that survive to reproduce) an individual leaves compared with others; it is relative, not absolute.
Selection coefficient s — the advantage of a variant: if the normal type has fitness 1, the variant has fitness 1 + s.
Genetic drift — random changes in how common a variant is, caused by chance in who reproduces; strongest in small populations.
Fitness landscape — a picture in which each genotype has a height equal to its fitness; evolution moves uphill, with some random wandering.
Epistasis — the effect of one gene depends on which other genes are present, which makes the landscape rough.
Neutral mutation — a change that does not affect fitness; most of the changes in a genome are neutral or nearly so.
history: Charles Darwin developed the idea of natural selection from the 1830s, after his voyage on the Beagle, and from breeders' experience with pigeons and livestock. In 1858 he received a letter from Alfred Russel Wallace, who had reached the same idea independently in the Malay Archipelago. Papers by both men were read together at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858, and Darwin published On the Origin of Species in November 1859. One weakness remained: Darwin had no sound account of heredity. Mendel's pea experiments (1866) showed that traits are passed as separate units, but they were ignored until 1900.
In the 1920s and 1930s Ronald Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane and Sewall Wright built population genetics, which showed with mathematics that Mendelian inheritance and natural selection fit together. Dobzhansky, Mayr, Simpson and Julian Huxley joined the fields into the modern synthesis, named in Huxley's 1942 book. Watson and Crick's structure of DNA (1953) gave the physical basis of heredity (the DNA read). Motoo Kimura's neutral theory (1968) argued that most molecular change comes from drift, not selection. George Price's equation (1970) wrote the whole process as one line of algebra.
Evolution entered computing early. John Holland developed genetic algorithms in the 1960s and described them in Adaptation in Natural and Artificial Systems (1975). Ingo Rechenberg and Hans-Paul Schwefel in Germany created evolution strategies at about the same time, and John Koza added genetic programming (1992). Richard Lenski started a long experiment in 1988 with twelve populations of E. coli, grown daily, which has run for tens of thousands of generations and has shown evolution in real time, including the appearance of a new ability to use citrate around generation 31,000. More recently, evolution-style search appears in neural architecture search (Real and colleagues, 2019) and in LLM-guided program search such as AlphaEvolve (2025).
how: Start with the loop. A population holds many individuals, each with a genotype (its DNA) and the phenotype that results (its body and behaviour). In each generation, individuals survive and reproduce at different rates because of their phenotype in the current environment. Offspring copy the parents' DNA. Copying introduces small random changes (mutations), and in sexual species the genes of two parents are shuffled (recombination). Over time, the variants that give more offspring become more common. Richard Lewontin's summary (1970) is the three conditions in the value paragraph, and any system that has them, in biology or software, evolves.
The mathematics of one gene is short. Let a variant have frequency p and fitness 1 + s, and the old type fitness 1. After one generation, the new frequency is p′ = p(1 + s) / (1 + sp). The odds p/(1 − p) are multiplied by (1 + s) every generation. This means that the log odds grow in a straight line, with slope about ln(1 + s) ≈ s. A variant with a 10% advantage takes the same number of generations to go from 1% to 50% as from 50% to 99%. Selection is fast for large advantages, and slow for small ones, and in both cases it is exponential growth in the odds.
Chance competes with selection. A new mutation starts as one copy among 2N copies in a diploid population of size N, so if it is neutral it has probability 1/(2N) of eventually taking over the population. A beneficial one with advantage s takes over with probability of about 2s (Haldane, 1927), which means that even a 1% advantage is lost by chance 98% of the time. Selection beats drift only when the product of s and the effective population size is much larger than 1 (Kimura). That is why small populations accumulate mildly harmful changes, and why big populations adapt more reliably.
For traits controlled by many genes, breeders use a simple formula: the response to selection is R = h² × S, where S is the difference between the selected parents and the population mean and h² is the heritability. If parents are 10 cm taller than the average and h² = 0.5, the next generation moves up by 5 cm. This is the equation behind both livestock breeding and the engineering rule that the speed of a genetic algorithm depends on selection strength and on inherited variation.
Here DNA (L5) supplies the specifics. Mutation rates are measured: roughly 1.2 × 10⁻⁸ per base per generation in humans, which makes about 70 new point mutations in each child's genome of 6 billion bases, and about 0.003 new mutations per genome per replication in many microbes (Drake's rule). Most are neutral, a few are harmful, and very few help. Recombination mixes variants found in different individuals, so two separate good mutations can meet in one descendant. Without it, in asexual populations, they must arise one after another in the same lineage (clonal interference slows adaptation).
The Price equation puts all of this in one line. If z is a trait value, w is fitness and w̄ is the average fitness, the change in the average trait per generation is Δz̄ = Cov(w, z) / w̄ + E(w × Δz) / w̄. The first term is selection: the covariance between fitness and the trait. The second term is transmission: how much offspring differ from their parents because of mutation, recombination or a changing environment. The equation is exact and needs no assumption about genes, so the same bookkeeping can be applied to cells, animals, firms or software variants, as long as the three conditions hold. It also shows why selection can only act on variation that already exists: if the covariance is zero, the first term is zero.
The landscape picture shows the limits. Selection pushes uphill locally, so a population can get stuck on a small peak. But it is not purely greedy: drift lets small populations wander across shallow valleys, recombination can jump across them, and neutral mutations form networks of equally fit genotypes that allow slow exploration. Environments also change, and other species evolve in response (the Red Queen effect), so the landscape moves while the population climbs it.

example: Take a variant that gives a 10% advantage (s = 0.10) in a population where it begins at frequency p = 0.01 (1%). Use the rule p′ = p × 1.1 / (1 + 0.1 × p). Generation 1: p′ = 0.01 × 1.1 / 1.001 = 0.01099. After 10 generations the frequency is 0.0255, after 25 it is 0.099, after 50 it is 0.543, after 75 it is 0.928 and after 100 it is 0.993. Check with the odds: initial odds are 0.01/0.99 = 0.0101. After 50 generations the odds are 0.0101 × 1.1⁵⁰ = 0.0101 × 117.4 = 1.19, so p = 1.19 / 2.19 = 0.543. The time from 1% to 99% is 2 × ln(99) / ln(1.1) = 2 × 4.595 / 0.0953 = 96 generations. If s were 0.01, it would take about 960 generations, ten times longer.
Now include chance. The variant started as a new mutation in a population of N = 10,000 diploid individuals. Its initial frequency is 1/20,000 = 0.00005, and its chance of surviving the early random loss is about 2s = 0.2 for s = 0.1. So about 4 out of 5 times the beneficial mutation is lost within a few generations, even though it is a strong one. If s = 0.01, the chance is 2%, and you need on average 50 independent appearances before one succeeds. In this population mutations appear at a rate of about 2N × 10⁻⁸ per site per generation = 0.0002 per site, so a specific base is hit about once every 5,000 generations. The wait for the right mutation is often longer than the sweep.
Now the engineering side with a toy genetic algorithm. Maximize the number of 1s in a 20-bit string (the OneMax problem). Population 100, random start: the average has 10 ones. Tournament selection of size 2 picks, between two random individuals, the one with more 1s. Each bit flips with probability 1/20 = 0.05 in the offspring. In a simulation of this setup, the best individual reached all 1s after 23 generations on average (between 2 and 45 in 50 runs), while the population average stayed near 16 or 17, because the mutation keeps breaking good strings. Mutation of 1/L per bit is a rule of thumb: it yields one flip per offspring on average. At 0.5 it is random search, and at 0 nothing new appears. The same three numbers (selection strength, mutation rate, population size) govern both nature and the algorithm.
intuition: Try many small variations at once, throw away the worse ones, copy the better ones with small changes, and repeat for a very long time.
The idea seems paradoxical because design usually needs a designer, but the loop needs only a small ingredient: a copying process that is good and not perfect. Once something copies itself with errors and has to compete for limited resources, the copies that happen to do better become more common. After many rounds, the survivors look as if they were planned, because the filter keeps what works and the huge number of trials (billions of cells, billions of years) supplies the rest. It resembles a search that uses a vast number of cheap experiments instead of intelligence.
mistakes: People often think evolution is "survival of the fittest" in the sense of strongest or best. In fact fitness is only the relative number of offspring in the current environment. A trait can win by being cheap, by being good at attracting mates, or by being lucky, and it can lose when the environment changes.
People often think evolution has a direction or a goal, such as increasing complexity. In fact it has no goal. Populations adapt to the present environment, and they often become simpler, as with parasites that lose organs. Complexity increases in some lineages because there is room to increase, not because there is a drive to do so.
People often think natural selection is the only force. In fact drift, mutation, gene flow (migration) and recombination also change populations. In small populations drift can outweigh selection, and Kimura's neutral theory holds that most differences between species' DNA are neutral.
People often think evolution is only random chance. In fact mutation is random with respect to need, but selection is not random: it consistently favours variants that leave more copies. The combination of random variation and non-random filtering is what makes the process produce structure.
tradeoffs: Choose an evolutionary method (genetic algorithm, evolution strategy, genetic programming) when the objective is a black box, non-differentiable, discrete or noisy, when you can run many evaluations in parallel, or when you want a set of diverse good solutions (multi-objective search such as NSGA-II). Examples are scheduling, circuit layout, hyperparameter and architecture search, and policy search where only a final score is available. You pay in evaluations: a gradient costs one backward pass, while a gradient estimate from random perturbations needs on the order of one sample per dimension. OpenAI's evolution-strategies work (2017) matched RL on some tasks with thousands of parallel workers, but it does not scale to billions of parameters on one problem as gradient descent does.
Choose gradient-based optimization when the objective is differentiable and evaluation is cheap: training neural networks with 10⁹ or more parameters is only feasible this way. Choose a hybrid when part of the problem is discrete: evolve architecture or program structure, and train the weights with gradients, as in AmoebaNet and in recent LLM-plus-evolution program search, where the language model proposes the mutations.
Choose the mutation rate and population size by the problem. About 1/L mutations per offspring for a string of length L is a safe start. Raise the rate when the population converges too early (loss of diversity), and lower it when the best solutions keep getting destroyed. A large population gives diversity and robustness against drift; a small one is cheap but gets stuck. Choose strong selection (small tournaments are mild, truncation to the top 10% is harsh) based on how rough the landscape is: harsh selection converges faster and gets trapped sooner.
Choose the biological model carefully when you use it as an analogy. Gene-level models (Price equation, population genetics) predict how frequencies change, and they work for simple traits and short times. They break down for traits with strong epistasis and for changing environments. For product development, A/B testing is selection with foresight: you pick variants on purpose, and you can stop the process. Real evolution cannot do that, so the analogy fails exactly where intent matters. No Free Lunch results (Wolpert and Macready, 1997) also say that no search method, evolution included, is better than another on all problems; its advantage comes from matching the structure of the problem.
friction: Evolution is slow and wasteful: most variants die, and the cost of an adaptation is paid in lost lives. It is also short-sighted. It cannot plan a multi-step path through a valley of lower fitness unless drift carries a population across, and it can only modify what already exists, which is why the vertebrate eye has a blind spot and the laryngeal nerve takes a long detour around the heart in giraffes. Designs are patched, not rewritten.
It optimizes what is rewarded and nothing else. The result is often a surprising solution that satisfies the measure and misses the intent: digital evolution experiments have produced organisms that exploit simulator bugs, and in the same way reinforcement learning agents find loopholes in reward functions. This parallel with reward hacking in AI is the central lesson for people who set targets. Selection also acts on short timescales: bacteria that cause infections adapt to antibiotics because a population of 10⁹ cells with a resistance mutation rate of about 10⁻⁹ per cell contains roughly one resistant cell at the start of treatment, and the drug then selects for it.
Finally, our knowledge has gaps. The origin of life, the exact role of drift and selection at the molecular level, and the pace of large innovations are active areas. Modelling real evolution over long periods is hard because of epistasis and the environment that moves.
practice: When you apply evolutionary ideas in engineering, start with the three conditions and ask which one is missing. For a genetic algorithm: define a genotype encoding that makes small changes produce small changes in behaviour (otherwise mutation is just noise), a fitness function that is cheap and not noisy, and operators that keep diversity. Use a population of 50 to 1,000, tournament selection, mutation at about 1/L per gene, and elitism (keep the best few unchanged). Track both best and mean fitness, and track diversity; if diversity collapses, the search has converged, possibly on a poor peak.
In practice, run several restarts, use multiple objectives where trade-offs are real, and keep a validation set separate from the fitness set, because evolution overfits the fitness function very efficiently. For ad tech and product work, treat each experiment as a mutation and your metric as the fitness: choose metrics that cannot be gamed cheaply (Goodhart's law), run experiments for long enough to see real effects, and watch the long-term metrics, not only the metric that is selected.

value: DNA is a molecule that stores information. It is a long chain of four chemical letters (A, C, G and T), which can be read as 2 bits each, and every cell in your body carries a copy of the same text of about 3.1 billion letters. The molecule has the three properties that any good storage system needs: it can be copied accurately, it can be read by other machines in the cell, and it can be repaired when damaged. All three are done by molecular machines that follow chemistry, with no central controller.
For computing, DNA is the existence proof that information can be stored, copied and error-corrected at the molecular scale. A human genome is about 775 MB if you write each base as 2 bits, and it lives in a nucleus a few micrometres wide. A cell copies this text in hours with roughly one new error per billion letters copied. The same chemistry gives engineers a storage medium that is dense (hundreds of exabytes per gram in theory), stable for thousands of years if kept cool and dry, and unaffected by changes in file formats, since reading DNA will always be a goal of biology.
This read stands on "information is physical" (L5): a bit must be stored in some physical state, and copying and erasing bits costs energy that has a lower bound. DNA shows this directly. The letters are the state of a chain of molecules, copying uses real chemical energy, and a copy that is nearly free of error can be made only by spending extra energy on proofreading. Evolution (L5) is built on top of DNA: natural selection needs a heredity system that copies with rare mistakes, and DNA is that system.
terms: Base (nucleotide) — one letter of the code: adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G) or thymine (T), attached to a sugar and phosphate backbone.
Base pairing — A binds only to T and C only to G, by hydrogen bonds; so the two strands of the double helix are complements of each other.
Gene — a stretch of DNA that is read to make a functional product, usually a protein; humans have about 20,000 protein-coding genes covering about 1.5% of the genome.
Codon — a group of three bases that specifies one amino acid (or a stop); there are 64 codons for 20 amino acids plus stop signals.
Transcription and translation — transcription copies a gene into messenger RNA (mRNA); translation reads the mRNA on a ribosome and builds a protein.
DNA polymerase — the enzyme that copies DNA by adding the matching base to a growing strand, with a built-in proofreading step.
Mutation — a change in the DNA sequence that is not repaired, such as a substituted, inserted or deleted base.
Sequencing — reading the order of bases in a DNA sample; synthesis is the opposite, writing a chosen sequence chemically.
history: In 1944 Oswald Avery, Colin MacLeod and Maclyn McCarty showed that DNA, and not protein, carried the information that changed one bacterial strain into another. Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase confirmed it in 1952 with viruses. Erwin Chargaff had found in 1950 that in every species A equals T and G equals C in amount. Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling's X-ray photographs, together with Maurice Wilkins's work, showed the helix, and in April 1953 James Watson and Francis Crick published the two-stranded structure in Nature. They ended the paper with the remark that the pairing "immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material".
Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl showed in 1958 that each new double helix keeps one old strand and one new one (semi-conservative copying). Crick stated the "central dogma" the same year: information flows from DNA to RNA to protein. Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei broke the first codon in 1961, and by the mid-1960s the whole genetic code was known, with contributions from Har Gobind Khorana and others. Frederick Sanger invented a practical method of reading DNA in 1977. The Human Genome Project produced a first draft in 2001, and in 2022 the Telomere-to-Telomere consortium published the first complete human genome sequence, 3.055 billion base pairs. Reading a genome has gone from about 100 million dollars in 2001 to a few hundred dollars today.
DNA as a storage medium started with Church, Gao and Kosuri, who in 2012 stored a 5.27-megabit book. Goldman and colleagues at the European Bioinformatics Institute stored 739 kilobytes in 2013 with error-correcting redundancy. Erlich and Zielinski (2017) used a fountain code (DNA Fountain) to store about 2 MB at 1.6 bits per base and recovered it perfectly. In 2018 a Microsoft and University of Washington team stored 200 MB and retrieved single files by random access, and automated machines for writing and reading followed.
how: The text is organized as a double helix. Each strand is a chain of nucleotides joined by a sugar-phosphate backbone, and it has a direction (5′ to 3′). The two strands run in opposite directions and are held by base pairing: A with T by two hydrogen bonds, C with G by three. This means that either strand contains the full information of the other. If you know one strand, the other is determined, which is a built-in redundancy and the key to copying.
The geometry sets the density. The helix is about 2 nanometres wide, with 0.34 nanometres between neighbouring bases and about 10.5 base pairs per turn. A diploid human cell has 6.2 billion base pairs, which is about 2 metres of molecule. It fits in a nucleus about 6 micrometres wide because it is wrapped around protein spools (histones): each nucleosome holds about 146 base pairs wound around eight histone proteins, and the chain of nucleosomes is folded again and again. Packaging also decides which parts can be read, since tightly wound DNA is hard for the reading machinery to reach.
Copying (replication) goes as follows. A helicase enzyme unzips the double helix at a replication fork. DNA polymerase then reads each old strand as a template and adds nucleotides, choosing at each step the one that pairs with the template base. It adds about 1,000 bases per second in a bacterium and about 50 per second in a human cell. The E. coli chromosome of 4.6 million bases has two forks moving in opposite directions, so it takes about 4.6 million / 2,000 ≈ 2,300 seconds, about 40 minutes, which matches the measured copying time. Human chromosomes are copied from tens of thousands of starting points at once.
The accuracy comes in layers. The first step, choosing the right base by shape and bonding, makes a mistake about once in 100,000 bases. The polymerase itself checks the last base added and removes it if wrong (proofreading), which improves accuracy by about 100 times. After copying, a mismatch repair system scans the new strand and fixes remaining errors, another 100 times or more. The final error rate is on the order of one in a billion to one in ten billion per base copied. A diploid human cell with 6.2 billion bases therefore makes only a few errors at each division. This is classic layered error control: a cheap first pass, a local check, and a global repair, each multiplying the reliability. Here the physics matters. Each added base costs about two ATP-equivalents, roughly 40 times the thermal energy kT, and proofreading costs more, while the minimum energy to write the 2 bits is about 1.4 kT. Reliability is bought with energy, as the physics of information predicts.
Reading the text uses a second code. A gene is transcribed by RNA polymerase into mRNA at about 50 bases per second. A ribosome reads the mRNA three bases at a time. Each codon is matched by a transfer RNA carrying an amino acid, and the ribosome joins the amino acids into a protein, adding about 20 per second in bacteria. There are 64 codons and only 20 amino acids plus stop, so the code is redundant (degenerate). Similar codons tend to encode similar amino acids, and the third base often does not matter, so many single-letter mutations are silent or mild. The code works like an error-tolerant encoding.
The raw count of 2 bits per base overstates the real information. Repeated sequences make up about half of the human genome, so a good compressor reaches roughly 1.6 to 1.9 bits per base. Only about 1.5% codes for protein, and much more is involved in controlling when and where genes are used. Cells also add a layer of chemical marks (methylation) and a 3D folding of the chromosome. The text is read in context, and the same text produces different cell types. DNA is a data store with a regulatory system around it, not a program that runs by itself.

example: Store the text "Hi" in DNA. Use a simple mapping: A = 00, C = 01, G = 10, T = 11. The letter H is the byte 01001000, which splits into 01 00 10 00, so it becomes C A G A. The letter i is 01101001, which splits into 01 10 10 01, so it becomes C G G C. The whole message is CAGACGGC, eight bases for two bytes.
Now read the other strand. The complement of CAGACGGC is GTCTGCCG. Because strands run in opposite directions, if you read the partner strand from its own 5′ end you get the reverse: GCCGTCTG. Either strand carries the message, so a sequencing machine can read whichever one is more convenient.
Real storage must avoid long runs of one base (such as AAAAAA), which are hard to synthesize and sequence, and keep the share of G and C near 50%. A constrained code that forbids runs longer than 3 uses about 1.6 to 1.9 bits per base instead of 2. Redundancy is added on top, because synthesis and sequencing make an error roughly once in a hundred to a thousand bases. DNA Fountain stores each piece of data in many overlapping short strands of 200 bases, any sufficient subset of which can rebuild the file. With about 1.6 bits per base and a copy count high enough to survive reading, Erlich and Zielinski reported 215 petabytes per gram.
Check the capacity yourself. An average nucleotide has a mass of about 330 g per mole. One gram holds 6.022 × 10²³ / 330 = 1.8 × 10²¹ nucleotides. At 2 bits each, that is 3.6 × 10²¹ bits, or 4.5 × 10²⁰ bytes, which is about 450 exabytes per gram for a single copy of each molecule. The reported 215 petabytes per gram is about 2,000 times lower because the data are stored with redundancy and many copies of each strand.
Last, size the genome. 3.1 billion bases × 2 bits = 6.2 billion bits = 775 MB. A person has two copies of each chromosome, so 1.5 GB per cell, but the two copies are about 99.9% identical, which is why the difference between two people can be written in about 4 to 5 million variants. The text is small by modern standards: it fits on a CD.
intuition: A self-copying tape written in a four-letter alphabet, where each letter carries its own complement, so that copying means pairing and the tape repairs itself against its mirror image.
The design is natural because the structure holds its own backup. A single strand is a template for the second, so copying is a chemical reflex, not an operation that needs a separate controller. Two strands also give an error check: a mismatch between them is visible as a shape defect, which repair enzymes can find. Digital coding is also what lets heredity work. Because the alphabet is discrete, small copying errors are either corrected or become a clean new letter, and not a blur. This is the reason a signal can be copied for billions of years without degrading in the way that an analog photocopy does.
mistakes: People often think DNA is a program. In fact it is closer to a parts list with regulation: the sequence says which proteins and RNAs can be made, and a network of regulators decides when. The same genome yields a neuron or a liver cell. There is no clean modular code with function calls, and the result of a change depends on the context.
People often think most of the genome is "junk". In fact only about 1.5% codes for protein, but a large part has roles in regulation, in RNA genes and in structure, and the share of the genome that is functional is still debated, estimates running from about 8% to more than 50% depending on the definition. The honest answer is that a large part is of unknown function.
People often think DNA copying is perfect. In fact it makes mistakes at roughly one per billion bases, and that is what gives evolution its raw material. Both cancer and evolution come from the errors that escape repair.
People often think DNA storage will soon replace disks and tape. In fact writing is slow and expensive and reading needs a sequencer, so the access time is hours or days. It is a candidate for cold archives that are written once and read rarely.
tradeoffs: Choose DNA for storage only for very cold, long-lived archives: data written once, kept for decades to centuries, and read rarely. Density is extreme (up to hundreds of exabytes per gram in theory), and the copies cost almost nothing to keep: no power, small space. Choose tape or disk for everything else: tape stores 18 TB per cartridge natively (LTO-9), writes at hundreds of MB per second and costs a small fraction of a cent per GB. DNA writing is slower by many orders of magnitude and costs many orders of magnitude more per byte, and reading needs sequencing. You give up access speed and cost to gain density and longevity (DNA from a bone has a half-life of about 500 years at 13 °C, and fragments over a million years old have been read from frozen sediment).
Choose a constrained code (no long runs, balanced G and C, about 1.6 to 1.9 bits per base) when you must write with real chemistry, and the raw 2 bits per base only for a teaching example. Choose the amount of redundancy by the error rate: more strands per file and an outer error-correcting code (Reed–Solomon, fountain code) reduce the chance of loss, at a cost per byte stored. Choose short-read sequencing (Illumina, about 150 bases, error around 0.1%) when you need cheap, accurate reads of many short strands, as in DNA storage. Choose long-read sequencing (nanopore, PacBio HiFi) for assembling genomes and repeats, where long contiguous reads of 10,000 bases or more matter more than the cost per base.
Choose the information view of DNA to count, compare and compress sequences, and to design codes. It breaks down for questions of function: the meaning of a sequence depends on the cell, the neighbouring regions, the epigenetic state and the environment. For those questions, use models of regulation and structure, or learned models of sequence function, and not Shannon entropy alone.
friction: Writing DNA is the bottleneck. Chemical synthesis adds one base at a time, with about 99% to 99.9% success per step, so strands are limited to a few hundred bases, and the cost per base is far above the cost of the same bits on any other medium. Reading costs far less but requires a sequencer, sample preparation and hours. Random access means using a primer to pull out a file from a mixed pool, which wastes material. Errors of three types, substitution, insertion and deletion, must all be handled, which is harder than the substitution errors typical of disk.
For biology, the friction is about meaning. We can read a whole genome for a few hundred dollars and cannot predict, from the sequence, the effect of most changes. Regulation, three-dimensional folding, and interaction with the environment make the map from sequence to organism hard to compute. Deep-learning models predict some properties (protein structure, some regulatory effects), but they are far from a full simulation.
Editing is possible (CRISPR, base editors), and brings safety and ethical limits for use in people. DNA is also personal data: a genome identifies a person and their relatives, which makes storage and sharing a privacy problem, a real concern for any ad-tech or health-data engineer.
practice: Where does an engineer meet DNA? In bioinformatics and health data pipelines, where files such as FASTQ (reads with quality scores), BAM and VCF (variants) are large and the toolchain is based on strings, indexes and probabilistic data structures. A raw 30× human genome is about 100 GB of reads, so storage, compression and indexing are real problems. The same ideas appear throughout computing: error-correcting codes, layered checks, content-addressed copying, and string matching with suffix arrays and the Burrows–Wheeler transform, which is used in the standard read aligners.
If you work on DNA storage, design from the error model of your synthesis and sequencing machines; simulate substitution, insertion and deletion errors; use an inner code for the strand and an outer code across strands; and measure the physical coverage needed to read each file. If you work with genomic data, treat it as sensitive personal information from the start.
To learn the biology well, read the first chapters of Molecular Biology of the Cell (Alberts and colleagues) on DNA, replication and the genetic code, and Watson and Crick's one-page 1953 paper. For the information view, read Cover and Thomas on entropy and coding, and the DNA Fountain paper. A good exercise is to write a short program that encodes a file as bases with a no-long-run rule, adds random errors, and decodes it again.

value: The immune system defends the body against bacteria, viruses, parasites and cancer cells, and it has a problem that every security engineer knows: it must catch attackers it has never seen without attacking the body it protects. It solves this in two layers. A fast, general layer (innate immunity) reacts within minutes to patterns common to many microbes. A slower, learning layer (adaptive immunity) builds a custom detector for each new threat in days, and keeps a memory of it, sometimes for decades. The second layer is the one that inspired computer science.
The adaptive layer works by a method that looks like an algorithm. The body makes an enormous number of random detectors, removes the ones that react to the body's own molecules (negative selection), and multiplies the ones that match an invader (clonal selection) while mutating them to improve the match. Memory cells keep the best detectors ready, which is the basis of vaccines. For the layer above, security architecture (L1), the key ideas are anomaly detection learned from "self" only, distributed detection with no central controller, and memory of past attacks. Forrest and colleagues turned the first idea into an algorithm in 1994.
This read stands on evolution (L5). The immune response is Darwinian evolution running inside one body, in days and not millennia: a population of cells with random variation, selection for fit to the invader, and inheritance when a cell divides. The immune system itself is a product of evolution over hundreds of millions of years, shaped by an arms race with pathogens. Both views are needed: selection acts on the species to build the machinery, and the machinery uses selection in each individual to adapt to threats no species ever met.
terms: Antigen — a molecule, or part of one, that the immune system can recognize; often a piece of a protein from a microbe.
Antibody — a Y-shaped protein made by B cells that binds a specific antigen; its binding site is the detector.
T cell receptor (TCR) — the detector on a T cell; it recognizes a small peptide shown on the surface of another cell.
Self and non-self — the body's own molecules, and everything else; the discrimination between them is the central task.
Negative selection — removal of developing immune cells whose receptors bind strongly to the body's own molecules; it happens mostly in the thymus for T cells.
Clonal selection — when a cell's receptor matches an antigen, the cell divides, and its copies (a clone) become the response and the memory.
Somatic hypermutation — a very high mutation rate in the antibody genes of dividing B cells, followed by selection of the best binders (affinity maturation).
MHC (HLA in humans) — molecules that display peptides from inside a cell on its surface so that T cells can inspect them.
history: Edward Jenner showed in 1796 that exposure to cowpox protected against smallpox, and Louis Pasteur and Robert Koch developed vaccines and the germ theory in the following decades. For the mechanism, scientists first guessed that antibodies were moulded around the antigen. In 1955 Niels Jerne, and in 1957–1959 Macfarlane Burnet and David Talmage, proposed the opposite and correct idea: the body already has many different antibodies before any infection, and the antigen selects the matching cells. This is the clonal selection theory, a direct use of Darwin's logic. Peter Medawar's 1953 experiments on tolerance showed that the system learns what is self during development.
How could a body with about 20,000 genes make millions of different antibodies? Susumu Tonegawa showed in 1976–1987 that antibody genes are assembled from a library of gene segments, cut and joined at random in each developing B cell (V(D)J recombination). He received the 1987 Nobel Prize. In 1989 Charles Janeway proposed that innate immune cells carry receptors for common microbial patterns, which was confirmed by the discovery of Toll-like receptors, honoured with the 2011 Nobel Prize. Polly Matzinger's "danger model" (1994) argued that the system responds to signs of damage and not to foreignness alone.
Computer science joined in the 1990s. Stephanie Forrest, Alan Perelson, Lawrence Allen and Rajesh Cherukuri published "Self-Nonself Discrimination in a Computer" in 1994, applying negative selection to detect changes in files and computer viruses. Hofmeyr and Forrest (1999–2000) built an intrusion detection system for network connections on the same principle. In 2002 de Castro and Von Zuben proposed clonal-selection algorithms for optimization, and Aickelin and Cayzer applied danger theory. In 2005 Stibor and colleagues showed that negative selection scales badly in high dimensions, which explains why it has not replaced ordinary machine learning.
how: Start with the detectors. A B cell or T cell carries receptors of one specific shape. During development, the receptor gene is built from segments chosen at random. For antibody heavy chains there are about 40 V segments, 25 D segments and 6 J segments, giving about 6,000 heavy chains; with about 300 light-chain options that is on the order of 2 million combinations. At each join, enzymes delete and add random nucleotides, which multiplies the diversity by thousands more. The body contains on the order of 10¹² lymphocytes, each with its own receptor, so it holds a huge random library of detectors, far larger than any list of known pathogens. This is the first use of evolution (L5): variation is created before any selection is applied.
Negative selection comes next. A young T cell is tested in the thymus against the body's own peptides, including tissue proteins that a special transcription factor (AIRE) causes the thymus to display. Cells that bind self too strongly are told to die. Cells that cannot recognize the body's own MHC molecules at all also die. Over 90% of developing T cells (figures of 95% or more are often quoted) do not pass. The survivors have been trained on "what normal looks like" and learn nothing about the attackers. Their reaction to anything else is, by construction, a sign of something abnormal. This is exactly the one-class anomaly detection that is described in the 1994 algorithm.
Detection happens in the tissues. Cells display samples of their inner proteins on MHC molecules. A virus-infected cell shows viral peptides on its surface, and a T cell whose receptor fits one of them becomes activated. Activation needs a second signal from the innate system (costimulation), usually produced by signs of infection or damage. This second check reduces false alarms: a self-reactive cell that escaped the thymus is not activated without evidence of danger. Innate cells (macrophages, dendritic cells) supply this evidence through receptors for microbial patterns such as bacterial cell-wall pieces and viral RNA, so the two layers cooperate.
Clonal selection and mutation follow. The activated cell divides every 6 to 12 hours, and within a week one cell can give a clone of many thousands. For B cells in a germinal centre, the antibody genes mutate at about one change per thousand bases per division (somatic hypermutation, driven by an enzyme called AID), which is about a million times the normal mutation rate of the genome. Cells whose antibodies bind the antigen better receive survival signals and divide more; the others die. Over weeks this affinity maturation raises binding strength by 100 times or more. This is a genetic algorithm with a population of cells, a mutation operator and a fitness test, running on a time scale of days.
The two layers differ in speed and in the cost of a mistake. The innate layer is always on. Neutrophils reach an infected tissue within hours, the complement proteins in blood punch holes in bacterial membranes within minutes, and natural killer cells destroy cells that have lost their MHC display, which is a trick many viruses use to hide. This layer has a fixed set of detectors, roughly a few dozen receptor types for patterns that microbes have and the body does not, such as bacterial flagellin and double-stranded viral RNA. Evolution built these detectors over hundreds of millions of years, so they work on the first day. The adaptive layer needs about a week to build a response to a new threat, which is why the innate layer must hold the line meanwhile and why the two have to talk to each other.
Memory is the last stage. After the infection, most of the clone dies, but a few memory cells remain, and they react faster and more strongly on the next exposure: days instead of a week or two. Vaccines train memory without the disease. Memory against smallpox vaccination can last for decades. The system as a whole has no central controller: signalling molecules (cytokines) and local cell contacts coordinate it, which makes it resilient to damage in one place.

example: Run the 1994 algorithm on a toy system. Strings are 6 bits long. Two strings match if they agree in at least r = 4 contiguous positions. The "self" set, the normal behaviour, is six strings: 000000, 000111, 001110, 011100, 111000 and 110001. A random detector is accepted only if it matches none of them.
Test three candidates. Candidate 000100 compared with 000111: the first four bits are 0001 in both, so they match in a run of four, and the candidate is deleted. Candidate 010010 compared with all six self strings has no run of four equal bits with any of them, so it survives. Candidate 001001 also survives. Doing this for all 64 possible strings, 22 detectors survive.
Now monitor. Take a new string, 010101, which is not in the self set. Detector 010100 shares the first five bits with it, so the match fires and the string is flagged. In total there are 58 strings that are not self, and the 22 detectors flag 56 of them (96.6%). Check the two that escape, 000001 and 110000. Every string that could detect 000001 also matches a self string, so it was deleted during censoring. These are holes, non-self patterns that look too much like self. All six self strings are never flagged, because every detector that matched one was removed, so there are no false alarms by construction.
The cost is the interesting part. A random detector matches a random string with probability P = 8/64 = 1/8 here. With six self strings, a random candidate would survive with probability about (1 − P)⁶ = 0.45 if the six events were independent; the real fraction is 22 of 64 = 0.34, because they are not independent. Real systems use longer strings, say 48 to 100 bits, and a large self set, so the chance of a candidate surviving falls roughly as (1 − P)^N for N self strings, and the number of random candidates you must generate and test grows exponentially with the size of the self set. D'haeseleer and colleagues (1996) gave faster algorithms that build detectors directly, but for rich, high-dimensional data the method remains impractical.
intuition: Generate many random detectors, delete the ones that fire on normal behaviour, and the ones that remain fire only on something new.
The design is natural because telling good from bad directly would need a list of all bad things, and the bad things keep changing. It is much easier to define normal, because the body can sample its own molecules, and then treat everything else as suspect. Randomness gives coverage without needing to know the future. Selection by cloning makes the response proportional to the threat. The cost is a tolerable rate of mistakes, since the system never has perfect information.
mistakes: People often think the immune system tells "self" from "foreign" in a simple way. In fact it relies on context. Many foreign things are tolerated (food proteins, the gut bacteria, a fetus), and many harmful things are self (cancer cells, cells infected by viruses). That is why Matzinger's danger model, and the need for a second costimulation signal, matter. Neither the 1994 algorithm nor the original self/non-self rule is the whole story.
People often think a vaccine contains a weakened disease that the body fights. In fact many vaccines contain only a piece of the microbe (a protein, a sugar, or the mRNA to make one protein), and none of them can cause the disease. Their job is to create memory cells and to show the system the target in a safe form.
People often think antibodies are designed in response to the infection. In fact the diversity pre-exists in random form. The infection selects and refines it, and this is why a primary response needs about a week while a secondary one is much faster.
People often think artificial immune systems are a successful branch of AI. In fact they are an interesting idea with limited practical use. Negative selection scales poorly with dimension, and standard classifiers, isolation forests and autoencoders usually beat it on real anomaly detection.
tradeoffs: Choose negative selection (train on self only, detect anything else) when you have plenty of normal data, almost no examples of attacks, and a small feature space (short strings, a handful of features). You get detection of unknown attacks and a guarantee of no false alarms on the training set. You give up coverage (holes, as in the example) and scale: the number of candidates grows exponentially with the self set and with dimension.
Choose supervised classification when you have labelled examples of both normal and bad behaviour and the attacks are stable: it is more accurate and cheaper. You give up detection of new attacks, because a classifier only knows the patterns it was trained on. Choose a statistical or learned anomaly detector (isolation forest, one-class models, autoencoders, density estimates) when you need to model normal behaviour in many dimensions. These methods are the practical descendants of the immune idea, and they let you set a score threshold that balances false positives and false negatives; negative selection gives only a yes/no.
Choose a layered design, as in the body, when mistakes are expensive: a fast, cheap, general filter first (rate limits, rules, signatures), then a slower, learned layer, and a human or a second signal before drastic action. The body requires a second signal before a T cell attacks. In software, require two independent signals before blocking a customer or quarantining a host. The cost is more latency and complexity.
Choose the strength of the response to match the false-positive cost. The body pays this price in autoimmune disease (roughly 5 to 10% of people have one) and in allergy, and when the response is too strong it can cause harm itself (cytokine storm). A security system that blocks too aggressively causes outages of the same kind.
friction: The first limit is false positives and false negatives that cannot both be zero. Holes in coverage let some attackers through, and cross-reactions create autoimmunity. Cancer cells and many pathogens escape by looking like self or by switching off the activation signals, and checkpoint drugs (the 2018 Nobel Prize to Allison and Honjo) work by removing such brakes.
Adversaries adapt. Pathogens change their surface proteins (influenza drifts every year, HIV mutates within one patient), so the body is always one step behind, and memory can mislead (original antigenic sin, where the response favours the first strain met). The same problem hits anomaly detectors: attackers learn what normal looks like and imitate it.
For computing, the main limits are scale and training data. Exponential candidate generation, high-dimensional distances that lose meaning, and the need for a clean "self" set (if the training data contain attacks, they become tolerated) make direct use hard. The deeper lesson, a layered system that combines a fast general layer, a learning layer and a second confirming signal, has proved more useful than the specific algorithm.
practice: Use the immune system as a design pattern for detection and defence. Define normal from your own data, and keep a clean baseline: audit it, because contamination teaches the system to tolerate the attacker. Use layers: cheap signature rules for known threats, learned anomaly scores for unknown ones, and a second signal (a different data source, or a delay and a recheck) before an irreversible action. Keep memory: store confirmed bad indicators and re-check them quickly, as memory cells do.
Keep the system distributed and loosely coupled where you can, so that one failure does not blind everything. Measure holes: test the detector with synthetic attacks that sit close to normal, to find the blind spots. For ad tech, the relevant case is invalid traffic and fraud detection: attackers copy human behaviour, so a single detector will be learned and evaded, and the stack needs several detectors with different views, retrained often.
To study the topic, read Forrest et al. (1994) and the survey by Stibor and others for the algorithmic side, and Janeway's Immunobiology (Murphy and Weaver edition) for the biology; the first chapters on innate immunity, antigen recognition and lymphocyte development are enough. Then implement the 6-bit example above and extend it to 16 bits to feel the exponential cost, and compare with an isolation forest on the same data.

@@ homeostasis
value: Homeostasis is the ability of a living body to keep its internal conditions nearly constant while the outside world changes. Core body temperature stays near 37 °C whether you stand in snow or in a desert. Blood sugar stays between about 70 and 100 mg/dL (3.9 to 5.6 mmol/L) between meals and after a large meal. Blood acidity stays within a pH range of 7.35 to 7.45, and a drift of a few tenths outside it is life-threatening. The body does this with no central computer, using hundreds of small feedback loops.
Each loop has the same parts. A sensor measures a variable. A comparator checks it against a set point. An effector pushes the variable back when it is off. The push is always in the direction that reduces the error, which is called negative feedback. This is the same structure as a thermostat, a cruise control, an autoscaler and a Kubernetes controller. Homeostasis is the original, older than any machine by hundreds of millions of years, and control theory (L4) is the mathematics that was developed to describe it and its engineering copies.
This read stands on evolution (L5). Nobody designed these loops. Natural selection kept organisms whose internal conditions stayed in the range where their enzymes work, and discarded the others, so the set points, the sensors and the gains are the product of selection. This also explains why there are many overlapping loops, not one perfect one: selection rewards robustness, and a system with backup loops survives the failure of one. The higher concept, control theory and feedback (L4), turns the idea into equations of stability, delay and gain, which you need to design any real controller.
terms: Set point — the target value of a variable that a control loop tries to maintain, such as 37 °C.
Negative feedback — a loop in which a change in a variable causes a response that opposes the change.
Positive feedback — a loop in which a change causes a response that amplifies it; used briefly in the body (blood clotting, childbirth contractions), and dangerous if it runs free.
Sensor and effector — the part that measures the variable, and the part that acts on it (a gland, a muscle, a blood vessel).
Gain — how strongly the effector responds per unit of error; high gain gives a small remaining error but risks oscillation.
Delay — the time between a change and the effect of the response; the main cause of oscillation in feedback loops.
Integral action — a controller that accumulates the error over time, which removes the remaining steady error completely.
Allostasis — the idea that the body changes its set points and anticipates needs, for example by raising blood pressure before you stand up, instead of only reacting.
history: In the 1850s and 1860s the French physiologist Claude Bernard argued that the cells of the body live in an internal environment (the "milieu intérieur") of blood and tissue fluid, and that the constancy of this environment is the condition for a free and independent life. Walter Cannon at Harvard studied how the body keeps that constancy, in work on the sympathetic nervous system and on the "fight or flight" response. In 1926 he proposed the word homeostasis, from the Greek for "staying similar", and he developed the idea in a 1929 article and in the 1932 book The Wisdom of the Body. He listed the features of the systems: they hold variables steady, they have limits to their range, and they can use opposing mechanisms (one to raise, one to lower).
Engineering supplied the mathematics. During the Second World War, Norbert Wiener worked on anti-aircraft fire control, which needed a machine that predicts and corrects. In 1943 Rosenblueth, Wiener and Bigelow published "Behavior, Purpose and Teleology", arguing that goal-directed behaviour in animals and machines is explained by feedback. Wiener's book Cybernetics (1948) named the field. W. Ross Ashby built a machine, the Homeostat, in 1948 to show an adaptive system that finds a stable state by itself, and wrote Design for a Brain (1952).
Later work added precision. Barkai and Leibler (1997) and Yi, Huang, Simon and Doyle (2000) showed that bacterial chemotaxis adapts perfectly to a constant stimulus because of a feedback of the integral type, a result from control engineering found in a cell. Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer introduced allostasis in 1988 to describe regulation by prediction. In the same years, engineers built closed-loop insulin pumps, an artificial replacement for one failed homeostatic loop.
how: Take body temperature. Sensors are of two kinds: warm- and cold-sensitive nerve endings in the skin, and temperature-sensitive neurons in the hypothalamus (in its preoptic area) that measure the blood. The hypothalamus compares the reading with a set point near 37 °C. If the reading is high, it triggers effectors that lose heat: blood vessels in the skin widen so more blood flows near the surface, and sweat glands secrete sweat. If it is low, the effectors conserve and make heat: skin vessels narrow, muscles shiver, and brown fat burns fuel. The numbers are large. A resting person produces roughly 100 watts of heat. Evaporating 1 litre of sweat per hour removes about 680 watts, because water takes 2.4 megajoules per kilogram to evaporate. That is more than enough to dissipate the heat of hard exercise, if the air is dry.
Blood sugar works through two opposing effectors. When glucose rises after a meal, beta cells in the pancreas release insulin, which makes muscle, fat and liver take up glucose and the liver store it. When glucose falls, alpha cells release glucagon, which makes the liver release glucose. The entire bloodstream holds only about 5 grams of glucose, while a meal can add 75 grams, so the loop must work fast and with strong gain. First-phase insulin release starts within minutes. Because there are two opposing effectors, the system can push in both directions, and it has no dead zone around the set point.
The mathematics of feedback explains the design. Let a disturbance d push a variable away from the set point. With a proportional controller of gain K, the remaining error settles at about d / (1 + K), where K is the loop gain. If the gain is 10, a disturbance that would cause 10 degrees of change leaves under 1 degree. High gain gives tight regulation. A proportional controller always leaves some error, and an integral controller removes it, since it keeps adding up the error and keeps pushing until the error is exactly zero. Cells implement integrals chemically, for example through a slowly modified protein whose level tracks the accumulated error. This gives "perfect adaptation": the output returns exactly to its set point after a lasting change of input, which is how bacteria keep their sensitivity to a chemical gradient at any background level.
Delay limits the gain. If the response arrives late, the controller pushes against an error that has already changed, and pushes too far. Each step then overshoots and the next correction overshoots the other way. Above a critical gain the oscillation grows. The effect is easy to see in a simple discrete model (see the example). The body deals with this in three ways: it keeps the gain moderate, it uses rate sensors that react to how fast a variable changes (so it can anticipate), and it uses feedforward control. In feedforward, the body reacts before the variable changes: the sight and smell of food starts insulin release (the cephalic phase), blood pressure rises as you stand, and the circadian clock raises cortisol before waking.
The loops are layered and linked. Temperature, fluid volume, salt, blood pressure and glucose loops share effectors (blood vessels, kidneys, hormones), so they interact, and they can fight each other. During a fever, a chemical signal (prostaglandin E2) raises the hypothalamic set point to about 39 °C. The body now treats 37 °C as too cold and produces chills and shivering until it reaches the new setpoint. The loop is not broken; its target moved. Evolution (L5) explains the choice of 37 °C as a compromise: warm enough for fast enzyme reactions and for resistance to many fungi, cold enough to limit the energy cost, as a warm-blooded animal burns many times the fuel of a cold-blooded one at the same size.

example: Use the smallest model of a loop with a delay. Let x be the deviation from the set point, so the goal is x = 0. Each step, the controller subtracts K times the deviation it measured one step ago: x(t+1) = x(t) − K × x(t−1). Start with x = 1 at the two previous steps, as if a disturbance had just arrived.
With K = 0.5, the sequence is x0 = 1, x1 = 1 − 0.5 × 1 = 0.5, x2 = 0.5 − 0.5 × 1 = 0, x3 = 0 − 0.5 × 0.5 = −0.25, x4 = −0.25 − 0 = −0.25, x5 = −0.25 + 0.125 = −0.125, x6 = −0.125 + 0.125 = 0, x7 = 0.0625. The error crosses zero, overshoots to −0.25 once, and dies away within ten steps.
With K = 0.9, x1 = 0.1, x2 = 0.1 − 0.9 = −0.8, x3 = −0.8 − 0.09 = −0.89, x4 = −0.89 + 0.72 = −0.17, x5 = −0.17 + 0.80 = 0.63, x6 = 0.78, x7 = 0.22, x8 = −0.49. The deviation swings from +1 to −0.89 to +0.78, so it overshoots by nearly the whole disturbance and decays slowly. This is the regime of a badly tuned thermostat.
With K = 1.2, x1 = −0.2, x2 = −1.4, x3 = −1.16, x4 = 0.52, x5 = 1.91, x6 = 1.29, x7 = −1.01, x8 = −2.55. The swings grow. The loop is unstable. The limit for a one-step delay is K = 1. In general, for a delay of d steps the loop is stable only if K < 2 sin(π / (2(2d + 1))): 2 for no delay, 1 for d = 1, about 0.62 for d = 2 and 0.45 for d = 3. The longer the delay, the lower the usable gain, and the larger the steady error of a proportional controller.
Apply this to an autoscaler. Kubernetes HPA computes desired replicas = ceil(current replicas × current metric / target metric). With 10 pods at 90% CPU and a target of 60%, desired = ceil(10 × 90 / 60) = 15. If a new pod needs 60 seconds to become ready and the metric is sampled every 15 seconds, the delay is several decision steps. A controller that over-reacts to a stale reading will add too many pods, then remove them, then add them again. The default scale-down stabilization window of 300 seconds and a 10% tolerance band exist for exactly this reason: they lower the effective gain and ignore small errors.
intuition: A thermostat that senses the error, pushes against it, and stops pushing when the error is gone, repeated in hundreds of small loops that share the same body.
Feedback is natural because the environment is unpredictable and the body cannot know the next disturbance. Measuring the result and correcting it needs no model of the cause: whatever pushes the variable up, the same response pushes it down. That is why one loop handles many kinds of disturbance, a heavy meal or a fast run, with the same effectors. The weak point is delay: the correction is based on the past, so a fast or strong correction can arrive when the situation has changed.
mistakes: People often think homeostasis means that everything in the body is constant. In fact it holds a few key variables within narrow bands by letting others change a lot. Body temperature varies by about 1 °C through the day, heart rate and blood flow vary by factors of five or more with exercise, and the set points themselves move with sleep, fever and stress.
People often think feedback is always good and always stabilizes. In fact feedback with delay or high gain makes systems oscillate. In the body, delayed loops are linked to conditions such as Cheyne–Stokes breathing and some oscillations of blood pressure. In software they produce thrashing autoscalers and retry storms.
People often think that homeostasis means returning to the old state. In fact the body often changes the target on purpose. Fever raises the set point. Long-term stress, high blood pressure and insulin resistance can be understood as set points that moved or as loops with reduced gain, which is the idea of allostasis. A system can be perfectly regulated around the wrong value.
People often think negative feedback is the only type. In fact positive feedback is used where a fast, all-or-none event is useful: a nerve spike, a blood clot, childbirth. These loops have a built-in stop condition, and without one they run away.
tradeoffs: Choose a proportional controller when a small remaining error is acceptable and simplicity matters: it has one parameter and cannot wind up. The residual error is d / (1 + K), so you need a high gain to make it small, and high gain with delay leads to oscillation. Choose proportional plus integral (PI) control when the target must be hit exactly (blood glucose averages, pH, a latency target), as cells and PID controllers do. You pay with slower response and the risk of "windup" (the integral grows while the effector is saturated, and then overshoots). Add a derivative term only when the signal is clean, since it amplifies noise.
Choose feedback when disturbances are unpredictable, and feedforward when they are predictable and you can act early: the sight of a meal, a scheduled traffic peak, a known batch job. Feedforward removes the delay problem for the part you predict, but it fails silently when the prediction is wrong. Use both: feedforward for the expected part and feedback to clean up what is left. For an autoscaler, this means scheduled scaling for known peaks and metric-based scaling for the rest.
Choose a tight set point and high gain when deviations are quickly dangerous (blood potassium, database replication lag limits). Choose a wide band, slow response and hysteresis when actions are costly or noisy (scaling a cluster, restarting a service), so the system does not chatter. The price of tightness is energy and the risk of instability. The body spends roughly a fifth of its resting energy on the pumps that hold ion gradients.
Choose many overlapping loops with different mechanisms (as in the body) for resilience, and one loop for clarity. Redundant loops tolerate failure, and they can fight each other (two autoscalers acting on the same service). Decide who owns each variable. For modelling, choose the classical set-point view (Cannon) for fast, simple regulated variables, and the allostatic view for chronic conditions where the targets move with context. It breaks down for slow adaptation, where "the set point" is not one number.
friction: Real control is harder than the textbook loop. Sensors are noisy, effectors saturate (a sweat gland cannot produce more than a maximum, a cluster has a maximum size), and the system changes with time: the gain of insulin action differs between morning and evening, and it falls in insulin resistance. Loops interact, and the interactions are hard to predict. The result is that tuning is a continuous task, not a one-time design.
Failures are instructive. In type 1 diabetes the effector is gone, since the beta cells are destroyed, so the loop must be closed artificially. In type 2 diabetes the effectors respond poorly (lower gain) and the sensor-effector link drifts. Commercial automated insulin delivery systems use PID or model-predictive controllers with a glucose sensor, and they struggle with exactly the issues above: sensor delay of about 5 to 15 minutes, insulin that takes an hour or more to act fully, and large disturbances from meals and exercise. That a good controller is hard to build with 20 years of engineering is a reminder of how much tuning evolution has done.
For software, the equivalent pitfalls are thrashing, cascading loops, and metrics that do not represent the real variable (for example scaling on CPU when the bottleneck is a database). Another open problem is robustness against shifts in the environment that the loop never saw, for which both biology and software depend on slower adaptation, such as learning, evolution or a human operator.
practice: Treat every autoscaler, rate limiter, retry policy, queue and capacity manager as a control loop, and ask the five questions: what is the sensor and how stale is it, what is the set point, what is the effector and what are its limits, how long is the delay from action to effect, and what is the gain. Then pick settings so the gain is well below the stability limit for your delay. In Kubernetes, use stabilization windows and tolerance bands, scale on a metric that tracks the real bottleneck, and set minimum and maximum replicas so that the effector cannot saturate or go to zero.
Prefer controllers that are level-triggered: they compare the desired and actual state every cycle and act on the difference, as Kubernetes controllers do, instead of reacting once to an event. If an event is lost, the next cycle repairs it, like the body's loops that never stop. Add jitter and backoff to retries so that many clients do not form a synchronized positive-feedback storm. Use feedforward for known peaks. Keep one owner for each variable. Instrument the loop: plot the error, the control action and the delay, and look for ringing.
To study this, read Cannon's The Wisdom of the Body for the biology, Åström and Murray, Feedback Systems (free online) for the mathematics, and the Yi and colleagues (2000) paper to see how integral control appears in a cell. Reproduce the delay example in a few lines and sweep K and d to find the stability boundary yourself.

@@ perception
value: Human eyes and ears are not recording devices. They discard most of what reaches them and pass on a compressed description, and they are blind to many things that a camera or microphone captures. The eye sees fine detail in brightness but only coarse detail in colour. The ear cannot hear a quiet sound that sits next to a loud one of similar pitch. Every lossy codec in use, JPEG, H.264, HEVC, AV1, MP3, AAC and Opus, is built on these blind spots: it throws away exactly the information that people cannot perceive.
The gains are large. Raw 1080p video at 60 frames per second is about 1.5 gigabits per second, and a streaming service sends it at about 5 megabits per second, a reduction by roughly 300 times. CD audio is 1,411 kilobits per second and a good MP3 or AAC file is 128, about 11 times smaller. Without models of human perception, a codec could not choose what to discard and the streaming video and music business would need many times more bandwidth. For a CTV and ad-tech engineer, the same facts decide how ad creatives are encoded, how quality is measured, and when a lower bitrate is invisible and when it is not.
This read stands on neurons (L5). Perception is computed by neural circuits, and the blind spots of vision and hearing come from the design of these circuits. Retinal cells with centre-surround fields respond to contrast and ignore uniform areas, which is a form of compression. Hair cells and auditory neurons have limited resolution in frequency and time, and neurons adapt, so that a constant stimulus fades. The higher concepts that depend on this read are video streaming and codecs (L1) and the discrete cosine transform (L2), which is the tool that separates an image into the frequencies that the eye does and does not see.
terms: Luma and chroma — luma is the brightness part of a colour image, and chroma is the colour-difference part (Cb and Cr in Y′CbCr); the eye resolves luma much more finely than chroma.
Chroma subsampling — storing chroma at lower resolution than luma; 4:2:0 keeps one chroma sample per 2 × 2 pixels, which cuts the data to half of full 4:4:4.
Contrast sensitivity function (CSF) — how sensitive the eye is to patterns of each spatial frequency; it peaks at about 2 to 6 cycles per degree and falls to zero near 50 or more.
Just-noticeable difference (JND) — the smallest change a person can detect, often about 1 to 2% for brightness.
Masking — a strong stimulus hides a weaker one nearby in frequency or time (in hearing) or in space and texture (in vision).
Critical band — a frequency range, roughly a Bark unit (24 of them across the hearing range), within which sounds compete to mask each other.
Quantization — rounding a value to a coarser step; the main lossy step in a codec, and the place where perception decides the step size.
VMAF, SSIM, PSNR — measures of video quality; PSNR compares raw pixel error, SSIM compares structure, and VMAF fuses several features and is trained to predict human scores.
history: The idea that perception is a compression system came first from information theory. Fred Attneave in 1954 and Horace Barlow in 1961 argued that sensory neurons are tuned to remove the redundancy of natural scenes, because most of a picture is predictable from its neighbours. Evidence came from the retina: Haldan Hartline's work on lateral inhibition (Nobel Prize 1967) explained why we see bands at edges (Mach bands) and why uniform areas are not coded strongly. Earlier, Helmholtz and Young had shown that colour vision uses three receptor types, and in 1933 Harvey Fletcher and Wilden Munson measured the sensitivity of the ear at different frequencies and loudnesses. Fletcher's 1940 work on critical bands, and Eberhard Zwicker's Bark scale (1961), described how the ear groups frequencies.
These results moved into engineering in steps. In 1979 Schroeder, Atal and Hall showed how to hide coding noise below the masking threshold. James Johnston at Bell Labs published perceptual transform coding in 1988. JPEG (standardized 1992) used an 8 × 8 discrete cosine transform and quantization tables shaped by the eye's sensitivity. The MP3 format (MPEG-1 Layer III, 1993) was developed at the Fraunhofer Institute with Karlheinz Brandenburg and others, and made digital music distribution practical. AAC followed in 1997 and Opus (RFC 6716) in 2012. For video, H.264 (2003), HEVC (2013), AV1 (2018) and VVC (2020) each added tools that remove more redundancy.
Measuring quality came later. PSNR, the simple pixel-error measure, was long used but matches human opinion poorly. Wang, Bovik, Sheikh and Simoncelli introduced SSIM in 2004. Netflix, with the University of Southern California, released VMAF in 2016, a model trained on human ratings that combines a visual information fidelity feature, a detail loss feature and a motion feature. Netflix reports that about 6 VMAF points correspond to one just-noticeable difference.
how: Start with vision. The retina has about 126 million photoreceptors (around 120 million rods for dim light and 6 million cones for colour and detail) and about 1.2 million ganglion cells whose axons form the optic nerve. That is roughly 100 to 1 compression before the signal leaves the eye. Cones come in three types, long, medium and short wavelength, with peak sensitivities near 564, 534 and 420 nm. The short-wavelength type is rare (5 to 10% of cones) and absent from the very centre, which is the physical reason the eye's colour detail is poor. Sharp vision covers only the central 1 to 2 degrees, about the width of a thumbnail at arm's length, and the brain builds the feeling of a sharp full scene from fast eye movements.
The ganglion cells encode contrast, not light level. Each has a centre-surround field: it fires when the centre is brighter than the surround, or the reverse. This means that a uniform area produces almost no signal, while edges produce strong signals. The cells also adapt to the average brightness, so the same circuits work from starlight to noon, a range of many powers of ten, while within a scene we resolve roughly a few thousand to one. Two things follow for codecs. The signal that matters is differences, so predicting a pixel from its neighbours (and a frame from the previous frame) and coding only the difference follows what the eye does. And sensitivity depends on spatial frequency (CSF): the eye is most sensitive to patterns of 2 to 6 cycles per degree and nearly blind to very fine patterns, so high-frequency detail can be quantized coarsely.
Colour is handled by splitting the signal. The eye works in opponent channels (light–dark, red–green, blue–yellow), and its spatial resolution for the colour channels is much lower than for brightness. A codec converts R′G′B′ to Y′CbCr. In the BT.709 standard, Y′ = 0.2126 R′ + 0.7152 G′ + 0.0722 B′, so green contributes most to brightness, in line with the cone sensitivity. Cb and Cr are stored at half the resolution in each direction (4:2:0), so a pixel needs on average 1.5 samples instead of 3, and almost nobody notices. Brightness itself is stored on a non-linear scale. The just-noticeable change is roughly a constant percentage of the level (Weber's law), so equal steps in a perceptual curve (gamma, or the PQ curve designed from a model of contrast sensitivity) waste fewer bits than equal steps in light.
Then hearing. The cochlea converts sound to a travelling wave on the basilar membrane, whose stiffness changes along its length, so each place responds best to one frequency. About 3,500 inner hair cells turn the movement into nerve signals carried by about 30,000 fibres. The ear can hear about 20 Hz to 20 kHz, has its greatest sensitivity between 2 and 5 kHz, and covers a range of about 120 dB, a factor of 10¹² in power, between the quietest sound and one that hurts. Neural timing locks to the waveform only up to about 4 or 5 kHz, which is why pitch above that is coded by place only.
Masking is the key to audio coding. A loud tone excites a region of the basilar membrane around its frequency and hides weaker sounds in the same region; the effect spreads more toward higher frequencies than lower ones. This happens in a critical band of widths from about 100 Hz at low frequencies to over 3 kHz near 15 kHz. There is also masking in time: a loud sound hides quieter sounds for roughly 100 to 200 ms after it and a few to 20 ms before it. An MP3 encoder splits the signal into 32 subbands and finer frequency lines, computes a masking threshold from a psychoacoustic model for each critical band, and allocates bits so that the quantization noise stays under that threshold. Sounds that are masked get few or no bits. Huffman coding then removes statistical redundancy. A video encoder works in the same way: a transform (DCT), quantization shaped by the CSF, and entropy coding.

example: First, a raw bit rate. A 1920 × 1080 frame has 2,073,600 pixels. In 4:4:4 with 8 bits per sample, that is 3 samples × 8 bits = 24 bits per pixel, which is 49.8 megabits per frame. At 60 frames per second that is 2.99 gigabits per second. With 4:2:0, a pixel needs on average 1.5 samples = 12 bits, so 24.9 megabits per frame and 1.49 gigabits per second, half as much. A streaming service delivers this at about 5 megabits per second. The ratio is 1,490 / 5 = about 300. The chroma subsampling gives the first factor of 2, and the remaining factor of about 150 comes from prediction, transform, quantization and entropy coding, all guided by what the eye cannot see.
Now quantization, as in JPEG. After an 8 × 8 DCT, each block has 64 coefficients, from the average brightness (top-left) to the finest diagonal pattern (bottom-right). The standard luminance table divides the top-left coefficient by 16 and the bottom-right by 99. Suppose a block has the average coefficient 37 and a fine diagonal coefficient 30. The first is quantized as 37 / 16 = 2.3, which rounds to 2 and decodes as 32, a small error in the value the eye weighs most strongly (a quality setting that scales the table down would keep more precision). The second is 30 / 99 = 0.3, which rounds to 0 and costs no bits. That fine pattern is simply gone, and at normal viewing distance nobody sees it. Most of the 63 other coefficients in a typical block become zero, and long runs of zeros are cheap to code.
Next, audio. CD audio is 44,100 samples per second × 16 bits × 2 channels = 1,411,200 bits per second. At 128 kilobits per second, an MP3 uses 1,411 / 128 = about 11 times less. If a loud 1 kHz tone is present, the psychoacoustic model may find that quantization noise 20 dB or more below the tone, within the same critical band, is inaudible. Choosing a quantization step that produces noise at that level uses about 3 or 4 bits per coefficient instead of 16 (each bit buys about 6 dB of signal-to-noise ratio, so 16 bits give 96 dB and 4 bits give about 24 dB). Coefficients in masked bands get 0 bits.
Finally, quality scoring. If encoding A scores VMAF 88 and B scores 82, Netflix's rule of thumb says that viewers would notice a difference of about one JND. If the scores are 88 and 86, most would not, so the cheaper encode is good enough. This is the reasoning behind choosing a bitrate for each title.
intuition: Don't spend bits on what nobody can see or hear, and the senses themselves already tell you what that is.
The approach is natural because the senses are themselves compressors. Natural images and sounds are highly redundant, and the nervous system evolved to send the surprising part and drop the predictable part, to save the energy and the nerve fibres that signals cost. A codec writer who understands the circuits can reuse their decisions. The rest is accounting: measure how much noise the sense tolerates at each frequency and place the noise exactly there.
mistakes: People often think a lossy codec removes quality evenly. In fact it removes quality unevenly, first from the places where the senses are least sensitive: fine colour detail, very high spatial frequencies, sounds under a masking threshold. At low bitrates the error grows into places that people notice, such as flat gradients (banding), edges (ringing) and motion (blockiness).
People often think the eye is a camera with a uniform sensor. In fact only the central 1 to 2 degrees is sharp, colour sensitivity falls off outside it, and the periphery is good for motion and low light. The sense of a sharp, colourful wide scene is constructed by the brain.
People often think that if two files have the same PSNR they look the same. In fact PSNR counts the error in each pixel equally, and humans do not. Noise in flat areas is visible and the same noise in texture is hidden. SSIM and VMAF were created because PSNR predicts human ratings poorly.
People often think "inaudible" or "invisible" is a fixed property. In fact it depends on the listener, the equipment and the conditions. A codec artifact hidden on laptop speakers may be clear on good headphones. Ageing also shifts hearing: many adults hear little above 15 kHz, and a young listener may.
tradeoffs: Choose a perceptual metric (VMAF, or a subjective test with a mean opinion score) to decide between encodes of different bitrates when the goal is what viewers see. Choose PSNR only to compare tiny changes within one codec in the same setting, or when you must have a cheap, deterministic number (for example in regression tests of a decoder). VMAF is trained on specific content, screens and viewing distances, so it can be misled by very different material (screen recordings, animation) or by encoders tuned to fool it. A subjective test is the ground truth but costs people and time.
Choose 4:2:0 for natural video and film: it halves the data with almost no visible loss. Choose 4:4:4 (or 4:2:2) for screen content with thin coloured text, UI graphics and graphics overlays, where red text on a blue background shows colour bleeding at half chroma resolution, and for studio work where later colour grading will exaggerate errors. You pay up to twice the data.
Choose a lossy perceptual codec for distribution and a lossless one (FLAC for audio, or a high-bitrate mezzanine format for video) for storage that will be edited or re-encoded. Every encode removes information that the next one cannot restore, and artifacts that were hidden can be amplified. Choose a higher bitrate when the viewing conditions are more demanding: large TVs at close distance, HDR, fast sports, and film grain. Choose a lower bitrate for small phone screens, which cannot show the detail anyway.
Choose per-title or per-shot encoding (a ladder tuned for each video, as Netflix does) when you have a catalogue that is watched many times, since the saving repeats on every view. Choose a fixed bitrate ladder when you encode a lot of short, one-off content, such as ad creatives, where the analysis would cost more than the saving, and where a safe setting is acceptable. The perceptual models break down at the edges: very low bitrates, HDR and wide colour, high frame rates, and any case where viewers examine a still frame.
friction: Perception differs between people, screens and rooms, so no single model is right for everyone. Metrics predict an average viewer and are not accurate for individual preferences, and they can be gamed: an encoder tuned for a metric can raise its score without improving the picture. Generation loss builds up when content passes through several encodes, as when a creative is encoded by an agency, an ad platform and a streaming service.
The models also have limits. The masking and CSF data come from experiments with simple test patterns, and real content is more complex. Motion changes the CSF, and viewers follow moving objects with their eyes, which makes motion blur visible in sports. HDR with wide colour changes the range in which the old models were measured. New contents such as screen sharing, games and synthetic video have statistics unlike natural images.
Learned codecs and neural upscalers add another problem: a model can produce a picture that looks sharp and is wrong in its details, so you must decide whether to judge by fidelity to the original or by viewer preference. Hearing and vision also change with age, so models fitted to young, healthy subjects are not accurate for everyone.
practice: When you choose encoding settings, work from the viewer's device and distance. Use a perceptual metric such as VMAF on a sample of your own content, find the bitrate at which the score stops rising by more than about one JND per step, and use that as the top of the ladder. Keep a lossless or very high-quality master and derive every delivery file from it, never from another delivery file. For ads in CTV streams, encode each creative at the resolution and frame rate of the surrounding content, so quality does not jump at the splice, and check for banding in dark gradients and for blocking in fast motion.
Use 4:2:0 and 8-bit for standard SDR video, and 10-bit for HDR and for content with smooth gradients, since 10-bit greatly lowers banding at a small cost. Do not use quality numbers alone: view the encodes on the real devices, with the sound on, at least for a sample. Build regression tests with a set of difficult clips (film grain, confetti, fast pans, text, dark scenes).
To study the topic, read the HEVC or AV1 overview papers for the tools and the Netflix tech blog posts on VMAF and per-title encoding for the measurement. For hearing, read the chapters on psychoacoustics in an audio coding text such as Spanias, Painter and Atti, and study the MP3 encoder structure. For vision, the retina chapters of Kandel's Principles of Neural Science explain the circuits. A practical exercise is to take a photo, convert it to Y′CbCr, blur Cb and Cr by a factor of 4, convert back and compare, then do the same with the Y channel.

@@ doping
value: Pure silicon is a poor conductor. At room temperature a cubic centimetre of it holds only about 10¹⁰ free charge carriers among 5 × 10²² atoms, so its resistivity is about 200,000 ohm-centimetres, closer to an insulator than to a metal. Doping means replacing a very small share of the silicon atoms with atoms of another element. Replace one atom in five million with phosphorus and the number of free electrons rises by a factor of a million, and the resistivity falls by a factor of several hundred thousand, to about 0.5 ohm-centimetres. The choice of element decides what carries the current: electrons (n-type) or holes (p-type).
This single trick is what makes electronics possible. A device that conducts only when you tell it to needs regions with different, controlled carrier types and densities: the n-type and p-type regions that form diodes and transistors. Doping is the way these regions are drawn. In a modern chip every transistor has heavily doped source and drain regions, a more lightly doped channel or well, and often doped layers for isolation, and each one is made by adding a precise amount of dopant atoms to a chosen volume of crystal.
This read stands on band theory and the band gap (L5). In an insulator or semiconductor, electrons occupy a valence band, and a forbidden gap of 1.12 eV separates it from the empty conduction band. Dopant atoms add allowed energy levels inside the gap, very near one of the band edges, only about 45 meV away. Room temperature gives thermal energy of about 26 meV per degree of freedom, so these levels are nearly all ionized and the dopants supply carriers, while the 1.12 eV gap almost never gets crossed. The transistor and p–n junction concepts (L5) are built directly on top of this: doped regions form the source, drain and channel, and the junction between a p-region and an n-region is the basic device.
terms: Intrinsic semiconductor — pure semiconductor; the only carriers are electron–hole pairs made by heat, about 10¹⁰ per cm³ in silicon at 300 K.
Donor and n-type — a donor atom (phosphorus, arsenic, antimony) has five valence electrons; four bond with the lattice and the fifth is easily freed, so electrons become the majority carrier.
Acceptor and p-type — an acceptor atom (boron, gallium) has three valence electrons; it takes one from the lattice, leaving a mobile hole as the majority carrier.
Hole — a missing electron in the valence band that behaves like a positive charge carrier.
Mass-action law — in equilibrium n × p = n_i², where n and p are electron and hole densities and n_i is the intrinsic density; raising one lowers the other.
Ion implantation — shooting dopant ions at the wafer at energies of a few hundred eV to about a million eV so they embed at a chosen depth.
Annealing (activation) — heating the wafer so that the crystal repairs implant damage and the dopant atoms move onto lattice sites, where they supply carriers.
Compensation — when both donors and acceptors are present, the net doping is the difference between them.
history: In 1940 Russell Ohl at Bell Laboratories was testing a piece of silicon with a crack, and found that it produced a voltage when light fell on it. The reason was that the sample had a region that was richer in one type of impurity on one side of the crack and another type on the other side, so it contained a p–n junction formed by accident. At that time nobody knew why some silicon samples conducted by electrons and others by holes. The cause was traces of impurities in the melt: those that were phosphorus-like made n-type and those that were boron-like made p-type. Doping was discovered before it was understood.
The next step was control. William Shockley, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain made the first transistor in germanium in 1947. Gordon Teal at Bell Labs developed methods to grow single crystals, and in 1954, at Texas Instruments, he made the first silicon transistor. William Pfann's zone refining (1951–52) removed impurities, and Fuller and others in the 1950s learned to diffuse dopants into silicon at about 1,000 °C. Carl Frosch and Lincoln Derick found in 1955–1957 that a layer of silicon dioxide blocks the diffusion, which allowed patterns of dopants to be defined with masks. Jean Hoerni used this in the planar process (1959), which Robert Noyce used for the first practical integrated circuit.
Ion implantation came later: Ohl and Shockley patented the idea in the early 1950s, and it became a production tool in the 1970s because it controls the dose and depth more precisely than diffusion. From the 1990s, devices became so small that the dopant atoms themselves became a problem (random counts), and since the 2010s FinFETs (Intel, 22 nm, 2011) and gate-all-around transistors (Samsung 2022, and TSMC and Intel from about 2025) use nearly undoped channels, with doping concentrated in the source and drain regions.
how: Silicon sits in group 14 of the periodic table, with four valence electrons. In the crystal each atom shares one electron with each of four neighbours in the diamond lattice, whose cubic cell is 0.543 nm wide, and the bonds use all valence electrons. There are no free carriers except those created by heat, which lifts an electron across the 1.12 eV gap and leaves a hole. The number is small: n_i is about 10¹⁰ per cm³ at 300 K, and it doubles for about every 8 to 9 °C of warming, because it depends on the exponential of the gap over twice the thermal energy.
Replace one silicon atom with phosphorus (five valence electrons). Four electrons form bonds. The fifth has no bond to join, but it is still attracted to the extra positive charge of the phosphorus nucleus. In silicon this attraction is weakened by the high dielectric constant (11.7) and the electron's small effective mass (about 0.26 of a free electron), so it resembles a hydrogen atom that is much looser. The simple hydrogen-like formula 13.6 eV × 0.26 / 11.7² gives about 26 meV, and the measured binding is 45 meV. The orbit has a radius of about 2.4 nm, which spans thousands of silicon atoms. Since 45 meV is less than twice the thermal energy at 300 K, nearly every phosphorus atom loses its fifth electron to the conduction band. Each phosphorus atom thus supplies one free electron and stays as a fixed positive ion. This is the band-gap concept (L5) at work: the donor level lies just below the conduction band, not in the middle of the gap.
Boron (three valence electrons) works in the opposite way. It lacks one electron for the fourth bond, so it takes an electron from a nearby bond, leaving a hole that can move as neighbouring electrons hop into it. The acceptor level is about 45 meV above the valence band. Each boron atom supplies one hole and stays as a fixed negative ion.
The mass-action law links the densities. In n-type silicon with donor density N_D of 10¹⁶ per cm³, the electron density is about 10¹⁶, so the hole density is n_i² / n = (10¹⁰)² / 10¹⁶ = 10⁴ per cm³. The majority carrier outnumbers the minority by twelve orders of magnitude. Conductivity is q × n × mobility, so with mobility about 1,250 cm²/V·s the resistivity is 1 / (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁶ × 1,250) = 0.5 ohm·cm, and mobility falls as doping rises because the ionized atoms scatter the carriers.
Making a doped region is a four-step process. Start with ultrapure silicon (99.9999999% or better, from the Siemens process and Czochralski crystal growth), usually lightly doped with boron during growth. Open windows in a mask. Implant ions: the energy sets the depth (boron at 10 keV goes about 30 to 40 nm deep) and the dose sets the amount (10¹¹ to 10¹⁶ ions per cm²). Finally anneal at about 1,000 °C for seconds, or use millisecond laser heating, to repair the lattice damage and put the atoms on lattice sites. Heating moves the dopants, so modern flows keep the annealing time and temperature as small as possible. For the source and drain of the newest transistors, engineers also grow doped silicon or silicon–germanium epitaxially, with dopants added in the gas, which gives abrupt profiles and high activation.
Finally, the junction. When p-type and n-type regions meet, electrons diffuse to the p-side and holes to the n-side, leaving a region of fixed ions that builds an electric field, the depletion region. The built-in voltage is V = kT ln(N_A N_D / n_i²). This field is the rectifier of a diode, and, when a gate controls it, the switch of a transistor.

example: Compute the properties of silicon doped with 10¹⁶ phosphorus atoms per cm³. Silicon has 5.0 × 10²² atoms per cm³, so there is one dopant for every 5 × 10²² / 10¹⁶ = 5 million silicon atoms, which is 0.2 parts per million. At room temperature almost all are ionized, so n = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³. By n × p = n_i², p = 10²⁰ / 10¹⁶ = 10⁴ cm⁻³. Electrons outnumber holes by 10¹².
The resistivity is ρ = 1 / (q n μ). With μ = 1,250 cm²/V·s, q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, n = 10¹⁶: q n μ = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁶ × 1,250 = 2.0 S/cm, so ρ = 0.5 ohm·cm. Pure silicon has σ = q n_i (μ_n + μ_p) = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 10¹⁰ × (1,350 + 480) = 2.9 × 10⁻⁶ S/cm, so ρ is about 3 × 10⁵ ohm·cm (published values range from 2 to 3 × 10⁵ depending on n_i). The doped sample conducts about 600,000 times better. Copper is 1.7 × 10⁻⁶ ohm·cm, so the doped silicon is still 300,000 times more resistive than copper, which is a useful semiconductor and not a metal.
Where is the Fermi level? In n-type silicon it sits above the middle of the gap by kT ln(n / n_i) = 0.0259 eV × ln(10⁶) = 0.0259 × 13.8 = 0.36 eV. It is now 0.56 − 0.36 = 0.2 eV below the conduction band. Put this material next to p-type silicon doped with 10¹⁶ boron atoms. The built-in voltage is V = kT ln(N_A N_D / n_i²) = 0.0259 × ln(10³² / 10²⁰) = 0.0259 × 27.6 = 0.715 V. This is the origin of the 0.6 to 0.7 V forward drop of a silicon diode.
How do you make this by implantation? Aim for 10¹⁸ phosphorus atoms per cm³ in a layer 100 nm (10⁻⁵ cm) thick. The required dose is 10¹⁸ × 10⁻⁵ = 10¹³ ions per cm². An implanter delivering a current of 1 mA over a 300 mm wafer (706 cm²) gives 10⁻³ / 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 6.2 × 10¹⁵ ions per second, or 8.8 × 10¹² per cm² per second, so the dose takes about a second. Implantation is fast and well controlled.
Finally, the problem of counting atoms. A transistor channel 20 nm long, 20 nm wide and 10 nm deep has a volume of 4,000 nm³ = 4 × 10⁻¹⁸ cm³. At a channel doping of 10¹⁸ per cm³, it contains on average 4 dopant atoms. The count follows a Poisson distribution with a standard deviation of √4 = 2, which is 50%. Two transistors drawn identically differ by one or more dopant atoms, and so in threshold voltage. This is why such devices cannot rely on channel doping.
intuition: Seasoning a crystal with a pinch of an atom that has one electron too many or too few, so that the crystal conducts by whichever carrier you chose.
The trick works because the crystal is almost perfect. In a perfect crystal every electron is locked in a bond, so a single foreign atom is a large perturbation: its extra electron (or hole) is the only free carrier within a large neighbourhood. The effect on conduction is out of proportion with the amount added. Because the dopant levels sit close to the band edge, heat alone releases the carriers, and the number of carriers equals the number of dopant atoms, which an engineer can control by choosing the dose.
mistakes: People often think doped silicon is charged. In fact each dopant atom becomes a fixed ion, and the free carrier it releases carries an opposite charge, so the crystal as a whole stays neutral. Charge separation appears only near junctions, where the depletion region forms.
People often think a hole is only a lack of an electron and therefore not a real carrier. In fact a hole behaves as a particle with positive charge and its own effective mass and mobility (about 480 cm²/V·s in silicon, lower than the 1,350 of electrons). That difference is why n-channel transistors are faster than p-channel ones of the same size.
People often think that the more doping the better the conductor. In fact mobility falls as doping rises, because charged dopants scatter carriers, and the number of active carriers is limited by the solid solubility of the dopant (about 5 × 10²⁰ per cm³ for boron and 2 × 10²¹ for arsenic). Past that limit extra atoms cluster and do nothing. Heavy doping also narrows the band gap and makes leakage by tunnelling larger.
People often think the dopants are placed where the implanter aims. In fact atoms stop at a distribution of depths, scatter sideways, channel along crystal directions, and diffuse during the anneal, sometimes faster than normal near implant damage (transient enhanced diffusion). Engineers design with these profiles, not with sharp boxes.
tradeoffs: Choose heavy doping (10¹⁹ to 10²¹ per cm³) for source and drain regions and contacts, where you need low resistance and a good ohmic connection to metal. You give up mobility, accept some band-gap narrowing, and suffer higher junction leakage from tunnelling. Choose light doping (10¹⁴ to 10¹⁶) for regions that must carry a high voltage or have low capacitance: power devices use a lightly doped drift region of 10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ per cm³ to hold hundreds of volts. You give up conductivity: the resistance of that region is the main loss. Choose moderate doping (10¹⁷ to 10¹⁸) for wells and channels in older planar transistors, where it sets the threshold voltage.
Choose ion implantation when you need accurate dose and depth for most steps: it works at room temperature, is controlled by measuring beam current, and allows masking with resist. You give up a clean crystal: implants cause damage that needs annealing, and the anneal causes diffusion. Choose thermal diffusion for deep, wide, simple regions such as solar cells, where cost matters and depth control is not critical. Choose in-situ doped epitaxy for the source and drain of the smallest transistors, where abrupt profiles and activation above the implant limit matter more than the extra cost.
Choose a doped channel for planar transistors at about 28 nm and above, where it is cheap and the variation is acceptable. Choose an undoped or very lightly doped channel (FinFET, gate-all-around, fully depleted SOI) below about 22 nm, with the threshold set by the gate metal's work function instead of the dopants. You avoid the random dopant fluctuation and gain mobility, and you pay for a more complex gate stack and a three-dimensional structure.
Choose silicon for almost everything (abundant, stable oxide, cheap to dope), and silicon carbide or gallium nitride (band gaps 3.3 and 3.4 eV) for high voltage, high temperature and fast switching in power electronics, and give up easy doping: the aluminium acceptor in 4H-SiC sits about 0.2 eV above the valence band, so at room temperature only a part of it is ionized, and wafers are costly and have more defects.
For modelling, choose the classical picture (full ionization, Boltzmann statistics, uniform doping) for doping between about 10¹⁴ and 10¹⁸ per cm³ at room temperature. It fails above about 3 × 10¹⁸, when the electron density approaches the effective density of states (2.8 × 10¹⁹) and the material becomes degenerate (use Fermi–Dirac statistics); at low temperatures (below about 100 K), when carriers freeze out onto the donors; and in volumes with only a handful of dopant atoms, which need atomistic simulation.
friction: The main limit is variability and control at the nanometre scale. Random dopant fluctuations, diffusion of a few nanometres, and the need to keep dopants active while the thermal budget shrinks all constrain what shapes and profiles are possible. Making very shallow, very heavily doped junctions is hard: the dopant must be activated well above its solid solubility at times of milliseconds, and any extra heat spreads it. Resistance in the source, drain and contacts now limits the speed of the smallest transistors, and no further doping increase will fix it.
Temperature is another limit. Leakage from thermally generated carriers roughly doubles every 8 to 10 °C, so hot chips leak more. At very low temperatures, dopants freeze out. Materials limits matter as well: wide-band-gap semiconductors are hard to dope in one polarity (for example p-type in GaN and n-type in diamond).
practice: An engineer meets doping in three ways. In device and process specifications: a PDK or process datasheet lists sheet resistance, threshold voltages, junction capacitance and leakage, and each of these is set by doping. In yield work: threshold-voltage spread is partly dopant statistics, and it limits the minimum voltage at which SRAM works. In power and cooling design: leakage grows quickly with temperature, so a chip's power at 85 °C is not its power at 45 °C.
When you read about a new process node, ask where the doping is: which regions are heavily doped, and whether the channel is undoped. When you compare a power device, look at the drift-region doping and thickness, because they set the breakdown voltage and the on-resistance. For a feeling of the numbers, compute n, p, resistivity, the Fermi level and the built-in voltage for a few doping levels (10¹⁴, 10¹⁶, 10¹⁸) with a ten-line script, and check the results against a textbook plot of resistivity versus doping.
To study the topic, read Pierret's Semiconductor Device Fundamentals (the first chapters on carriers and doping), Streetman and Banerjee's Solid State Electronic Devices, or the free MIT OpenCourseWare course 6.012 Microelectronic Devices and Circuits. For process, Plummer, Deal and Griffin, Silicon VLSI Technology, covers implantation, diffusion and annealing in depth.
quiz: Why does adding one phosphorus atom per five million silicon atoms raise the conductivity so much? || Each phosphorus atom has a fifth valence electron bound by only about 45 meV, which room-temperature heat frees, so each atom adds one conduction electron. That raises the electron density from about 10¹⁰ to 10¹⁶ per cm³, a factor of a million.

What dose of phosphorus gives 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ in a 100 nm layer? || Dose = concentration × thickness = 10¹⁸ cm⁻³ × 10⁻⁵ cm = 10¹³ ions per cm².

The count varies as a Poisson variable with a standard deviation of 2 (50%), so the threshold voltage varies strongly between transistors. This is why modern small transistors use undoped channels.
You design a 600 V power diode. Do you use a heavily or lightly doped drift region, and what do you pay? || Lightly doped (about 10¹⁴ to 10¹⁵ cm⁻³) and thick, so the depletion region can hold the voltage. The price is higher on-resistance and more conduction loss, which is why SiC and GaN are used when losses matter.

@@ lithography
value: Photolithography is the step that draws a chip. A wafer of silicon is coated with a thin layer of a light-sensitive material (resist). Light shines through a stencil, the mask or reticle, and a lens shrinks the pattern by four times onto the wafer. Where the light fell, the resist changes chemically and can be washed away, and the pattern is then etched into the layers below or used to guide the implantation of dopants. A modern chip needs dozens of such layers, each aligned to the ones below with an error of about 1 to 2 nanometres.
The smallest feature that this process can print sets how small a transistor and a wire can be, and that decides how many transistors fit on a chip. For 50 years each new generation of lithography has made features smaller, and this is the physical engine under Moore's law. Today ASML of the Netherlands is the only company that makes the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) machines used for the finest layers. A machine costs on the order of 200 million euros and weighs about 180 tonnes. Access to these tools decides which companies can make leading-edge chips, which is why export controls on them are a matter of national policy.
This read stands on optics and photonics (L5). Light is a wave, and a lens focuses a wave to a spot of a finite size, set by diffraction: the shorter the wavelength and the wider the lens, the smaller the spot. Everything in lithography is an effort to use this law: use shorter light (from 365 nm to 248, 193 and 13.5 nm), make the lens wider (immersion in water, then larger mirrors), and make the pattern on the mask work with diffraction instead of against it. The transistor (L5) and Moore's law with Dennard scaling (L5) build on the result: lithography prints the transistors, and its progress made the scaling possible.
terms: Resist — a light-sensitive polymer film on the wafer; after exposure, either the lit parts (positive resist) or the dark parts (negative resist) dissolve in the developer.
Reticle (photomask) — a quartz plate, or for EUV a mirror, carrying the chip pattern, usually at 4 times the size on the wafer.
Numerical aperture (NA) — a measure of how wide a cone of light the lens collects; NA = n sin θ, where n is the refractive index of the medium next to the wafer.
Critical dimension (CD) — the smallest feature width being printed; the Rayleigh formula is CD = k₁ λ / NA.
k₁ — a process factor that includes resist, mask tricks and illumination; it cannot go below 0.25 for a single exposure.
Immersion — placing a thin layer of ultrapure water (n = 1.44 at 193 nm) between the last lens and the wafer, which raises NA above 1.
Multiple patterning — printing one layer with two to four exposures and etches, each at a coarser pitch, to reach a finer one than a single exposure can.
Overlay — how accurately one layer lines up with the layer below.
Stochastic defects — random failures (a missing contact, a broken line) caused by the small number of photons and molecules involved in a tiny feature.
history: In the early 1950s the transistor was made by hand. The first use of photography to define the pattern came in 1955 at Bell Laboratories, when Jules Andrus and Walter Bond used a photosensitive coating (Kodak's resist) to etch patterns in silicon. Jean Hoerni's planar process (1959) and the first integrated circuits then required patterning on a flat wafer. Early machines pressed the mask in contact with the wafer, which damaged both; projection printers (the Perkin-Elmer Micralign, 1973) removed that problem, and step-and-repeat machines (steppers) such as GCA's DSW 4800 (1978) exposed one die at a time with a reduction lens, to get finer detail. ASML was founded in 1984 as a joint venture of Philips and ASM International and later became the leader.
The wavelength dropped by steps as light sources improved: the mercury lamp lines at 436 and 365 nm, then krypton fluoride (KrF) excimer lasers at 248 nm in the 1990s, then argon fluoride (ArF) at 193 nm in the 2000s. An attempt to move to 157 nm failed because of material problems. Burn Lin at TSMC proposed immersion in 2002, and it entered production around 2006–2007, raising the numerical aperture beyond 1. When the limit of a single exposure at 193 nm was reached, chipmakers used multiple patterning to continue to the 10 nm class and below.
EUV took a long time. Hiroo Kinoshita in Japan (1986) and teams at Bell Labs showed the idea in the 1980s, and a US consortium, EUV LLC (Intel, AMD, Motorola and others with three national laboratories), began a development programme in 1997. The difficulties were large: no lens works at 13.5 nm, so all optics must be mirrors; the light is absorbed by air, so the machine must run in vacuum; and the source was weak. In 2012 Intel, TSMC and Samsung invested in ASML to fund the work, and in 2013 ASML bought Cymer, the light source maker. EUV entered volume production in 2019 (TSMC N7+, Samsung 7LPP). Intel received the first modules of the first High-NA machine in December 2023.
how: Follow one layer through a scanner. Light from the source passes through an illuminator that shapes it. It passes through (or, for EUV, reflects from) the reticle, whose chrome or absorber pattern blocks light in some places. The projection optics collect the diffracted light and form a 4 times smaller image on the wafer. The light's wave nature is central: a pattern of lines with pitch p diffracts light into orders at angles with sin θ = λ / p. The lens can only collect light up to its angle limit NA. To form any image of a line pattern, it must collect at least two diffraction orders, so λ / p ≤ NA × (1 + σ), where σ describes the illumination angle. With the best illumination this gives the smallest half-pitch λ / (4 NA), which is the k₁ = 0.25 limit of the Rayleigh formula CD = k₁ λ / NA.
So there are three ways to print smaller features: lower λ, raise NA, and lower k₁. Lower λ was the traditional route, and EUV is a jump of a factor of 14 from 193 nm. Raising NA means a larger lens or an index above that of air: immersion lithography fills the gap between the lens and the wafer with water, whose index of 1.44 raises the maximum NA from about 0.93 to 1.35. Lowering k₁ means everything that computational lithography does: off-axis illumination, phase-shifting masks, and optical proximity correction, where the mask pattern is deliberately distorted (with extra serifs and assist features) so that the printed shape comes out right after diffraction. The mask for a leading chip layer looks nothing like the pattern that it prints.
After exposure, the resist chemistry takes over. Most modern resists are chemically amplified: light creates a small amount of acid in a photoacid generator, and during a bake each acid molecule triggers many reactions that change the polymer's solubility. This gives high sensitivity, at the cost of acid diffusing a few nanometres, which blurs the image. A developer then removes the soluble part. The patterned resist protects some areas while plasma etching cuts the exposed layer, or while ions are implanted. Then the resist is stripped and the cycle repeats for the next layer, dozens of times for a leading logic chip.
The EUV machine solves several hard problems at once. The light is made by firing a stream of tin droplets about 25 micrometres wide, at 50,000 per second, through a chamber. Each droplet is hit by a first laser pulse that flattens it into a thin disc, and then by a powerful carbon dioxide laser pulse that turns it into a plasma at several hundred thousand kelvin, which radiates at 13.5 nm. A large collector mirror gathers the light. Every part of the path is in vacuum, because gas absorbs EUV, and hydrogen flow keeps tin debris off the mirrors. All optics are multilayer mirrors made of 40 to 50 alternating thin layers of molybdenum and silicon, each pair a half wavelength thick, so that the many weak reflections add in phase, which is Bragg reflection from photonics (L5). Each mirror reflects about 70% at best, and with a dozen mirrors from source to wafer only a few percent of the light survives (0.7¹⁰ ≈ 3%). Mirrors must be polished to a smoothness of tens of picometres.
High-NA EUV raises NA from 0.33 to 0.55, to print features about 8 nm wide instead of 13 nm. A larger NA with 4 times reduction would need huge mirrors and masks, so the optics are anamorphic: they reduce 4 times in one direction and 8 times in the other. This halves the field size, so a wafer needs twice as many exposures per layer, with the stage and the scan speed matched to keep throughput high. The depth of focus shrinks with NA², so the resist must be thin and the wafer must be extremely flat.

example: Apply the Rayleigh formula, CD = k₁ λ / NA. For ArF immersion, λ = 193 nm, NA = 1.35 and the best case k₁ = 0.25: CD = 0.25 × 193 / 1.35 = 35.7 nm half-pitch, so the finest lines in one exposure have a pitch of about 72 nm (in practice 76 to 80 nm). A chip layer with 40 nm pitch lines cannot be printed in one exposure. With self-aligned double patterning, a layer is first printed at 80 nm pitch, then spacers are built on both sides of each line, and the original lines are removed, which doubles the density to 40 nm pitch. Repeating gives 20 nm pitch (quadruple patterning). Each repeat costs extra deposition, etch and inspection steps.
Now EUV with NA 0.33 and k₁ = 0.32 (a typical practical value): CD = 0.32 × 13.5 / 0.33 = 13.1 nm half-pitch, about a 26 nm pitch, in one exposure. High-NA with NA 0.55: CD = 0.32 × 13.5 / 0.55 = 7.9 nm. The wavelength is 14 times shorter and the NA is about four times smaller, and the CD is still 2.7 times smaller than that of immersion.
Next, light. A photon at 13.5 nm has energy 1,239.8 eV·nm / 13.5 nm = 91.8 eV. A 193 nm photon has 6.4 eV. Suppose a resist needs a dose of 30 mJ/cm². The number of photons per cm² is 0.030 J / (91.8 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ J) = 2.0 × 10¹⁵, which is 20 photons per nm². At 193 nm the same dose gives 2.9 × 10¹⁶ per cm², or 291 per nm², 14 times more. Now consider a feature 5 nm × 5 nm = 25 nm² at the EUV dose. It receives 25 × 20 = 510 photons. If the resist absorbs 30%, about 150 absorb, and the random variation of the count is √150 ≈ 12, or 8%. This random shot noise appears as rough edges and sometimes missing contacts. To halve the noise you need four times the dose, which costs throughput.
Finally, the energy budget. A 300 mm wafer has an area of 706 cm². At 30 mJ/cm², exposing one layer needs 706 × 0.030 = 21 J delivered to the wafer. At 160 wafers per hour, the average EUV power at the wafer is 21 × 160 / 3,600 = 0.94 W. Because only about 3% of the light survives the mirrors and some time is lost to stage motion, the source must supply about 250 W at the intermediate focus, and the machine uses on the order of a megawatt of electricity. Delivering one watt of useful EUV light is that expensive.
intuition: Photographic printing with a very small slide and a very good lens, where the limit is not the lens but the wavelength of the light itself.
The limit is natural because light cannot be focused to a spot much smaller than its wavelength, so printing smaller means using smaller waves. The rest of the story is what it costs to do so: shorter waves are absorbed by everything, so lenses become mirrors, air becomes vacuum, and the light source becomes a plasma made by lasers hitting droplets of tin. Each solution creates the next problem. At each step, the pattern, the resist and the optics have to be designed together, because a pattern at the edge of resolution depends on all of them.
mistakes: People often think that a "3 nm chip" has features 3 nanometres wide. In fact the node name stopped being a physical measurement long ago and is a marketing label for a generation. Typical minimum metal pitches for "3 nm" chips are about 20 to 30 nm, and gate pitches are around 45 nm.
People often think that EUV simply replaced the old machines. In fact DUV immersion machines still print most of the layers of a leading chip, and EUV is used for the finest few layers, where it replaces several DUV steps. EUV tools are used together with DUV tools in the same flow.
People often think the lens is the heart of an EUV machine. In fact there are no lenses at 13.5 nm. The optics are mirrors made of nanometre-thick layers, and the light source is as hard as the optics. Both are produced by suppliers (Zeiss for optics, Trumpf for the laser, Cymer within ASML for the source).
tradeoffs: Choose multi-patterning with DUV immersion when you already own the tools and the layer pitch is moderate (about 40 nm and above for a double patterning, 20 nm with quadruple). The machines are cheaper (on the order of 50 to 100 million euros) and fast (close to 300 wafers per hour), but each extra pass adds masks, etch steps, overlay error and defects, so the cost per layer grows with the number of passes. Choose single-exposure EUV when the pitch is below about 40 nm and the layer would otherwise need three or four passes: it replaces several steps, shortens cycle time and improves yield per step. You give up cost per tool and throughput of 160 or so wafers per hour, plus a high energy use, a dose that is limited by stochastic effects, and a smaller set of suppliers.
Choose High-NA EUV (0.55) when the layer is so fine that low-NA EUV would need two exposures, or when the added resolution lets you simplify the flow. You give up a tool that costs roughly twice as much, a half-size field (a die larger than half of the usual field needs stitching), a thinner depth of focus, and a less mature ecosystem. Choose low-NA EUV with double patterning when it is cheaper than moving to High-NA, as long as the extra layer count is acceptable. This economic choice, not the physics, is what chipmakers actually argue about.
Choose a higher dose when defects from stochastic effects dominate, and a lower dose when throughput does. Doubling the dose reduces photon noise by about 30% (a factor of √2) and halves the number of wafers per hour on a source-limited tool. Choose a chemically amplified resist for sensitivity, and a metal-oxide resist for resolution and etch resistance, accepting the cost and contamination issues of metal-containing materials.
Choose alternatives only in niches: nanoimprint lithography (Canon has shipped a tool, and Kioxia has tested it for memory) stamps the pattern and promises low cost and low energy, but suffers from defects and overlay; electron-beam writing gives a very fine resolution and is used to make masks, but it is far too slow for wafers. For models, the Rayleigh formula is a good guide at the first order. It breaks down near k₁ = 0.25 where vector and 3D mask effects, resist chemistry and stochastics matter, and there you need full simulation of the optics and of the resist.
friction: Cost and concentration are the first problem. One company makes the EUV tools, with a handful of suppliers inside it, and only three companies (TSMC, Samsung and Intel) use them for leading-edge logic, together with the main DRAM makers. A new fab costs tens of billions of dollars. Export controls apply: no EUV tool has been sold to China, and the Netherlands and the US also restrict the most advanced immersion DUV tools. That makes lithography a lever of national industrial policy.
Physics also pushes back. Stochastic defects limit the dose and resolution of EUV, so engineers trade throughput for yield. Masks need defect-free multilayers, and the inspection and repair of EUV masks and pellicles is hard. Overlay of 1 to 2 nm must be kept while the wafer heats up under the exposure. Edge roughness of a few nanometres is now a large part of the feature width. The energy used per wafer is high, and the supply of helium, hydrogen and rare gases, and of the lasers and mirrors, can limit production.
practice: Most engineers will never run a scanner, but they meet its effects. When you read about a process node, ask three things: which layers use EUV, what pitch is printed in a single exposure, and what the cost per wafer is. These determine the price and the supply of chips, and therefore the cost of accelerators and servers. When you plan hardware for a data centre or CTV devices, remember that capacity of leading-edge chips depends on a small number of fabs and tools, and lead times can be long.
To learn the subject, start with ASML's free explanations of how lithography works, then Chris Mack's Fundamental Principles of Optical Lithography, which derives the Rayleigh limit and the process window, and Chris Miller's Chip War for the history and the policy. For the physics of the source and the optics, use the SPIE EUV proceedings and Bakshi's EUV Lithography. A useful exercise is to compute CD, depth of focus, and photon counts for three systems in a spreadsheet and see why each step required a new technology.
