@@ eigen
value: An eigenvector of a square matrix \( A \) is a direction that the matrix does not turn. When \( A \) acts on it, the vector comes out on the same line, only longer, shorter or flipped. The factor is the eigenvalue \( \lambda \). In symbols, \( A\mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v} \) with \( \mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0} \). A general matrix mixes and rotates every vector in a complicated way. Along its eigenvectors it is just multiplication by a number.
This matters because most questions about a linear system are questions about repeated multiplication. What does a web surfer's position look like after 50 clicks? Will a feedback loop settle or blow up? Which direction carries most of the variance in a data set? If you write a starting vector as a sum of eigenvectors, then applying \( A \) a hundred times is just raising each eigenvalue to the hundredth power. A hard matrix problem turns into a list of one-number problems, and the sizes of the eigenvalues tell you the long-term story directly.
For the layers above, this is the engine under several well-known tools. PageRank is the leading eigenvector of a link matrix. PCA finds eigenvectors of a covariance matrix. A Markov chain converges to an eigenvector of its transition matrix, and the second eigenvalue sets the speed. A linear control loop or a linear difference equation is stable when all its eigenvalues are small enough. The layers below supply the meaning: linear algebra gives us matrices, bases and the determinant, and the idea of a matrix as a function (relations and functions) tells us that "a matrix has fixed directions" is a statement about a map.
terms: Eigenvector — a nonzero vector \( \mathbf{v} \) such that \( A\mathbf{v} \) is a scalar multiple of \( \mathbf{v} \).
Eigenvalue — the scalar \( \lambda \) in \( A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} \); how much the matrix stretches that direction.
Characteristic polynomial — \( \det(A - \lambda I) \); its roots are exactly the eigenvalues.
Eigenspace — all eigenvectors for one eigenvalue, plus the zero vector; it is a subspace.
Diagonalizable — a matrix that has a full set of independent eigenvectors, so \( A = V\Lambda V^{-1} \) with \( \Lambda \) diagonal.
Spectrum — the set of all eigenvalues of a matrix.
Spectral radius — the largest absolute value among the eigenvalues, written \( \rho(A) \); it controls long-run growth.
Symmetric matrix — a matrix with \( A = A^\top \); it has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors.
Power iteration — repeatedly multiplying a vector by \( A \) and rescaling; it converges to the dominant eigenvector.
history: The idea came from geometry before it had a name. In the 1700s Euler studied the axes about which a rigid body spins freely, and Lagrange studied the principal axes of quadratic surfaces. Both are eigenvector problems. In 1829 Cauchy showed that a real symmetric matrix has real "characteristic roots", which settled the question for the matrices that appear in mechanics and geometry.
The word comes later. David Hilbert used the German word "eigen" (own, or characteristic) in 1904, in his work on integral operators, and "eigenvalue" spread from there. Quantum mechanics made the idea famous around 1925 to 1926: in Heisenberg's and Schrödinger's theories, measurable quantities such as energy levels are eigenvalues of operators. This is why physicists treat the word as ordinary.
Computation came next. Richard von Mises described power iteration in 1929. Jacobi had an eigenvalue method for symmetric matrices from 1846. Cornelius Lanczos (1950) gave an iteration for large sparse matrices. The big step was the QR algorithm, found independently by John Francis and Vera Kublanovskaya around 1959 to 1961. It is still the basis of dense eigenvalue routines in LAPACK, and it is often listed among the most important algorithms of the 20th century. Perron (1907) and Frobenius (1912) proved the key facts about matrices with positive entries, which later explained why PageRank has a unique answer. In 1998 Page and Brin made the dominant eigenvector a business.
how: Start with the definition and why it forces a polynomial. We want a nonzero \( \mathbf{v} \) with \( A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} \). Move everything to one side: \( (A - \lambda I)\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{0} \). A nonzero solution exists only if the matrix \( A - \lambda I \) is singular, that is, if it squashes some direction to zero. From linear algebra we know this happens exactly when the determinant is zero. So the eigenvalues are the roots of
\[ \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 \]
For an \( n \times n \) matrix this is a polynomial of degree \( n \), so there are \( n \) eigenvalues counted with multiplicity, though some may be complex. For each eigenvalue, the eigenvectors are the null space of \( A - \lambda I \), which is again linear algebra: solve a homogeneous system by row reduction. Two facts help with checking work: the sum of the eigenvalues equals the trace of \( A \) (sum of diagonal entries), and their product equals \( \det A \).
Now the main payoff. Suppose \( A \) has \( n \) independent eigenvectors. Put them as columns of a matrix \( V \), and put the eigenvalues on the diagonal of \( \Lambda \). Then \( AV = V\Lambda \), so
\[ A = V \Lambda V^{-1}, \qquad A^k = V \Lambda^k V^{-1} \]
Read the second formula as a recipe. Step 1: change coordinates with \( V^{-1} \), so the vector is written in eigenvector coordinates. Step 2: scale each coordinate by \( \lambda_i^k \). Step 3: change back with \( V \). Here the "matrix is a function" view helps: \( A \) is one linear map, and \( V \) is just a change of basis in which that map looks diagonal. The map did not change; we only chose coordinates in which it is simple. The same trick gives the matrix exponential \( e^{At} = V e^{\Lambda t} V^{-1} \), which solves linear differential equations \( \dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} \).
The size of the eigenvalues then decides the long run. If \( |\lambda_i| < 1 \) for all \( i \), then \( A^k \mathbf{x} \to 0 \) (a stable difference equation). If one eigenvalue has the largest absolute value, \( |\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| \ge \dots \), then for large \( k \) the term \( \lambda_1^k \) dominates and \( A^k\mathbf{x} \) points almost along \( \mathbf{v}_1 \). The error shrinks like \( (|\lambda_2|/|\lambda_1|)^k \). This is why power iteration works, why PageRank iterations converge, and why a Markov chain's mixing speed is set by its second eigenvalue. For differential equations the condition is on the real parts: stable if every eigenvalue has a negative real part.
Which matrices behave well? A symmetric real matrix is the best case (the spectral theorem): all eigenvalues are real, eigenvectors for different eigenvalues are orthogonal, and \( V \) can be chosen with orthonormal columns, so \( V^{-1} = V^\top \) and \( A = Q\Lambda Q^\top \). Covariance matrices are symmetric and positive semi-definite, so their eigenvalues are real and non-negative, and they are the variances along the principal axes. That is exactly PCA. General matrices can fail in two ways. A rotation matrix has complex eigenvalues (no real direction is preserved). A "shear" such as \( \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \) has a repeated eigenvalue 1 but only one eigenvector direction, so it is not diagonalizable; the nearest replacement is the Jordan form, where powers grow like \( k\lambda^{k-1} \) instead of \( \lambda^k \).
How are eigenvalues computed? Nobody solves the characteristic polynomial for \( n > 4 \): there is no closed formula for degree five and above (Abel and Ruffini), and the roots of a polynomial are very sensitive to rounding. Practical methods are iterative. Power iteration multiplies by \( A \) and normalizes; it finds the dominant pair only. The QR algorithm factors \( A_k = Q_k R_k \) and sets \( A_{k+1} = R_k Q_k \); this is a similarity transform, so eigenvalues stay the same, and the matrix converges to a triangular form with eigenvalues on the diagonal. After first reducing to Hessenberg form, one iteration costs \( O(n^2) \) and the whole dense solve about \( O(n^3) \). For huge sparse matrices (the web graph, a graph Laplacian) you use Lanczos or Arnoldi, which need only matrix-vector products. This is the link to numerical linear algebra.

example: Take \( A = \begin{pmatrix}4&1\\2&3\end{pmatrix} \). The trace is 7 and the determinant is \( 4\cdot3 - 1\cdot2 = 10 \). The characteristic equation is \( \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 10 = 0 \), which factors as \( (\lambda-5)(\lambda-2) = 0 \). So \( \lambda_1 = 5 \) and \( \lambda_2 = 2 \). Check: they add to 7 (trace) and multiply to 10 (determinant).
Find the eigenvectors. For \( \lambda = 5 \), \( A - 5I = \begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\2&-2\end{pmatrix} \). The first row says \( -x + y = 0 \), so \( \mathbf{v}_1 = (1,1) \). Test: \( A(1,1) = (5,5) = 5\cdot(1,1) \). For \( \lambda = 2 \), \( A - 2I = \begin{pmatrix}2&1\\2&1\end{pmatrix} \), so \( 2x + y = 0 \) and \( \mathbf{v}_2 = (1,-2) \). Test: \( A(1,-2) = (4-2, 2-6) = (2,-4) = 2\cdot(1,-2) \).
Now use them. Start with \( \mathbf{x}_0 = (3,0) \). Write it in eigenvector coordinates: \( a(1,1) + b(1,-2) = (3,0) \) gives \( a + b = 3 \) and \( a - 2b = 0 \), so \( a = 2 \) and \( b = 1 \). Then
\[ A^k \mathbf{x}_0 = 2\cdot 5^k\,(1,1) + 1\cdot 2^k\,(1,-2) \]
For \( k = 3 \): \( 2\cdot125\cdot(1,1) + 8\cdot(1,-2) = (250+8,\ 250-16) = (258, 234) \). Check by brute force: \( A\mathbf{x}_0 = (12, 6) \), then \( A(12,6) = (54, 42) \), then \( A(54,42) = (258, 234) \). The two methods agree. For \( k = 10 \), the formula gives \( (2\cdot 9765625 + 1024,\ 2\cdot9765625 - 2048) = (19532274,\ 19529202) \) in one step, without ten multiplications. The two components differ by only 0.016%. The direction of \( \mathbf{x}_k \) has become the direction of \( \mathbf{v}_1 \), and each further step multiplies the length by about 5. The ratio \( 2/5 = 0.4 \) per step is how fast the second direction fades relative to the first.
To see why this is the PageRank idea, replace \( A \) by a column-stochastic matrix (columns sum to 1) such as \( \begin{pmatrix}0.9&0.5\\0.1&0.5\end{pmatrix} \). Its eigenvalues are 1 and 0.4. The eigenvector for 1 is proportional to \( (5,1) \), so the long-run distribution is \( (5/6, 1/6) \). Any start converges there, and the error shrinks by a factor 0.4 per step.
intuition: Think of a stretching rubber sheet: almost every line drawn on it gets rotated, but a few special lines only get longer or shorter, and those lines tell you everything about the stretch.
Most vectors are pushed and turned by a matrix in ways that are hard to follow over many steps. The eigenvectors are the exception: following them is trivial, because after one step the vector is the same arrow with a different length. If the matrix has enough such arrows to describe every vector, then following any vector means following a few numbers that never interact. That is why the same idea appears wherever "do the same linear thing again and again" appears: surfing links, moving through a Markov chain, stepping a simulation, or running a feedback loop.
mistakes: People often think every matrix has real eigenvectors. In fact a rotation by 90 degrees in the plane moves every real vector off its line, so its eigenvalues are \( \pm i \) and there is no real eigenvector. Complex eigenvalues are normal for real matrices; they show up as oscillation.
People often think the eigenvalues of \( A + B \) are the sums of the eigenvalues, or that those of \( AB \) are the products. In fact this holds only when the matrices share eigenvectors (for example when they commute). In general there is no simple rule, which is why you cannot compute the spectrum of a sum from the parts.
People often think every square matrix can be diagonalized. In fact a matrix needs \( n \) independent eigenvectors. A shear has one repeated eigenvalue and only one direction, so it cannot. Defective matrices are rare in random data but common in structured problems, and they cause polynomial growth factors such as \( k\lambda^k \).
People often think small eigenvalues mean the matrix is safe to use. In fact for non-symmetric matrices the eigenvectors can be nearly parallel, so the system can grow a lot for many steps before it decays (transient growth), even though all \( |\lambda_i| < 1 \). For such matrices singular values and pseudo-spectra tell the real story, not eigenvalues alone.
friction: The first limit is that eigenvalues describe long-run behaviour and exact linear maps. For symmetric matrices that is nearly everything. For non-normal matrices (those where \( AA^\top \ne A^\top A \)) eigenvalues can be extremely sensitive: a tiny change in an entry can move them far, and the eigenvectors can be nearly dependent. A classic example is a matrix with ones just above the diagonal, where changing one corner entry by \( 10^{-10} \) moves the eigenvalues by about \( 0.1 \) for \( n = 10 \).
The second limit is cost. Dense eigendecomposition is \( O(n^3) \): for \( n = 10{,}000 \) that is about \( 10^{12} \) floating-point operations times a constant near 10 to 25, which takes minutes on a multicore CPU and needs 800 MB for the matrix alone in double precision. For a web graph with billions of nodes you can only do matrix-vector products, so you accept a few leading eigenvectors computed by power iteration, Lanczos or Arnoldi, and you accept a convergence rate set by the gap \( |\lambda_2|/|\lambda_1| \). When that gap is close to 1, convergence is slow, and when eigenvalues are repeated or clustered, eigenvectors are not even unique.
The third limit is that eigenvectors are only defined up to a scale (and sign), and, for repeated eigenvalues, up to any basis of the eigenspace. Code that compares eigenvectors between runs, for example comparing PCA components across two training runs, must normalize signs or compare subspaces, not vectors. Finally, the whole story is linear. Real systems with non-linear updates need linearization near a fixed point (the Jacobian's eigenvalues) and are valid only locally.
practice: In day-to-day engineering you meet eigenvalues through four doors. PCA and covariance analysis: the eigenvalues are variances along principal directions, and the ratio of the top \( k \) eigenvalues to their total tells you how much structure \( k \) components keep. Graph and Markov problems: PageRank, random walks on graphs, spectral clustering with the Laplacian, and mixing time of samplers. Stability: for \( \mathbf{x}_{t+1} = A\mathbf{x}_t \) check \( \rho(A) < 1 \); for \( \dot{\mathbf{x}} = A\mathbf{x} \) check that all real parts are negative. Optimisation: the eigenvalues of the Hessian describe curvature, and the ratio of largest to smallest sets how slowly gradient descent converges.
In code, use `numpy.linalg.eigh` for symmetric matrices (it is faster and returns real, sorted eigenvalues with orthonormal vectors) and `numpy.linalg.eig` only for general ones. For large sparse matrices use `scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh` (symmetric) or `eigs`, and ask only for the top few. Do not compute eigenvalues via the characteristic polynomial. When you need the principal directions of data, prefer the SVD of the centred data matrix, which avoids forming the covariance matrix and squaring its sensitivity.
To study this well, work three examples by hand: a \( 2\times2 \) matrix like the one above, a symmetric matrix with a repeated eigenvalue, and a rotation. Then read the first eigenvalue chapters of Strang's lectures and Trefethen and Bau for the numerical side.
tradeoffs: Choose `eigh` (symmetric eigendecomposition) when the matrix is symmetric, such as a covariance matrix or graph Laplacian; it is about two to three times faster than the general routine and gives real, orthogonal output. Choose the general `eig` only when the matrix is truly non-symmetric, for example a Markov transition matrix, and be ready for complex results and for nearly dependent eigenvectors.
Choose a full decomposition when \( n \) is a few thousand or less and you need all eigenpairs. Choose iterative methods (power iteration, Lanczos, Arnoldi) when \( n \) is large and the matrix is sparse and you need only the top few pairs; you pay with convergence that depends on the eigenvalue gap, and with no guarantee for the small end of the spectrum without shift-and-invert tricks.
Choose eigenvalues when the question is about repeated application of one matrix (powers, stability, steady state). Choose singular values (SVD) when the matrix is rectangular, or non-normal, or when you care about the worst-case amplification of one application; singular values never give complex surprises and are numerically more stable.
Choose a closed form \( V\Lambda^kV^{-1} \) when you need many different powers or a symbolic answer, for example the \( n \)-th term of a recurrence. Choose repeated multiplication when \( k \) is small or the matrix is badly conditioned: forming \( V^{-1} \) with nearly parallel eigenvectors loses precision.
quiz: What is the defining equation of an eigenvector and eigenvalue? || \( A\mathbf{v} = \lambda\mathbf{v} \) with \( \mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0} \): the matrix maps \( \mathbf{v} \) to a multiple of itself. The eigenvalues are the roots of \( \det(A - \lambda I) = 0 \).
Why does \( A = V\Lambda V^{-1} \) make \( A^{100} \) cheap? || The inner factors \( V^{-1}V \) cancel, so \( A^{100} = V\Lambda^{100}V^{-1} \). Raising a diagonal matrix to a power only raises each diagonal entry to that power, which is 100 scalar powers instead of 99 matrix products.
Why does the second eigenvalue control how fast power iteration or a Markov chain converges? || After \( k \) steps the component along \( \mathbf{v}_i \) is scaled by \( \lambda_i^k \). Relative to the top component, the next one shrinks like \( (|\lambda_2|/|\lambda_1|)^k \), so a gap close to 1 means slow convergence.
A matrix has trace 7 and determinant 12. What are its eigenvalues? || They sum to 7 and multiply to 12, so they solve \( \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 12 = 0 \): \( \lambda = 3 \) and \( \lambda = 4 \).
You need the top 5 eigenvectors of a sparse 50-million-node graph matrix. Dense `eig` or an iterative method, and why? || Iterative (Lanczos or Arnoldi, or power iteration). A dense decomposition needs \( O(n^3) \) time and \( n^2 \) memory, which is impossible at this size, while iterative methods need only sparse matrix-vector products and give the few leading pairs.

@@ svd
value: The singular value decomposition says that every matrix, of any shape, can be written as a rotation, then a stretch along coordinate axes, then another rotation. In symbols, \( A = U\Sigma V^\top \), where \( U \) and \( V \) have orthonormal columns and \( \Sigma \) is diagonal with non-negative entries \( \sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0 \), called the singular values. Eigen-decomposition only works for some square matrices. The SVD works for all matrices, including rectangular data tables, and it is numerically very well behaved.
The reason people use it is the best-approximation property. If you keep only the \( r \) largest singular values and set the rest to zero, you get a matrix of rank \( r \) that is the closest possible rank-\( r \) matrix to \( A \). So a table of one million rows and 5,000 columns can often be replaced by two thin matrices that capture most of its structure, and the sizes of the discarded singular values tell you exactly how much you lose.
For the layers above, SVD is the computational core of PCA (the principal components are the right singular vectors of the centred data), of matrix factorization for recommenders (the Netflix-style user-by-item table), of latent semantic indexing in search, and of the low-rank idea behind LoRA fine-tuning. The layers below give it its meaning: linear algebra provides orthogonality, rank and the idea that a matrix is a linear map between spaces, and eigenvalue theory explains where the numbers come from, since the singular values are square roots of eigenvalues of \( A^\top A \).
terms: Singular value — a number \( \sigma_i \ge 0 \) on the diagonal of \( \Sigma \); it is the stretch factor along the \( i \)-th pair of singular directions.
Left singular vectors — the columns of \( U \); orthonormal directions in the output space.
Right singular vectors — the columns of \( V \); orthonormal directions in the input space.
Rank — the number of nonzero singular values; the dimension of the output the matrix can actually reach.
Truncated SVD — keeping only the top \( r \) singular values and vectors: \( A_r = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top \).
Frobenius norm — \( \|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{ij} a_{ij}^2} \), which also equals \( \sqrt{\sum_i \sigma_i^2} \).
Spectral norm — \( \|A\|_2 \), the largest stretch of any unit vector; it equals \( \sigma_1 \).
Condition number — \( \sigma_1/\sigma_n \) for an invertible matrix; how much the matrix can amplify relative errors.
Pseudo-inverse — \( A^+ = V\Sigma^+U^\top \), built by inverting only the nonzero singular values.
history: The decomposition was found several times in the 1870s by people studying quadratic forms. Eugenio Beltrami published it in 1873 for real square matrices, and Camille Jordan found it independently in 1874. James Joseph Sylvester contributed in 1889, and Erhard Schmidt in 1907 worked on the version for integral operators, where he also stated the approximation property. Carl Eckart and Gale Young proved in 1936 that truncating the SVD gives the best low-rank approximation (Mirsky later extended it to every norm that depends only on singular values). Their paper appeared in the journal Psychometrika, so the first use was already about finding a few factors in a table of test scores.
For a long time it was too expensive to compute. The turning point was Gene Golub and William Kahan (1965), who showed how to reduce a matrix to bidiagonal form with Householder reflections and then find the singular values with a QR-type iteration. Golub and Reinsch (1970) turned it into the algorithm that became the standard in LINPACK and later LAPACK.
Applications then grew. Deerwester and colleagues (1990) used truncated SVD of a term-document matrix for latent semantic indexing in search. In the Netflix Prize (2006 to 2009), SVD-like factorization of the ratings matrix was the central idea of the best models. Halko, Martinsson and Tropp (2011) showed that randomized sketching can find the top singular vectors of huge matrices in a few passes over the data. Since 2021 low-rank updates in neural networks (LoRA, by Hu and colleagues) rely on the same approximation idea.
how: Start with the geometry. Take the unit sphere in the input space and apply \( A \). The image is an ellipsoid. Its longest axis has length \( \sigma_1 \), its next axis \( \sigma_2 \), and so on, and these axes point along the left singular vectors \( \mathbf{u}_i \). The input directions that land on those axes are the right singular vectors \( \mathbf{v}_i \). So \( A\mathbf{v}_i = \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \). Putting this for all \( i \) together gives \( AV = U\Sigma \), and since \( V \) is orthogonal, \( A = U\Sigma V^\top \). Read it right to left: \( V^\top \) rotates the input to the right axes, \( \Sigma \) stretches each axis, \( U \) rotates the result into the output space.
Why do such directions exist? Here is where eigenvalues enter. The matrix \( A^\top A \) is symmetric and positive semi-definite (since \( \mathbf{x}^\top A^\top A \mathbf{x} = \|A\mathbf{x}\|^2 \ge 0 \)). By the spectral theorem, from the eigenvalue concept, it has real non-negative eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors. Call them \( \lambda_i \) and \( \mathbf{v}_i \). Then
\[ \sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^\top A)}, \qquad \mathbf{u}_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A\mathbf{v}_i \]
The \( \mathbf{u}_i \) come out orthonormal because \( \mathbf{u}_i^\top\mathbf{u}_j = \mathbf{v}_i^\top A^\top A\mathbf{v}_j/(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = \lambda_j \mathbf{v}_i^\top \mathbf{v}_j/(\sigma_i\sigma_j) = 0 \) for \( i \ne j \). So the SVD of \( A \) is hidden inside the eigendecomposition of \( A^\top A \) (and also of \( AA^\top \), which has the same nonzero eigenvalues). This is the link that the map records. The linear algebra supplies the symmetric structure, the eigenvalue theory supplies the real, orthogonal answer.
The main theorem is Eckart–Young–Mirsky. Write \( A = \sum_i \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^\top \), a sum of rank-one pieces ordered from the largest to the smallest. Keep the first \( r \):
\[ A_r = \sum_{i=1}^{r}\sigma_i\,\mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^\top, \qquad \|A - A_r\|_2 = \sigma_{r+1}, \qquad \|A - A_r\|_F = \sqrt{\textstyle\sum_{i>r}\sigma_i^2} \]
No other rank-\( r \) matrix is closer in either norm. The error is exactly the size of what you discarded, so you can choose \( r \) from the singular value list: keep enough that the discarded energy \( \sum_{i>r}\sigma_i^2 \) is, say, below 1% of \( \sum_i \sigma_i^2 \).
Storage gives the practical motive. An \( m\times n \) matrix of rank \( r \) can be stored as \( U_r \) (\( m\times r \)), \( \sigma \) (\( r \) numbers) and \( V_r \) (\( n \times r \)), which is \( r(m+n+1) \) numbers instead of \( mn \). A \( 1000\times1000 \) matrix at rank 20 needs 40,020 numbers instead of 1,000,000, about 4%. Multiplying a vector by the factored form costs \( r(m+n) \) instead of \( mn \).
Computation follows the classical route. Do not form \( A^\top A \) in code: it squares the condition number (the ratio \( \sigma_1/\sigma_n \) becomes \( \sigma_1^2/\sigma_n^2 \)), so small singular values drown in rounding noise. Instead, reduce \( A \) to bidiagonal form with Householder reflections (cost about \( 4mn^2 \) operations for \( m \ge n \)), then run an implicit QR iteration on the bidiagonal matrix. This is backward stable, which the numerical linear algebra concept explains. For huge matrices with a fast-decaying spectrum, randomized SVD multiplies \( A \) by a thin random matrix, orthonormalizes the result to find the dominant range, and decomposes the small projected matrix; the cost is roughly \( O(mn\log r) \) or \( O(mnr) \) instead of \( O(mn^2) \).
Three other uses follow from the same formula. The pseudo-inverse \( A^+ = V\Sigma^+U^\top \) gives the minimum-norm least-squares solution of any linear system, even when \( A \) has no inverse. The condition number \( \sigma_1/\sigma_n \) says how much input errors can grow. And for PCA: centre the data matrix \( X \) (subtract column means); then the right singular vectors of \( X \) are the principal directions and \( \sigma_i^2/(m-1) \) are the variances, with no need to build the covariance matrix.

example: Let \( A = \begin{pmatrix}3&0\\4&5\end{pmatrix} \). First compute \( A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix}25&20\\20&25\end{pmatrix} \). Its trace is 50 and determinant is \( 625-400=225 \), so the eigenvalues solve \( \lambda^2 - 50\lambda + 225 = 0 \), giving \( \lambda = 45 \) and \( \lambda = 5 \). The singular values are therefore \( \sigma_1 = \sqrt{45} \approx 6.708 \) and \( \sigma_2 = \sqrt5 \approx 2.236 \). Check with the determinant: \( |\det A| = 15 \) and \( \sigma_1\sigma_2 = \sqrt{225} = 15 \). Also \( \|A\|_F^2 = 9+0+16+25 = 50 = 45 + 5 \).
Now the vectors. The eigenvector of \( A^\top A \) for 45 is \( (1,1)/\sqrt2 = \mathbf{v}_1 \), and for 5 it is \( (1,-1)/\sqrt2 = \mathbf{v}_2 \). Compute \( A\mathbf{v}_1 = (3, 9)/\sqrt2 \), so \( \mathbf{u}_1 = A\mathbf{v}_1/\sigma_1 = (3,9)/(\sqrt2\sqrt{45}) = (1,3)/\sqrt{10} \). Compute \( A\mathbf{v}_2 = (3,-1)/\sqrt2 \), so \( \mathbf{u}_2 = (3,-1)/\sqrt{10} \). The vectors \( \mathbf{u}_1,\mathbf{u}_2 \) are orthogonal: \( 3 - 3 = 0 \).
Rank-1 approximation: \( A_1 = \sigma_1\mathbf{u}_1\mathbf{v}_1^\top = \sqrt{45}\cdot\frac{(1,3)^\top(1,1)}{\sqrt{10}\sqrt2} = \frac{\sqrt{45}}{\sqrt{20}}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\3&3\end{pmatrix} = 1.5\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\3&3\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}1.5&1.5\\4.5&4.5\end{pmatrix} \). The error matrix is \( A - A_1 = \begin{pmatrix}1.5&-1.5\\-0.5&0.5\end{pmatrix} \). Its squared Frobenius norm is \( 2.25+2.25+0.25+0.25 = 5 \), so the error is \( \sqrt5 = \sigma_2 \), exactly as the theorem says. The rank-1 matrix keeps \( 45/50 = 90\% \) of the squared size of \( A \).
A larger picture: a ratings table of 10,000 users by 2,000 films has \( 2\times10^7 \) entries. If the top 30 singular values hold 85% of the energy, the rank-30 factorization stores \( 30\times(10{,}000+2{,}000+1) \approx 360{,}000 \) numbers, 1.8% of the table, and each user becomes a point in 30 dimensions. The user vector times the film vector (weighted by \( \sigma \)) predicts a rating. That is the basis of SVD-style recommenders, with the practical change that missing ratings are not treated as zeros.
intuition: A matrix is a machine that turns a round ball into an ellipsoid; the singular values are the lengths of the ellipsoid's axes, and cutting off the short axes gives the best flatter copy.
The idea is natural once you ask where a linear map does its work. Most of the "action" of a data matrix is in a few directions, and the rest is small variation or noise. The SVD sorts the directions by importance and cleanly separates them: because the directions are orthogonal, the contribution of each rank-one piece is independent, and the squared sizes simply add up. That is why dropping the smallest pieces is provably the best you can do, and why the error is known in advance.
mistakes: People often think the SVD and the eigendecomposition are the same thing. In fact eigenvectors need a square matrix, can be complex, and need not be orthogonal. Singular vectors exist for every matrix, are real for real matrices, and are always orthonormal. They coincide only for symmetric positive semi-definite matrices.
People often think the largest singular value is the "most important" feature of the data in every sense. In fact singular values measure variance or energy, which depends on scaling. If one column is measured in large units, it dominates the first singular vector. Standardize columns first when units differ, and centre the data before using the SVD for PCA.
People often think low-rank truncation can be applied to a table with missing values by filling in zeros. In fact zeros are values, and the SVD will try to reproduce them. Recommender factorization must fit only the known entries (alternating least squares or gradient descent with regularization), so it is related to SVD but is not a plain SVD of the table.
People often think computing \( A^\top A \) and taking its eigenvalues is a fine way to get the SVD. In fact this squares the condition number. A singular value of \( 10^{-9} \) relative to the largest becomes \( 10^{-18} \) in \( A^\top A \), below double-precision rounding (about \( 10^{-16} \)), so it is lost. Use a proper SVD routine.
friction: The cost is the first problem. A dense full SVD of an \( m\times n \) matrix is \( O(mn\min(m,n)) \). For a \( 10^5 \times 10^4 \) matrix that is about \( 10^{13} \) operations times a small constant, which takes minutes to hours and \( 8 \) GB for the matrix itself. For bigger data you need truncated or randomized methods, which give only the top \( r \) components and have error that depends on how fast the spectrum decays. If the singular values decay slowly (as for noisy or generic data), no small rank captures the table and the low-rank assumption simply fails.
Interpretation is the second problem. Singular vectors are linear combinations with positive and negative entries, so they rarely correspond to a human concept. They are also not unique: when two singular values are equal, any rotation inside their plane is valid, and every singular vector pair can flip sign together. Do not compare raw singular vectors across runs; compare subspaces.
Third, SVD finds the best linear low-rank fit under squared error. It is sensitive to outliers (one extreme row can claim the first component), and it cannot model non-linear structure such as a curved manifold. Robust PCA, non-negative factorization and autoencoders are used where those limits matter. Finally, streaming and changing data are hard: updating an SVD when one row arrives is possible with incremental methods, but errors accumulate.
practice: Use `numpy.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)` for dense matrices: the economy form returns only the \( \min(m,n) \) useful columns and saves memory. For large sparse or tall data use `scipy.sparse.linalg.svds`, scikit-learn's `TruncatedSVD` (which works on sparse term-document matrices without centring) or `randomized_svd`. In Spark MLlib, `RowMatrix.computeSVD` and `PCA` handle distributed tall-and-skinny matrices (many rows, a modest number of columns).
Choose the rank by looking at the singular value plot (a "scree" plot) for a clear elbow, or by the cumulative energy \( \sum_{i\le r}\sigma_i^2/\sum_i\sigma_i^2 \) reaching 90% to 99%, depending on how much noise you tolerate. Check that the discarded part really looks like noise: reconstruct, and measure the error on held-out entries, not only on the training table.
In ML systems: embedding compression (replace a \( d\times d \) weight by two \( d\times r \) matrices), LoRA (train the low-rank update \( BA \) while the base weight stays frozen), fast approximate nearest-neighbour search after reducing dimension, and de-noising. In ad tech, low-rank factorization of a user-by-segment or user-by-app matrix yields compact audience embeddings, and the singular value spectrum is a quick check of how much real structure the data has. To study it, derive the SVD of a \( 2\times2 \) matrix by hand once, then read Trefethen and Bau, lectures 4 and 5.
tradeoffs: Choose a full SVD when the matrix fits in memory (up to about \( 10^4 \times 10^4 \)) and you need all singular values, the pseudo-inverse, or exact rank decisions. Choose truncated or randomized SVD when you need only the top \( r \ll n \) components of a big matrix: cost drops from \( O(mn^2) \) to about \( O(mnr) \), and the price is a small approximation error that grows when the spectrum decays slowly.
Choose SVD over the eigendecomposition of \( A^\top A \) (or the covariance matrix) when small singular values matter or the data is ill-conditioned, because forming \( A^\top A \) squares the condition number. Choose the covariance route when \( n \) is small (a few thousand features) and \( m \) is huge or distributed, because forming and decomposing an \( n\times n \) matrix is cheap and easy to parallelize.
Choose truncated SVD when the goal is the best linear compression and you want an error guarantee. Choose a method that handles missing data (alternating least squares) for recommender tables, or non-negative factorization when the factors must be readable as parts, or an autoencoder when the structure is non-linear; you give up the closed-form optimality and the uniqueness in each case.
quiz: State the SVD and the shape of each factor. || \( A = U\Sigma V^\top \): for an \( m\times n \) matrix, \( U \) is \( m\times m \) orthogonal, \( \Sigma \) is \( m\times n \) diagonal with \( \sigma_1\ge\sigma_2\ge\dots\ge0 \), and \( V \) is \( n\times n \) orthogonal. The economy form keeps only the first \( \min(m,n) \) columns.
How do singular values relate to eigenvalues? || \( \sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^\top A)} \). \( A^\top A \) is symmetric positive semi-definite, so its eigenvalues are real and non-negative and its eigenvectors are the right singular vectors.
Why is truncating to the top \( r \) singular values the best rank-\( r \) approximation, and what is the error? || By the Eckart–Young–Mirsky theorem no rank-\( r \) matrix is closer. The spectral-norm error is \( \sigma_{r+1} \) and the Frobenius error is \( \sqrt{\sum_{i>r}\sigma_i^2} \), because the orthogonal rank-one pieces contribute independently.
A \( 2000 \times 500 \) matrix is approximated at rank 10. How many numbers are stored, compared with the full matrix? || \( 10\times(2000+500+1) = 25{,}010 \) numbers versus \( 1{,}000{,}000 \), about 2.5%.
You want 20 principal components of a sparse matrix with \( 10^7 \) rows and \( 10^5 \) columns. Full SVD, covariance eigendecomposition or randomized SVD? || Randomized or iterative truncated SVD. A full SVD is far too costly, and a \( 10^5\times10^5 \) covariance matrix is 80 GB dense. Randomized SVD touches the sparse matrix with a few multiplications and returns the top 20 directions with small error.

value: Causal inference asks "what would have happened if we had acted differently?" It is not the same as asking "what is associated with what?". Customers who saw an ad bought more, but did the ad cause the purchase, or did the system simply show ads to people who were already about to buy? A correlation in logged data cannot answer this by itself, because we never observe the same person both with and without the ad. The unobserved outcome is called the counterfactual, and all of causal inference is a set of rules for estimating it honestly.
There are two main routes. The first is to make the counterfactual observable by design: randomize who gets the treatment, so the two groups differ only by chance. The second, when you cannot randomize, is to write down your assumptions about how the data was produced (a causal graph or a list of adjustment variables) and then use statistics to remove the bias that those assumptions identify. Matching, regression adjustment, inverse-probability weighting, difference-in-differences, instrumental variables and synthetic controls are tools of the second route.
For the layers above, this is what makes measurement trustworthy: A/B tests are randomized causal estimates; incrementality studies and attribution in advertising ask how many conversions the ads caused rather than how many followed them; recommender systems must correct for the fact that users only rate what they were shown; and machine-learning models trained on correlations break when the world shifts, while a model of the causes does not. The layers below supply the ingredients. Probability gives potential outcomes as random variables. Statistics gives estimators, confidence intervals and power. Graph theory gives the directed acyclic graph (DAG) that encodes assumptions. Bayesian networks give the factorization that a causal graph reads in a causal way. Epistemology explains why correlation alone cannot give causes: induction needs assumptions beyond the data.
terms: Treatment — the action whose effect we want, such as showing an ad; written \( T \in \{0,1\} \).
Potential outcomes — \( Y(1) \) and \( Y(0) \): the outcome a unit would have with and without treatment. Only one is observed per unit.
Average treatment effect (ATE) — \( E[Y(1) - Y(0)] \), the mean effect over the whole population.
Confounder — a variable that affects both treatment and outcome, so it creates a spurious association.
Selection bias — a difference between treated and untreated groups that exists before treatment and is not caused by it.
Randomization — assigning treatment by a coin flip, which makes treatment independent of everything else.
Causal DAG — a directed acyclic graph where an arrow \( A \to B \) means "A directly affects B"; it records what we assume.
Backdoor criterion — a graph test that says which variables to adjust for so that the remaining association is causal.
Counterfactual — what the outcome would have been under the action that did not happen.
Incrementality (lift) — the outcome caused by the treatment, in advertising: conversions that would not have happened without the ads.
history: The oldest ideas are experimental. Ronald Fisher (1925 to 1935) argued that random assignment is what licenses a causal conclusion, in his agricultural trials at Rothamsted. Jerzy Neyman (1923) first wrote the potential-outcomes notation, for the analysis of field experiments, and Donald Rubin (1974) turned it into a general framework for observational data too, which is why the approach is called the Neyman–Rubin model. Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983) introduced the propensity score, the probability of receiving treatment given covariates, which reduces matching on many variables to matching on one.
A second line came from graphs. Sewall Wright invented path diagrams in the 1920s for genetics. Judea Pearl, from the 1990s, joined graphs to probability: his do-operator, \( do(T=t) \), separates "seeing" from "doing", and his book "Causality" (2000) gave rules (do-calculus) for deciding when an effect can be computed from observational data. Pearl received the Turing Award in 2011.
Economists developed quasi-experiments: John Snow's 1854 cholera study compared two water companies, an early difference-in-differences. Card and Krueger (1994) used the same design to study a minimum wage rise in New Jersey with Pennsylvania as the comparison. Angrist and Imbens (1994) formalized instrumental variables with heterogeneous effects. Abadie, Diamond and Hainmueller (2010) introduced synthetic controls. Card, Angrist and Imbens received the 2021 Nobel Prize in economics for this line of work. In advertising, Lewis and Rao (2015) showed that even very large randomized ad experiments often have too little statistical power to detect real returns, which made incrementality testing a topic of its own.
how: The central problem is simple. For each person \( i \) there are two potential outcomes, \( Y_i(1) \) and \( Y_i(0) \), and the individual effect \( Y_i(1) - Y_i(0) \) is never observable because we see only one. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference. We therefore aim at averages. The average effect we want is \( \text{ATE} = E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)] \). What data gives us is \( E[Y \mid T=1] - E[Y \mid T=0] \), the naive difference between treated and untreated. The two are related by
\[ E[Y\mid T=1] - E[Y\mid T=0] = \underbrace{E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid T=1]}_{\text{effect on the treated}} + \underbrace{E[Y(0)\mid T=1] - E[Y(0)\mid T=0]}_{\text{selection bias}} \]
The second term asks: would the treated group have had a different baseline outcome even without treatment? In targeted advertising it is large and positive, because ads go to likely buyers.
Route one is randomization. If treatment is assigned by a coin flip, then \( T \) is independent of \( (Y(0), Y(1)) \), the selection bias is zero in expectation, and the plain difference of group means is an unbiased estimate of the ATE. The statistics concept supplies the rest: a standard error \( \sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_0^2/n_0} \), a confidence interval, and a sample-size calculation. For rare outcomes this is the hard part: with a 1% conversion rate and a true lift of 5% relative (0.05 percentage points), you need roughly 640,000 users per arm for 80% power at the 5% significance level (and about 16 million per arm for a 1% relative lift). Randomization also needs the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA): one person's treatment must not change another person's outcome, which fails in marketplaces and with auctions.
Route two is adjustment. Suppose treatment is not random but depends on observed covariates \( X \) (age, past purchases, device). If, once we fix \( X \), treatment is as good as random (conditional ignorability: \( (Y(0),Y(1)) \perp T \mid X \)), and every kind of person has some chance of both treatment and no treatment (overlap, \( 0 < P(T=1\mid X) < 1 \)), then we can compare like with like inside each stratum of \( X \) and average:
\[ \text{ATE} = \sum_x P(X=x)\,\big(E[Y\mid T=1,X=x] - E[Y\mid T=0,X=x]\big) \]
This is the adjustment formula. With many covariates we cannot form strata, so we use regression adjustment, matching, or weighting each unit by the inverse of its propensity score \( e(x) = P(T=1\mid X=x) \), so the treated and untreated groups both look like the whole population. Doubly robust estimators combine an outcome model with a propensity model and stay correct if either one is right.
The causal DAG tells you what \( X \) should contain. Here graph theory and Bayesian networks come in. A graph's arrows encode assumptions. A path from \( T \) to \( Y \) that starts with an arrow into \( T \) (\( T \leftarrow Z \to Y \)) is a backdoor path and carries confounding; you block it by conditioning on \( Z \). But not every variable should be adjusted for. Conditioning on a collider (a variable caused by both \( T \) and \( Y \), \( T \to C \leftarrow Y \)) opens a spurious path, and conditioning on a mediator (\( T \to M \to Y \)) removes part of the effect you want. The backdoor criterion says: adjust for a set that blocks all backdoor paths and contains no descendant of \( T \). Without a causal graph, "control for everything" can make estimates worse.
Quasi-experimental designs give identification when \( X \) is not enough. Difference-in-differences compares the change over time in a treated group with the change in an untreated group, which removes any fixed difference between the groups, under the assumption of parallel trends. Instrumental variables use something that moves treatment but affects the outcome only through treatment (a random ad-server bug, a lottery, distance), and estimate the effect for those who respond to it. Regression discontinuity compares units just above and just below a cutoff. Synthetic control builds a weighted mix of untreated units that tracks the treated unit before treatment, and uses it as the counterfactual afterwards. Each design is a different story about why the comparison is fair, and each is only as good as that story.

example: Suppose 1,000,000 users. A targeting system shows an ad to 80% of "high-intent" users (300,000 of them) and only 10% of "low-intent" users (700,000). The true effect of the ad is +2 percentage points of conversion for high-intent users (from 20% to 22%) and +1 point for low-intent users (from 2% to 3%).
Count the groups. High-intent: 240,000 exposed and 60,000 unexposed. Low-intent: 70,000 exposed and 630,000 unexposed. Conversions among exposed users: \( 240{,}000\times0.22 + 70{,}000\times0.03 = 52{,}800 + 2{,}100 = 54{,}900 \), out of 310,000 exposed users, which is a 17.7% rate. Conversions among unexposed: \( 60{,}000\times0.20 + 630{,}000\times0.02 = 12{,}000 + 12{,}600 = 24{,}600 \), out of 690,000, which is 3.57%. The naive difference is \( 17.7\% - 3.57\% = 14.1 \) percentage points.
The naive number claims the ad adds 14 points. The true average effect is \( 0.3\times2 + 0.7\times1 = 1.3 \) points. The naive estimate is about 11 times too large, because exposed users are mostly high-intent users who convert at 20% anyway: the selection-bias term is huge. Now adjust: within the high-intent stratum, \( 22\% - 20\% = 2 \) points; within low-intent, \( 3\% - 2\% = 1 \) point; weight by stratum sizes \( (0.3, 0.7) \): 1.3 points. The adjustment formula recovers the truth because intent (the confounder) is observed. If "intent" were hidden, no amount of data would fix the estimate, which is why advertisers run holdout tests.
Now a quasi-experiment. A campaign runs in test cities, with similar control cities left alone. Weekly sales per 1,000 people: test cities 100 before and 130 after; control cities 80 before and 95 after. Naive before/after in test cities: +30, but sales also grew in control cities by +15 for other reasons (season, weather). Difference-in-differences: \( (130-100) - (95-80) = 15 \). The campaign effect is estimated at 15 units, half of the naive figure. The estimate is valid only if test cities would have grown by the same +15 without the campaign (parallel trends), which you check by comparing several pre-period weeks.
intuition: To know what a treatment did, you must compare the world with the treatment to a world without it, and since you can only live in one, you either build the other world by a coin flip or you argue carefully that a comparison group stands in for it.
The reason this feels hard is that the missing piece is not noise, it is an unobserved fact. More data about the same biased comparison only makes a wrong answer more precise. What helps is a mechanism that makes the comparison fair, and the only fully reliable one is randomization, because a coin has no way to know who is likely to buy. Every other method replaces the coin with an assumption you must defend, and the causal graph is the place to write that assumption down.
mistakes: People often think a very large data set makes the correlation causal. In fact bias does not shrink with sample size. With a million users the naive estimate is a very precise measurement of the wrong quantity, as the example shows (14.1 points versus 1.3).
People often think we should control for as many variables as we can. In fact adjusting for a collider or a mediator introduces bias or removes part of the effect. Which variables to adjust for is decided by the causal structure, not by the size of the data table.
People often think A/B testing is only for product buttons and cannot measure ad effects. In fact randomized holdouts (intent-to-treat, ghost ads, geo experiments) are the standard for incrementality. The hard part is power and interference, not the principle. Last-touch attribution is the thing that is not causal: it counts conversions that follow ads.
People often think machine learning predictions estimate causal effects. In fact a model that predicts conversion well can rank users by their chance of buying, which is different from their chance of buying because of the ad. Targeting the "sure things" wastes money; uplift modelling targets the users whose behaviour the ad changes.
friction: Everything rests on assumptions that data cannot verify. Ignorability (no hidden confounders) cannot be tested from the observed data; you can only probe it with sensitivity analysis (how strong would a hidden confounder have to be to erase the result?) and with placebo tests (do we find "effects" where there should be none?). Parallel trends can be checked before treatment, but its value after treatment is a belief.
Randomized experiments have their own problems. Power is a big one: ad outcomes are rare and noisy, so detecting a 5% relative lift on a 1% base rate needs about 640,000 users per arm, and a 1% relative lift needs about 16 million. Interference breaks the independence assumption: in an auction, treating some users changes prices for others; in a social network, treated users influence their friends. Compliance matters too: users assigned to see an ad may not see it, so the estimate of "assignment" is diluted and needs instrumental-variable scaling to recover the effect on those who actually saw it. Variance-reduction methods such as CUPED (using pre-period behaviour as a covariate) can cut the needed sample by 20% to 50% when pre-period data predicts the outcome.
Finally, a causal estimate is local. It answers a question for one population, treatment and time. Effects change with ad frequency, creative and season, and heterogeneous effects mean the average can hide groups that are helped and groups that are harmed. Causal discovery (learning the graph from data) is still weak without strong assumptions or interventions.
practice: Start every analysis by writing the causal question and the DAG in five minutes: what is the treatment, what is the outcome, what affects both, what is a consequence of the treatment. Then choose a design. If you can randomize, do it, at the level where interference is small (user, household, or geography), and decide the sample size before the test from a minimum effect worth detecting. Record the assignment, not only exposure, and analyse by assignment (intent-to-treat).
When you cannot randomize, prefer designs with a clear story: geo experiments with matched markets, difference-in-differences with several pre-period weeks and a plot of both groups, synthetic controls for a single large treated unit. Use propensity weighting or doubly robust estimators when you have rich covariates, check covariate balance and overlap, and trim extreme weights. Report the sensitivity of the answer to hidden confounding, and run placebo tests on periods or outcomes where the effect must be zero.
In ad tech, compare three numbers for a campaign: attributed conversions (correlation), modelled lift (adjusted observational), and measured lift (randomized holdout or geo test). Large gaps between them are the information. For tooling, Python libraries such as `DoWhy`, `EconML` and `CausalML` implement estimators and refutation tests, and `statsmodels` covers regression and difference-in-differences. To study: read Hernán and Robins "Causal Inference: What If" (free) for the epidemiology view, and Angrist and Pischke "Mostly Harmless Econometrics" for the economics view.
tradeoffs: Choose a randomized experiment when you control assignment and can afford the sample size; you give up speed and some revenue (the holdout does not get ads) but you remove selection bias without needing to know the confounders. Choose an observational design when randomization is impossible, unethical or too slow, and accept that the estimate depends on untestable assumptions.
Choose user-level randomization when interference is low and you want power; choose geo-level (cluster) randomization when outcomes cross users, as with TV, out-of-home or auction-wide effects, or when user IDs cannot be joined. You lose power: with 50 geos instead of millions of users, only large effects (often more than 5% to 10% lift) are detectable.
Choose regression or matching on observed covariates when you believe the important confounders are measured and overlap is good. Choose difference-in-differences or synthetic control when there is an untreated comparison over time and confounders are fixed or unobserved. Choose an instrument when you have an unusually clean source of random variation but accept that you estimate the effect only for compliers, with a large variance if the instrument is weak.
Choose a prediction model (such as conversion probability) when the task is to forecast under the current policy. Choose an uplift or causal model when the task is to decide whom to treat; predictions that are accurate under today's policy can be useless under a changed policy.
quiz: Why can we not read the effect of an ad from the difference in conversion rates of exposed and unexposed users? || Because exposure was not random: the exposed users differ from the unexposed in ways (such as purchase intent) that also affect conversion. The difference equals the true effect plus selection bias.
Why does randomization remove confounding? || A coin flip makes treatment independent of every other variable, including hidden ones, so the two groups have the same expected potential outcomes and their difference estimates the average treatment effect.
In the difference-in-differences example, test cities go from 100 to 130 and control cities go from 80 to 95. What is the estimate, and what must hold? || \( (130-100)-(95-80)=15 \). The parallel trends assumption must hold: without the campaign, test cities would have risen by the same 15 as the control cities.
In a causal DAG, why should you not condition on a collider? || A collider is caused by both treatment and outcome, \( T \to C \leftarrow Y \). Conditioning on it opens a path between \( T \) and \( Y \) and creates a spurious association where none existed.
When would you prefer a geo experiment to a user-level A/B test for an ad campaign, and what do you lose? || When ads or outcomes cannot be tied to one user, or interference crosses users (TV, auctions). You lose statistical power because the unit count is small, so only large lifts can be detected.

value: A probability distribution says how likely each value of an uncertain quantity is. Thousands of distributions exist, but four of them cover most of what an engineer meets, because each one comes from a simple story. The binomial counts successes in a fixed number of independent tries. The Poisson counts rare events in a large population or a time window. The exponential measures the waiting time between those events. The normal (Gaussian) describes the sum of many small independent effects. If you can say which story your data follows, you get a formula for every probability, the mean, the spread, and often a way to simulate it.
These shapes are the working vocabulary of performance and measurement. Requests arriving at a server are modelled as a Poisson process, so the time between requests is exponential; a queue then follows from those two assumptions. The number of clicks among 10,000 ad impressions is binomial; the number of errors per minute in a healthy service is roughly Poisson; request latency noise, measurement error and averages of many samples are close to normal. Capacity planning, retry back-off, alert thresholds, and A/B test statistics all start with one of these four.
For the layers above, queueing theory (M/M/1 means Poisson arrivals and exponential service), hypothesis testing (test statistics are compared with normal or related distributions) and stochastic processes (a Poisson process is built from exponential gaps) lean directly on them. The layers below supply the machinery: probability defines a random variable and its distribution, combinatorics gives the binomial its counting factor \( \binom{n}{k} \), and calculus turns continuous densities into probabilities by integration and gives the exponential and normal their formulas.
terms: Random variable — a number whose value depends on chance, such as the count of clicks in a batch.
PMF (probability mass function) — for a discrete variable, the probability \( P(X=k) \) of each value.
Density (PDF) — for a continuous variable, a curve \( f(x) \) whose area between two points is the probability of landing between them.
CDF (cumulative distribution function) — \( F(x) = P(X \le x) \); it rises from 0 to 1.
Mean and variance — the average value \( E[X] \) and the average squared distance from it, \( \text{Var}(X) \); the standard deviation \( \sigma \) is the square root of the variance.
Independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) — events that do not influence each other and follow the same rule.
Rate \( \lambda \) — the average number of events per unit of time (or per window) in a Poisson process.
Memoryless — a waiting time whose remaining distribution does not depend on how long you have already waited; only the exponential has this property (among continuous distributions).
Central limit theorem (CLT) — the sum or mean of many i.i.d. variables with finite variance is close to normal, whatever the original shape.
history: The binomial is the oldest. Jacob Bernoulli's "Ars Conjectandi" (published 1713, after his death) studied repeated yes/no trials and proved the first law of large numbers. In 1733 Abraham de Moivre approximated the binomial for large \( n \) with a bell curve, the first form of the normal distribution and of the central limit theorem. Laplace generalized the result in 1810, and Gauss (1809) used the same curve to model measurement errors in astronomy, which is why it is also called the Gaussian.
Siméon Denis Poisson introduced his distribution in 1837, as the limit of the binomial for many trials with a small success chance, in a study of jury verdicts. It stayed a curiosity until Ladislaus Bortkiewicz (1898) fitted it to the number of Prussian soldiers killed by horse kicks per army corps per year, in a book titled "The Law of Small Numbers", and showed it matched real data well. In the twentieth century, Agner Erlang used the Poisson and exponential models for telephone calls (1909), which started queueing theory. Lyapunov (1901) and Lindeberg (1922) proved the central limit theorem under general conditions, which is why the normal distribution appears so often.
The exponential entered as the other face of the Poisson process: if events arrive at rate \( \lambda \) with no memory, the gaps between them have density \( \lambda e^{-\lambda t} \). Radioactive decay (Rutherford and Soddy, 1902) showed the same law in physics. These four are now taught first because they form a connected family, not four separate facts.
how: Binomial. Run \( n \) independent trials, each succeeding with probability \( p \). The chance of exactly \( k \) successes is a counting argument. There are \( \binom{n}{k} \) ways to choose which trials succeed, and each such pattern has probability \( p^k(1-p)^{n-k} \). So
\[ P(X=k) = \binom{n}{k}\,p^k (1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad E[X] = np, \qquad \text{Var}(X) = np(1-p) \]
The mean and variance follow because \( X \) is a sum of \( n \) independent 0/1 variables; means add, and variances of independent variables add. This is the place where combinatorics enters: the factor \( \binom{n}{k} = n!/(k!(n-k)!) \) is the number of ways to place \( k \) successes. Use it for clicks out of impressions, defective items in a batch, or replicas that survive out of \( n \).
Poisson. Now let \( n \) become large and \( p \) small so that the mean \( \lambda = np \) stays fixed. Then \( \binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k} \to e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k! \). So
\[ P(X=k) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}, \qquad E[X] = \text{Var}(X) = \lambda \]
The equality of mean and variance is a useful check: if the variance of your event counts is much larger than the mean, the events are clustered (bursty) and the Poisson model is wrong. A Poisson process has three properties: events in disjoint windows are independent, the chance of an event in a tiny window \( dt \) is \( \lambda\,dt \), and two events at the same instant have negligible chance. Under these, the count in a window of length \( t \) is Poisson with mean \( \lambda t \).
Exponential. In a Poisson process, the probability of no event in a window of length \( t \) is \( e^{-\lambda t} \) (the Poisson formula with \( k=0 \)). So the waiting time \( T \) to the next event has \( P(T > t) = e^{-\lambda t} \), that is, density \( f(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t} \) for \( t\ge0 \). Here calculus supplies the link between a density and a probability: \( P(T>t) = \int_t^\infty \lambda e^{-\lambda s}\,ds = e^{-\lambda t} \). Then
\[ E[T] = \frac{1}{\lambda}, \qquad \text{Var}(T) = \frac{1}{\lambda^2}, \qquad P(T > s+t \mid T > s) = P(T > t) \]
The last identity is the memoryless property: having waited \( s \) seconds already tells you nothing about the remaining wait. That is the reason an exponential service time makes queue analysis simple, and also the reason it is a poor model when real service times have a typical size (a request that has run 5 seconds is probably nearer to done, not equally far).
Normal. The bell curve with mean \( \mu \) and standard deviation \( \sigma \) has density
\[ f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \]
It has no closed-form CDF, so we tabulate it or call a function. Its key role comes from the central limit theorem: if \( S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n \) with i.i.d. terms of mean \( \mu \) and variance \( \sigma^2 \), then \( (S_n - n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt n) \) approaches the standard normal. The "68–95–99.7 rule" says that about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of the mass lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean (more exactly 1.96 for 95%). The sample mean of \( n \) observations has standard error \( \sigma/\sqrt n \): to halve the uncertainty you need four times the data.
How the four connect. A binomial with large \( n \) and small \( p \) is close to Poisson. A binomial with large \( n \) and moderate \( p \) is close to normal (de Moivre). A Poisson with large \( \lambda \) is also close to normal. The waiting time of a Poisson process is exponential, and the sum of \( k \) exponential waits is a Gamma (Erlang) variable, which becomes normal as \( k \) grows. Each approximation lets you replace a heavy exact computation (a sum of 1,000 terms) with one formula. The approximations need checking in the tails, where they fail first.

example: Take a service that receives requests at an average of 2 per second, with no memory between them. The count per second is Poisson with \( \lambda = 2 \). The probabilities are \( P(0) = e^{-2} = 0.135 \), \( P(1) = 0.271 \), \( P(2) = 0.271 \), \( P(3) = 0.180 \), \( P(4) = 0.090 \), \( P(5) = 0.036 \), \( P(6) = 0.012 \). They add to 0.9955 for 0 to 6, so \( P(X \ge 7) = 0.0045 \). If the server can handle at most 6 requests in one second, then 0.45% of seconds (about 1 in 220) exceed the limit, even though the average load is only one third of capacity. This is the cost of randomness: the peak is much higher than the mean.
Now the gaps. The wait between requests is exponential with rate 2, so the mean gap is 0.5 s. The chance the next request takes more than 1 second is \( e^{-2\cdot1} = 0.135 \). It is the same number as \( P(0) \) above, as it must be: "no request for a second" is the same event as "zero in a one-second window". If you have already waited 3 seconds, the chance of waiting another second is still 0.135.
Binomial and its approximations. Show 10,000 ad impressions with a 2% click chance. The click count is Binomial(10,000, 0.02), with mean \( 10{,}000\times0.02 = 200 \) and variance \( 10{,}000\times0.02\times0.98 = 196 \), so \( \sigma = 14 \). By the normal approximation, 95% of the time the count is within \( 200 \pm 1.96\times14 \), which is 173 to 227. That is a relative spread of about \( \pm14\% \). With 1,000,000 impressions, \( \sigma = 140 \) on a mean of 20,000, a relative spread of \( \pm1.4\% \): ten times the data, \( \sqrt{100} \) times narrower relative error.
Poisson as a shortcut: 1,000 impressions with a 0.5% chance each, so \( \lambda = 5 \). The exact binomial chance of 10 or more is 0.0315. The Poisson formula gives 0.0318. The two agree to three decimal places, and the Poisson formula needs only one parameter. Notice also that the normal approximation (mean 5, \( \sigma = 2.2 \)) is poor here: it is symmetric and puts probability on negative counts. Small means need Poisson, not normal.
intuition: Each distribution is the answer to a simple question: how many yes answers out of n (binomial), how many rare arrivals (Poisson), how long until the next one (exponential), and what do many small pushes add up to (normal).
They are natural because they are what is left when you strip a situation down to its structure. When events are independent and rare, only their average rate matters, and the details wash out, which gives the Poisson. When effects add up, the individual shapes wash out and only the mean and variance survive, which gives the normal. The shapes are limits that many different messy systems converge to, and that is why a few formulas explain so many real data sets.
mistakes: People often think the average is enough to describe a random quantity. In fact the same mean can hide very different spreads. In the example, an average load of 2 requests per second is far below the limit of 6, yet 0.45% of seconds still exceed it, because variance equals the mean for Poisson counts.
People often think "random" means "normal". In fact many real quantities are not: latencies are right-skewed with heavy tails (often modelled by log-normal or Pareto shapes), and file sizes and counts per user follow power laws. The normal appears for sums and averages, not for every measurement. Check with a histogram or a quantile plot before using it, and never use mean plus three standard deviations as a latency limit.
People often think the exponential means "most events happen quickly, so a long wait is unlikely to continue". In fact the exponential is memoryless: after waiting a long time, the remaining wait has the same distribution as at the start. Real repair times and request durations are usually not memoryless, so queueing results that assume it can be off by a lot.
People often think the Poisson model holds for any count. In fact it needs independence and a constant rate. Traffic with daily cycles, retries after failures, or users who click in bursts all have variance much larger than the mean ("overdispersion"), and then the negative binomial or a time-varying rate is the better model.
friction: The first limit is that real data is rarely exactly any of these. Arrival streams are bursty and correlated (retry storms, batch jobs, a TV commercial driving a spike), so the Poisson assumption underestimates the tails. Service times have a characteristic size and a long tail, so the exponential is both too memoryless and too thin-tailed. Heavy-tailed quantities can have infinite variance in theory (Pareto with shape below 2), and then the central limit theorem does not apply, and the sample mean converges very slowly or erratically. Always look at the tail separately.
The second limit is that approximations fail where you often care most: the extreme tail. The normal approximation to a binomial can have the right centre and a wrong 99.99th percentile by a large factor. The CLT also says nothing about how large \( n \) must be; for skewed data you may need hundreds or thousands of samples before the mean is close to normal. A rule of thumb of \( np \ge 10 \) and \( n(1-p) \ge 10 \) for the binomial-to-normal step is only a starting point.
Third, parameters are estimated, not known. A rate \( \lambda \) measured from a 5-minute window has its own uncertainty (about \( \sqrt{\text{count}} \)). Confidence intervals, not point values, belong in capacity plans. Finally, continuous models treat time as infinitely precise, while real systems have discrete clocks, batching and rounding that matter when the numbers are small.
practice: In capacity planning, model arrivals as Poisson only after checking the index of dispersion (variance divided by mean of counts per fixed window); a value near 1 supports Poisson, a value of 3 or more means burstiness, so plan for peaks with a margin or fit a negative binomial. For retries and back-off, remember that exponential waits with random jitter spread out clients, while fixed intervals make them collide. For SLO analysis, use empirical quantiles of latency, not mean and standard deviation.
In A/B testing, a conversion count is binomial, and the difference of two rates is approximately normal with standard error \( \sqrt{p_1(1-p_1)/n_1 + p_2(1-p_2)/n_2} \); this is the base of a z-test and of sample-size formulas. For rare events (\( np < 10 \)) use exact binomial or Poisson methods. In Python, use `scipy.stats` (`binom`, `poisson`, `expon`, `norm`) for the PMF, CDF and inverse CDF (`ppf`), and simulate with `numpy.random.default_rng()` when a closed form does not exist: simulation is the check on every formula.
A good habit is to plot the data against the candidate distribution (a Q–Q plot) and to compare the simulated and observed counts of the extreme cases. To study, derive the Poisson as a limit of the binomial once by hand, then read Blitzstein and Hwang's "Introduction to Probability", chapters 3 to 5 and 10, with free lectures from Harvard Stat 110.
tradeoffs: Choose the exact binomial when \( n \) is small (up to a few hundred) or the probability is near 0 or 1; software computes it cheaply. Choose the Poisson approximation when \( n \) is large and \( p \) is small (say \( n \ge 100 \) and \( np \le 10 \)); you give up the upper bound \( n \) on the count. Choose the normal approximation when \( np \) and \( n(1-p) \) are both at least 10; you give up accuracy in the tails and for skewed cases.
Choose Poisson arrivals with exponential service times (M/M/1) for a quick first estimate of queue length and delay; the formulas are closed and instructive. Choose more realistic service distributions (general service times, M/G/1) when the coefficient of variation of service times differs a lot from 1, because waiting time scales with \( (1+C_v^2)/2 \), and a heavy tail can double the wait.
Choose a parametric model when you have little data and trust the story; you get tail estimates beyond the observed range. Choose empirical quantiles or the bootstrap when you have plenty of data and doubt the shape; you give up extrapolation beyond the largest observation.

value: Numerical linear algebra is linear algebra as it is actually done on a computer, where every number has about 16 digits and every operation rounds. The task sounds easy: solve \( A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \), fit a model, find eigenvalues. But the textbook method may give a wrong answer on a machine, or be impossibly slow. The field tells you which method to use, how much it costs, and how many correct digits to expect in the result.
Its main tools are factorizations. Instead of inverting a matrix, you write it as a product of simple matrices (triangular, orthogonal, diagonal) and then solve with those pieces. LU factorization solves general square systems, Cholesky solves symmetric positive-definite ones in half the work, and QR solves least-squares problems stably. For very large sparse matrices, iterative methods such as conjugate gradient avoid factorization and use only matrix-vector products. A single number, the condition number, predicts how much any method can lose.
This is the layer under almost all numerical computing. BLAS and LAPACK (level 3 operations built on blocked matrix multiplication) run inside NumPy, SciPy, MATLAB, R, Julia, and the solvers behind regression fits, Kalman filters, finite-element programs and optimizers. The concepts below it supply the content: linear algebra defines the problem, numerical analysis supplies the language of rounding error, conditioning and stability, least squares provides the problem that QR solves, and eigenvalues give the problem that the QR algorithm and Lanczos method solve.
terms: Floating-point number — a number stored as a sign, a fixed number of significant digits and an exponent; double precision has 53 bits, about 16 decimal digits.
Unit roundoff — the largest relative error in rounding one number, \( u \approx 1.1\times10^{-16} \) in double precision.
Condition number — \( \kappa(A) = \|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\| = \sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min} \); how much the answer of \( A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} \) can change relative to a change in the input.
Backward stable — an algorithm whose computed answer is the exact answer for a slightly perturbed input (perturbation of size about \( u \)).
LU factorization — writing \( PA = LU \) with \( L \) lower triangular, \( U \) upper triangular and \( P \) a row permutation (pivoting).
Cholesky factorization — \( A = LL^\top \) for symmetric positive-definite \( A \); half the cost of LU.
QR factorization — \( A = QR \) with \( Q \) orthogonal and \( R \) upper triangular.
Residual — \( \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}} \); how well a computed answer satisfies the equation.
Krylov method — an iterative method (conjugate gradient, GMRES, Lanczos) that searches in the span of \( \mathbf{b}, A\mathbf{b}, A^2\mathbf{b}, \dots \).
history: The subject began as a worry. In 1947 John von Neumann and Herman Goldstine analysed Gaussian elimination for large matrices and feared that rounding errors would destroy the answer for matrices of order in the hundreds. Alan Turing (1948, "Rounding-off errors in matrix processes") studied the problem again, introduced the idea of a condition number for matrices and showed which errors one should expect. James Wilkinson, who worked with Turing, then proved in the early 1960s that Gaussian elimination with pivoting is backward stable in practice: the computed answer is the exact answer to a nearby problem. This "backward error analysis" is the core idea of the field, and it earned Wilkinson the Turing Award in 1970.
Algorithms came in waves. Cholesky devised his factorization around 1910 for geodesy; it was published after his death in 1924. Hestenes and Stiefel (1952) published the conjugate gradient method, which was first seen as a direct method and later, from the 1970s, understood as an excellent iterative method for large sparse systems. Householder (1958) introduced reflections for QR, and Francis and Kublanovskaya (about 1961) the QR algorithm for eigenvalues. Golub and Kahan (1965) did the same for the SVD.
Software made the methods common. EISPACK and LINPACK in the 1970s collected the algorithms as Fortran libraries. The BLAS (Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms; levels 1 to 3, 1979 to 1990) defined vector, matrix-vector and matrix-matrix kernels. LAPACK (1992) rewrote the algorithms to use level-3 BLAS, so they run fast on machines with caches. Today OpenBLAS, Intel oneMKL and GPU libraries such as cuBLAS and cuSOLVER provide the same interfaces; NumPy and SciPy call LAPACK underneath.
how: First, why not invert? The textbook solution is \( \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} \). Computing \( A^{-1} \) costs about \( 2n^3 \) operations, three times the cost of an LU solve, it is less accurate, and it destroys sparsity (the inverse of a sparse matrix is usually dense). So you never form the inverse; you factorize and solve.
LU factorization is Gaussian elimination written as matrices. Elimination subtracts multiples of the pivot row from the rows below it to create zeros under the diagonal. The multipliers are collected into \( L \) and the final triangular matrix is \( U \). With row swaps (partial pivoting, which picks the largest available entry in the column as the pivot to keep multipliers at most 1 in size), you get \( PA = LU \). Then \( A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} \) takes two cheap triangular solves: forward substitution \( L\mathbf{y} = P\mathbf{b} \), then back substitution \( U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y} \). Factorization costs about \( \tfrac23 n^3 \) floating-point operations and each triangular solve about \( n^2 \), so you pay once for the factorization and then solve for many right-hand sides cheaply. For symmetric positive-definite matrices (covariance matrices, normal equations, finite-element stiffness matrices) Cholesky costs \( \tfrac13 n^3 \), needs no pivoting, and is a fast test of positive-definiteness (it fails if the matrix is not).
QR factorization writes \( A = QR \) with \( Q^\top Q = I \). Because orthogonal transformations preserve lengths, they do not amplify rounding errors, which makes QR the stable choice. Householder reflections zero the sub-diagonal entries of one column at a time. Cost for an \( m\times n \) matrix (\( m\ge n \)) is about \( 2mn^2 - \tfrac23 n^3 \). The link to least squares matters here. To minimize \( \|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_2 \), the normal equations \( A^\top A\mathbf{x} = A^\top\mathbf{b} \) look simple, but \( \kappa(A^\top A) = \kappa(A)^2 \), so you lose twice as many digits. With QR, solve \( R\mathbf{x} = Q^\top\mathbf{b} \) instead: the conditioning stays \( \kappa(A) \). For rank-deficient or very ill-conditioned problems, use the SVD, which exposes small singular values that can be thrown away.
Conditioning is the central idea. Suppose we perturb the right-hand side by \( \delta\mathbf{b} \). The solution changes by \( \delta\mathbf{x} \), and
\[ \frac{\|\delta\mathbf{x}\|}{\|\mathbf{x}\|} \le \kappa(A)\,\frac{\|\delta\mathbf{b}\|}{\|\mathbf{b}\|}, \qquad \kappa(A) = \|A\|\,\|A^{-1}\| = \frac{\sigma_{\max}}{\sigma_{\min}} \]
This is a property of the problem, not of the algorithm. Even a perfect algorithm cannot beat it, because the input is already rounded by about \( u = 1.1\times10^{-16} \). A backward stable algorithm returns an answer with relative error about \( \kappa(A)\cdot u \). The rule of thumb: you lose about \( \log_{10}\kappa(A) \) of your 16 digits. A matrix with \( \kappa = 10^{10} \) leaves about 6 correct digits, and a matrix with \( \kappa\ge10^{16} \) can leave none. Notice that a small residual does not mean a small error: for an ill-conditioned problem the computed solution can satisfy the equations to machine precision and still be far from the true solution.
Iterative methods take the other road for large sparse systems. A matrix from a 3-D simulation or a graph can have \( n=10^7 \) rows with only 10 to 30 non-zeros per row. An LU factorization would fill in the zeros and need terabytes, but a matrix-vector product costs only \( O(\text{nnz}) \). Conjugate gradient (CG) for symmetric positive-definite \( A \) builds, at step \( k \), the best approximation to the solution in the Krylov space spanned by \( \mathbf{b}, A\mathbf{b}, \dots, A^{k-1}\mathbf{b} \), measured in the \( A \)-norm. Its error shrinks by a factor about \( \dfrac{\sqrt\kappa-1}{\sqrt\kappa+1} \) per iteration, so reaching accuracy \( \varepsilon \) takes roughly \( \tfrac12\sqrt\kappa\,\ln(2/\varepsilon) \) iterations. Condition number again decides the cost, and the standard remedy is a preconditioner \( M\approx A \) that is cheap to apply and makes \( M^{-1}A \) better conditioned. For non-symmetric matrices, GMRES and BiCGSTAB play the same role. For eigenvalues, the QR algorithm handles dense matrices (about \( 10n^3 \) operations in practice), and Lanczos or Arnoldi find a few extreme eigenvalues of huge sparse matrices.
Finally, speed is mostly about memory. A good implementation reorganizes LU, QR and Cholesky into blocks, so that most operations are matrix-matrix products on tiles that fit in cache; that is why LAPACK runs at a large fraction of peak speed while a naive triple loop does not. This is where blocked matrix multiplication (GEMM) supports the layer above.

example: First a factorization by hand. Take \( A = \begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\4&3&3\\8&7&9\end{pmatrix} \) and \( \mathbf{b} = (4, 10, 24) \). Eliminate column 1: row 2 minus \( 2\times \) row 1 gives \( (0,1,1) \); row 3 minus \( 4\times \) row 1 gives \( (0,3,5) \). Eliminate column 2: row 3 minus \( 3\times \) row 2 gives \( (0,0,2) \). So the multipliers are 2, 4, 3 and
\[ L = \begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\2&1&0\\4&3&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad U = \begin{pmatrix}2&1&1\\0&1&1\\0&0&2\end{pmatrix} \]
Check the bottom row of \( LU \): \( 4\cdot(2,1,1) + 3\cdot(0,1,1) + 1\cdot(0,0,2) = (8, 7, 9) \). Correct. Forward substitution \( L\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{b} \): \( y_1 = 4 \); \( y_2 = 10 - 2\cdot4 = 2 \); \( y_3 = 24 - 4\cdot4 - 3\cdot2 = 2 \). Back substitution \( U\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y} \): \( x_3 = 2/2 = 1 \); \( x_2 = 2 - 1 = 1 \); \( x_1 = (4 - 1 - 1)/2 = 1 \). The solution is \( (1,1,1) \). The work was about \( \tfrac23\cdot27 = 18 \) operations to factor and 9 to solve; for large \( n \) the factor cost \( \tfrac23n^3 \) dominates by a factor of \( n \).
Now conditioning. Take \( A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1.0001\end{pmatrix} \). The two rows are almost parallel. Its singular values are about 2.0000 and 0.00005, so \( \kappa \approx 40{,}002 \). For \( \mathbf{b} = (2, 2.0001) \) the solution is \( \mathbf{x} = (1, 1) \). Change \( \mathbf{b} \) to \( (2, 2.0002) \), a change of 0.0001 in one entry, about 0.0035% of \( \|\mathbf{b}\| \approx 2.83 \). The new solution is \( \mathbf{x} = (0, 2) \). The relative change in \( \mathbf{x} \) is \( \|(-1,1)\|/\|(1,1)\| = 100\% \). The amplification is \( 100\%/0.0035\% \approx 28{,}000 \), below the bound \( \kappa=40{,}002 \), as the inequality says. A change in the fifth significant digit of the data changes the answer completely. No algorithm can fix this, because the problem itself is sensitive.
Normal equations versus QR: for the matrix with columns \( (1,1,1) \) and \( (1,2,3) \) (a line fit through three points), \( \kappa(A) \approx 6.8 \) and \( \kappa(A^\top A) \approx 46 \), the square. This is harmless here. For a degree-10 polynomial fit on \( [0,1] \) with monomial columns, \( \kappa(A)\approx2\times10^7 \) and the normal equations have \( \kappa\approx4\times10^{14} \); then \( \kappa u\approx0.05 \), so they return at most one or two correct digits, while QR keeps about 8.
Cost for iterative versus direct: a 2-D Poisson problem on a \( 1000\times1000 \) grid has \( n = 10^6 \) unknowns and \( \kappa\approx4\times10^5 \). A dense LU would need \( \tfrac23\cdot10^{18} \) operations and 8 TB of memory: impossible. Unpreconditioned CG needs about \( \tfrac12\sqrt{4\times10^5}\,\ln(2\times10^8)\approx 6{,}000 \) iterations, each about \( 10^7 \) operations, so roughly \( 6\times10^{10} \) in total: seconds on one core. With a good preconditioner (incomplete Cholesky or multigrid) it falls to a few dozen iterations.
intuition: A computer solves equations with slightly blurred numbers, so the real question is how much a problem magnifies a blur, and a good algorithm is one that adds no more blur than the problem already forces on it.
Seen this way, the whole field is a set of choices that avoid unnecessary magnification. Orthogonal matrices are the safe building blocks, because they rotate without stretching, so QR does not add error. Triangular systems are cheap and safe. Squaring a matrix (as the normal equations do) squares its sensitivity, so you avoid it. When a matrix is huge, you stop trying to take it apart and instead probe it with matrix-vector products, taking the best answer in a growing search space. The condition number is the dial that tells you how hard the problem is, for direct and iterative methods alike.
mistakes: People often think a small residual means an accurate answer. In fact \( \|\mathbf{x}-\hat{\mathbf{x}}\|/\|\mathbf{x}\| \) can be as large as \( \kappa(A)\|\mathbf{r}\|/\|\mathbf{b}\| \). For the 2×2 example above, a computed answer far from the truth still fits both equations to about 5 digits.
People often think a matrix with a tiny determinant is ill-conditioned, and one with determinant 1 is safe. In fact the determinant measures volume, not sensitivity. The matrix \( 10^{-10}I \) (size 10) has determinant \( 10^{-100} \) but condition number 1, and a matrix with determinant 1 can have a huge condition number.
People often think the way to solve \( A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} \) is `inv(A) @ b`. In fact `numpy.linalg.solve(A, b)` is faster and more accurate, and it keeps the factorization available for reuse. Computing the inverse is almost never needed; when you need a quadratic form like \( \mathbf{c}^\top A^{-1}\mathbf{c} \), solve once and take a dot product.
People often think more precision or a better algorithm can rescue any problem. In fact if \( \kappa(A)\cdot u\ge1 \), the data itself no longer determines a unique answer at that precision. The remedies are to reformulate (regularize, rescale the columns, change the basis, as in orthogonal polynomials instead of monomials) or to go to higher precision.
friction: Dense direct methods have cubic cost and quadratic memory. A dense \( n=100{,}000 \) system needs 80 GB just to hold the matrix and about \( 6.7\times10^{14} \) operations to factor, which is a few minutes on a GPU with fast double precision but impossible on a laptop. Sparse direct solvers reduce this with reordering, but the fill-in for 3-D problems grows quickly, so iterative methods and preconditioners are used instead, and choosing a good preconditioner is partly art. There is no universal one.
Iterative methods have their own problems: convergence depends on the spectrum, can stall, and in floating point loses the orthogonality that the theory assumes (Lanczos vectors drift and need re-orthogonalization). GMRES needs memory that grows with each iteration, so it is restarted, which can destroy convergence. Stopping criteria are tricky: a small residual may still mean a large error when \( \kappa \) is large.
Mixed precision is the current pressure point. GPUs run half and single precision many times faster than double, so modern solvers factor in low precision and correct in high precision (iterative refinement). This works only while \( \kappa(A)\,u_{\text{low}}<1 \). Reproducibility is a further cost: parallel sums add in different orders, so the last bits of results differ between runs and machines. Finally, many practical matrices (from graphs, statistics, or neural networks) are not close to what the theory handles well, and the available libraries assume a dense or sparse layout that must fit your data.
practice: Use library routines; do not write your own factorization. In NumPy and SciPy: `numpy.linalg.solve` for general systems, `scipy.linalg.cho_factor` and `cho_solve` for symmetric positive-definite ones, `scipy.linalg.lu_factor` when you solve with many right-hand sides, `numpy.linalg.lstsq` or `scipy.linalg.qr` for least squares, and `scipy.sparse.linalg` (`spsolve`, `cg`, `gmres`, `eigsh`) for sparse problems. In Julia, MATLAB and R, the backslash operator or `solve` makes the same choices.
Check the condition number before trusting a result: `numpy.linalg.cond(A)` is cheap for small matrices, and for large ones use an estimate. If \( \log_{10}\kappa \) approaches 16, stop and fix the model. Rescale the columns of a design matrix to similar sizes (standardize features), centre variables, avoid raw monomials and use orthogonal bases, and add a small ridge term \( \lambda I \) to turn a nearly singular problem into a well-posed one. When a covariance matrix fails Cholesky because rounding makes it slightly indefinite, add a small multiple of the identity (jitter), as Gaussian-process code does.
For large problems, reuse factorizations when the matrix does not change, exploit structure (symmetric, banded, sparse, low rank), and choose the iterative method by matrix type: CG for symmetric positive-definite, GMRES or BiCGSTAB otherwise, with a preconditioner. To learn the subject, read Trefethen and Bau cover to cover (it is short), and run Wilkinson-style experiments yourself: build a Hilbert matrix, solve with known \( \mathbf{x} \), and watch the digits disappear.
tradeoffs: Choose a direct method (LU, Cholesky) when the matrix is dense and up to about \( 10^4 \) to \( 10^5 \) rows, or when you will solve many right-hand sides; you pay cubic time and quadratic memory, and you get a reliable answer in a fixed number of operations. Choose an iterative method (CG, GMRES) when the matrix is sparse and huge, or exists only as a matrix-vector product; you pay with a dependence on conditioning and the need for a preconditioner.
Choose Cholesky over LU when the matrix is symmetric positive-definite: half the work, no pivoting, and a built-in check. Choose LU with partial pivoting otherwise. Choose QR for least squares unless the problem is huge and well-conditioned, in which case the normal equations with Cholesky are about twice as fast, and choose SVD when the matrix may be rank-deficient and you need a truncated or minimum-norm solution; SVD costs several times more than QR.
Choose double precision by default. Choose single or half precision with iterative refinement when the matrix is well-conditioned (\( \kappa\le10^4 \) or so) and speed or memory matters, for example on GPUs; you give up accuracy and need the refinement step. Choose exact or arbitrary-precision arithmetic only for small, structured problems, because it is orders of magnitude slower.
quiz: Why do we solve \( A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b} \) with LU instead of computing \( A^{-1} \)? || An LU solve costs about \( \tfrac23n^3 \) operations versus about \( 2n^3 \) for the inverse, it is more accurate, and it preserves sparsity. The inverse of a sparse matrix is generally dense.
What does the condition number tell you, and how many digits do you expect to lose? || \( \kappa(A)=\sigma_{\max}/\sigma_{\min} \) bounds how much relative input error is amplified in the solution. You lose about \( \log_{10}\kappa \) of the roughly 16 digits of double precision.
Why is QR preferred over the normal equations for least squares? || The normal equations square the condition number, \( \kappa(A^\top A)=\kappa(A)^2 \), so they lose twice the digits. QR uses orthogonal transformations that do not amplify errors, so the conditioning stays \( \kappa(A) \).
A solver returns a solution with a residual of \( 10^{-14} \) on a system with \( \kappa=10^{12} \). What accuracy can you claim? || Only about 4 digits (relative error up to about \( \kappa\cdot u\approx10^{12}\times10^{-16}=10^{-4} \)). A tiny residual does not guarantee a small error when the problem is ill-conditioned.
You must solve a sparse symmetric positive-definite system with 20 million unknowns. Which method and what makes it fast? || Preconditioned conjugate gradient. Each iteration needs only a sparse matrix-vector product, and iterations scale with \( \sqrt{\kappa} \), so a preconditioner (incomplete Cholesky or multigrid) that reduces \( \kappa \) cuts the iteration count. A dense factorization would not fit in memory.

@@ leastsquares
value: Least squares answers a very common situation: you have more equations than unknowns, the equations contradict each other slightly because of noise, and you still need one best answer. Fitting a line through 1,000 measured points is the simplest case. The method picks the answer that makes the sum of squared errors as small as possible. It has a clean geometric meaning: the best prediction is the point in the model's reachable set that lies closest to the data, which is the orthogonal projection of the data onto that set.
This one idea sits under linear regression, trend lines, GPS position fixes (more satellites than the four unknowns of position and clock), camera calibration, sensor fusion, curve fitting, and the final layers of many machine-learning models. It is also the engine of the Kalman filter, which is a recursive weighted least-squares fit, and of Gauss–Newton and Levenberg–Marquardt methods for non-linear fitting, which solve a least-squares problem at every step. The answer has a closed form, so it is fast, predictable and easy to check.
The layers below make it work. Linear algebra gives the picture: the columns of the matrix \( A \) span a subspace, and the vector \( \mathbf{b} \) usually lies outside it, so the closest point is a projection, and the leftover error is perpendicular to the subspace. Calculus gives the second route to the same answer: take the derivative of the squared error, set it to zero, and get the normal equations \( A^\top A\mathbf{x} = A^\top \mathbf{b} \). The two views agree, which is why the method is trusted.
terms: Overdetermined system — more equations than unknowns, so usually no exact solution exists.
Residual — the error vector \( \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x} \) for a chosen \( \mathbf{x} \).
Column space — all vectors \( A\mathbf{x} \) the model can produce; a subspace of the data space.
Orthogonal projection — the closest point of a subspace to a given vector; the leftover is perpendicular to the subspace.
Normal equations — \( A^\top A\mathbf{x}=A^\top\mathbf{b} \); the condition that the residual is perpendicular to every column of \( A \).
Projection (hat) matrix — \( P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top \), which maps data to fitted values.
Design matrix — the matrix \( A \) whose rows are observations and whose columns are features (including a column of ones for the intercept).
Leverage — the diagonal entry \( P_{ii} \); how strongly observation \( i \) pulls the fit toward itself.
Ridge regression — least squares with a penalty \( \lambda\|\mathbf{x}\|^2 \), solved by \( (A^\top A+\lambda I)\mathbf{x}=A^\top\mathbf{b} \).
history: The method was born in astronomy and geodesy, where people had many noisy measurements and few parameters. Adrien-Marie Legendre published it first, in 1805, in an appendix on the orbits of comets. Carl Friedrich Gauss published it in 1809 and said he had used it since 1795. In 1801 he had used it to compute the orbit of the dwarf planet Ceres from a few weeks of observations, so that astronomers could find it again after it passed behind the sun; this made the method famous. A dispute about priority followed, but both men saw the same practical point: when measurements disagree, minimizing squared error gives a single answer.
Gauss also gave a reason. If measurement errors are independent and normally distributed, the least-squares estimate is the maximum-likelihood estimate (1809). Laplace tied it to the central limit theorem in 1810. Later, Andrey Markov (1900) and Gauss's own work led to the Gauss–Markov theorem: among linear unbiased estimators, least squares has the smallest variance when errors are uncorrelated with equal variance.
The twentieth century added numerics and recursion. Householder (1958) and Golub (1965) showed how to solve least squares with QR factorization instead of the normal equations. Rudolf Kalman (1960) published the recursive form, in which each new measurement updates the estimate without refitting everything, and it flew in the Apollo navigation computers. Hoerl and Kennard (1970) introduced ridge regression for ill-conditioned problems, and Tibshirani (1996) the lasso, which replaces the squared penalty with an absolute-value penalty.
how: Set up the problem. We have \( m \) equations and \( n \) unknowns with \( m>n \): \( A\mathbf{x}\approx\mathbf{b} \), where \( A \) is \( m\times n \). The reachable predictions are the vectors \( A\mathbf{x} \), which fill the column space of \( A \) (an \( n \)-dimensional subspace in \( m \)-dimensional space). The data \( \mathbf{b} \) is generally not in it. We choose \( \mathbf{x} \) to minimize
\[ \|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|_2^2 = \sum_{i=1}^m \big(\mathbf{a}_i^\top\mathbf{x}-b_i\big)^2 \]
Route one is calculus. The function above is a quadratic bowl in \( \mathbf{x} \): expanding it gives \( \mathbf{x}^\top A^\top A\mathbf{x} - 2\mathbf{b}^\top A\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{b} \). Its gradient (derivative with respect to \( \mathbf{x} \)) is \( 2A^\top A\mathbf{x} - 2A^\top\mathbf{b} \). Setting it to zero gives the normal equations
\[ A^\top A\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^\top\mathbf{b}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top\mathbf{b} \]
when the columns of \( A \) are independent. Because \( A^\top A \) is positive definite then, the bowl has exactly one minimum, so the stationary point is the global solution.
Route two is geometry, and it explains why the formula looks the way it does. The closest point to \( \mathbf{b} \) in the column space is the point where the error \( \mathbf{r}=\mathbf{b}-A\hat{\mathbf{x}} \) is perpendicular to the space. Perpendicular to the space means perpendicular to every column: \( A^\top\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{0} \), and substituting \( \mathbf{r} \) gives the same normal equations. The fitted values are \( \hat{\mathbf{b}} = P\mathbf{b} \) with \( P = A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top \). This matrix is symmetric and satisfies \( P^2=P \) (projecting twice changes nothing). Its trace equals \( n \), the number of parameters. The Pythagorean theorem holds: \( \|\mathbf{b}\|^2 = \|\hat{\mathbf{b}}\|^2+\|\mathbf{r}\|^2 \). This is also what the statistical \( R^2 \) measures, the share of variance on the projected side.
Solving it stably is a numerical matter. Forming \( A^\top A \) squares the condition number, so for nearly dependent columns the normal equations lose accuracy. The QR route avoids this: factor \( A=QR \), where \( Q \) has orthonormal columns and \( R \) is upper triangular. The projection onto the column space is \( QQ^\top \), so the problem becomes \( R\hat{\mathbf{x}}=Q^\top\mathbf{b} \), a triangular solve. When the columns are close to dependent (or exactly dependent), use the SVD \( A=U\Sigma V^\top \): \( \hat{\mathbf{x}}=V\Sigma^{+}U^\top\mathbf{b} \), which gives the minimum-norm solution. Cost: normal equations plus Cholesky \( \approx mn^2 + \tfrac13n^3 \) operations, QR \( \approx 2mn^2-\tfrac23n^3 \), SVD several times more.
Statistical meaning comes with assumptions. If \( \mathbf{b}=A\mathbf{x}_0+\boldsymbol\varepsilon \) with independent noise of variance \( \sigma^2 \), then \( \hat{\mathbf{x}} \) is unbiased with covariance \( \sigma^2(A^\top A)^{-1} \), and if the noise is also Gaussian, \( \hat{\mathbf{x}} \) is the maximum-likelihood estimate. The covariance formula shows two things: more data (larger \( A^\top A \)) shrinks the uncertainty like \( 1/\sqrt m \), and nearly collinear columns make \( (A^\top A)^{-1} \) huge, so coefficients become unstable.
Several extensions follow from the same geometry. Weighted least squares minimizes \( \sum w_i r_i^2 \), which is the right method when measurements have different noise levels (weights \( 1/\sigma_i^2 \)); this is exactly how the Kalman update combines a prediction with a measurement. Ridge regression minimizes \( \|A\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\|^2+\lambda\|\mathbf{x}\|^2 \), which adds \( \lambda I \) to \( A^\top A \), makes it invertible, and shrinks coefficients. Non-linear least squares (Gauss–Newton) linearizes the model around the current guess and solves a linear least-squares problem for the step.
Why squares, and not another power? Three reasons work together. The squared error is smooth, so calculus gives a closed-form minimum, while the absolute error has a corner at zero and needs an iterative method. The squared error is the ordinary length in Euclidean geometry, so orthogonal projection, which is a cheap and stable operation, is the exact answer. And under Gaussian noise the squared error is the negative log-likelihood, so the method is also the statistically natural one. The price is the sensitivity to outliers that the example below shows.
Least squares can also be updated cheaply. Suppose you have the solution for \( m \) rows and a new row \( \mathbf{a}^\top \) with value \( b \) arrives. You do not need to refit: you keep \( (A^\top A)^{-1} \) and apply a rank-one correction (the Sherman–Morrison formula), which costs \( O(n^2) \) instead of \( O(mn^2) \). The result is recursive least squares, and the Kalman filter is the same idea with a state that also moves in time. This is how tracking and navigation systems fit a model to a stream of measurements in constant time per measurement.

example: Fit a line \( y = c + d\,t \) through the three points \( (t,y) = (1,1), (2,2), (3,2) \). A line has two unknowns and there are three equations, and no line passes through all points. The design matrix has a column of ones and a column of \( t \) values:
\[ A = \begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&3\end{pmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix} \]
Compute \( A^\top A = \begin{pmatrix}3&6\\6&14\end{pmatrix} \) and \( A^\top\mathbf{b} = (1+2+2,\ 1+4+6) = (5, 11) \). The normal equations are \( 3c+6d=5 \) and \( 6c+14d=11 \). Multiply the first by 2: \( 6c+12d=10 \); subtract from the second: \( 2d=1 \), so \( d=0.5 \), and then \( c=(5-3)/3=2/3 \). The best line is \( y = 0.667+0.5t \).
Check the geometry. Fitted values: \( 1.167,\ 1.667,\ 2.167 \). Residuals \( \mathbf{r}=\mathbf{b}-\hat{\mathbf{b}} = (-0.167,\ 0.333,\ -0.167) \). The sum of residuals is 0 (perpendicular to the column of ones), and \( 1(-0.167)+2(0.333)+3(-0.167)=0 \) (perpendicular to the column of \( t \)). The squared error is \( 0.0278+0.1111+0.0278=0.1667 \). The total variation of \( \mathbf{b} \) about its mean 5/3 is \( 0.444+0.111+0.111=0.667 \), so \( R^2=1-0.1667/0.667=0.75 \). The projection matrix is \( P=\tfrac16\begin{pmatrix}5&2&-1\\2&2&2\\-1&2&5\end{pmatrix} \), with trace 2, the number of parameters. The leverages are \( 5/6,\ 1/3,\ 5/6 \): the end points have much more pull than the middle point.
Leverage matters. Add a fourth point \( (4,10) \) that is far above the trend. The new fit becomes \( y=-3+2.7t \): one point changed the slope from 0.5 to 2.7, and the line now misses the first three points badly. Squaring the errors gives large errors a very large vote. Finally, ridge with \( \lambda=1 \) on the original three points solves \( (A^\top A+I)\mathbf{x}=A^\top\mathbf{b} \) and gives \( c=0.375,\ d=0.583 \): the intercept shrinks toward zero and the slope grows a little, as the penalty trades fit for smaller coefficients.
intuition: Standing a stick upright from a point to a floor gives the shortest distance only when the stick is perpendicular to the floor; the best fit is the same idea, with the "floor" being everything your model can say and the "stick" the error you cannot explain.
The picture also explains the algebra. If the leftover error still had a part along some column of the matrix, you could shift your answer a little in that direction and reduce the error, so the best answer has no such part. That is the normal equation, restated: the error must be perpendicular to everything the model can express. Squared error is chosen partly because it makes this picture exact, as the length in ordinary geometry, so all the tools of orthogonality (projections, Pythagoras, QR) apply directly.
mistakes: People often think least squares fits only straight lines. In fact the model must be linear in the parameters, not in the inputs. A polynomial \( c_0+c_1t+c_2t^2 \), a sum of sinusoids, or any set of fixed basis functions gives a linear least-squares problem, because each basis function is just another column of \( A \).
People often think least squares assumes the errors are normal. In fact the calculation works for any data. Normality is needed only to say that the estimate is the maximum-likelihood one and to give exact confidence intervals. The Gauss–Markov theorem needs only uncorrelated errors with equal variance, and says least squares is then the best linear unbiased estimator.
People often think that squaring the error is a neutral choice. In fact it is a decision about outliers. The example showed a single bad point moving the slope from 0.5 to 2.7. If the data has outliers, a robust loss (absolute error, Huber loss, or RANSAC) is the better model; if the data has no outliers and Gaussian noise, squares are optimal.
People often think that if the columns are nearly collinear, the fit must be poor. In fact the predictions can be excellent while the coefficients are meaningless. With two almost identical features, the model can put weight \( +1000 \) on one and \( -999 \) on the other, giving stable predictions and unstable, uninterpretable coefficients; the variance \( \sigma^2(A^\top A)^{-1} \) is huge. Do not read coefficient values as effects in that case.
friction: The answer is only as good as the model. If the real relationship is non-linear or the important features are missing, least squares returns a precise fit to the wrong model. Residual plots against each feature and against the fitted values show structure (curves, funnels) that the assumptions deny. Heteroscedastic noise (variance that grows with the level) makes plain least squares inefficient and its standard errors wrong; use weights or robust standard errors. Correlated errors, such as time series, break the standard error formulas even when the coefficients are fine.
Numerically, the normal equations lose accuracy when \( \kappa(A) \) is large, since the condition number is squared; for a 10-column design matrix with \( \kappa = 10^8 \) all precision is lost. Use QR or SVD. For huge data, \( m\times n \) with \( m=10^9 \) rows, you cannot hold \( A \), but you can compute \( A^\top A \) and \( A^\top\mathbf{b} \) as sums over rows in one distributed pass (that is how Spark MLlib solves linear regression for a modest \( n \)), or use stochastic gradient methods for large \( n \). Sketching methods reduce \( m \) first with random projections.
Finally, least squares makes no promise outside the data it saw. Prediction errors grow when you extrapolate, and more parameters always reduce the training error, so overfitting is built in: with \( n=m \) the fit is perfect and useless. Regularization (ridge, lasso), cross-validation and held-out data are needed to choose model size, and the \( R^2 \) on training data is not evidence of predictive power.
practice: Recognise it. A problem with "find parameters so that predictions match many observations" and a model linear in the parameters is a least-squares problem, however it is presented. Write down \( A \) and \( \mathbf{b} \) first. Standardize or scale the columns, include an intercept column, and check \( \kappa(A) \) with `numpy.linalg.cond`. In NumPy, call `numpy.linalg.lstsq(A, b)`, which uses the SVD and handles rank-deficient cases; in SciPy, `scipy.linalg.lstsq`, and `scipy.optimize.least_squares` for the non-linear version with robust loss options. In scikit-learn, `LinearRegression` and `Ridge` wrap the same solvers.
Inspect the leverage and residuals. Large leverage \( P_{ii} \) (above about \( 2n/m \)) flags points that control the fit; large standardized residuals flag outliers. Use a robust loss if either exists, or investigate them. Add ridge (\( \lambda>0 \)) when features are many or correlated, and choose \( \lambda \) by cross-validation. When measurements have known, different noise levels, weight them by inverse variance.
Where you meet it in engineering: forecasting and trend lines in capacity planning, calibration (mapping sensor readings to true values), cost models fitted to benchmarks (time \( \approx c_0+c_1 n + c_2 n\log n \)), regression layers in ML, and the Kalman filter in tracking and sensor fusion. To study: derive the normal equations both ways (calculus and geometry), do the three-point example by hand, then read Boyd and Vandenberghe's "Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra" chapters 12 to 14, which are free online.
tradeoffs: Choose the normal equations with Cholesky when \( n \) is small, \( m \) is huge, and the columns are well-conditioned: you form an \( n\times n\) matrix in one pass over the data and it is the fastest method. Choose QR when accuracy matters and \( \kappa(A) \) may be large (up to about \( 10^8 \)). Choose SVD or a column-pivoted QR when the matrix may be rank-deficient or you want to cut small singular directions; you pay several times the cost.
Choose ordinary least squares when noise is roughly Gaussian with equal variance and you want interpretable, unbiased coefficients. Choose ridge when features are correlated or numerous: you accept a small bias to get a large reduction in variance. Choose lasso when you want a sparse model with few non-zero coefficients; there is no closed form and the fit is iterative.
Choose squared loss when large errors are truly worse than small ones in proportion to the square and outliers are rare. Choose absolute or Huber loss, or a RANSAC-style fit, when outliers are likely; the problem is no longer a linear solve, but a convex program or an iteration.
Choose a batch solve when the data fits and arrives once. Choose recursive least squares or a Kalman filter when data arrives continuously and you must update the estimate for each observation at constant cost, with the extra modelling work of a state model.
quiz: What does the normal equation \( A^\top A\hat{\mathbf{x}}=A^\top\mathbf{b} \) say geometrically? || The residual \( \mathbf{b}-A\hat{\mathbf{x}} \) is perpendicular to every column of \( A \), so \( A\hat{\mathbf{x}} \) is the orthogonal projection of \( \mathbf{b} \) onto the column space and the closest point to \( \mathbf{b} \).

value: A recursive algorithm solves a problem by solving smaller copies of it, so its running time is defined in terms of itself. Merge sort sorts two halves and then merges them, so its time \( T(n) \) satisfies \( T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n \). That is a recurrence. It is a correct description of the cost, but it is not yet an answer: you cannot tell from it whether the algorithm takes \( n\log n \) steps or \( n^2 \). Solving the recurrence turns the self-referential description into a closed growth rate such as \( \Theta(n\log n) \).
The master theorem makes this almost mechanical for the most common shape, divide and conquer: split into \( a \) subproblems, each of size \( n/b \), and spend \( f(n) \) extra work to split and combine. Compare \( f(n) \) with the number \( n^{\log_b a} \), which is the number of leaves in the recursion tree. Whichever is bigger decides the answer, or, if they are equal, the work is the same on every level and you get an extra \( \log n \). With this one comparison you can read off the cost of binary search, merge sort, Karatsuba multiplication, Strassen matrix multiplication and the fast Fourier transform.
This is the tool that the algorithm layers above use to justify their bounds: Big-O analysis of recursive code, the \( O(n\log n) \) of merge sort and of the FFT, and the design of divide-and-conquer algorithms in general. The layers below supply the three parts of the method: asymptotic analysis gives the language of growth rates in which the answers are written, combinatorics counts the nodes and levels of the recursion tree, and proofs by induction are what make a guessed solution into a theorem.
terms: Recurrence relation — an equation that defines a sequence or a cost in terms of its own earlier values, such as \( T(n)=2T(n/2)+n \).
Base case — the smallest input, where the recurrence stops, usually \( T(1) = \) constant.
Recursion tree — a drawing with the problem at the root, its subproblems as children, and the work done at each node written beside it.
Branching factor \( a \) — the number of subproblems created at each step.
Shrink factor \( b \) — the factor by which the input size falls from one level to the next; the size at depth \( i \) is \( n/b^i \).
Critical exponent \( c = \log_b a \) — the exponent for which the number of leaves is \( n^c \).
Θ (theta) — a tight bound: the cost is bounded above and below by constant multiples of the same function.
Substitution method — guess a closed form, then prove it by induction.
Akra–Bazzi theorem — a generalization of the master theorem to subproblems of unequal sizes.
history: Recurrences are old: Fibonacci defined his sequence by one in 1202, and de Moivre and Euler solved linear recurrences in the 1700s with characteristic equations. Computer science needed a new kind, because divide-and-conquer algorithms generate recurrences with a division inside, \( T(n/2) \), not a subtraction. John von Neumann described merge sort in 1945, and by the 1960s several fast algorithms had the same shape: Karatsuba's multiplication (1960, published 1962) with three half-size products, and Strassen's matrix multiplication (1969) with seven half-size products. Each was analysed with its own tailor-made argument.
Jon Bentley, Dorothea Haken and James Saxe gave the general solution in "A General Method for Solving Divide-and-Conquer Recurrences" (1980), the source of what we call the master theorem. Their paper treated the recurrence \( T(n)=aT(n/b)+f(n) \) in one theorem. The theorem was spread to students by the textbook of Cormen, Leiserson and Rivest (1990, now CLRS), which gave the three-case form most people learn. Mohamad Akra and Louay Bazzi (1998) proved a more general theorem for recurrences such as \( T(n)=T(n/5)+T(7n/10)+n \), which the master theorem cannot handle; Tom Leighton later gave a simpler version.
Recurrences also drive modern results. The Cooley–Tukey FFT (1965) is \( T(n)=2T(n/2)+O(n) \). Strassen's exponent \( \log_2 7\approx2.807 \) started a line of research on fast matrix multiplication, whose best known exponent is now about 2.37, still found by analysing recursive constructions.
how: The cleanest way to understand the master theorem is to draw the recursion tree and add up the work level by level. Take \( T(n) = aT(n/b) + f(n) \). The root does \( f(n) \) work and creates \( a \) children, each on size \( n/b \). At depth \( i \) there are \( a^i \) nodes, each of size \( n/b^i \), so the work on that level is \( a^i f(n/b^i) \). The recursion stops when the size is 1, which happens at depth \( \log_b n \). The number of leaves is \( a^{\log_b n} \), and by a change of base this is \( n^{\log_b a} \). Counting nodes and levels like this is the combinatorics contribution. The total cost is the sum over all levels:
\[ T(n) = \sum_{i=0}^{\log_b n - 1} a^i\, f\!\left(\frac{n}{b^i}\right) + \Theta\!\left(n^{\log_b a}\right) \]
The first term is the work in the internal nodes, the second term is the work at the leaves (each leaf costs a constant). Now ask how the level cost changes as you go down. The number of nodes grows by a factor \( a \) per level, while the size of each shrinks by \( b \). If \( f \) is a polynomial \( n^d \), the level cost is \( a^i (n/b^i)^d = n^d (a/b^d)^i \), a geometric series with ratio \( a/b^d \). Geometric series have three behaviours, and these are the three cases of the theorem.
Set \( c=\log_b a \). Case 1: if \( f(n) \) is polynomially smaller than \( n^c \) (for example \( f(n)=O(n^{c-\varepsilon}) \)), the ratio is greater than 1, work grows with depth, and the leaves dominate: \( T(n)=\Theta(n^c) \). Case 2: if \( f(n)=\Theta(n^c) \), every level costs about the same, \( n^c \), and there are \( \log_b n \) levels: \( T(n)=\Theta(n^c\log n) \). Case 3: if \( f(n) \) is polynomially larger than \( n^c \) and satisfies a regularity condition (\( af(n/b)\le kf(n) \) for some \( k<1 \)), the ratio is below 1, work shrinks with depth, and the root dominates: \( T(n)=\Theta(f(n)) \). Written together:
\[ T(n)=aT(n/b)+f(n)\ \Longrightarrow\ T(n)=\begin{cases}\Theta(n^{c}) & f(n)=O(n^{c-\varepsilon})\\ \Theta(n^{c}\log n) & f(n)=\Theta(n^{c})\\ \Theta(f(n)) & f(n)=\Omega(n^{c+\varepsilon}),\ af(n/b)\le kf(n)\end{cases} \]
There is an extended case 2: if \( f(n)=\Theta(n^c\log^k n) \) with \( k\ge0 \), then \( T(n)=\Theta(n^c\log^{k+1}n) \). So \( T(n)=2T(n/2)+n\log n \) is \( \Theta(n\log^2 n) \).
The theorem does not cover everything. If \( f(n) \) falls in the gap between cases, for example \( f(n)=n/\log n \) with \( a=b=2 \), it is too close to \( n^c \) for the polynomial gap, and you must go back to the recursion tree. If the subproblems have different sizes (\( T(n)=T(n/3)+T(2n/3)+n \)), use the recursion tree again or Akra–Bazzi. If the problem shrinks by subtraction, \( T(n)=T(n-1)+n \), it is a different family: expand it to \( n+(n-1)+\dots=\Theta(n^2) \). And for linear recurrences with constant coefficients such as \( F(n)=F(n-1)+F(n-2) \), use the characteristic equation \( x^2=x+1 \), which gives growth like \( \varphi^n \), with \( \varphi\approx1.618 \); this is the same eigenvalue idea that governs matrix powers.
When a recurrence has no standard form, use the substitution method. Guess the answer, say \( T(n)\le dn\log n \), and prove it by induction: assume it for smaller \( n \), plug it into the recurrence, and show it holds for \( n \). This is where proofs by induction enter, and where care is needed. A guess that is correct but too tight in the leading term can fail by a lower-order term, and the fix is to subtract a lower-order term from the guess (for example, prove \( T(n)\le dn-e \) rather than \( T(n)\le dn \)). Induction also reminds you to check the base case, since the recurrence only starts to hold for large enough \( n \).

example: Classify five familiar recurrences with \( c=\log_ba \).
Binary search: \( T(n)=T(n/2)+1 \). Here \( a=1,\ b=2,\ c=0 \), so \( n^c=1 \). The extra work \( f(n)=1=\Theta(n^0) \) matches, so case 2 gives \( \Theta(\log n) \). Merge sort: \( T(n)=2T(n/2)+n \). Here \( c=1 \), \( f(n)=n \) matches \( n^1 \), so case 2 gives \( \Theta(n\log n) \). Tree traversal: \( T(n)=2T(n/2)+1 \). Here \( c=1 \) and \( f=1 \) is smaller than \( n \), so case 1 gives \( \Theta(n) \). A split costing \( n^2 \): \( T(n)=2T(n/2)+n^2 \). Here \( c=1 \), \( f=n^2 \) is larger, and the regularity condition holds because \( 2(n/2)^2=n^2/2\le\tfrac12n^2 \), so case 3 gives \( \Theta(n^2) \). Strassen: \( T(n)=7T(n/2)+n^2 \). Here \( c=\log_27\approx2.807 \) and \( n^2 \) is smaller, so case 1 gives \( \Theta(n^{2.807}) \), beating the \( \Theta(n^3) \) of the standard method \( 8T(n/2)+n^2 \).
Now trace Karatsuba with numbers. Multiplying two \( n \)-digit numbers by the school method takes \( n^2 \) digit multiplications. Karatsuba splits each number into two halves and uses 3 half-size multiplications instead of 4, plus additions that cost about \( n \): \( T(n)=3T(n/2)+n \) with \( T(1)=1 \). Take \( n=1024=2^{10} \). The tree has 11 levels. Work per level: level 0 is 1024; level 1 is \( 3\times512=1536 \); level 2 is \( 9\times256=2304 \); and so on, growing by 1.5 each time, until level 10 has \( 3^{10}=59{,}049 \) leaves of size 1. The exact closed form is \( T(n)=3n^{\log_23}-2n \), which gives \( 3\times59{,}049-2048=175{,}099 \). The school method needs \( 1024^2=1{,}048{,}576 \) multiplications. The recursion therefore uses about 6 times fewer operations in total (and 17.8 times fewer leaf multiplications), and the gap widens with \( n \) because the exponent is 1.585 instead of 2. In practice the extra additions mean Karatsuba wins only above a threshold of roughly 20 to 100 words in real libraries.
Finally merge sort by levels for \( n=1024 \): each of the 10 levels does \( 2^i\cdot(1024/2^i)=1024 \) merge work in total, so \( T(1024)=10\times1024=10{,}240 \) comparisons at most, against \( 1024^2/2\approx524{,}000 \) for a quadratic sort: about 51 times fewer. Every level costs the same, which is exactly what case 2 means.
intuition: In a recursion tree, each level either gets more expensive, stays the same, or gets cheaper as you go down, and the total is decided by whichever end of the tree is heaviest: the leaves, the whole tree evenly, or the root.
This is why the one comparison between \( f(n) \) and \( n^{\log_b a} \) is enough. It asks whether the work you add at each step grows or shrinks relative to the explosion in the number of subproblems. If splitting is cheap and branching is fast, almost all the work is in the many tiny leaves. If splitting is expensive, the top call dominates and recursion barely matters. When the two balance, you pay the same on every level and the only thing left to count is the number of levels, which is logarithmic.
mistakes: People often think that dividing the problem always gives \( n\log n \). In fact the result depends on the balance of branching and combining. Karatsuba, with \( a=3 \), is \( n^{1.585} \), the tree traversal with \( a=2 \) is linear, and a divide-and-conquer with \( a=4 \) and linear combining is \( n^2 \), no better than the direct method.
People often think the master theorem solves every divide-and-conquer recurrence. In fact it needs equal-sized subproblems, a polynomial gap between \( f(n) \) and \( n^c \), and in case 3 the regularity condition. Quicksort's average case, \( T(n)=T(n/3)+T(2n/3)+n \) type splits, and \( f(n)=n/\log n \) need other tools.
People often think constants and base cases do not matter. In fact they decide which algorithm is faster at real sizes. Karatsuba has a much larger constant than the school method; Strassen's advantage appears only for matrices of several hundred rows and above, and the asymptotically best matrix multiplication algorithms are not used at all because their constants are enormous. Asymptotic statements describe growth, not speed at \( n=1000 \).
People often think that dropping floors and ceilings (using \( n/2 \) for \( \lceil n/2\rceil \)) changes the answer. In fact for the standard cases it does not change the growth rate, which is why the theorem is stated for exact divisions. It can matter in the details of a proof, and you should check it if you use the substitution method.
friction: The master theorem has narrow reach. Many real recursions have unequal splits (quicksort with random pivots, median of medians), non-polynomial combine costs, or subproblem sizes that depend on the data. For these you need the recursion tree, the Akra–Bazzi integral, probabilistic arguments (expected cost with random pivots), or amortized analysis. The theorem also says nothing about the case \( f(n)=\Theta(n^c/\log n) \) and similar "gap" functions.
Worst-case recurrences describe an algorithm on an adversarial input, which may be far from typical behaviour. Quicksort has worst-case \( \Theta(n^2) \) and typical \( \Theta(n\log n) \); the recurrence for the worst case, \( T(n)=T(n-1)+n \), is not even of master-theorem form. A recurrence for time also ignores the cost model: memory access patterns and cache misses can dominate, so a theoretically better recursion can lose to a simpler one on real hardware. External memory and parallel models have their own recurrences.
Finally, a recurrence is only as good as the model of the algorithm behind it. Choosing the wrong measure (counting comparisons when the real cost is data movement), forgetting the cost of copying in the split step, or letting hidden recursion depth consume the stack, all make the recurrence true but irrelevant. Verify the prediction against measurements: doubling \( n \) should multiply time by about \( 2^{c} \) in case 1, and by slightly more than 2 for \( n\log n \).
practice: When you write or read a recursive algorithm, write its recurrence in one line before anything else: how many recursive calls, on what fraction of the input, and what work outside the calls. Then compute \( c=\log_ba \) and compare with the exponent of the outside work. A fast check for linear outside work \( f(n)=n \): \( a<b \) gives linear time (the root dominates), \( a=b \) gives \( n\log n \), and \( a>b \) gives \( n^{\log_b a} \) (the leaves dominate).
Use this reading to design. If a divide-and-conquer algorithm is too slow, ask whether you can reduce \( a \) (Karatsuba reduced 4 to 3, Strassen reduced 8 to 7) or reduce \( f(n) \) (a linear-time merge instead of a quadratic one). Where the work at small sizes is wasteful, stop the recursion at a cutoff and switch to a simple method; production sort routines switch to insertion sort for sizes of about 16 to 32, and big-number libraries switch from Karatsuba to school multiplication below a threshold they tune per machine.
To check a bound, test it: run the code for \( n=2^{10},2^{11},\dots \), plot time against \( n \) on log–log axes, and compare the slope with \( c \). For hard cases, write the recursion-tree table by hand for a few levels, as in the diagram. In distributed data processing the same reasoning appears when you plan tree aggregations: with fan-in \( b \), a reduction over \( n \) partitions takes \( \log_b n \) rounds, with \( f=O(b) \) work per node. To learn it well, solve ten recurrences by tree, then by theorem, then verify one with induction; the free text "Mathematics for Computer Science" (Lehman, Leighton, Meyer) and CLRS chapter 4 are enough.
tradeoffs: Choose the master theorem when the recurrence has equal-sized subproblems, a clear polynomial relation between \( f(n) \) and \( n^{\log_b a} \), and you want an answer in a minute. Choose the recursion tree when the theorem does not apply or when you want to see where the work is, at the cost of a little more drawing and care with sums.
Choose the substitution method with induction when you need a proof and not just a derivation, or when the recurrence is irregular; the cost is that you must guess the answer first and then manage lower-order terms. Choose Akra–Bazzi when subproblem sizes are unequal, for example \( T(n)=T(n/5)+T(7n/10)+n \), where the result is \( \Theta(n) \). Choose the characteristic-equation method for linear recurrences with subtracted indices, such as Fibonacci, which grow exponentially.
Choose a divide-and-conquer algorithm with a better exponent (Karatsuba, Strassen) only when \( n \) is above the crossover point where its savings beat the larger constant and extra memory: roughly tens of machine words for Karatsuba and a few hundred matrix rows for Strassen. Below that, the simpler method is faster. In both cases you pay in numerical stability (Strassen) or code complexity.
Choose worst-case recurrences for guarantees, such as real-time systems and adversarial inputs, and expected-case analysis for performance planning when randomization or typical data makes the worst case rare.

@@ mcmc
value: Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is a way to draw random samples from a probability distribution that you can evaluate but cannot sample from directly. This is the usual situation in Bayesian statistics. The posterior distribution of a model's parameters is proportional to "likelihood times prior", which is easy to compute for any given parameter value, but its normalizing constant is a high-dimensional integral that nobody can calculate. MCMC avoids that constant. It builds a random walk that wanders through parameter space and spends time in each region in proportion to its probability. After enough steps, the visited points are samples from the target.
Once you have samples, every question becomes an average. The posterior mean is the average of the draws. A 95% credible interval is the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile of the draws. The probability that a variant beats control in an A/B test is the fraction of draws in which it does. This is why MCMC lets you fit models with hundreds or thousands of parameters, such as hierarchical models, mixed-effects models and Bayesian networks, where exact formulas do not exist. Stan, PyMC and NumPyro are tools built around it; statistical physics uses it to simulate systems of particles, which is where it started.
For the layers above, Gibbs sampling is the standard inference method in large probabilistic graphical models, and Langevin dynamics (a gradient-guided random walk) links MCMC to sampling in diffusion models. The layers below supply the three ingredients. Markov chains give the walk and the guarantee that, under mild conditions, it converges to a unique long-run distribution. Monte Carlo methods explain why averages of samples estimate expectations, and how fast the error falls. Bayesian inference supplies the typical target: a posterior known only up to a constant.
terms: Target distribution \( \pi \) — the distribution we want samples from; we only need to evaluate it up to a constant factor.
Proposal \( q(y\mid x) \) — the rule that suggests a candidate next point \( y \) from the current point \( x \).
Acceptance probability — the chance \( \alpha \) of moving to the candidate; if rejected, the chain stays where it is and repeats the current value.
Detailed balance — the condition \( \pi(x)P(x\to y)=\pi(y)P(y\to x) \); it guarantees that \( \pi \) is stationary for the chain.
Stationary distribution — a distribution that does not change when one more step of the chain is applied.
Burn-in (warm-up) — the first steps, discarded because the chain has not yet reached the typical region of \( \pi \).
Autocorrelation — the correlation between draws \( k \) steps apart; consecutive draws of a chain are not independent.
Effective sample size (ESS) — the number of independent draws that would give the same accuracy as the correlated chain.
\( \hat R \) (R-hat) — a diagnostic that compares several chains started from different points; values near 1 suggest they agree.
history: The method was invented in physics. In 1953 Nicholas Metropolis, Arianna Rosenbluth, Marshall Rosenbluth, Augusta Teller and Edward Teller published "Equation of State Calculations by Fast Computing Machines", which simulated a system of hard spheres on the MANIAC computer at Los Alamos. They needed average properties of a gas under the Boltzmann distribution \( \propto e^{-E/kT} \), whose normalizing constant is impossible to compute, and they found that a random walk with a simple accept-or-reject rule gave correct averages. The central trick was to use only ratios of probabilities, so the constant cancels.
W. Keith Hastings generalized the rule in 1970 to proposals that are not symmetric, which is the Metropolis–Hastings algorithm used today. Stuart and Donald Geman (1984) introduced Gibbs sampling for image restoration and named it after the physicist Gibbs. The turning point for statistics came in 1990, when Alan Gelfand and Adrian Smith showed that these samplers could handle ordinary Bayesian models, and the field of applied Bayesian statistics grew quickly. The BUGS software of the 1990s made it practical.
The next advance was to use gradients. Duane, Kennedy, Pendleton and Roweth (1987) proposed hybrid Monte Carlo, now called Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC), which treats the parameters as a particle moving under the force of the log-density. Radford Neal developed it for statistics in the 1990s, and Hoffman and Gelman (2014) made it automatic with the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS), the default in Stan and PyMC. Roberts, Gelman and Gilks (1997) showed how to tune step sizes. Convergence checks by Gelman and Rubin (1992) and the improved rank-normalized \( \hat R \) of Vehtari and colleagues (2021) became standard practice.
how: The goal is a Markov chain \( X_0,X_1,X_2,\dots \) whose stationary distribution is the target \( \pi \). From Markov chain theory we know the main result: if a chain is irreducible (it can reach any region) and aperiodic, and \( \pi \) is stationary, then the distribution of \( X_t \) converges to \( \pi \) from any starting point, and long-run averages along the chain converge to expectations under \( \pi \). So the only design task is to build transition probabilities \( P(x\to y) \) that have \( \pi \) as stationary distribution. The easy way to guarantee that is detailed balance: if \( \pi(x)P(x\to y)=\pi(y)P(y\to x) \) for all pairs, then summing over \( x \) shows \( \pi P=\pi \).
Metropolis–Hastings builds such a chain in two steps. Step 1: from the current point \( x \), propose a candidate \( y \) from a proposal distribution \( q(y\mid x) \), for example a Gaussian centred at \( x \). Step 2: accept the candidate with probability
\[ \alpha(x,y)=\min\!\left(1,\ \frac{\pi(y)\,q(x\mid y)}{\pi(x)\,q(y\mid x)}\right) \]
Otherwise stay at \( x \) (and count \( x \) again). Check detailed balance: the move rate from \( x \) to \( y \) is \( \pi(x)q(y\mid x)\alpha(x,y) \), and one of the two ratios inside the min is at least 1, so \( \pi(x)q(y\mid x)\alpha(x,y)=\min\big(\pi(x)q(y\mid x),\ \pi(y)q(x\mid y)\big) \), which is symmetric in \( x \) and \( y \). Hence detailed balance holds. The key point for Bayesian use: \( \pi \) appears only as a ratio, so a posterior \( \pi(\theta)\propto p(D\mid\theta)p(\theta) \) can be used without the normalizing constant. If the proposal is symmetric, \( q(x\mid y)=q(y\mid x) \), the rule reduces to the original Metropolis rule: always accept a move to higher probability, and accept a move to lower probability with probability equal to the ratio.
The estimate then comes from the Monte Carlo principle: after discarding burn-in, \( \frac1N\sum_{t}g(X_t) \) approximates \( E_\pi[g(X)] \). But because consecutive draws are correlated, the error is larger than for independent draws. If \( \sigma^2 \) is the variance of \( g \) under \( \pi \), the standard error is about \( \sigma/\sqrt{\text{ESS}} \), where
\[ \text{ESS}\approx\frac{N}{1+2\sum_{k\ge1}\rho_k} \]
and \( \rho_k \) is the autocorrelation at lag \( k \). A sampler is good when this denominator (the integrated autocorrelation time) is small.
Gibbs sampling is the special case that updates one variable (or block) at a time by drawing it from its exact conditional distribution given all others, \( \pi(x_i\mid x_{-i}) \). It is Metropolis–Hastings with acceptance probability always 1. It is natural for graphical models, because the conditional of a node depends only on its neighbours, but it moves slowly when variables are strongly correlated.
Tuning the proposal decides the efficiency. For a random-walk Metropolis with Gaussian steps of size \( s \): too small, and nearly every proposal is accepted but the chain crawls; too large, and almost every proposal is rejected and the chain stays still. In high dimensions the optimal acceptance rate is about 0.234, and about 0.44 in one dimension (Roberts, Gelman and Gilks). The cost grows with dimension \( d \): random-walk Metropolis needs \( O(d) \) steps per independent sample, while HMC needs about \( O(d^{1/4}) \) because it follows the gradient \( \nabla\log\pi \) along smooth trajectories, using the leapfrog integrator, and then applies a Metropolis correction for the integration error. The Langevin variant of this idea, \( x'=x+\epsilon\nabla\log\pi(x)+\sqrt{2\epsilon}\,z \), is the sampler behind score-based diffusion models.
Finally, diagnostics. Run at least four chains from dispersed starting points. Compare within-chain and between-chain variance: \( \hat R \) should be below about 1.01. Check ESS (hundreds per parameter at least), look at trace plots for sticking, and watch for divergences in HMC, which signal regions of high curvature where the sampler cannot be trusted. None of these diagnostics prove convergence; they can only detect some failures.

example: Take a target on four states \( \{1,2,3,4\} \) with probabilities \( \pi=(0.1,0.2,0.3,0.4) \). Imagine we know only the unnormalized weights \( (1,2,3,4) \); the sum 10 is not needed. The proposal picks one of the other three states uniformly, so \( q \) is symmetric, and accepts with probability \( \min(1,\pi_y/\pi_x) \).
Transition probabilities from state 1: to state 2 it is \( \tfrac13\min(1,2/1)=\tfrac13 \); the same for 3 and 4; so it always leaves, \( P(1\to1)=0 \). From state 4: to 3 it is \( \tfrac13\cdot0.75=0.25 \); to 2 it is \( \tfrac13\cdot0.5=0.167 \); to 1 it is \( \tfrac13\cdot0.25=0.083 \); and it stays with probability \( 1-0.5=0.5 \). Check detailed balance for the pair (3,4): \( \pi_3P(3\to4)=0.3\times\tfrac13=0.1 \) and \( \pi_4P(4\to3)=0.4\times0.25=0.1 \). They are equal. Check stationarity for state 1: the chance to arrive is \( \pi_2P(2\to1)+\pi_3P(3\to1)+\pi_4P(4\to1)=0.2\times\tfrac16+0.3\times\tfrac19+0.4\times\tfrac1{12}=0.0333+0.0333+0.0333=0.1 \), which equals \( \pi_1 \). The other eigenvalues of this transition matrix are \( \tfrac16,\ 0,\ -\tfrac16 \), so the chain forgets its start very fast: the error shrinks about six-fold per step.
Now run the chain by hand. Start at \( x_0=1 \). Step 1: propose 4, ratio \( 4>1 \), accept: \( x_1=4 \). Step 2: propose 1, ratio 0.25, draw \( u=0.60 \); since \( 0.60\ge0.25 \), reject: \( x_2=4 \). Step 3: propose 2, ratio 0.5, \( u=0.30<0.5 \), accept: \( x_3=2 \). Step 4: propose 3, ratio 1.5, accept: \( x_4=3 \). Step 5: propose 4, accept: \( x_5=4 \). Step 6: propose 3, ratio 0.75, \( u=0.90 \), reject: \( x_6=4 \). Step 7: propose 2, ratio 0.5, \( u=0.20 \), accept: \( x_7=2 \). Step 8: propose 4, accept: \( x_8=4 \). The samples \( x_1,\dots,x_8 = 4,4,2,3,4,4,2,4 \) have mean 3.375. The true mean is \( 0.1+0.4+0.9+1.6=3.0 \). Eight draws from a start in an unlikely state give a noisy answer, which is expected.
Cost of accuracy: suppose the posterior standard deviation of a parameter is 0.5, and you want the posterior mean accurate to ±0.01 (Monte Carlo standard error). You need \( \text{ESS}=(0.5/0.01)^2=2{,}500 \). If the chain's integrated autocorrelation time is 10, you need \( 25{,}000 \) post-warm-up draws. If the autocorrelation time is 100 (a poorly tuned sampler), you need 250,000.
intuition: Imagine a hiker on a foggy hillside who can only compare the height of the spot they stand on with the height of a nearby spot; if they always accept uphill steps and accept downhill steps only sometimes, they end up spending their time in each place in proportion to its height.
You cannot see the whole landscape (the normalizing constant), but local ratios are enough. The accept-reject rule is built so that traffic between any two places is balanced in both directions: as many walkers move from A to B as from B to A when the walkers are spread according to the target. When traffic is balanced, the target distribution does not change, so it is what the walk settles into. The algorithm is a recipe for making the long-run behaviour exactly what you want.
mistakes: People often think MCMC draws are independent samples. In fact consecutive draws are correlated, because each step starts from the last point. The effective sample size can be 10 to 1,000 times smaller than the number of draws, so 10,000 draws may be worth only 100 independent ones.
People often think the chain converges once the trace plot looks flat. In fact one chain can sit in one mode of a multi-modal target and look perfectly stable while missing others. Run several chains from dispersed starts, compare them with \( \hat R \), and be suspicious when the target may have several separated modes.
People often think a high acceptance rate means a good sampler. In fact a 95% acceptance rate often means the steps are tiny, so the chain moves slowly and autocorrelation is high. For random-walk Metropolis in high dimensions about 23% is closer to ideal.
People often think MCMC finds the most likely parameter value. In fact it explores the whole distribution, including spread and shape. The mode (maximum a posteriori point) is an optimization problem; MCMC gives the mean, quantiles and joint structure. In high dimensions, typical draws do not sit near the mode at all, because the volume around the mode is small.
friction: Cost is the main problem. Each step needs one (or, for HMC, tens of) evaluations of the unnormalized posterior and its gradient, and for large data sets each evaluation touches every observation. A model with a million data points makes each step expensive, so MCMC can take hours or days where variational inference or optimization takes minutes. Subsampling methods (stochastic-gradient MCMC) exist but add bias and tuning.
Geometry is the second problem. Strongly correlated parameters, very different scales, narrow curved ridges ("funnels" in hierarchical models) and multiple separated modes make samplers slow or wrong. Random-walk Metropolis needs time that grows with dimension; even HMC can fail when curvature changes sharply, which shows up as divergent transitions and calls for reparameterization (for example the non-centred form of hierarchical models). Discrete parameters cannot use gradients and need Gibbs or marginalization.
Third, correctness is hard to certify. Convergence can be shown to fail but never be proved from finite output, and burn-in length is a judgement. The error bars you quote are valid only if the chain mixes well; a chain that has not found a second mode gives confident wrong answers. Practical remedies are many chains, longer runs, posterior predictive checks and simulation-based calibration (fit data generated from known parameters and see whether the truth is recovered). Parallelism helps only across chains, because within a chain the steps are sequential.
practice: Use a probabilistic programming system rather than writing a sampler: Stan (HMC/NUTS), PyMC or NumPyro with NUTS. You write the model (priors and likelihood) and get posterior draws. The standard workflow is: run four chains, 1,000 warm-up and 1,000 sampling iterations each, check \( \hat R<1.01 \), bulk and tail ESS above a few hundred, no divergent transitions, then look at posterior predictive checks. If a check fails, reparameterize or simplify the model before running longer.
Write your own Metropolis–Hastings only for learning or for a small discrete problem. If you do, work with log-probabilities (compare \( \log u<\log\pi(y)-\log\pi(x) \)), because probabilities of real models underflow to 0. Tune the step size to reach a 20% to 40% acceptance rate, and always discard warm-up.
In practice at a company like an ad-tech business, MCMC appears in Bayesian A/B analysis (hierarchical models that share strength across campaigns or segments), in conversion-rate models with sparse data per segment, and in uncertainty estimates for forecasts. For fast, large-scale systems it is usually replaced by closed-form conjugate updates (beta-binomial for rates) or variational methods, and MCMC is kept for validation. To study it, implement the 4-state example above, then random-walk Metropolis on a 2-D Gaussian with correlation 0.95, and watch how slowly it moves; then read Betancourt's conceptual introduction to HMC.
tradeoffs: Choose MCMC when you need the full posterior of a complex model and accuracy matters more than speed: samples converge to the exact target as the run grows. Choose variational inference or a Laplace approximation when you need an answer in seconds on large data and can accept approximation bias and underestimated uncertainty. Choose a conjugate closed form whenever one exists (beta-binomial, gamma-Poisson, normal-normal): it is exact and instant.
Choose HMC/NUTS for continuous parameters in tens to thousands of dimensions with a differentiable density; you pay for gradients and need smooth geometry. Choose Gibbs sampling when conditional distributions are easy and the variables are not strongly correlated, as in many graphical models; you pay with slow mixing when they are. Choose random-walk Metropolis for small problems, discrete or non-differentiable targets, where simplicity wins.
Choose several short chains over one long chain: they give diagnostics (\( \hat R \)) and use parallel hardware, at the cost of repeating warm-up. Choose a long single chain only when warm-up is very expensive and the target is known to be unimodal.
Choose simple Monte Carlo (independent samples by direct simulation) whenever the target can be sampled exactly; MCMC is the fallback for when it cannot, and its correlation costs you a factor of the autocorrelation time in efficiency.
quiz: Why does the Metropolis–Hastings rule not need the normalizing constant of the target? || The acceptance probability uses only the ratio \( \pi(y)/\pi(x) \) (with the proposal ratio), so any constant factor in \( \pi \) cancels. A posterior known only as likelihood times prior is enough.

value: Many real decisions are whole-number or yes/no choices: open this warehouse or not, assign this campaign to this slot or not, run this job on this machine or not, hire 3 people, not 2.7. Integer programming (IP, also called integer linear programming, ILP) is a language for such decisions. You declare variables that must be integers, write the objective and the constraints as linear expressions, and ask for the best feasible choice. Mixed-integer programming (MIP) allows some variables to stay continuous.
The catch is that integer programs are hard in general: they are NP-hard, so no known algorithm is fast on every instance. Linear programs (LPs), the same problem without the integer requirement, are easy and solved in practice in seconds even with millions of variables. Branch-and-bound uses the easy problem to attack the hard one. It solves the LP relaxation (ignore integrality), which gives an upper bound on what any integer solution can achieve. If the LP answer is fractional, it splits the problem in two by forcing that variable to the integer below or above, and repeats. Any branch whose LP bound is not better than the best integer solution found so far is discarded without looking inside it.
For the layers above, this is the exact tool behind crew and shift scheduling, ad allocation and pacing plans, cluster and container placement, network design, and the benchmark against which approximation algorithms are measured: an approximation guarantee says "within a factor of the integer optimum". The layers below carry the method. Linear programming and duality give the relaxation, its bound and the fast re-solves. Combinatorics describes the enormous space of discrete choices (2 to the 100th for 100 yes/no variables) that the search must avoid enumerating. Convexity explains why the relaxation is easy: its feasible region is a convex polytope, and integrality is exactly what removes convexity.
terms: Decision variable — an unknown quantity the solver chooses; integer, binary (0 or 1) or continuous.
Objective — the linear function to maximize or minimize, such as profit or cost.
Constraint — a linear inequality or equation that every solution must satisfy.
LP relaxation — the same problem with the integer requirement dropped; its optimal value bounds the integer optimum.
Incumbent — the best integer-feasible solution found so far; it gives a bound from the other side.
Branching — splitting a problem into two subproblems by adding \( x\le\lfloor v\rfloor \) in one and \( x\ge\lceil v\rceil \) in the other.
Pruning — discarding a subproblem because it is infeasible, because its LP solution is already integer, or because its bound cannot beat the incumbent.
Cutting plane — an extra linear inequality that removes the fractional LP solution but keeps every integer solution.
Optimality gap — the relative distance between the best bound and the incumbent; the solver can stop at, for example, 1%.
Totally unimodular matrix — a constraint matrix for which every LP vertex is integral, so no branching is needed (assignment and network-flow problems).
history: The first ideas came from the 1950s, soon after Dantzig's simplex method (1947) made LP practical. Dantzig, Fulkerson and Johnson (1954) solved a 49-city travelling-salesman problem by solving an LP, adding violated constraints by hand and repeating; this was the first use of cutting planes at scale. Ralph Gomory (1958) then gave a systematic way to generate cuts from the LP tableau, and proved that the process finishes.
Ailsa Land and Alison Doig (1960), at the London School of Economics, published the branch-and-bound method: split on a fractional variable and bound with the LP. Dakin (1965) simplified it into the form used today. Hoffman and Kruskal (1956) identified totally unimodular matrices, which explained why assignment and network flow problems have integer LP solutions. Richard Karp (1972) listed 0–1 integer programming among the 21 NP-complete problems, which showed that no general fast method should be expected. Hendrik Lenstra (1983) proved the opposite for a fixed number of variables, where integer programs can be solved in polynomial time.
In practice, progress came from engineering. In the 1990s, solvers combined branch-and-bound with cuts (branch-and-cut), preprocessing, and heuristics, and CPLEX (from 1988) and later Gurobi (2008) turned MIP into a commodity. The open-source side has SCIP, CBC, and HiGHS, which SciPy has used for its LP and MIP routines for several years. Google's OR-Tools adds the CP-SAT solver, which comes from constraint programming. Modern solvers handle many problems with millions of variables, though a problem of similar size can still defeat them.
how: Write the problem as: maximize \( \mathbf{c}^\top\mathbf{x} \) subject to \( A\mathbf{x}\le\mathbf{b} \), \( \mathbf{x}\ge0 \), with some or all entries of \( \mathbf{x} \) required to be integers. Drop the integer requirement to get the LP relaxation. Its feasible region is a convex polytope, which is where convexity helps: the optimum of a linear function over a convex polytope is at a vertex, and the simplex or interior point method finds it quickly. Because the relaxation allows more points than the integer problem, its optimum \( z_{LP} \) is an upper bound on the true integer optimum \( z^* \) (for maximization): \( z^*\le z_{LP} \). If the LP solution happens to be integral, it is optimal for the integer problem and you are done.
Otherwise some variable \( x_j \) has a fractional value \( v \), such as 1.5. No integer solution has \( x_j \) strictly between 1 and 2, so every integer solution lies in one of two parts: \( x_j\le\lfloor v\rfloor \) or \( x_j\ge\lceil v\rceil \). Create two child subproblems by adding each of these constraints, and solve their LP relaxations. Each child's LP can be warm-started from the parent's solution by the dual simplex method, which usually needs only a few pivots, because duality makes a violated new constraint easy to repair. This produces a binary tree of subproblems.
The tree is made small by pruning. Keep the incumbent \( \underline z \), the best integer solution found so far. A node is pruned in three cases: its LP is infeasible; its LP solution is integral (record it as a candidate incumbent and stop); or its LP value is no better than \( \underline z \), since every integer point in this subtree has value at most the LP value. When the objective coefficients are integers, the LP bound can be rounded down before the comparison, which prunes more. The search ends when no open node remains, and the incumbent is optimal. At any time the best bound over open nodes and the incumbent bracket the true optimum, giving the gap
\[ \text{gap}=\frac{\bar z-\underline z}{|\underline z|} \]
where \( \bar z \) is the largest LP bound among open nodes. You can stop early when the gap is small enough.
The worst case is still exponential: with 100 binary variables there are \( 2^{100}\approx1.3\times10^{30} \) assignments, and a bad instance can force the tree to explore a huge part of them. What makes it work in practice is a set of techniques that make bounds tight and trees small. Presolve removes redundant variables and tightens constraints before solving. Cutting planes are added at the root and sometimes deeper (branch-and-cut): Gomory cuts, cover cuts for knapsack rows, and flow cuts cut off the fractional vertex and make the relaxation closer to the integer hull. Branching rules choose which fractional variable to split; strong branching tests candidates by solving LPs and picks the one that raises the bound most, and pseudocosts learn it cheaply from history. Node selection chooses between depth-first (quick incumbents, little memory) and best-bound (smallest tree). Heuristics such as rounding, diving and the feasibility pump find incumbents early; early incumbents prune more.
Formulation matters as much as the algorithm. Two formulations of the same problem can have very different LP relaxations; the tighter one (closer to the convex hull of integer points) gives much smaller trees. "Big-M" constraints (\( x\le My \), where \( y \) is a binary switch) are common but give weak relaxations when \( M \) is large, so choose \( M \) as small as is valid. Matrices that are totally unimodular (bipartite assignment, shortest paths, max flow) need no branching: the LP vertex is already integral.

example: Solve the problem in the diagram by hand. Maximize \( 5x+4y \) subject to \( 6x+4y\le24 \), \( x+2y\le6 \), \( x,y\ge0 \) integer.
Node 0, the root (LP relaxation). The two constraints meet where \( 6x+4y=24 \) and \( x+2y=6 \). From the second, \( x=6-2y \); substituting, \( 36-12y+4y=24 \), so \( y=1.5 \) and \( x=3 \). The objective is \( 15+6=21 \). Since \( y \) is fractional, we branch. The upper bound on the answer is 21.
Branch \( y\le1 \) (node 1). With \( y\le1 \), the best LP point has \( 6x+4\cdot1\le24 \), so \( x\le3.33 \), and \( x+2\le6 \) allows \( x\le4 \). Take \( x=3.33,\ y=1 \): objective \( 16.67+4=20.67 \). Fractional in \( x \). Branch \( y\ge2 \) (node 2). Now \( x+2y\le6 \) gives \( x\le2 \) at \( y=2 \), and \( 6x+8\le24 \) gives \( x\le2.67 \). The LP optimum is \( x=2,\ y=2 \), objective \( 10+8=18 \), integral. This is our first incumbent: 18. Node 2 is closed.
Node 1 has a bound of 20.67, better than 18, so we must continue. Branch on \( x \). Node 3 adds \( x\le3 \): the LP optimum is \( x=3,\ y=1 \), because \( 6\cdot3+4=22\le24 \) and \( 3+2=5\le6 \); the objective is \( 15+4=19 \), integral, a new incumbent: 19. Node 4 adds \( x\ge4 \) (with \( y\le1 \)): from \( 6x+4y\le24 \), \( x=4,\ y=0 \) is feasible with \( 24\le24 \) and the objective is 20, integral: the new incumbent is 20. All open nodes are now closed. The optimum is \( x=4,\ y=0 \) with value 20.
The search used 5 LP solves. Enumeration would also be easy for two variables, but not for 100 binary variables, where pruning is what saves the work. Notice two lessons. First, rounding the root LP fails: rounding \( (3,1.5) \) down gives \( (3,1) \) with 19, not optimal, and rounding up gives \( (3,2) \), which violates \( 6\cdot3+8=26>24 \). Second, order matters: if we had found the incumbent 20 first, node 1's bound 20.67 could be rounded down to 20 (the objective only takes integer values), which is not better than 20, and the whole subtree would be pruned. This is why heuristics that find good incumbents early speed up the search.
A scale example: choosing which of 1,000 ad campaigns to run in a limited inventory, as a binary knapsack with one budget row, has \( 2^{1000} \) subsets but is typically solved to proven optimality in a fraction of a second, because the LP bound is very tight (at most one variable is fractional). A bin-packing placement of 10,000 containers on 500 machines is far harder, because its LP bound is weak and the problem is highly symmetric.
intuition: To find the best whole-number plan, first find the best plan allowing fractions (an easy question that gives a ceiling), and then keep cutting the plan space only where the fractions lie, discarding any region whose ceiling is already below a plan you hold.
The method is natural because it uses a problem we can solve to guide a problem we cannot. The LP answer says how good things could possibly be. Branching adds only what is needed to remove the fractional answer, and pruning uses the ceiling to avoid whole families of solutions in a single comparison. You never prove that a solution is best by trying all the others; you prove it by showing that all the others, grouped in regions, have a ceiling that is not higher.
mistakes: People often think the integer solution is the LP solution rounded. In fact rounding can be infeasible or far from optimal. In the example, rounding \( (3,1.5) \) either way gives a worse or infeasible point, while the true optimum \( (4,0) \) is not even near the LP point.
People often think integer programming is solved by trying all combinations, so it is hopeless beyond tiny sizes. In fact branch-and-cut routinely solves problems with thousands to millions of variables, because bounds prune almost all of the space. It is hopeless only for some instances, which cannot be predicted from size alone.
People often think a bigger formulation is slower. In fact adding constraints (valid cuts, tighter linking constraints) usually makes the solver faster, because the relaxation gets closer to the integer hull. A compact but weak "big-M" model can be orders of magnitude slower than a longer, stronger one.
People often think the solver's answer after a time limit is optimal. In fact it returns the best solution found plus a bound. The two are only equal when the gap is zero. A reported 3% gap means the solution is at most 3% from optimal, which is often enough, but it is not a proof of optimality.
friction: The theoretical problem is NP-hard, and some small-looking instances (certain scheduling and packing problems) resist all solvers. Performance is erratic: renaming variables or changing a random seed can change the solving time by a factor of 10 or more ("performance variability"), which makes benchmarks and production SLAs hard. Memory is also an issue: a best-bound search can hold millions of open nodes.
Modelling is the second pain. You need to express logic (if-then, either-or, at most k) with linear inequalities and binary switches, often with big-M constants. Weak formulations and symmetry (many equivalent solutions, such as identical machines) destroy pruning; symmetry-breaking constraints help but require insight. Numerical issues, such as coefficients from \( 10^{-6} \) to \( 10^{9} \) in one model, make the LP solves unstable and the tolerance on "integer" (usually \( 10^{-6} \)) can return values like 0.9999995 that need rounding.
Third, the solvers are a mix of art and tuning. The best commercial solvers (Gurobi, CPLEX) are often several times faster than open-source ones on hard models and are licensed per machine or per core; open-source solvers (HiGHS, SCIP, CBC) are solid but weaker on very hard instances. Real-time use is difficult: the solve time cannot be bounded, so production systems set time limits and fall back to heuristics or the best incumbent. Uncertainty in the data (forecast errors in ad inventory) makes the "optimal" plan optimal only for the forecast, and robust or stochastic formulations are bigger.
practice: Model first, solve second. Define binary variables for decisions, write constraints as linear inequalities, and test the model on a tiny instance whose answer you know. Learn the standard patterns: assignment (\( \sum_j x_{ij}=1 \)), capacity (\( \sum_i s_ix_{ij}\le C_j \)), at-most-k, implication (\( x\le y \)), and fixed-charge (\( x\le My \)). Keep \( M \) as small as the data justifies, and look for totally unimodular structure that makes the LP integral for free.
In Python, use `scipy.optimize.milp` for small to medium models (it calls HiGHS), `OR-Tools` (the MPSolver interface or CP-SAT) for scheduling and assignment, `PuLP` or `Pyomo` as modelling layers, and `gurobipy` when you have a licence. Set a time limit and a gap tolerance (for example 0.5% to 1%). Read the log: if the best bound barely moves, strengthen the formulation; if the incumbent is poor, supply a good starting solution from a heuristic (a warm start) or add a primal heuristic.
For production use: for ad allocation or pacing, solve the LP relaxation and round with a repair step if integrality is cosmetic (impressions are large integers, so rounding error is tiny); use true integer models only when the discreteness is real (which campaigns to enable, which servers to run). Re-solve on a schedule with warm starts, and keep a greedy fallback. To study the subject, build a knapsack and an assignment model, look at the branch-and-bound log, then read Wolsey's "Integer Programming" and the HiGHS or SCIP documentation.
tradeoffs: Choose the LP relaxation (with rounding) when variables are large counts, such as impressions or dollars, and rounding error is negligible; you get solves that are orders of magnitude faster and a provable bound. Choose a true integer model when variables are small integers or yes/no decisions and rounding would violate constraints.
Choose an exact MIP solver when the instance has up to thousands of integer variables, a decision with real money riding on a few percent, and you can wait seconds to hours; you get a proof of optimality or a certified gap. Choose a heuristic (greedy, local search, genetic algorithm) or an approximation algorithm when the model is too large, the answer is needed in milliseconds, or a 5% to 10% loss is acceptable; you give up the bound and any guarantee.
Choose CP-SAT or constraint programming when the problem is full of logic and scheduling structure (no-overlap, calendars, precedence) and few arithmetic bounds; choose MIP when the LP relaxation is informative (costs, capacities, flows). Choose a commercial solver for the hardest instances and a deadline; choose HiGHS or SCIP when licence cost or deployment matters and the problems are moderate.
Choose a compact model when it is already tight; choose an extended formulation with more variables and constraints when the compact one has a weak relaxation, accepting a larger LP in exchange for a smaller tree.
quiz: In branch-and-bound, why is the LP relaxation value an upper bound for a maximization problem? || The relaxation allows every integer point plus fractional points, so its feasible set is larger. The best value over a larger set is at least as large as the best over the integer subset.
When can a node be pruned? || When its LP is infeasible, when its LP solution is already integral (record it and stop), or when its LP bound is no better than the incumbent, since no integer point inside can beat the incumbent.
Why not round the LP solution? || The rounded point may violate constraints, and even when feasible it can be far from optimal. In the example the root LP gives (3, 1.5) with 21, rounding gives at best (3, 1) with 19, and the true optimum is (4, 0) with 20.
Root LP gives 41.8 with integer objective coefficients, and the incumbent is 41. Can you prune? || Yes. Every integer solution has an integer objective, so the best possible in this subtree is floor(41.8) = 41, which is not better than the incumbent 41.
You have a model with a 30% gap after an hour. What do you try before buying a bigger machine? || Strengthen the formulation (tighter big-M values, valid inequalities, symmetry breaking), supply a warm start from a heuristic, and check numerics and branching priorities. A tighter relaxation reduces the tree far more than more hardware.

@@ semantics
value: A program is text. What does that text mean? "It does what the compiler does" is circular, and "it does what the manual says" is vague. Formal semantics answers by giving a mathematical definition of the meaning of every construct of a language, precise enough that two people (or two compilers) cannot disagree, and precise enough that a theorem about all programs can be proved. There are three classic styles. Operational semantics defines meaning as rules that move an abstract machine from one state to the next. Denotational semantics defines meaning as a mathematical function from inputs to outputs. Axiomatic semantics defines meaning as the logical facts that a program guarantees, for example "if \( x\ge0 \) before, then \( y=x! \) after".
The reason this matters in engineering is correctness at scale. A compiler is correct if the compiled code has the same meaning as the source, and you cannot even state that without a definition of meaning. A type system is safe if well-typed programs never get stuck, which is a theorem about an operational semantics. A concurrent memory model, which says what values a read may see when threads race, is a formal semantics of memory operations. WebAssembly was designed with a complete formal specification, and its soundness has been machine-checked. CompCert is a C compiler whose compilation to assembly is proved to preserve semantics. The K framework lets language designers write a semantics once and get an interpreter and verification tools from it.
For the layers above, Hoare logic is an axiomatic semantics, type systems are proved safe against an operational one, WebAssembly and memory models are given by formal rules, and verified compilers rely on all of it. The layers below provide the material: lambda calculus is the first small language with an operational meaning (substitution and reduction), propositional and first-order logic is the language in which axiomatic claims and inference rules are written, set theory gives the objects that meanings are (functions, sets, relations), and order theory gives the partially ordered sets and fixed points needed to give loops and recursion a denotation.
terms: Syntax vs semantics — syntax says which strings are programs; semantics says what each program means.
State (store) — a mapping from variable names to values, written \( \sigma \), that represents the memory at one moment.
Configuration — a pair \( \langle c,\sigma\rangle \) of the program fragment still to run and the current state.
Big-step (natural) semantics — rules that relate a program and a state directly to the final result: \( \langle c,\sigma\rangle\Downarrow\sigma' \).
Small-step (structural) semantics — rules that describe a single computation step: \( \langle c,\sigma\rangle\to\langle c',\sigma'\rangle \).
Denotation — the mathematical object a program stands for, written \( \llbracket c\rrbracket \), usually a function from states to states.
Inference rule — premises above a line and a conclusion below; if all premises are derivable, so is the conclusion.
Fixed point — a value \( x \) with \( F(x)=x \); a loop's meaning is the least fixed point of the function that describes one unrolling.
Hoare triple — \( \{P\}\,c\,\{Q\} \): if \( P \) holds before \( c \) runs and \( c \) terminates, then \( Q \) holds after.
Soundness and adequacy — soundness says every provable statement is true in the model; adequacy says two semantic styles agree.
history: The need came with the first big languages. John McCarthy (1960) defined Lisp by an interpreter written in Lisp, an early operational definition. Peter Landin (1964) defined the meaning of expressions through the SECD abstract machine. At the same time the Algol 60 report showed how to specify syntax formally (BNF), but its meaning was still described in English, with ambiguities that took years to find. Robert Floyd (1967) in "Assigning Meanings to Programs" attached logical assertions to flowcharts, and Tony Hoare (1969) turned this into the axiomatic system of triples and rules.
The denotational approach came from Christopher Strachey, who wanted to treat programs as mathematical functions, and Dana Scott, who found the right mathematics. Scott's work (1969 to 1971) on domains, partial orders where limits of increasing chains exist, gave a model for the untyped lambda calculus and made recursive definitions meaningful: the "Scott–Strachey" approach. Gordon Plotkin (1981), in "A Structural Approach to Operational Semantics", showed that small-step rules over the syntax are simple, compositional and flexible; this is the style most used today. Gilles Kahn (1987) introduced natural (big-step) semantics. Felleisen and Hieb (1992) added evaluation contexts, and Wright and Felleisen (1994) gave the "progress and preservation" method for type safety.
Large definitions followed: "The Definition of Standard ML" (Milner, Tofte, Harper, MacQueen; 1990 and 1997) gave a full formal semantics of a real language. Xavier Leroy's CompCert (2006 onwards) proved a C compiler correct in Coq. Haas and colleagues (2017) published the WebAssembly design, with a formal semantics and a proved type system, and Watt (2018) mechanized it. Roşu and Şerbănuţă's K framework (2010) turned semantics into executable definitions, now used for the Ethereum virtual machine and C.
how: Fix a tiny imperative language, often called IMP. Expressions are numbers, variables, sums and comparisons. Commands are \( x:=e \), \( c_1;c_2 \), \( \text{if } b \text{ then } c_1 \text{ else } c_2 \), \( \text{while } b \text{ do } c \), and \( \text{skip} \). The state \( \sigma \) is a function from variable names to integers, a set-theoretic object. The notation \( \sigma[x\mapsto n] \) is the state like \( \sigma \) but with \( x \) set to \( n \). Everything that follows is a way of saying what each command does to \( \sigma \).
Operational semantics, big-step. Write rules as premises over a line. Evaluating an expression: \( \langle x,\sigma\rangle\Downarrow\sigma(x) \), and a sum evaluates both sides and adds. Assignment evaluates the right side and updates the state:
\[ \frac{\langle e,\sigma\rangle\Downarrow n}{\langle x:=e,\sigma\rangle\Downarrow\sigma[x\mapsto n]} \qquad \frac{\langle c_1,\sigma\rangle\Downarrow\sigma'\quad\langle c_2,\sigma'\rangle\Downarrow\sigma''}{\langle c_1;c_2,\sigma\rangle\Downarrow\sigma''} \]
and the loop has two rules, one for a false test (stop) and one for a true test (run the body, then the loop again):
\[ \frac{\langle b,\sigma\rangle\Downarrow\text{true}\quad\langle c,\sigma\rangle\Downarrow\sigma'\quad\langle\text{while }b\text{ do }c,\sigma'\rangle\Downarrow\sigma''}{\langle\text{while }b\text{ do }c,\sigma\rangle\Downarrow\sigma''} \]
A program "means" the set of derivations that these rules allow. A proof tree is a finished computation. Programs that loop forever have no derivation, which is why big-step semantics cannot distinguish a stuck program from a non-terminating one: neither has a derivation. Rules are inductive definitions, and proofs about them use induction on the derivation. This is where logic (judgments, derivations, induction) is the foundation.
Operational semantics, small-step. Here one rule is one step, such as \( \langle x:=n,\sigma\rangle\to\langle\text{skip},\sigma[x\mapsto n]\rangle \) and \( \langle\text{skip};c,\sigma\rangle\to\langle c,\sigma\rangle \), with rules that let a step happen inside the left part of a sequence. A run is a chain of steps. Small-step is better for what big-step cannot say: infinite runs, stuck states, concurrency (interleave steps from several threads), and errors. Type safety is proved here in two lemmas. Progress: a well-typed, non-final configuration can take a step. Preservation: a step keeps the type. Together, well-typed programs never get stuck.
Denotational semantics. Instead of rules, define a function \( \llbracket c\rrbracket \) from states to states for each command, compositionally, meaning that the meaning of a compound command is a function of the meanings of its parts. Assignment and sequence are easy: \( \llbracket x:=e\rrbracket\sigma=\sigma[x\mapsto\llbracket e\rrbracket\sigma] \), and \( \llbracket c_1;c_2\rrbracket=\llbracket c_2\rrbracket\circ\llbracket c_1\rrbracket \). Here set theory is used: commands denote partial functions, sets of input–output pairs, since a loop may not terminate. The difficulty is the loop. The meaning of a loop must satisfy the equation "run the body then loop again, or stop", which is a recursive definition: \( W=F(W) \), where
\[ F(f)(\sigma)=\begin{cases}f(\llbracket c\rrbracket\sigma)&\text{if }\llbracket b\rrbracket\sigma\text{ is true}\\ \sigma&\text{otherwise}\end{cases} \]
Which solution of \( W=F(W) \) do we take? Order theory answers. Order partial functions by "is extended by": \( f\sqsubseteq g \) if \( g \) is defined wherever \( f \) is and agrees with it. The nowhere-defined function \( \bot \) is the bottom. The operator \( F \) is monotone and continuous, so by the Kleene fixed-point theorem the least fixed point is the limit of the chain \( \bot\sqsubseteq F(\bot)\sqsubseteq F(F(\bot))\sqsubseteq\dots \):
\[ \llbracket\text{while }b\text{ do }c\rrbracket=\bigsqcup_{k\ge0}F^k(\bot) \]
\( F^k(\bot) \) is the loop that is allowed at most \( k \) iterations and is undefined if it would need more. The union of these approximations is the loop's meaning, and where all are undefined, the loop does not terminate.
Axiomatic semantics defines meaning by what can be proved. The assignment axiom is \( \{Q[e/x]\}\;x:=e\;\{Q\} \): to be sure \( Q \) holds for \( x \) afterwards, \( Q \) with \( e \) substituted for \( x \) must hold before. For a loop, one finds an invariant \( I \) that is true before, preserved by the body when the test holds, and gives the post-condition together with the negated test. Soundness theorems then connect the three styles: whatever is provable in the logic is true of the operational runs, and the denotation agrees with the operational result on terminating programs (adequacy). A language definition usually picks one style as primary and proves the others consistent with it.

example: Take the program P: `x := 3; y := 0; while x > 0 do (y := y + x; x := x - 1)`. It sums 3+2+1.
Small-step run. Write the state as (x, y). Start with the empty state. Step 1: `x := 3` gives (3, ?). Step 2: `y := 0` gives (3, 0). Now the loop: the test \( 3>0 \) is true, so unfold: run the body, then the loop again. Body: `y := y + x` gives (3, 3), then `x := x - 1` gives (2, 3). Test \( 2>0 \) true: body gives y = 3 + 2 = 5, x = 1: state (1, 5). Test \( 1>0 \) true: y = 5 + 1 = 6, x = 0: state (0, 6). Test \( 0>0 \) is false: the loop ends. The final state is x = 0, y = 6. The big-step derivation for the loop has exactly three nested uses of the true-test rule and one use of the false-test rule.
Denotational view of a simpler loop, `while x > 0 do x := x - 1`. The loop function is \( F(f)(x)=f(x-1) \) if \( x>0 \) and \( x \) otherwise. Then \( F^0(\bot)=\bot \) is undefined everywhere. \( F^1(\bot) \) is defined only where the test is false at once, \( x\le0 \), and returns \( x \). \( F^2(\bot) \) is defined for \( x\le1 \): for \( x=1 \) it takes one iteration and returns 0. In general \( F^k(\bot) \) is defined for \( x\le k-1 \) and returns \( 0 \) if \( x>0 \). The least upper bound over all \( k \) is defined for every integer \( x \), mapping \( x\le0 \) to itself and every positive \( x \) to 0. For `while true do skip` every \( F^k(\bot)=\bot \), so the denotation is the nowhere-defined function: the loop never terminates.
Axiomatic proof for P with starting value \( n \) instead of 3: precondition \( x=n\ge0\wedge y=0 \), postcondition \( y=n(n+1)/2 \). Invariant: \( y+x(x+1)/2=n(n+1)/2\wedge x\ge0 \). Check before the loop: \( 0+n(n+1)/2 \) equals \( n(n+1)/2 \). Check preservation: the body gives \( y'=y+x \), \( x'=x-1 \), and \( y'+x'(x'+1)/2=y+x+(x-1)x/2=y+x(x+1)/2 \), unchanged. At exit \( x\le0 \) and \( x\ge0 \) so \( x=0 \), hence \( y=n(n+1)/2 \). For \( n=3 \): 6. The loop terminates because \( x \) is a non-negative integer that decreases by 1 in each iteration. All three descriptions give 6.
intuition: A language definition is a rulebook for a game: operational semantics lists the legal moves, denotational semantics describes the finished score, and axiomatic semantics lists what is guaranteed to be true when the game ends.
It is natural to want all three, because each answers a different question. To run a program or build a machine, you want the moves. To compare programs, or to say that two different programs are the same, you want a function, since equal functions are equal programs. To prove that a program is right, you want logical statements you can chain together. The mathematics of order and fixed points is there for one reason: a loop is defined in terms of itself, and a definition like that only makes sense if you choose, among all the possible solutions, the one built up from "nothing yet known" step by step.
mistakes: People often think a formal semantics is just a more detailed specification document. In fact it is a mathematical object that supports proofs. A document can be ambiguous, but a set of inference rules can be run, tested, and used to prove theorems such as type safety. The WebAssembly spec is read by people, and the same rules have been mechanized in a proof assistant.
People often think big-step and small-step semantics are interchangeable. In fact they agree on terminating programs but differ elsewhere. Big-step cannot tell a non-terminating program from a stuck one, and cannot describe interleaving of threads, while small-step can. Choose small-step when you want to reason about errors or concurrency.
People often think a program's denotation is just what it computes when it runs. In fact the denotation of a loop is defined without running anything: it is the least fixed point. This gives the meaning of non-terminating programs (the undefined function) and allows reasoning about whole families of programs at once, such as proving two loops equivalent.
People often think that a proved-correct compiler or a verified program has no bugs. In fact the guarantee is relative to a specification and to the semantics assumed. CompCert preserves the semantics of C as formalized; if the formalization does not match the hardware, the standard, or the programmer's expectation (for example undefined behaviour), the proof does not catch it. The parts outside the proof, such as the assembler and the linker, are unverified.
friction: Real languages are large and messy. A full formal semantics of C, JavaScript or Java with concurrency and floating point runs to hundreds of pages of rules, and it still has to decide corner cases that the standards leave undefined. Writing and maintaining one is expensive, which is why only a few languages have complete formal definitions (Standard ML, WebAssembly, parts of C through CompCert and Cerberus, JavaScript through projects such as KJS and JSCert), and why most specifications remain informal.
Different styles scale differently. Denotational models become mathematically heavy for features such as concurrency, higher-order state, exceptions and dynamic memory, which need advanced domain theory or game semantics. Operational semantics scales easily but is tied to one machine description, and proving that two programs are equivalent needs extra tools (bisimulation, logical relations). Axiomatic reasoning needs invariants chosen by a human, which is the main cost of program verification. Concurrency is the hardest area: weak memory models (for example for C11 or ARM) took many years and several revisions to formalize, and surprising bugs were found in the original standards.
Finally, proofs are costly. Mechanized proofs of compilers and kernels take person-years (the seL4 proof was reported at about 20 person-years, a large part of it reusable tooling, for a kernel of under 10,000 lines of C) and are fragile when the language changes. A formal semantics gives certainty about a model, and it leaves the question of whether the model is the right one.
practice: Most engineers will not write a full semantics, but they use the ideas daily. When designing a DSL, query language or configuration language, write its meaning as a few small-step rules or as an interpreter, because the act of writing rules exposes ambiguities (evaluation order, scoping, null handling). Make the interpreter small and obviously right, and use it as a reference to test the optimized implementation against; this is differential testing and it is the practical form of "semantics preservation".
Use the ideas to read documents. The WebAssembly core specification gives its rules in the inference-rule notation shown above, and reading it is good practice in the style. The C and C++ memory model documents, the Java memory model and the SQL standard's formal parts all fit. For verification tools, learn the Hoare-logic view: pre-conditions, post-conditions and loop invariants appear in Dafny, Why3, Frama-C and the contracts of many languages, and in the assertions you write when you reason about a tricky loop.
For compilers and optimizers, test equivalence by thinking in terms of semantics: a transformation is valid if every behaviour of the output is a behaviour of the input. To study the subject, read the first chapters of Winskel's "The Formal Semantics of Programming Languages" (IMP in operational, denotational and axiomatic form, exactly as here), then Pierce's "Types and Programming Languages" for operational semantics and type safety, and the Software Foundations series for mechanized versions in Coq.
tradeoffs: Choose small-step operational semantics when you need to reason about non-termination, errors, concurrency or type safety, and you want rules close to an implementation; you give up compositional reasoning, because the meaning of a program is the set of its runs and not a function. Choose big-step semantics for terminating, deterministic languages and for proofs about compilers where you only need the final result; it is shorter, but it cannot express infinite runs or interleaving without extra machinery.
Choose denotational semantics when you want to prove two programs are equal, to reason about program fragments in isolation, or to model features such as recursion with domains; you pay in mathematical overhead and in difficulty with concurrency and state. Choose axiomatic semantics (Hoare logic, separation logic) when you want to verify specific programs against specifications; you pay in invariants and proof effort, and gain checkable guarantees about a particular program.
Choose a mechanized definition (Coq, Isabelle, K) when the language is security- or safety-critical, as in smart contracts, kernels and compilers; you pay person-years and gain machine-checked proofs and an executable reference. Choose an informal but careful specification plus a reference interpreter and a test suite for most languages and DSLs, accepting that corner cases will stay ambiguous.
quiz: What are the three classic styles of formal semantics, and what does each take as the meaning of a program? || Operational: the sequence of state transitions of an abstract machine. Denotational: a mathematical function (for imperative code, from states to states). Axiomatic: the logical assertions (pre/post-conditions) that hold of it.
Why does the denotation of a while loop use a least fixed point? || The loop is defined in terms of itself, \( W=F(W) \), which may have many solutions. The least fixed point, the limit of \( \bot, F(\bot), F(F(\bot)),\dots \), is the one built only from what finitely many iterations determine, and it is undefined exactly where the loop does not terminate.
Why is small-step semantics preferred over big-step for proving type safety? || Type safety needs the statement that well-typed programs never get stuck. Small-step has configurations and single steps, so progress (a step exists) and preservation (the type is kept) can be stated. In big-step, a stuck program and a non-terminating program both just have no derivation.
For the program `x := 4; y := 0; while x > 0 do (y := y + x; x := x - 1)`, what are the final values and how many times does the loop body run? || The body runs 4 times, adding 4, 3, 2, 1, so y = 10 and x = 0. This matches \( n(n+1)/2 \) for \( n=4 \).
When would you choose to mechanize a language semantics in a proof assistant instead of writing it in a document? || When errors are very costly and the language is small and stable enough to afford the work: kernels, compilers, smart-contract VMs, WebAssembly. You get machine-checked theorems and an executable reference, at the cost of person-years of effort.

@@ transforms3d
value: To draw a 3-D scene, or to understand a camera image, you must move points around: rotate a model, slide it to its place in the world, scale it, look at it from a camera, and finally flatten it onto a 2-D screen. Rotation and scaling are matrix multiplications. Translation (sliding) is not: adding a vector is a different kind of operation. Perspective projection (distant things look smaller) involves a division. If each step used a different kind of operation, you could not combine them into one.
Homogeneous coordinates fix this with one trick: add a fourth number \( w \) to every 3-D point, so that \( (x,y,z) \) becomes \( (x,y,z,1) \). With the extra coordinate, translation becomes a 4×4 matrix multiplication, so does rotation, so does scaling, and so does perspective, where the division is postponed to a final step: divide by \( w \). Since all steps are matrices, any chain of them multiplies into a single 4×4 matrix. A mesh of a million vertices is then transformed with one matrix–vector product per vertex, and a camera and an object placed in a scene are described by the same kind of object.
Every graphics API (OpenGL, Vulkan, Direct3D, Metal), the model–view–projection pipeline in rasterization, the rays of a ray tracer, the camera poses of a NeRF or Gaussian splatting scene, and the camera models of computer vision and robotics, use these matrices. The layers below supply the content: linear algebra gives matrices, products, inverses and the idea that composing transforms is multiplying matrices, and geometry (metric spaces) explains what each transform preserves: rotations keep distances and angles, scaling keeps angles but not distances, and projection keeps neither, only straight lines.
terms: Homogeneous coordinates — a point \( (x,y,z) \) written as \( (x,y,z,w) \) with \( w\ne0 \); \( (x,y,z,w) \) and \( (kx,ky,kz,kw) \) are the same point.
Affine transformation — a linear map plus a translation; it keeps straight lines and parallelism (rotation, scaling, shear, translation).
Rigid transformation — rotation plus translation; it keeps all distances and angles.
Model, view and projection matrices — object-to-world placement, world-to-camera placement, and camera-to-clip-space projection; their product is the MVP matrix.
Clip space — the 4-D space after the projection matrix; a point is visible if \( -w\le x,y,z\le w \).
Perspective divide — dividing \( x,y,z \) by \( w \) to get normalized device coordinates in a cube (OpenGL uses \( [-1,1]^3 \)).
Point at infinity — a homogeneous vector with \( w=0 \); it represents a direction and is not changed by translation.
Camera intrinsics — focal lengths \( f_x,f_y \) and image centre \( c_x,c_y \) in pixels; they map camera coordinates to pixels.
Extrinsics (pose) — the rotation and translation of the camera in the world.
Orthogonal matrix — a matrix with \( R^\top R=I \); its columns are orthonormal, so it preserves lengths and angles.
history: The idea is almost 200 years old. August Ferdinand Möbius introduced homogeneous coordinates in his book "Der barycentrische Calcul" (1827), to describe points as weighted centres of mass, and Julius Plücker developed them in the 1830s for projective geometry. Projective geometry itself came from painters: Brunelleschi and Alberti described perspective in the early 1400s, and Desargues and Poncelet turned it into a mathematical subject in the 1600s and 1800s. Felix Klein's Erlangen programme (1872) organised geometries by the transformations that leave their properties unchanged: Euclidean, similarity, affine, projective.
The computing use started in the 1960s. Lawrence Roberts, in his MIT work on three-dimensional hidden-line removal (1963 to 1965), saw that a 4×4 matrix could represent every transformation of 3-D graphics, including perspective, and described it in "Homogeneous Matrix Representation and Manipulation of N-Dimensional Constructs" (1965). Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad (1963) and the first flight simulators built on the same ideas. Riesenfeld (1981) explained the projective view for the graphics community.
Hardware standardised it. Silicon Graphics' IRIS GL (1980s) and then OpenGL (1992) made the model-view-projection pipeline the standard, with matrices on a stack, and the first graphics cards implemented the matrix multiply in hardware. Programmable shaders (2001 onwards) moved it into user code, but the same 4×4 matrices stayed. In the 1990s computer vision (Hartley, Zisserman, Faugeras) reached the same mathematics from the other side, with the pinhole camera as a 3×4 projective matrix, and today structure-from-motion, SLAM and neural rendering use both views.
how: Start with the problem of translation. A 3×3 matrix \( M \) acts on a vector by \( \mathbf{p}\mapsto M\mathbf{p} \), which always maps the origin to the origin, so no 3×3 matrix can translate. Add a coordinate: represent the point as \( \mathbf{p}=(x,y,z,1)^\top \) and use a 4×4 matrix:
\[ T(t_x,t_y,t_z)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0&t_x\\0&1&0&t_y\\0&0&1&t_z\\0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad T\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x+t_x\\y+t_y\\z+t_z\\1\end{pmatrix} \]
Translation is a shear in four dimensions, and a linear map there. A general rigid or affine transform is the block matrix \( \begin{pmatrix}R&\mathbf{t}\\\mathbf{0}^\top&1\end{pmatrix} \), where \( R \) is a 3×3 rotation (or rotation times scaling) and \( \mathbf{t} \) is the translation. A direction (a vector like a surface normal or a light direction) is written with \( w=0 \), so it is rotated but not translated, which is the correct behaviour.
Rotation and metric structure. A rotation matrix has orthonormal columns, \( R^\top R=I \), and determinant \( +1 \). Because \( \|R\mathbf{p}\|^2=\mathbf{p}^\top R^\top R\mathbf{p}=\|\mathbf{p}\|^2 \), it preserves length, and likewise angles. This is the metric-space contribution: the group of rigid motions is exactly the group that preserves the Euclidean distance. Scaling preserves angles but not lengths, a shear preserves neither but still maps lines to lines and keeps parallels parallel. The inverse of a rigid transform is cheap: \( \begin{pmatrix}R&\mathbf{t}\\0&1\end{pmatrix}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}R^\top&-R^\top\mathbf{t}\\0&1\end{pmatrix} \). Normals need care: under a transformation \( M \), a surface normal transforms by \( (M^{-1})^\top \), which equals \( M \) only for rotations (with uniform scale).
Composition. Applying \( A \) first and \( B \) second is the matrix product \( BA \) (with column vectors; the rightmost matrix acts first). Matrix multiplication is associative, so a chain \( P\,V\,M\,\mathbf{p} \) can be grouped as \( (PVM)\mathbf{p} \): multiply the three 4×4 matrices once per object per frame on the CPU (3 matrix products of 64 multiplications each) and then apply the single matrix to every vertex (16 multiplications each). It is also not commutative: "rotate then translate" and "translate then rotate" give different results. This is the most common source of bugs.
The pipeline has named stages. The model matrix \( M \) places the object in the world. The view matrix \( V \) is the inverse of the camera's pose: it moves the world so the camera sits at the origin looking down the \( -z \) axis (OpenGL convention). The projection matrix \( P \) maps the visible region, the view frustum, to a cube. For a symmetric perspective frustum with vertical field of view \( \theta \), aspect ratio \( a \), near plane \( n \) and far plane \( f \):
\[ P=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{a\tan(\theta/2)}&0&0&0\\0&\frac{1}{\tan(\theta/2)}&0&0\\0&0&\frac{f+n}{n-f}&\frac{2fn}{n-f}\\0&0&-1&0\end{pmatrix} \]
The last row is the key: it copies \( -z \) into \( w \). The output \( (x_c,y_c,z_c,w_c) \) has \( w_c=-z \), the distance in front of the camera. The perspective divide, \( (x_c/w_c,\ y_c/w_c,\ z_c/w_c) \), then gives normalized device coordinates. Dividing by distance is exactly what makes far things small: by similar triangles, a point at height \( y \) and depth \( d \) lands at height \( y/d \) on an image plane at distance 1. A line in space maps to a line on screen, but equal distances in space do not map to equal distances on screen, which is why projection is not an affine map: parallel lines meet at a vanishing point, the image of a point at infinity (\( w=0 \)).
After the divide, a viewport transform (a scaling and translation) maps the cube to pixel coordinates, and the \( z \) value is stored in the depth buffer. Depth is non-linear in distance: with \( n=1 \) and \( f=100 \), a point at distance 10 already has depth 0.82 on a scale from \(-1\) to \(1\), so most depth precision is spent near the camera. This is why you should push the near plane as far out as the scene allows, and why modern engines use reversed-Z with floating-point depth buffers.
In vision, the same model goes the other way. A pinhole camera maps a camera-space point \( (X,Y,Z) \) to pixels by \( u=f_xX/Z+c_x \) and \( v=f_yY/Z+c_y \), which in homogeneous form is the 3×4 matrix \( K[R\mid\mathbf{t}] \) applied to \( (X,Y,Z,1) \). Calibration estimates \( K \) and the pose from known points by solving a least-squares problem; given one pixel you recover only a ray, since depth is lost in the division, and with two or more views you can triangulate depth.

example: First the order of transforms. Take the point \( \mathbf{p}=(1,0,0) \), written \( (1,0,0,1) \). Let \( R \) be a rotation by 90 degrees about the \( z \)-axis, which sends \( (x,y,z) \) to \( (-y,x,z) \), and let \( T \) translate by \( (5,0,0) \). Rotate first, then translate: \( R\mathbf{p}=(0,1,0) \), then add 5 to \( x \): \( (5,1,0) \). As one matrix, this is \( TR \) applied to \( \mathbf{p} \). Translate first, then rotate: \( T\mathbf{p}=(6,0,0) \), then \( R \) gives \( (0,6,0) \). The matrix \( RT \) gives a different answer, because rotation turns the displacement too. Pick the order that matches the story: "object turns in place, then is placed in the world" is \( TR \).
Now the projection numbers. Use a 90-degree vertical field of view (\( \tan 45^\circ=1 \)), aspect ratio 1, near plane \( n=1 \), far plane \( f=100 \). The matrix entries are \( P_{11}=P_{22}=1 \), \( P_{33}=(f+n)/(n-f)=101/(-99)=-1.0202 \), \( P_{34}=2fn/(n-f)=200/(-99)=-2.0202 \), and the last row \( (0,0,-1,0) \). Project the camera-space point \( (2,1,-4) \), which is 4 units in front of the camera. Clip coordinates: \( x_c=2 \), \( y_c=1 \), \( z_c=-1.0202\times(-4)-2.0202=4.0808-2.0202=2.0606 \), \( w_c=-(-4)=4 \). Divide by \( w_c \): \( (0.5,\ 0.25,\ 0.5152) \). All lie in \([-1,1]\), so the point is visible. On a 1920×1080 viewport with the origin at the top-left, \( u=(0.5+1)/2\times1920=1440 \) and \( v=(1-0.25)/2\times1080=405 \).
Check the depth mapping: a point at \( z=-1 \) (the near plane) gives \( z_c=1.0202-2.0202=-1 \) and \( w_c=1 \), so depth \(-1\). A point at \( z=-100 \) gives \( z_c=102.02-2.0202=100 \), \( w_c=100 \), depth \(+1\). A point at \( z=-10 \) gives \( z_c=10.202-2.0202=8.18 \), \( w_c=10 \), depth \( 0.818 \). So the first 10 units of the 99-unit range use 91% of the depth range \((0.818-(-1))/2\).
A vision example: a camera has \( f_x=f_y=800 \) pixels and centre \( (320,240) \) for a 640×480 image. A point at \( (X,Y,Z)=(0.5,0.25,5) \) metres in camera coordinates maps to \( u=800\times0.5/5+320=400 \) and \( v=800\times0.25/5+240=280 \). A point at \( (1.0,0.5,10) \), twice as far on the same ray, maps to the same pixel (400, 280). This is the lost depth: one pixel is a ray.
intuition: A perspective picture is what you get if you slide every point along its line to the eye and read it where it meets the picture plane, and homogeneous coordinates keep the position and the distance together so that "slide and divide" becomes one multiplication and one division.
The extra coordinate works because several different things are really one thing seen from different angles. A shift is a shear in a higher dimension; a point is a line through the origin of 4-D space (all scalings of \( (x,y,z,w) \) are the same point); and the division by \( w \) is just choosing the representative of that line with \( w=1 \). Seen this way, there is no special case: all the transforms are linear in the higher space, and the non-linearity of perspective is isolated into the one divide at the end.
mistakes: People often think a 3×3 matrix is enough for 3-D graphics. In fact a 3×3 linear map fixes the origin, so it cannot translate, and it cannot do perspective. The fourth coordinate is what lets all of these be matrix products.
People often think transform order does not matter, because matrix multiplication looks like multiplication. In fact \( AB\ne BA \) in general. With column vectors the rightmost matrix is applied first, but with row vectors (Direct3D's older convention) the order is reversed and matrices are transposed. Mixing conventions between a math library, a shader and a file format is a classic bug; always state which one you use.
People often think the \( w \) coordinate is only a technical detail of the GPU. In fact \( w \) carries the depth in front of the camera and decides the interpolation of texture coordinates (perspective-correct interpolation divides by \( w \)), clipping (a vertex is inside if \( -w\le x\le w \)), and the distinction between points (\( w=1 \)) and directions (\( w=0 \)).
People often think that applying the model matrix to a normal vector is correct. In fact normals must be transformed by the inverse transpose of the upper 3×3 block, otherwise non-uniform scaling tilts them and lighting is wrong. Also, floating-point depth is not linear: the same bits cover tiny differences near the camera and large ones far away, so a very small near plane causes z-fighting (flickering surfaces) at a distance.
friction: Rotation is the awkward part. A 3×3 rotation matrix has 9 numbers for 3 degrees of freedom, so after many multiplications rounding errors make it no longer orthogonal and it must be re-orthonormalized. Euler angles are compact but suffer from gimbal lock (two axes align and a degree of freedom is lost) and from awkward interpolation. Quaternions (4 numbers) avoid both and interpolate smoothly (slerp), but they are less intuitive and need converting to matrices for rendering.
Precision limits are real. A 32-bit float has 24 bits of mantissa, about 7 decimal digits, so a scene with large coordinates (a planet-sized world, geographic coordinates in metres) loses detail far from the origin: at 10 km from the origin, float resolution is around a millimetre, and at 1,000 km around 6 cm. Engines use camera-relative rendering, or double precision on the CPU and floats on the GPU. Depth-buffer precision is the other limit, because of the non-linear mapping noted above.
Conceptually, projection loses information: the divide discards depth, so a pixel does not determine a 3-D point, and a 3-D scene must be inferred from several views or from priors. Real cameras also depart from the pinhole model: lens distortion (radial and tangential) must be modelled separately, because it is non-linear and cannot be put into a matrix; fisheye and rolling-shutter cameras need other models. Finally, matrices do not model everything in a scene: skinning, non-rigid motion and non-linear deformations need per-vertex blends of several matrices or learned deformations.
practice: Fix your conventions before you write code: right- or left-handed coordinates, column or row vectors, which axis is up, what range clip-space depth has (OpenGL \([-1,1]\), Vulkan and Direct3D \([0,1]\), and Vulkan's \( y \) axis points down). Write them in a comment. Use a tested maths library (`glm` in C++, `numpy` for prototyping, `glMatrix` in JavaScript) for look-at, perspective and quaternion functions, and unit-test them with points whose answer you know, such as the near-plane and far-plane checks above.
Build the model matrix as translation times rotation times scale (scale first, then rotate, then translate) for the usual object placement. Compute the MVP once per object per frame and pass it to the vertex shader as a uniform, so the GPU performs one 4×4 multiply per vertex. Keep the near plane as far out as you can, consider reversed-Z with a float depth buffer for large scenes, and transform normals with the inverse transpose.
In vision and robotics, use the pinhole model with calibrated intrinsics (OpenCV's `calibrateCamera`), undistort images before using linear geometry, represent poses as 4×4 rigid transforms (or Lie group \( SE(3) \) elements) and compose them by multiplication. For debugging, project a few known 3-D points into the image and see whether they land where expected. To study the subject, work through the derivation of the perspective matrix by similar triangles, then read the first chapters of "Multiple View Geometry in Computer Vision" (Hartley and Zisserman) and the transformation chapters of "Real-Time Rendering".
tradeoffs: Choose 4×4 homogeneous matrices when you compose many transforms, send them to the GPU, or need perspective; the cost is 16 numbers instead of 6 for a rigid pose and a risk of drift away from orthogonality. Choose a quaternion plus a translation vector (7 numbers) when you store, interpolate or accumulate rotations over time, as in animation and SLAM; convert to a matrix only for rendering.
Choose Euler angles only for human-facing input and output, such as an editor's rotation fields; avoid them for computation because of gimbal lock. Choose axis–angle or Lie-algebra (\( \mathfrak{so}(3) \)) parameters for optimization, as in bundle adjustment, where a minimal 3-parameter rotation is useful.
Choose an orthographic projection (no divide) for CAD, 2-D games, UI and shadow maps from distant lights, where parallel lines must stay parallel and size must not depend on distance. Choose a perspective projection when you want realism or must model a real camera.
Choose standard depth with a close near plane when precision near the camera matters and the scene is small. Choose reversed-Z with a floating-point depth buffer, or a logarithmic depth, for large scenes: you pay in setup and some compatibility, and you gain far more uniform precision.
Choose single-precision matrices on the GPU for speed. Choose double precision on the CPU for large worlds and for scientific or geodetic data, and make the transform camera-relative before sending to the GPU.
quiz: Why does a 4×4 matrix let us represent translation, and what is the extra coordinate for points and for directions? || A translation is a shear in 4-D: with \( (x,y,z,1) \), the last column adds \( (t_x,t_y,t_z) \). Points have \( w=1 \) so they are translated; directions have \( w=0 \) so they are not.
What does the last row \( (0,0,-1,0) \) of the perspective matrix do? || It copies \( -z \) (the distance in front of the camera) into \( w \). The later division by \( w \) shrinks \( x \) and \( y \) in proportion to distance, which creates perspective.
Why must the order of a model transform be chosen carefully? || Matrix multiplication is not commutative. Rotating then translating (\( TR \)) places the rotated object at the new position, while translating then rotating (\( RT \)) rotates the displacement too and moves the object elsewhere. In the example, \( (1,0,0) \) goes to \( (5,1,0) \) versus \( (0,6,0) \).
Using a 90-degree field of view, aspect 1, near 1 and far 100, where does the camera-space point \( (2,1,-4) \) land in NDC? || \( w_c=4 \), \( x_c=2 \), \( y_c=1 \), \( z_c=2.0606 \), so NDC is \( (0.5,\,0.25,\,0.515) \).
When would you pick quaternions over 4×4 matrices or Euler angles? || When composing, interpolating or storing many rotations (animation, SLAM, physics). They are compact (4 numbers), avoid gimbal lock, interpolate smoothly, and are easy to renormalize; you convert to a matrix only when rendering.
