value: A language model trained only to predict the next token is a good imitator of the internet, but it is not an assistant. Ask it a question and it may continue with more questions, or with a rude forum reply, or with a confident wrong answer. What you want is a model that does what the user asked, is honest about what it does not know, and refuses clearly harmful requests. Nobody can write a formula for "a helpful answer". But people can look at two answers and say which one is better. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) turns that cheap, reliable kind of judgement into a training signal.
The recipe is: collect comparisons between pairs of model answers, train a reward model that predicts which answer a person would prefer, and then change the language model so that it produces answers the reward model scores highly, while not drifting too far from the model you started with. The 1.3-billion-parameter InstructGPT model, trained this way, was preferred by human raters over the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, which is 100 times larger. The size of that gap is the reason the method became standard: post-training with preferences gives more perceived quality per dollar than a bigger base model.
RLAIF and Constitutional AI replace part of the human labelling with an AI judge that follows written principles. DPO (direct preference optimization) removes the separate reward model and the reinforcement-learning loop, and trains on the preference pairs with a simple classification-style loss. The newer "RL with verifiable rewards" used by reasoning models replaces the learned reward with a checker (a unit test, a maths answer key).
Three lower-layer ideas make this work. Probability gives the Bradley–Terry model, which turns "A beat B" labels into a score for each answer. Optimization gives the objective "maximize reward minus a penalty for moving away from the starting model", and the tools to solve it. Policy gradients and PPO (L2) are the algorithm that lets you improve a text generator using a reward that is not differentiable.
terms: Policy — the language model viewed as a decision maker: given a prompt (the state) it chooses a whole answer, token by token (the actions). Written \( \pi_\theta \).
Reference model — a frozen copy of the model before RL starts (usually the supervised fine-tuned model). Written \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \). It is the anchor that stops the policy from drifting.
Reward model — a network, usually the language model with a scalar head, that gives a number r(x, y) to an answer y for a prompt x. It is trained to agree with human comparisons.
Bradley–Terry model — a probability model for pairwise choices: the chance that answer A beats answer B is sigmoid(r(A) − r(B)).
KL penalty — a term \( \beta \times \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}) \) that measures, in nats, how far the policy's output distribution has moved from the reference. It is subtracted from the reward.
PPO — proximal policy optimization, a policy-gradient algorithm that limits how much the policy can change in one update. It also needs a value network (a critic) to reduce variance.
DPO — direct preference optimization; a loss on preference pairs that gives the same optimum as the KL-regularized reward objective without training a reward model or running RL.
Reward hacking — the policy finds answers that score high on the reward model but are not actually better, such as long, flattering or confidently styled text.
RLVR — reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards; the reward comes from an automatic checker, not from a learned model.
history: The idea of learning a reward from human comparisons is older than language models. Christiano, Leike and colleagues (OpenAI and DeepMind, 2017) trained agents in simulated robot and Atari tasks from human comparisons of short video clips (a few hundred to a few thousand in the tasks they report), instead of a hand-written reward function. Ziegler et al. (2019) applied the same loop to language models for text continuation and summarisation. Stiennon et al. (OpenAI, 2020) showed that summaries written by a model tuned from human feedback were preferred to human-written reference summaries on Reddit posts, and gave the recipe in clean form.
The step that changed the industry was InstructGPT (Ouyang et al., OpenAI, published in 2022). Its three-stage pipeline is the template that others copied: supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations (about 13,000 prompts), a reward model trained on comparisons (about 33,000 prompts), and PPO against that reward model (about 31,000 prompts). The numbers are small compared with pre-training. A team of roughly forty contractors produced the data. ChatGPT, released in November 2022, used the same family of methods.
Anthropic published two parallel lines. Bai et al. (2022) trained helpful and harmless assistants with human feedback, and then Constitutional AI (Bai et al., December 2022) let an AI critique and revise its own answers using a short list of written principles, and used AI preference labels for the harmlessness part. Lee et al. (Google, 2023) compared RLAIF with RLHF directly and found similar quality on summarisation and helpfulness tasks.
PPO is heavy: you hold four large models in memory and tune many hyper-parameters. Rafailov et al. (Stanford, 2023) showed that the KL-regularized objective has a closed-form optimal policy, which lets you write the reward in terms of policy log-probabilities and train directly on pairs. This is DPO, and it made preference tuning possible for small teams. Next, DeepSeekMath (2024) introduced GRPO, which drops the critic by comparing each sampled answer with the other answers for the same prompt. Tülu 3 (Allen AI, 2024) named the idea of using automatic checkers "RLVR", and DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) showed that RL with such rewards can produce long reasoning on its own.
how: Stage 1 is supervised fine-tuning (SFT). You take the pre-trained model and train it on a few thousand to a few hundred thousand high-quality (prompt, answer) demonstrations written by people or selected from strong models. This gives a model that already follows the format of a conversation. This model becomes both the starting policy and the frozen reference \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \).
Stage 2 is the reward model. For each prompt, sample several answers from the SFT model and ask raters to rank them or pick the better one in each pair. Comparisons are used instead of absolute scores because raters disagree much more about "is this a 6 or a 7?" than about "is A better than B?". The reward model is the same architecture as the language model, with a single number as output. It is trained with the Bradley–Terry loss. For a pair where y_w was preferred over y_l, the loss is −log sigmoid(r(x, y_w) − r(x, y_l)). Probability theory supplies the reason for this form: if each answer has a hidden "quality" and the choice is noisy, the chance of choosing A over B is a logistic function of the difference in quality, and maximum likelihood on the observed choices gives exactly this loss. Only differences of scores matter, so the absolute scale is arbitrary.
Stage 3 is policy optimization. The objective is: maximize \( \mathbb{E}\big[ r(x, y) - \beta \times \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)) \big] \), where y is sampled from the current policy. The first term pushes towards what the reward model likes. The second term is a leash. Without it, the optimizer will find strange text where the reward model is wrong, because the reward model was only trained on answers that looked like the SFT model's outputs. In practice the penalty is applied per token, using \( \log \pi_\theta(\text{token}) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(\text{token}) \), so it can be added into the reward at each step.
The optimizer is PPO. For each batch of prompts, the policy generates answers. The reward model scores them, the KL penalty is subtracted, and a value network (the critic) estimates how good each partial answer is, so that the update can credit individual tokens. PPO clips the ratio between new and old token probabilities to a small range, for example 0.8 to 1.2, so a single update cannot change the policy too much. This is the policy-gradient machinery from the policygrad concept. A typical run therefore holds four models: the policy, the reference, the reward model and the critic. For a 7-billion-parameter model, the weights alone are four copies of about 14 GB in 16-bit format, before optimizer states for the policy and the critic.
DPO takes a different path. The optimal policy for the KL-regularized objective has the form \( \pi^*(y \mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp\!\big(r(x, y)/\beta\big) \). Solve this for the reward: \( r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} \) + a term that depends only on x. When you put this into the Bradley–Terry loss, the x-only term cancels in the difference, and you get a loss that uses only the policy and reference log-probabilities: \( -\log \sigma\!\Big( \beta \Big[ \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} \Big] \Big) \). The policy itself plays the role of the reward model (an "implicit reward"). Training is ordinary supervised learning on a fixed dataset: no sampling during training, no critic. The price is that it is offline. It learns from pairs that came from some other model, and cannot explore.
Verifiable rewards follow the same outer loop with a different reward. For maths, the reward is 1 if the final answer matches the key. For code, it is the fraction of unit tests that pass. GRPO samples a group of, for example, 8 to 64 answers per prompt and uses each answer's reward minus the group average (divided by the group standard deviation) as its advantage, so no critic is needed. The KL term is often kept small or removed in these settings, because a checker is much harder to fool than a learned reward model.
Across all variants, optimization theory supplies the same pattern: a regularized objective with a strength β. A small β allows large improvement in reward but risks exploiting the reward model. A large β keeps the model close to the reference and the gains small. Choosing β is the main practical decision.

example: Reward model step. A rater sees two answers to "Explain what a Bloom filter is". Answer W is clear and correct; answer L is vague. The reward model currently gives r(W) = 2.0 and r(L) = 0.5. The Bradley–Terry probability that W wins is sigmoid(1.5) = 0.818. The loss for this pair is −log(0.818) = 0.201. This is small, so the gradient is small. If the model had given r(W) = 0.5 and r(L) = 2.0, the probability would be sigmoid(−1.5) = 0.182 and the loss 1.70, a much larger push.
KL-penalized reward. The policy writes an answer of 200 tokens. The reward model gives it 3.0. The policy's log-probability of this answer is higher than the reference's by 20 nats in total (the answer is 20 nats "more likely" under the tuned policy than under the reference). With β = 0.05 the penalty is 0.05 × 20 = 1.0, so the training reward is 3.0 − 1.0 = 2.0. An answer with reward 3.4 but KL of 40 nats would get 3.4 − 2.0 = 1.4 and be worth less. The leash trades reward against drift.
DPO step. Take β = 0.1. For the chosen answer, the policy now assigns log-probability 2.0 higher than the reference does (the answer is e² ≈ 7.4 times more likely). For the rejected answer, it assigns 1.0 lower. The margin is 0.1 × (2.0 − (−1.0)) = 0.3. The probability that the implicit reward ranks the pair correctly is sigmoid(0.3) = 0.574 and the loss is 0.554. The gradient weight is sigmoid(−0.3) = 0.426. This is high, so the update still pushes hard. As the margin grows to 3, the weight falls below 0.05 and the pair stops contributing. Note that nothing stops both log-probabilities from falling together, as long as the rejected one falls more; this is a known DPO failure where the likelihood of good answers also decreases.
Cost sketch for PPO on a 7B model. Weights of policy, reference, reward model and critic: 4 × 14 GB = 56 GB. Adam states for the policy and the critic in 32-bit: each is roughly 8 bytes per parameter on top of weights, so 2 × 56 GB = 112 GB more. This is why PPO-based RLHF runs on many GPUs with sharding, while DPO needs only the policy plus a reference (whose log-probabilities can even be pre-computed once).
intuition: A coach who cannot play the game but can say "that second move was better" is enough to train a champion, as long as the player is not allowed to invent tricks that fool the coach.
The idea is natural because judging is easier than creating. A person cannot write the perfect reply to every prompt, but can tell which of two replies is better in seconds. Pairwise choices also remove the problem of calibration, since only differences matter. The language model already contains the abilities; pre-training put the knowledge there. The preference signal does not teach new facts, it picks which of the many behaviours the model could show it should show. So the update can be small, and the KL leash is a natural expression of that: stay close to what you know, move only as far as the evidence supports.
mistakes: People often think RLHF teaches the model new knowledge. In fact it mostly changes style, format and which existing behaviour is chosen. Knowledge comes from pre-training. If raters prefer confident answers, the model may learn to sound sure even when it is not, which is why preference tuning can increase hallucination if not handled.
People often think the reward model measures truth or quality. In fact it measures what raters preferred in its training data. Raters tend to prefer longer, more polite and agreeable answers, so reward models carry a length bias and a sycophancy bias. Sharma et al. (2023) showed that preference models and human raters sometimes prefer answers that match the user's stated opinion over correct answers.
People often think DPO is simply a cheaper version of RLHF that gives the same result. In fact DPO is offline: it learns only from the responses in the dataset, while PPO samples from the current policy and sees its own mistakes. Several comparisons found that well-tuned online methods do better on hard tasks such as reasoning and code, while DPO is competitive on style and chat quality.
People often think a lower KL penalty is always better because it allows more reward. In fact, past a point the true quality goes down while the proxy reward keeps rising. Gao et al. (OpenAI, 2022) measured this with a larger "gold" reward model as a stand-in for the truth: the proxy score rose steadily with KL distance, while the gold score rose and then fell.
friction: Reward hacking is the central problem. The reward model is an imperfect proxy, and optimization finds its flaws: longer answers, flattering openings, lists and bold text, or confident formatting. Gao et al. found that the gap between proxy and gold reward grows with the KL distance from the starting model and follows smooth trends in reward model size. Mitigations include the KL penalty, early stopping, ensembles of reward models, length-controlled rewards and periodically refreshing preference data with the current policy's outputs.
Human labels are slow, costly and inconsistent. Agreement between raters on subtle comparisons is often only 70% to 80%. Labels reflect the culture and instructions of the labelling team, and a single reward model averages over very different users. For tasks where a rater cannot judge the answer (long code, advanced maths, long documents), the label may be wrong in exactly the cases that matter. This is the motivation for AI feedback, debate and process-based supervision, and for verifiable rewards where they exist.
Sycophancy and over-refusal are two sides of the same issue. A model rewarded for pleasing the rater agrees with wrong claims. A model rewarded for avoiding harm refuses safe requests. Both come from a scalar reward that squeezes many goals (helpful, harmless, honest, concise) into one number.
Engineering cost is also real. PPO is unstable, sensitive to learning rate, KL coefficient and reward scale, and needs a pipeline that alternates generation (slow, memory-bound) and training. Generation dominates the time, so modern stacks split inference engines and trainers across GPUs. Finally, RLVR works only where answers can be checked, and a checker with a loophole (for example a test suite that the model can special-case) will be exploited just like a learned reward.
practice: Start with a strong SFT model. Preference tuning amplifies what is there; it cannot repair a weak base. Spend effort on prompt diversity and on the quality of the chosen answers before spending it on algorithm choice.
For most teams, begin with DPO or one of its variants (IPO, KTO, SimPO), using a library such as TRL or a framework in your training stack. Use pairs where the two answers are from the same model and differ in a meaningful way, not pairs of obviously good against obviously terrible. Use β around 0.1 as a first value, and watch both the preference accuracy and the log-probability of the chosen answers. If chosen log-probabilities fall sharply, lower the learning rate, add an SFT term on the chosen answer, or switch to an online method.
Go online (PPO, GRPO, or online DPO with fresh samples) when you need the model to improve on tasks where it can be checked, or when offline data no longer matches the current policy. For RLVR, build the checker first, test it with adversarial answers, and track the reward alongside held-out evaluations, since a rising training reward with a flat evaluation means hacking.
Always evaluate beyond the reward. Track average length, refusal rate on safe prompts, agreement with false user claims, and a few hundred human-read samples at each checkpoint. Keep the reference model's log-probabilities cached if memory is tight. Version your preference data, reward model and prompts together, because a change in any one changes the optimum. If you only use a hosted model, you meet RLHF as the reason the same prompt behaves differently across model versions: each release changes the preference data.
tradeoffs: Choose PPO-style online RL when you have a reliable reward (a verifier, or a reward model you trust on the policy's own outputs) and enough GPUs for four models plus generation. It can explore beyond the dataset and improve hard tasks. You give up simplicity and stability: expect days of tuning of learning rate, KL coefficient and reward normalisation.
Choose DPO or a similar offline method when you have a fixed set of preference pairs, a small team and a budget of a few GPUs. It trains like supervised fine-tuning, with roughly two forward passes through the policy and reference per pair. You give up exploration, and you inherit any mismatch between who wrote the dataset answers and your current model, which can make the gains plateau.
Choose GRPO over PPO when the reward is cheap and verifiable and you can afford 8 to 64 samples per prompt. It saves the memory of the critic, and group baselines work well for outcome rewards. You give up token-level credit assignment, and long answers with a single final reward carry noisy gradients.
Choose human labels when the target is taste, tone or safety policy that no checker captures; choose AI feedback when you need hundreds of thousands of labels and have a clear written rubric. AI labels cost a small fraction of human ones per item, but they copy the judge model's biases, and a model that grades its own family tends to favour its own style.
Choose a larger β (stay close to the reference) for regulated or safety-critical assistants where regressions are costly; choose a smaller β for narrow tasks with a trusted reward, where you want the maximum gain. Either way you give up something: closeness means smaller gains, freedom means more risk of hacking.
interview: Why does the RLHF objective include a KL penalty to a reference model? || The reward model is only reliable near the distribution of answers it was trained on. Without a penalty the policy moves to regions where the reward model is wrong and finds high scores for bad text (reward hacking), and it can also lose fluency or diversity. The KL term keeps the policy close to the reference and has a clean interpretation: the optimum is the reference reweighted by \( \exp(r/\beta) \).
Walk me through how DPO is derived from the RLHF objective, and what it gives up. || The KL-regularized objective has the closed-form optimum \( \pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}} \exp(r/\beta) \). Rearranging gives the reward as \( \beta \log(\pi^*/\pi_{\text{ref}}) \) plus a prompt-only term. Substituting into the Bradley–Terry loss cancels that term and leaves a loss on policy and reference log-ratios for the chosen and rejected answers. It gives up online exploration and a separate reward model, so it depends on the dataset's coverage and can reduce the likelihood of both answers.
Your reward keeps rising during PPO but human evaluators say quality is flat or falling. What do you check? || This is reward over-optimization. Check KL from the reference, the average answer length, and formatting patterns that the reward model likes. Compare the reward model on fresh human labels from current policy samples. Fixes: raise β or stop early, retrain the reward model on on-policy samples, use an ensemble or length normalisation, and add held-out evaluations that the reward model never sees.
Why do pairwise comparisons work better than asking raters for a score from 1 to 10? || People are inconsistent about absolute scales and differ in how harsh they are, but they are much more consistent in relative judgement. Pairwise data also fits the Bradley–Terry model directly, where only score differences matter. The cost is that you need several comparisons per prompt to rank many answers, and ties carry little information.
How is RL with verifiable rewards different from RLHF, and when does it fail? || The reward comes from a program (answer check, unit tests) and not from a model of human taste, so it is exact, cheap and hard to flatter. It works only for tasks with checkable outcomes. It fails when the checker has loopholes (special-casing tests, formatting tricks), when the reward is too sparse because the model never succeeds, or when it improves the checked skill but not related unchecked skills.
quiz: What are the three stages of the classic InstructGPT-style RLHF pipeline? || Supervised fine-tuning on demonstrations, training a reward model on human comparisons with the Bradley–Terry loss, and optimizing the policy with PPO against the reward model with a KL penalty to the SFT model.
Why does RLHF train on pairwise comparisons, and what loss is used for the reward model? || Raters are more consistent when choosing the better of two answers than when assigning absolute scores. The Bradley–Terry loss is −log sigmoid(r(winner) − r(loser)), which maximizes the likelihood that the winner is ranked above the loser.
How does DPO avoid training a separate reward model? || The optimal policy of the KL-regularized objective is \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \exp(r/\beta) \), so the reward can be written as \( \beta \log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}) \) plus a prompt-only term. Putting this in the Bradley–Terry loss gives a loss in terms of policy and reference log-probabilities only.
With β = 0.05, a sampled answer has reward 3.0 and a total log-probability 20 nats higher under the policy than under the reference. What is the penalized reward? || The KL penalty is 0.05 × 20 = 1.0, so the penalized reward is 3.0 − 1.0 = 2.0.
When would you pick DPO over PPO, and when would you not? || Pick DPO when you have a fixed preference dataset, limited GPUs and want stable training close to supervised learning. Prefer online RL (PPO or GRPO) when you have a trustworthy reward or verifier and want the model to improve beyond the data by exploring its own answers.

value: A modern language model has billions of weights. Fine-tuning it the usual way means computing a gradient and storing optimizer state for every one of them. With mixed-precision training and the Adam optimizer this costs roughly 16 bytes per parameter, so a 7-billion-parameter model needs about 110 GB of GPU memory for the training state alone, before activations. Each task then produces a full copy of the model, another 14 GB or more. A team that wants one adapted model per customer or per task cannot afford this.
LoRA (low-rank adaptation) freezes the pre-trained weights and learns only a small correction for each chosen weight matrix. The correction is the product of two thin matrices, so it has very few parameters: for a 7B model, adapting every linear layer with rank 16 trains about 40 million parameters, which is about 0.6% of the model. Optimizer state shrinks in the same proportion. The saved adapter is tens of megabytes, not gigabytes. One base model in memory can serve many adapters, chosen per request.
For the layer above, LoRA is the default way to customise open-weight LLMs: instruction tuning, domain adaptation, style, and also reinforcement learning. QLoRA goes further by holding the frozen base model in 4-bit precision, so a 65-billion-parameter model can be fine-tuned on a single 48 GB GPU.
The idea rests on linear algebra. A weight matrix update ΔW with d × k entries can be written as the product B × A of a d × r and an r × k matrix. If r is small, the product has at most rank r, which means the update lives in a small subspace of directions. The bet of LoRA is that fine-tuning updates are of low rank, so this subspace is enough.
terms: Full fine-tuning — updating every weight of the model on new data.
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) — a family of methods that train a small number of added or selected parameters while freezing the rest. LoRA, adapters, prefix tuning and prompt tuning belong here.
Low-rank matrix — a matrix whose rows are combinations of only r independent rows. A d × k matrix of rank r can be stored as two matrices with r × (d + k) numbers in total.
Rank r — the inner dimension of the adapter. Typical values are 8 to 64. It limits how complex the update can be.
Adapter — the small trained module (here the matrices A and B) that is added to a frozen layer and stored separately from the base model.
Scaling factor α — a constant that multiplies the update by \( \alpha/r \). It controls the strength of the adapter relative to the rank.
Merge — adding the update into the base weight, \( W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} \times B \times A \), so inference has no extra cost.
Quantization — storing numbers with fewer bits (for example 4 bits instead of 16) to save memory.
QLoRA — LoRA training on top of a base model quantized to 4 bits (NF4 format), with the adapters kept in 16-bit.
history: Before LoRA, the cheap way to adapt a large model was to add small modules. Houlsby et al. (Google, 2019) inserted "adapter" layers between the existing layers of BERT and trained only those. It worked, but the modules stay in the forward path, so every query pays extra latency of a few percent. Li and Liang (2021) proposed prefix tuning, which trains vectors prepended to the attention keys and values; this uses up part of the context window and can be hard to optimize.
A different line of work asked how many parameters are really needed to fine-tune. Aghajanyan, Zettlemoyer and Gupta (2020) measured the "intrinsic dimension" of fine-tuning: they found that a model like RoBERTa could reach about 90% of full fine-tuning accuracy on some tasks while training in a random subspace of only a few hundred to a few thousand dimensions. Large pre-trained models need fewer dimensions, not more. This suggested that the useful change is small in a mathematical sense.
Hu, Shen, Wallis and colleagues (Microsoft, 2021) turned this into LoRA. They applied it to GPT-3 175B and reported that the number of trainable parameters could be cut by about 10,000 times and the GPU memory for training by about three times, with quality equal to or better than full fine-tuning on the tasks they tested. Their update can be merged into the weights, so there is no added latency. The paper was published at ICLR 2022.
LoRA became popular only when open models appeared. After LLaMA (February 2023) and the Hugging Face PEFT library, thousands of community adapters were trained on consumer GPUs. Dettmers et al. introduced QLoRA in May 2023, with NF4 quantization, double quantization and paged optimizers, and used it to train the Guanaco models. Serving systems followed: Punica and S-LoRA (2023) showed how to serve many adapters on one GPU in one batch. In 2024 and 2025 the question changed to when LoRA is enough: Biderman et al. (2024) found that LoRA learns less than full fine-tuning on hard continued-pretraining tasks (code and maths) but also forgets less, and the Thinking Machines article "LoRA Without Regret" (Schulman et al., September 2025) found that LoRA matches full fine-tuning on small to medium datasets when applied to all layers, and even at rank 1 for reinforcement learning.
how: Take one weight matrix W of shape d × k inside a pre-trained layer; for example the query projection in attention, 4096 × 4096. In normal fine-tuning, training learns a change ΔW of the same shape. LoRA instead writes \( \Delta W = \frac{\alpha}{r} \times B \times A \). Matrix A has shape r × k and matrix B has shape d × r. The base W is frozen: no gradient, no optimizer state. Only A and B are trained.
The forward pass computes \( y = W x + \frac{\alpha}{r} \times B (A x) \). Read it from the right: first A projects the input x (k numbers) down to r numbers, then B projects it back up to d numbers. The cost of the extra path is \( 2 \times r \times (d + k) \) multiply-adds per token, which for r = 16 and d = k = 4096 is about 262,000 operations, compared with 16.8 million for W x. So the adapter adds about 1.6% to the compute of that layer.
Initialization matters. A is filled with small random values (Gaussian or Kaiming-uniform) and B is filled with zeros. The product \( B \times A \) is therefore zero at the start, so the model begins exactly as the pre-trained model and training moves it away smoothly. If both were random, the first step would damage the model. The \( \alpha/r \) factor keeps the effective step size roughly constant when you change r, so the learning rate does not need to be re-tuned for every rank. Some later work (rank-stabilized LoRA) argues for \( \alpha/\sqrt{r} \) when the rank is large.
Backpropagation (the L2 concept) is what makes the memory saving real. The chain rule still needs to pass gradients through the frozen layers back to the adapters, so activations must still be stored. But gradients for W (as big as W) are never formed, and Adam keeps two extra numbers (momentum and variance) only for A and B. For a 7B model with adapters of 40M parameters, the optimizer state is under 1 GB instead of about 56 GB. Weights are held in bf16 (14 GB), and with activation checkpointing the job fits on one 24 GB to 40 GB GPU. Compute per step falls to roughly two-thirds of full fine-tuning, because the weight-gradient matrix multiplications for W are skipped.
Where to attach adapters is a design choice. The original paper adapted only the query and value projections of attention and held the rest fixed, to keep parameter counts tiny. The current practice, supported by experiments, is to adapt all linear layers, including the MLP layers (gate, up and down projections), which hold most of the parameters. The Thinking Machines study found attention-only LoRA underperforms even when the parameter count is matched to a MLP-inclusive setup. Rank needs to match the amount of information in the data: a few thousand instruction examples are fine with r = 8 or 16, while large datasets need more capacity.
Linear algebra explains the capacity limit. Any d × k matrix of rank at most r can be written as B × A, and by the Eckart–Young theorem the best rank-r approximation of any matrix comes from its largest r singular values. LoRA does not compute an SVD of the true update (you never see it); it lets gradient descent find a good rank-r update directly. The number of trainable parameters per matrix is \( r \times (d + k) \) instead of \( d \times k \). For d = k = 4096 and r = 8, this is 65,536 against 16,777,216, which is 0.39%. When the data requires changing the model in many independent directions (new language, new large body of facts), rank r runs out and the adapter underfits.
After training you have two choices. You can merge: \( W' = W + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A \), giving a normal model with zero extra latency. Or you keep adapters separate and apply them on the fly. Serving systems batch requests that use different adapters: the base matrix multiplication is shared by all requests, and a small grouped kernel applies each request's own B A. The memory for hundreds of adapters (each 20 to 200 MB) is small compared with the base model.
QLoRA adds one more idea. The frozen base weights are stored in 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4), a code designed so that each of the 16 levels is equally likely for normally distributed weights, and a second quantization compresses the scaling constants. During the forward and backward pass, each block of weights is de-quantized to bf16 just in time, used, and discarded. The adapters and their gradients remain in 16-bit. A 7B model's base weights then take about 3.5 to 4 GB, and a 65B model's fit in less than 48 GB, which is what the paper demonstrated. Quality is close to 16-bit LoRA, at the price of slower steps from de-quantization.

example: Count the parameters for a 7B model of the Llama-2 shape: hidden size 4096, MLP size 11008, 32 layers, about 6.74 billion parameters. Adapt all seven linear layers in each block with rank r = 16.
Attention has four 4096 × 4096 matrices (query, key, value, output). Each gets r × (4096 + 4096) = 16 × 8192 = 131,072 adapter parameters, so 524,288 for the four. The MLP has three matrices between 4096 and 11008: each gets 16 × (4096 + 11008) = 241,664, so 724,992 for the three. One layer: 1,249,280. Over 32 layers: 39,976,960, which is about 40 million, or 0.59% of the model.
Memory for training with Adam in mixed precision. Full fine-tuning: weights (2 bytes), gradients (2), fp32 master weights (4), Adam momentum and variance (8) is 16 bytes per parameter, so 6.74B × 16 = about 108 GB. LoRA: frozen weights in bf16, 13.5 GB. Adapter state: 40M × 16 bytes = 0.64 GB. Total about 14 GB plus activations, so it fits on a 24 GB card with a small batch and activation checkpointing. QLoRA: the base shrinks to about 3.8 GB (4 bits per weight plus constants), so the total is under 6 GB plus activations.
Storage and serving. The adapter file is 40M × 2 bytes = 80 MB, compared with 13.5 GB for a full copy. A server with 100 customers each having their own adapter stores 8 GB of adapters instead of 1.35 TB of models.
Rank test. Suppose the true best update for a matrix has singular values 10, 5, 1, 0.5, 0.2 and smaller. A rank-2 adapter can capture the directions with values 10 and 5. The squared error left is 1² + 0.5² + 0.2² + … ≈ 1.3, against a total of 10² + 5² + … ≈ 126.3 (about 1% of the update's energy is lost). If instead the singular values decay slowly (10, 9, 9, 8, 8, …), rank 2 captures little, and you need a larger r or full fine-tuning. This is the mathematical reason LoRA suits "small behavioural changes" and struggles with "learn a lot of new content".
intuition: Instead of rewriting a whole reference book to adapt it, you keep the book as it is and attach a few pages of margin notes that readers see together with the text.
The idea is natural because a pre-trained model has already built most of what it needs. A change to behaviour (a new format, a new tone, a narrow domain) is a small push in a few directions, not a rearrangement of all knowledge. A few directions are exactly what a low-rank matrix describes. Setting B to zero at the start makes the notes empty at first, so the model begins from its known good state. And because notes are separate from the book, one book can carry many sets of notes.
mistakes: People often think LoRA is always as good as full fine-tuning. In fact it matches full fine-tuning when the dataset is small to medium and the adapter covers all layers, but it falls behind when the new data holds more information than the adapter can store, for example continued pre-training on billions of code tokens. Biderman et al. (2024) measured this gap.
People often think a higher rank is always better. In fact, once the rank covers the real complexity of the task, more rank brings no gain, and it costs memory, can cause overfitting on small data and may need a different learning rate. Start with 8 to 32 and increase only if the training loss stalls well above what full fine-tuning reaches.
People often think LoRA training can use the same learning rate as full fine-tuning. In fact the best learning rate for LoRA is typically around ten times larger (Schulman et al., 2025). The reason is that the adapters start at zero and are small, so they need larger steps to matter.
People often think a LoRA adapter is a portable skill. In fact it is a delta on one specific base model. It cannot be moved to another model, or even to a later version of the same family, because the directions in weight space are defined relative to those exact weights. Merging several adapters by adding them also does not simply sum their skills, as updates interfere.
People often think LoRA makes inference slower. In fact a merged adapter adds zero cost. An unmerged adapter adds only a small matrix multiplication per layer, a few percent.
friction: Capacity is the main limit. The adapter is a rank-r correction, so tasks that need large changes (a new language, a large body of new facts, long continued pre-training) can underfit. Rank, α, learning rate, and the set of target layers all interact, and the defaults in tutorials are often not tuned for your data. Large batch sizes also hurt LoRA more than full fine-tuning, and raising the rank does not fix that.
LoRA reduces memory for optimizer state, not for activations. Training on long sequences still needs large activation memory, and the frozen base weights must stay in GPU memory. QLoRA trades speed for memory: de-quantizing on the fly makes each step slower than in 16-bit LoRA, and the quantization error in the base model is not corrected by the adapter.
Adapters are tied to a base. When the provider releases a new base model, every adapter must be retrained, so a company with 500 customer adapters has a 500-job migration. Quantizing a merged model can also lose some of the adapter's effect, since the update is small compared with the weights and rounding can hide it.
Safety and evaluation have their own issues. A small adapter can undo safety training of an open model (research on fine-tuning attacks shows that very few examples can be enough), and it is hard to audit what an adapter changed. In multi-tenant serving, a batch that mixes many different adapters needs special kernels, and cache memory is shared, so the number of simultaneous adapters is limited in practice.
practice: Use LoRA when you want to adapt an open-weight model to your data on limited hardware, or to keep many task variants. Apply it to all linear layers, begin with r = 16 and α = 32 (so α/r = 2), and use a learning rate of roughly 1e-4 to 2e-4 for supervised tuning. This is about ten times what you would use for full fine-tuning. Train for 1 to 3 epochs; adapters overfit quickly on small data. Hold out a validation set and watch both validation loss and a task metric.
Check the quality of the data before changing the method. A few thousand clean, diverse, correctly formatted examples usually beat a large noisy set. Use the same chat template at training and serving time; a mismatch is the most common reason a good adapter behaves badly in production.
Choose between knowledge in the weights and knowledge at query time. LoRA is good for style, format, tool-use patterns and domain vocabulary. For facts that change (product catalogues, policies), retrieval-augmented generation is cheaper and easier to update, because you edit documents and not retrain. Many production systems use both.
For serving, merge the adapter if you have one model per deployment. If you have many adapters, use a server that supports multi-LoRA batching (vLLM, S-LoRA-style systems, or your provider's managed fine-tuning) and keep the base model resident. Version each adapter with the exact base checkpoint, the data hash and the hyper-parameters. Use the PEFT library from Hugging Face for training, and consider QLoRA when the base model does not fit in 16-bit. Evaluate on a set of prompts unrelated to the tuning task too, to see whether general abilities have degraded.
tradeoffs: Choose LoRA when memory is the limit, when you need many task variants of one base model, or when your data has fewer than about 100,000 to a few million examples. You give up some capacity and, on hard knowledge-heavy tasks, final quality.
Choose full fine-tuning when the task changes the model deeply: continued pre-training on billions of tokens, a new language, or when you have the cluster and need the last few points of quality. You pay about 16 bytes per parameter in training state and one full model copy per task.
Choose QLoRA over 16-bit LoRA when the base model does not fit on your GPU in 16-bit (for example a 70B model on one 80 GB card). You give up speed (de-quantization overhead) and a small amount of accuracy, but gain a four-fold reduction in the memory for the base weights.
Choose a small rank (4 to 16) for style, format and classification-like tasks and for reinforcement learning, where the signal carries little information per sample. Choose a large rank (64 to 256) for tasks with rich supervision, such as code or maths data in the hundreds of thousands of examples. A higher rank costs more memory and more risk of overfitting.
Choose merged serving for one dominant model with the lowest latency; choose unmerged multi-adapter serving when you have tens to thousands of tenants. The merged option gives up flexibility (one copy of model per variant), the unmerged one gives up a few percent of throughput and needs a more complex server.
Choose RAG over LoRA when the knowledge changes weekly and must be traceable to a source; choose LoRA over RAG when you need behaviour, such as an output format or a tone, that no retrieved text can enforce. Either choice gives up something: RAG adds retrieval latency and a retrieval failure mode, LoRA adds retraining cycles.
interview: Explain LoRA and why it saves memory. Where exactly does the saving come from? || LoRA freezes W and learns \( \Delta W = \frac{\alpha}{r} B A \) with r much smaller than d and k. There are no gradients or Adam states for W, only for A and B, which have \( r(d+k) \) parameters per matrix. For a 7B model, that is about 40M trainable parameters, so optimizer state drops from tens of GB to under 1 GB. Activations are still stored, so the saving is in weights' gradients and optimizer state, not in activation memory.
Why is B initialized to zero and A to random values? || With B = 0, the product B A is zero, so the model starts exactly at the pre-trained function and the first updates are small and stable. A must be non-zero so that B receives non-zero gradients (the gradient for B depends on A x). If both were zero, no learning would start. If both were random, you would add noise to the model at step 0.
When does LoRA fail to match full fine-tuning? How would you diagnose it? || When the task needs more information than a rank-r update can hold, such as continued pre-training on large code or maths corpora, or a big domain shift. Symptoms are a training loss that plateaus above the full fine-tuning curve even at a higher rank and learning rate. Diagnose by raising the rank, applying LoRA to all layers including MLP, tuning the learning rate (about 10 times that of full fine-tuning) and comparing with a small full fine-tuning baseline.
You must serve 2,000 customer-specific fine-tuned models. How do you do it? || Train one LoRA adapter per customer on the same base, and keep the base model resident on the GPU. Batch requests across adapters: the base matrix multiplications are shared and per-request low-rank paths use grouped kernels (as in Punica or S-LoRA, now available in serving engines like vLLM). Keep hot adapters in GPU memory and the rest in CPU memory or disk, with an LRU policy. At 80 MB per adapter, 2,000 adapters take about 160 GB in total, which fits in host memory.
How is LoRA different from adapters or prefix tuning, and why did it win? || Adapter layers add sequential modules that stay in the forward path and add latency. Prefix tuning uses context length and can be hard to optimize. LoRA adds a parallel low-rank path that can be merged into the weights after training, so there is no inference overhead, and it works with any linear layer without changing the architecture.
quiz: What do A and B look like in LoRA, and what are their shapes for a d × k weight? || A has shape r × k and B has shape d × r, with r much smaller than d and k. The update is \( \frac{\alpha}{r} \times B \times A \), which has the same shape as W but rank at most r.
Why does the forward pass computing B(Ax) cost little compared with W x? || A x first reduces the input to r numbers, then B expands to d numbers. This costs about \( r \times (d + k) \) multiply-adds per token instead of \( d \times k \), so for r = 16 and d = k = 4096 it is roughly 1.6% of the cost of W x.
Why is B initialized to zero? || So the adapter contributes nothing at the start, and the fine-tuned model begins exactly as the pre-trained model. Learning then moves it away gradually, and A being random lets gradients reach B.
How many parameters does rank-8 LoRA add to one 4096 × 4096 matrix, and what fraction is that? || r × (d + k) = 8 × 8192 = 65,536 parameters, against 16,777,216 in the matrix, which is about 0.39%.
You have a 70B model, one 80 GB GPU and 20,000 clean instruction examples. What do you choose? || QLoRA: the 70B base in 4-bit takes about 35 to 40 GB, leaving room for adapters, optimizer state and activations. Full fine-tuning is impossible at that size, and 16-bit LoRA needs 140 GB for the weights alone. Accept slower steps and a small quality loss from quantization.

value: The best models are too large and too slow to run everywhere. A model with hundreds of billions of parameters needs several GPUs for one request, costs real money per token, and cannot run on a phone. Distillation is the standard way to move quality from a large model (the teacher) into a small one (the student). The student is trained not only on the correct answers but on the teacher's behaviour: its full output probabilities, or the text it writes.
The gain is large. DistilBERT (2019) kept about 97% of BERT's language-understanding score with 40% fewer parameters and about 60% faster inference. In the LLM era, most small and fast production models are trained, at least partly, on the outputs of a stronger model. Meta trained its Llama 3.2 1B and 3B models with the output logits of the larger Llama 3.1 8B and 70B models as targets, and DeepSeek released small models fine-tuned on reasoning text written by its large R1 model.
The reason it works is that a teacher's output carries much more information than a label. A hard label says "this image is a dog". The teacher's probabilities say "dog 90%, wolf 8%, cat 1.5%, truck 0.0001%", which tells the student how classes relate. Each training example then teaches many things at once, so the student learns faster than from labels alone and with less data.
Two lower-layer ideas matter. Information theory (L4) provides the training objective: the student minimizes the KL divergence from the teacher's distribution to its own, which is the extra cost in bits of using the student's code when the teacher's is the truth. Probability gives the softmax with temperature, the tool that makes the small probabilities visible. Gradient descent and backpropagation do the rest.
terms: Teacher — the large, already trained model whose behaviour we want to copy.
Student — the small model being trained to imitate the teacher.
Hard label — the correct answer as a single class (one-hot vector). It says nothing about the other classes.
Soft target — the teacher's full probability distribution over classes (or over next tokens). It shows which wrong answers are nearly right.
Logits — the raw scores before softmax. The probability of class i is \( \frac{\exp(z_i)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j)} \).
Temperature T — a number that divides the logits before softmax. T > 1 flattens the distribution and makes small probabilities visible; T = 1 is normal.
KL divergence — a measure, in nats or bits, of how much one distribution differs from another. It is zero only if they are equal.
Sequence-level distillation — training the student on whole texts sampled from the teacher, not on its per-token probabilities.
On-policy distillation — the student generates its own text, and the teacher scores each token of it; training follows the student's own distribution.
history: The idea is older than deep learning. Buciluǎ, Caruana and Niculescu-Mizil (2006) compressed a large ensemble of models into one small neural network by training it on the ensemble's predictions for a large set of unlabeled data. Ba and Caruana (2014), in "Do Deep Nets Really Need to be Deep?", trained shallow networks to match the logits of deep ones and got much better results than training on labels.
Hinton, Vinyals and Dean (2015) gave the method its name and its standard form: "Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network". They proposed raising the softmax temperature to expose the information in small probabilities, which Hinton called "dark knowledge", and combining the soft-target loss with the normal label loss. Their motivating example was compressing an ensemble of models into one that is practical to deploy.
For language models, Kim and Rush (2016) introduced sequence-level distillation for translation: have the teacher translate a corpus with beam search and train the student on those translations. This avoids the need to match the output vocabulary and the whole sequence distribution. In 2019, Sanh et al. released DistilBERT, and TinyBERT and MiniLM followed with extra losses that match hidden states and attention maps.
After ChatGPT (2022), distillation changed in kind. In March 2023 Stanford's Alpaca fine-tuned LLaMA 7B on 52,000 instruction-response pairs generated by OpenAI's text-davinci-003 and got chat behaviour for a few hundred dollars of compute; Vicuna, Orca and many others followed. Terms of service for several commercial APIs forbid using outputs to train competing models, which made the legal side a live issue. Agarwal et al. (2023) proposed generalized knowledge distillation with on-policy samples, and Gu et al. (2023, MiniLLM) argued for reverse KL. From 2024, small models from major labs (Gemma 2, Llama 3.2) were trained with teacher logits during pre-training, and in January 2025 DeepSeek released R1 together with smaller models fine-tuned on about 800,000 samples generated with R1; they reported that this beat running reinforcement learning directly on the small models.
how: Classic logit distillation starts with a trained teacher and a student of a different, smaller architecture. For each training input x, the teacher produces logits z_t and the student produces logits z_s. Both are turned into probabilities with a temperature: \( p_t = \mathrm{softmax}(z_t / T) \) and \( p_s = \mathrm{softmax}(z_s / T) \). The distillation loss is the KL divergence \( \mathrm{KL}(p_t \,\|\, p_s) \), which is the sum over classes of \( p_t \log (p_t / p_s) \). It is zero when the student reproduces the teacher exactly.
Why a temperature? A confident teacher gives probabilities such as 0.999 for the top class and 0.0001 for a distant class. These small numbers are almost invisible in the loss, even though their ratios carry the information that one wrong class is much more plausible than another. Dividing the logits by T = 2 to 5 makes the distribution flatter, so the relationships between wrong answers matter in the loss. Because the gradient of the soft loss scales as \( 1/T^2 \), Hinton recommended multiplying it by \( T^2 \). The student is usually evaluated at T = 1. The total loss is \( \alpha T^2 \,\mathrm{KL}(p_t \,\|\, p_s) + (1 - \alpha)\, \mathrm{CE}(y, \mathrm{softmax}(z_s)) \), mixing teacher information with the true labels.
Information theory explains why this transfers a lot. A one-hot label for a 1,000-class problem carries at most \( \log_2 1000 \), about 10 bits. A full distribution can carry far more, since the exact ratios between 999 other classes are informative. Minimizing \( \mathrm{KL}(p_t \,\|\, p_s) \) is the same as minimizing cross-entropy against the teacher's distribution, since the teacher's entropy is a constant; so the student is maximum-likelihood fitting a richer, smoother target. A smooth target also acts as a regularizer. It removes label noise and makes the student's loss landscape easier to optimize.
For language models the "class" is the next token and the vocabulary is large (32,000 to 256,000 entries). Token-level distillation runs the same text through teacher and student and matches their next-token distributions at every position. The teacher must share the student's tokenizer. Storing full distributions is impractical: one trillion tokens times a 128,000-entry vocabulary at 2 bytes each is 256 petabytes. So practical systems either run the teacher online alongside the student, or save only the top-k probabilities (for example, the top 20 to 100 tokens) and renormalize.
When tokenizers differ, or when the teacher is only available through an API that returns text, you use sequence-level distillation: sample teacher outputs for a large set of prompts and fine-tune the student on them with the ordinary next-token loss. This is what "distilled from model X" usually means for instruction-following and reasoning models. The signal is less rich (one sampled path, not the whole distribution), but with millions of samples it is effective. For reasoning, the samples include the teacher's long chains of thought, which teaches the student the pattern of working step by step.
The direction of the KL matters. Forward KL(p_t ‖ p_s) forces the student to put mass wherever the teacher does ("mean seeking"). A small student cannot cover everything, so it spreads probability and may produce odd, low-quality samples between modes. Reverse KL(p_s ‖ p_t) makes the student focus on the teacher's main modes ("mode seeking"), which often gives better generation from a limited-capacity student. Reverse KL needs samples from the student, which leads to on-policy distillation: the student writes text, the teacher scores every token, and the loss is the per-token reverse KL. It fixes a known weakness of training on teacher text, where the student never sees its own mistakes and so cannot learn to recover from them (exposure bias).
Dense gradients explain why this helps pre-training too. With a hard label, only the correct token's logit gets a strong learning signal at each position. With a teacher distribution, every one of the V entries in the vocabulary gets a signal at every position, so a single training token teaches the student about many plausible alternatives. A common recipe mixes the two losses on real text: a weight of 0.5 on the usual next-token cross-entropy, and 0.5 on the KL to the teacher at the same positions. This is the pattern used when small models are trained with a large model's logits as extra targets.
Other methods transfer internal signals: matching intermediate hidden states or attention maps with a learned projection when the dimensions differ, and pruning plus distillation, in which a large model is pruned to a smaller shape and then "healed" by distillation, which is cheaper than training from scratch.

example: Take a 3-class problem and teacher logits z = [5.0, 3.0, −1.0] for classes "dog", "wolf", "truck". At T = 1: exp values are 148.4, 20.1 and 0.37, sum 168.9, so p = [0.879, 0.119, 0.002]. The hard label says only "dog". The soft target says wolf is about 50 times more plausible than truck. Now use T = 4: logits become [1.25, 0.75, −0.25]. The exp values are 3.49, 2.12 and 0.78, sum 6.39, so p = [0.547, 0.332, 0.122]. The wolf and truck probabilities are now large enough to matter in the loss.
Suppose the student at T = 4 produces [0.40, 0.35, 0.25]. \( \mathrm{KL}(p_t \,\|\, p_s) \) = 0.547 × ln(0.547/0.40) + 0.332 × ln(0.332/0.35) + 0.122 × ln(0.122/0.25) = 0.547 × 0.313 + 0.332 × (−0.053) + 0.122 × (−0.717) = 0.171 − 0.018 − 0.087 = 0.066 nats. After multiplying by T² = 16, the soft loss is about 1.04. If the student matched the teacher exactly, the loss would be zero. The gradient moves the student's wolf-versus-truck ordering to agree with the teacher's, which a hard label alone would never teach.
Now a deployment calculation. A teacher with 70 billion parameters needs about 140 GB in 16-bit weights, so at least two 80 GB GPUs. Per generated token, the compute is roughly 2 × 70 × 10⁹ = 140 GFLOPs. A distilled student with 8 billion parameters takes 16 GB (one GPU, or less in 8-bit) and 16 GFLOPs per token. Decoding is limited by memory bandwidth, not compute: each token must read all weights once, so on a GPU with 3 TB/s of bandwidth, the 8B student can reach about 3,000 / 16 = roughly 190 tokens per second for one stream (an upper bound), against about 21 for the 70B model on one GPU's bandwidth (and it would not fit). If distillation keeps the student at 90% of the teacher's quality on your task, you pay about one eighth of the cost for most traffic. This is why many systems run the small model for the common path and escalate hard cases to the teacher.
Sequence-level cost. Generating 1 million teacher answers of 500 tokens each is 500 million output tokens. At a price of, say, 10 dollars per million output tokens this is 5,000 dollars; with a self-hosted teacher the cost is GPU hours. Fine-tuning an 8B student on 500 million tokens is a modest job of a few GPU-days to a couple of weeks depending on hardware. The one-time cost is paid back by the lower cost per query.
intuition: Learning from a teacher's explanation, not only from the answer key.
An answer key tells you that the answer is "B". A good teacher also tells you "A is almost right, C is silly, D is nearly the same as B". That extra structure is what the teacher's probabilities are. A student who sees it learns the shape of the problem, not only the single point. It is natural that this is a faster way to learn than guessing the structure from many bare labels. Information theory gives the exact measure: KL divergence counts the bits the student wastes by believing the wrong distribution.
mistakes: People often think distillation produces a student that is as good as the teacher. In fact the student is capped by its own capacity and, usually, by the teacher. It copies the behaviour on the distribution it was trained on and loses ground on rare or hard cases. A student can sometimes beat the teacher on a narrow task, but on general ability it falls short.
People often think distillation means matching the final text only. In fact there are several forms: matching full probability distributions (logit distillation), matching generated text (sequence-level), and matching internal states. They need different access to the teacher. With only an API that returns text, you can do the second form only.
People often think a high temperature is a magic setting. In fact temperature only changes how visible the small probabilities are. Too high and the target becomes uniform noise; too low and it is the same as a hard label. It is a tuning parameter, usually between 1 and 5 for classifiers, and for LLM token-level distillation many systems simply use T = 1.
People often think that copying a teacher's reasoning traces teaches the student to reason. In fact a student can learn the style of step-by-step text (the words, the structure) without the underlying skill, and may produce confident but wrong chains. Quality depends on verified traces, enough diverse problems and a student with enough capacity. Careful work filters traces by correctness.
People sometimes think distillation is free of legal and safety issues. In fact outputs of a commercial model may be covered by terms that forbid training other models, and a student also inherits the teacher's biases, mistakes and sometimes its unsafe behaviours.
friction: The student can never be better than the teacher on what the teacher does not know, and errors in the teacher are copied together with its skills. Because the teacher's outputs on the training prompts are the only signal, the prompt set controls the result: if it is narrow, the student is narrow. Capacity gaps are real; a 1B student cannot hold what a 70B teacher knows, so the practical question is which abilities survive. Factual knowledge and long-tail skills degrade first, while format, style and common reasoning patterns transfer well.
Logit distillation needs access to the teacher's logits and a shared tokenizer, and it is expensive in storage or in compute because the teacher must run on every training token. API-only teachers permit only sequence-level distillation, which wastes the distribution information, and is often restricted by terms of service. Sampling bias is a further issue: training on teacher text only shows the student states the teacher would visit, so when the student makes a mistake at test time it is in a state it never saw. On-policy methods fix this at the price of generating samples during training.
Evaluation is also hard. Matching the teacher's outputs on a held-out set is not the same as matching its quality on your task; use task metrics, human or model-based judgement, and tests for robustness. Finally, as small models trained on the outputs of other models are used to train yet more models, errors and a narrowing of diversity can accumulate ("model collapse" in the extreme case of training on only synthetic data).
practice: Decide first what you need to keep: accuracy on a defined task, latency, or cost. For a classifier or ranking model, use classic logit distillation: train the student with a loss of the form \( \alpha \times T^2 \times \mathrm{KL} + (1 - \alpha) \times \mathrm{CE} \), with T between 2 and 4 and α near 0.5 to start. Use a large set of unlabeled inputs: the teacher labels them for free, and this is where distillation gains most over supervised training.
For LLMs, start with sequence-level distillation: build a diverse, deduplicated prompt set from your real traffic, generate teacher answers (several per prompt at moderate temperature if you want diversity), filter them (by correctness checks, a reward model or a judge), and fine-tune the student. Keep a held-out set of real prompts for evaluation. If you control both models and share a tokenizer, add token-level logit matching on the top-k tokens, which usually gives extra quality for little extra cost. Consider on-policy distillation when the student drifts on long outputs.
Choose the student size from a budget: measure latency and memory on your hardware first, then pick the largest model that fits. Combine distillation with quantization (to 8-bit or 4-bit) and, where available, speculative decoding, in which a small draft model proposes tokens that the large model verifies; a distilled draft model agrees with the target model more often, which increases the speedup.
Check the licence and terms of the teacher before you generate data. Log the teacher version, prompts and filters with the data, because a new teacher version changes the result. Test the student against the teacher on rare and safety-relevant cases, not only on the average.
tradeoffs: Choose distillation over training a small model from scratch when a strong teacher exists and you have many unlabeled inputs. A student trained on a teacher's soft targets typically reaches a higher accuracy than the same model trained on labels, and needs fewer steps. You give up the independence of the result: the student inherits the teacher's errors.
Choose logit (token-level) distillation when you own the teacher and tokenizer and want the most signal per example; choose sequence-level distillation when the teacher is behind an API, tokenizers differ, or you only need the style and format of its answers. Logit matching needs teacher inference on every training token and storage of top-k probabilities; sequence-level needs only generated text but conveys less per token.
Choose on-policy distillation when the student's outputs are long and errors accumulate (reasoning, agents, code); choose off-policy teacher text when simplicity and throughput matter more. On-policy methods need generation inside the training loop, which can make each step several times more expensive.
Choose distillation over quantization alone when you need a smaller architecture, such as 3B instead of 70B; choose quantization when you need the same model with a 2 to 4 times smaller memory footprint without retraining. They combine well: distil, then quantize.
Choose distillation over a larger prompt or retrieval when the task is stable and high-volume and you can amortize a training run; choose the large model with prompting when the task changes often, volume is low, or quality on rare cases is more important than cost.
interview: Why does training on a teacher's soft targets work better than training on hard labels? || A soft target contains the full ratios between classes, so each example teaches the student which wrong answers are close to right. That is far more information than the roughly \( \log_2 C \) bits of a one-hot label. It also regularizes: the target is smooth, so the student does not become over-confident. Minimizing the KL to the teacher is a cross-entropy fit to a richer target.
What does temperature do in distillation and why is the loss multiplied by \( T^2 \)? || Temperature divides the logits before softmax, so the small probabilities of wrong classes become large enough to affect the loss. The gradient of the soft-target loss with respect to the logits scales as \( 1/T^2 \), so multiplying by \( T^2 \) keeps its magnitude comparable to the hard-label loss when T changes.
You can only call a teacher through an API that returns text. How do you distil it, and what is lost? || Use sequence-level distillation: generate answers for a large and varied prompt set, filter by correctness or a judge, and fine-tune the student on them. You lose the per-token probability distribution, which carries most of the "dark knowledge", and you inherit the teacher's style. You must also check the provider's terms.
What is the difference between forward and reverse KL for distilling a language model, and what is on-policy distillation? || Forward KL(teacher ‖ student) makes the student cover all of the teacher's modes, and a small student spreads its mass and may produce poor samples. Reverse KL(student ‖ teacher) is mode-seeking and often generates better samples. On-policy distillation samples text from the student and has the teacher score each token, so the student is trained on the states it actually visits and learns to recover from its own mistakes.
Your distilled 7B model matches the teacher on your benchmark but fails in production. What do you suspect? || A distribution gap between the benchmark or distillation prompts and real traffic, so rare or long-tail inputs are not covered. Also possible are benchmark contamination in the teacher data, exposure bias on long outputs, and loss of safety behaviours. Fix it with prompts sampled from real traffic, on-policy data, hard-case mining, and tests on slices.
quiz: What does the student minimize in classic knowledge distillation? || A weighted sum of the KL divergence between the teacher's and the student's temperature-softened output distributions (scaled by \( T^2 \)) and the normal cross-entropy with the true labels.
Why are soft targets more informative than hard labels? || They give the teacher's probability for every class, which shows how similar the wrong classes are to the right one. A one-hot label carries at most about \( \log_2 C \) bits, while a full distribution carries the structure of the whole problem.
Why raise the softmax temperature during distillation? || A confident teacher gives tiny probabilities to wrong classes, which barely change the loss. A higher temperature flattens the distribution so that their relative sizes are visible and the student can learn them.
A 70B teacher decodes at about 21 tokens per second on bandwidth-limited hardware and an 8B student at about 190. Roughly how much faster is the student and why? || About nine times faster. Decoding reads all weights once per token, so speed scales with the inverse of the parameter count: 70 / 8 ≈ 8.75.
When would you use sequence-level distillation instead of logit distillation? || When you only have text from the teacher (API access), when the teacher and student use different tokenizers, or when storing teacher distributions is too costly. It is simpler but transfers less information per token.

@@ scalinglaws
value: Training a frontier language model costs tens to hundreds of millions of dollars and cannot be repeated if it goes wrong. Before spending that money, a lab needs to know what it will get, and how to split the budget between a bigger model and more training text. Scaling laws are the empirical answer. When you train many models of different sizes on different amounts of data and plot the test loss on log-log axes, the points fall on a straight line. In plain terms: loss falls as a smooth power law in the number of parameters N, the number of training tokens D, and the compute C. The relation holds over many orders of magnitude.
This turns model building from guesswork into planning. A lab can train small models for a few thousand dollars, fit the curve, and predict the loss of a model that is 1,000 times larger. The GPT-4 technical report stated that the final loss was predicted from runs that used a thousand times less compute. The curves also give a rule for the split between N and D under a fixed compute budget. The Chinchilla result (2022) said that parameters and tokens should grow together, at roughly 20 tokens per parameter, and that most large models of that time were too big and under-trained.
For the layer above, scaling laws set the shape of LLM training programmes: how big a model to train, how many tokens to buy or generate, when more data is needed, and whether a bigger model or more reasoning at answer time is the better use of money. They also drive investment decisions about clusters and power.
Three lower-layer ideas are used. Power laws (L4) are the functional form: a straight line on a log-log plot. Statistics supplies the method: the laws are curve fits to a few hundred noisy training runs, with all the issues of fitting, extrapolation and uncertainty. Optimization supplies the allocation: minimizing the fitted loss under the constraint C = 6ND is a small constrained optimization problem with a closed-form answer.
terms: Loss — the average cross-entropy of the model on held-out text, measured in nats per token. Lower is better.
N — the number of model parameters (often counting non-embedding parameters).
D — the number of training tokens the model sees.
C — training compute in floating-point operations. For a dense transformer, \( C \approx 6 \times N \times D \).
Power law — a relation \( y = a \times x^{-b} \). On a log-log plot it is a straight line with slope \( -b \).
Irreducible loss — the part of the loss that no model can remove, because text has real randomness. It is the constant E in the fitted formula.
Compute-optimal — the choice of N and D that gives the lowest loss for a fixed C.
IsoFLOP curve — loss plotted against model size for a family of runs that all use the same compute.
Overtraining — training a model on more tokens than the compute-optimal amount, to get a smaller model that is cheaper to serve.
Test-time compute — compute spent when answering (for example long reasoning chains), as opposed to compute spent in training.
history: Early hints came from Hestness et al. (Baidu, 2017), who showed power-law learning curves in translation, speech and image tasks, and from Henighan et al. (OpenAI, 2020) on several data types. The paper that started the LLM era of scaling laws was Kaplan, McCandlish and colleagues (OpenAI, January 2020). They trained transformers of up to 1.5 billion parameters and found that test loss follows power laws in N, D and C, with exponents of about 0.076 for parameters, 0.095 for data and 0.050 for compute. Shape of the network (depth versus width) mattered little. For a fixed compute budget they concluded that most of the budget should go into a larger model, with relatively few tokens. GPT-3 (2020, 175B parameters on about 300B tokens) followed that advice.
Hoffmann et al. (DeepMind, March 2022) re-ran the study with more than 400 models, from 70 million to over 16 billion parameters, trained on 5 to 500 billion tokens, and with learning-rate schedules matched to the length of each run. They found that model size and token count should scale in equal proportion. They tested the idea by training Chinchilla, with 70B parameters and 1.4T tokens, using the same compute as the 280B-parameter Gopher trained on 300B tokens. Chinchilla was better on most benchmarks, and it is also four times cheaper to run. LLaMA (Meta, 2023) built on this logic.
Later work explained the gap between the two studies. Porian et al. (2024) showed that most of the difference came from how Kaplan counted parameters (leaving out the embedding layer matters at small scale) and from a warmup and learning-rate schedule not tuned for small models. The exact fitted constants of Chinchilla have also been questioned by replication attempts.
After 2023 the questions changed. Sardana and Frankle (2023) added the cost of inference: if a model will serve a lot of traffic, you should train a smaller model for much longer. Meta trained Llama 3 8B on about 15 trillion tokens, about 1,900 tokens per parameter, and reported that it still improved. Muennighoff et al. (2023) studied what to do when data runs out: repeating data for up to about four epochs works almost as well as new data. Schaeffer et al. (2023) argued that sudden "emergent abilities" are partly a result of discontinuous metrics, because the loss itself is smooth. From 2024, Snell et al. and OpenAI's o1 showed a second axis: more compute at inference, spent on long reasoning, also follows scaling curves.
how: The basic procedure is an experiment. Choose a family of models with the same architecture and different sizes, for example 10 sizes from 10 million to 1 billion parameters. Train each on several amounts of data, using a learning-rate schedule that is tuned and decays to near zero at the end of each run. Record the final loss on a held-out set. This gives a table of (N, D, loss) points, each costing a known number of FLOPs, \( C \approx 6ND \). The factor 6 comes from about 2 FLOPs per parameter per token for the forward pass and about 4 for the backward pass.
The first law is a straight line. With enough data, loss as a function of N is \( L(N) \approx (N_c / N)^{\alpha} \) with \( \alpha \approx 0.076 \). On a log-log plot, \( \log L \) falls linearly in \( \log N \). The fit is done by linear regression in log space or by non-linear least squares. The power-law form is what statisticians call a scale-free relationship: multiplying N by any constant factor multiplies the reducible loss by the same fixed factor, no matter where you start. That is why one fit at small scale can say something about large scale.
The second step is a joint law for both N and D. Hoffmann et al. used a parametric form:
\[ L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}} \]
The term E is the irreducible loss (the entropy of text). The term \( A/N^{\alpha} \) is the penalty for having a finite model, and \( B/D^{\beta} \) is the penalty for finite data. The parameters E, A, B, α and β are fitted to the grid of runs with a robust loss function and a numerical optimizer, and then checked for fit on held-out runs. This is the statistics layer in action: choose a functional form, estimate parameters with uncertainty, and be careful about extrapolation outside the fitted range.
The third step is the allocation, which is a small optimization problem. Minimize \( L(N, D) \) subject to \( 6ND = C \). Substitute \( D = C / (6N) \) and set the derivative to zero (a Lagrange multiplier gives the same answer). The result is \( N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)} \) and \( D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)} \). If α and β are close, both exponents are near 0.5, so doubling the compute should multiply both the model size and the token count by about \( \sqrt{2} \approx 1.41 \). Chinchilla's three fitting methods gave exponents between 0.46 and 0.54. Using \( C = 6ND \) with \( D = 20N \) gives \( N_{\text{opt}} = \sqrt{C / 120} \).
Where the loss comes from is useful to know. Finite N means the model cannot store enough of the patterns, so the \( A/N^{\alpha} \) term falls as you add parameters. Finite D means the model sees too few examples and generalizes badly, so the \( B/D^{\beta} \) term falls as you add data. At the compute-optimal point the two terms are balanced: the next unit of compute gives the same loss reduction whether you spend it on N or D.
Real use departs from the compute-optimal recipe on purpose. Training cost is paid once but inference cost is paid on every request, and it scales with N. If a model is expected to serve trillions of tokens, a smaller model trained on far more tokens than 20 per parameter has lower total cost for the same quality. This is "overtraining". Then the useful law is the one that shows how loss keeps falling with D at fixed N, which Llama 3 confirmed up to 15T tokens.
Fitting needs care. Points from very small models are dominated by other effects, so labs fit only above a minimum size, and they report how the exponents change when points are dropped. Each run must also be complete: a loss taken in the middle of a run with a high learning rate is not comparable to the final loss of a short run, which is why schedules are matched to run length.
Scaling laws extend beyond pre-training loss. Fine-tuning, transfer, mixture-of-experts (where active and total parameters differ), multimodal data and reinforcement learning each have their own fitted curves. For test-time compute, studies show accuracy on maths or code rising roughly log-linearly with the number of reasoning tokens or samples, up to a limit, so the total budget can be split between a bigger model and more thinking. Downstream task accuracy is less smooth than loss, because accuracy is a thresholded function of many underlying skills, and a sudden jump on a benchmark can hide a smooth change in loss.

example: Reading the exponents. Kaplan's parameter law is \( L \propto N^{-0.076} \) for the reducible part. Doubling N multiplies it by \( 2^{-0.076} = 0.949 \), a 5.1% reduction. Multiplying N by 10 gives \( 10^{-0.076} = 0.839 \), a 16% reduction. The compute law, \( L \propto C^{-0.050} \), says that 10 times more compute reduces the reducible loss by only \( 1 - 10^{-0.05} \) = 11%. This is the cost of progress: each fixed drop in loss needs a constant multiple of compute, so cost grows exponentially for linear gains.
Compute-optimal sizing. A lab has 10²⁴ FLOPs. Using \( C = 6ND \) and \( D = 20N \): 6 × 20 × N² = 120 N² = 10²⁴, so N² = 8.33 × 10²¹ and N = 9.1 × 10¹⁰ (91B parameters). Then D = 20N = 1.83 × 10¹² tokens. Check: 6 × 9.1 × 10¹⁰ × 1.83 × 10¹² = 10²⁴. In GPU time: an H100 delivers about 10¹⁵ FLOP/s in bf16 on paper, and large training jobs reach roughly 40% utilization, so about 4 × 10¹⁴ FLOP/s per GPU. Then 10²⁴ / (4 × 10¹⁴) = 2.5 × 10⁹ GPU-seconds, which is about 700,000 GPU-hours, or about 3 days on 10,000 GPUs.
Gopher against Chinchilla. Gopher: 6 × 280 × 10⁹ × 300 × 10⁹ = 5.0 × 10²³ FLOPs. Chinchilla: 6 × 70 × 10⁹ × 1.4 × 10¹² = 5.9 × 10²³ FLOPs. About the same compute, but the 70B model sees 4.7 times more tokens and uses a quarter of the parameters, so serving costs a quarter as much per token. Gopher had 300B / 280B ≈ 1 token per parameter; Chinchilla 20.
Overtraining and the cost of serving. Llama 3 405B was trained on about 15.6T tokens, roughly 38 tokens per parameter, using about 6 × 405 × 10⁹ × 15.6 × 10¹² ≈ 3.8 × 10²⁵ FLOPs. Llama 3 8B was trained on the same 15T tokens: 6 × 8 × 10⁹ × 1.5 × 10¹³ = 7.2 × 10²³ FLOPs, which is 1,900 tokens per parameter. Compute-optimal training of an 8B model would need 160B tokens, about 10⁻² of what was used. Now count a service that will generate 10¹³ tokens in its lifetime. Inference costs about 2N FLOPs per token: for the 8B model 2 × 8 × 10⁹ × 10¹³ = 1.6 × 10²³; for a 70B model 1.4 × 10²⁴. If the two reach a similar quality, the smaller model's total of 7.2 × 10²³ + 1.6 × 10²³ ≈ 8.8 × 10²³ is far below the 70B model's training plus serving cost. This is the logic of overtraining.
Extrapolation check. Suppose a ladder of small runs between 10¹⁸ and 10²¹ FLOPs, costing in total a small fraction of the main budget, falls on a line with slope −0.05 on log-log axes. You extend the line to the 10²⁴ FLOP target, three orders of magnitude beyond the largest run, which is about the same distance the GPT-4 team described. A prediction error of 2% in loss may sound small, but the loss difference between two good models can also be about 2%, so the fit must be validated on an intermediate run before you rely on it.
intuition: Doubling effort gives a steady, predictable improvement, like a reliable exchange rate between compute and quality, with diminishing returns that never quite turn into a wall.
A power law is what you expect when a task consists of very many pieces of different difficulty. Some patterns in text are common and easy to learn, and some are rare and need a lot of capacity or data. Learning the next tier of rare patterns costs a constant multiple of the previous tier, so the loss falls by a constant fraction per multiplication of resources. A larger model captures more of the rare patterns; more data shows more examples of them. Both limits are real, so both terms matter, and the balance between them is what the allocation step finds.
mistakes: People often think a scaling law is a law of nature that explains why models work. In fact it is an empirical fit to experiments with a given architecture, data mix, tokenizer and training recipe. There is no complete theory that derives the exponents, and changing the data quality or the architecture shifts the curve. Explanations in terms of data manifold dimension or heavy-tailed feature frequencies are active research, not settled.
People often think Chinchilla says "train every model on 20 tokens per parameter". In fact 20 tokens per parameter is the optimum for minimizing loss at a fixed training compute, ignoring inference. If you serve the model a lot, the right amount of data per parameter is much higher, and current open models use hundreds to thousands.
People often think that smooth loss means smooth skills. In fact the loss is an average over all tokens; a specific ability, such as multi-digit arithmetic, can stay near zero accuracy and then rise quickly as loss decreases a little. Schaeffer et al. showed that some "emergent" jumps vanish if you use a continuous metric.
People often think we will run out of data, and so scaling stops. In fact there are limits (the stock of high-quality public text is finite), but the response includes repeating data for a few epochs, filtering for quality, synthetic data, multimodal data, and spending more compute on reasoning at answer time. Whether these keep the curves straight is not yet known.
friction: The fits are extrapolations. A curve fitted between 10⁷ and 10¹⁰ parameters does not guarantee the same slope at 10¹². Small errors in the exponent become large errors in predicted compute needs. Constants differ between studies, and the choice of what counts as a parameter, how the learning rate is tuned and which data are used all move the result.
Loss does not directly give the abilities people care about. Two models with the same loss can differ in coding or tool use because of the data mix. Teams therefore fit scaling laws for specific benchmark scores and for data-mixture choices, with noisier results. Optimal hyper-parameters (learning rate, batch size) also depend on scale and must be re-tuned, and a poor schedule at one point can make a curve look flatter than it is.
Costs grow exponentially for linear gains: a 10× compute increase buys about 11% lower reducible loss under the Kaplan compute exponent. Data is a bottleneck for the largest runs, because each doubling of tokens needs roughly twice as much clean text. Energy, chips and memory bandwidth limit what can be built. Mixtures of experts, long contexts, multimodal inputs and reinforcement learning each add new axes that the simple N-D-C picture does not capture. And the laws describe average loss for the training distribution, not safety, truthfulness or behaviour on rare inputs.
practice: Use scaling laws as a planning and risk tool. Before a big run, train a ladder of small models (for example 5 to 8 sizes spanning 100 times in compute), with the same data pipeline and a properly tuned schedule, and fit loss against compute. Predict the loss of the target run and compare after the first few percent of training. If the live curve departs from the prediction, you have a bug (a data issue, an optimizer problem) before you have wasted the budget.
Choose N and D from your serving plan, not only from the Chinchilla ratio. Estimate lifetime inference tokens, price them at 2N FLOPs per token, and add the training cost 6ND; choose the smallest model that meets your quality bar and train it on as many tokens as the budget allows. Consider memory and latency targets: a model that fits on one GPU in 8-bit may be worth overtraining by 10 to 100 times.
Do the same for data decisions. Fit small-scale curves for different data mixtures or filters and compare them at equal compute, as data quality changes the intercept of the line. For fine-tuning, expect the same shape: more examples help with diminishing returns. If you are not training models yourself, scaling laws still help you read the field. A claim of a new architecture should be tested for whether its curve is shifted or has a different slope. A change that wins at 100M parameters but loses slope is not a win at 100B. For reasoning, treat test-time compute as a separate budget line: measure accuracy against tokens or samples on your task, and choose the point where the marginal gain no longer pays for the latency and cost.
tradeoffs: Choose compute-optimal training (about 20 tokens per parameter) when training is the main cost: a research model, a one-off model, or a run where you have a fixed cluster-time budget and no serving plan. You give up the option of a cheaper model to serve.
Choose heavy overtraining (hundreds to thousands of tokens per parameter) when the model will serve large traffic, or must fit a device. Total cost is training plus 2N per generated token, and a smaller N wins when inference tokens exceed training tokens by a large factor. You give up some training efficiency: the last tokens reduce loss more slowly than the same compute spent on a bigger model.
Choose more parameters over more data when data is scarce or low in quality, for example a niche language or domain, and when you can repeat data for up to about four epochs with little loss. Choose more data over more parameters when your inference budget is tight. Either way, check the learning-rate schedule, since a bad one hides the real curve.
Choose to spend compute at test time (long reasoning, many samples, search) when the task has a checkable answer and the question is hard; choose a bigger base model when questions are easy and latency matters. Test-time compute scales cost per query and so is expensive at high volume, while training compute is paid once.
Choose to trust a scaling-law prediction for loss, which is smooth and well-behaved; be careful when using it for benchmark accuracy or new abilities. Use a margin of error and plan for a smaller run to check the prediction at an intermediate scale.
interview: What is a scaling law, and how do you fit one in practice? || It is a fitted relation, usually a power law, between loss and a resource such as parameters, tokens or compute. You train a ladder of models of different sizes on different token counts with a tuned schedule, record the final held-out loss, and fit a form like \( L = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}} \) by regression in log space or robust least squares. You then check the prediction on a held-out larger run and report the uncertainty.

Substituting \( D = C/(6N) \) and setting the derivative to zero gives \( N_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)} \) and \( D_{\text{opt}} \propto C^{\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)} \). With α and β close to each other, both exponents are near 0.5, so tokens per parameter stays roughly constant, about 20.

If a model will generate trillions of tokens, inference at 2N FLOPs per token dominates the total, so a smaller model trained on much more data gives the same quality at a lower lifetime cost. Loss keeps falling with more tokens, only more slowly.
Your 7B training run's loss curve is above the scaling-law prediction from your small runs. value: A transformer language model does a fixed amount of computation for each token it writes: one pass through its layers. If you ask a hard question and require the answer in the very next token, all the thinking must fit inside that one pass. For a multi-step problem (arithmetic with carries, a logic puzzle, a bug in code) it often does not fit, and the model gives a fluent, wrong answer. Chain of thought (CoT) fixes this by letting the model write out the intermediate steps before the answer. Each step is a token, and each token buys another full pass of computation. The written text also works as memory: later steps can read what earlier steps found.
This simple change gave large gains. On the GSM8K school maths benchmark, PaLM 540B went from about 18% with ordinary prompting to about 57% when the prompt included worked examples with reasoning steps (Wei et al., 2022). Adding the sentence "Let's think step by step" to a prompt, with no examples, raised a GPT-3 model from about 18% to about 79% on the MultiArith set (Kojima et al., 2022). Later, models were trained to produce long reasoning on their own with reinforcement learning (OpenAI o1 in September 2024, DeepSeek-R1 in January 2025, Claude's extended thinking from February 2025). Accuracy on maths, coding and science tasks rises as the model is allowed to think longer. This made inference-time compute a second axis of scaling, next to training compute.
For the layer above, reasoning models, agents and tool-using assistants are built on this: they plan, check and revise in text before acting. Two lower-layer ideas explain why it works. Probability explains self-consistency: sample several reasoning chains and take the most common answer, and the errors of individual chains average out. Learning theory and computational complexity (L4) explain the capacity argument: a model with a fixed number of layers can compute only a limited class of functions in one pass, and every extra generated token extends the serial computation it can perform.
terms: Chain of thought — a sequence of intermediate reasoning steps written in natural language before the final answer.
Few-shot CoT — a prompt that includes a few worked examples, each showing the steps and then the answer.
Zero-shot CoT — a prompt with no examples that only asks the model to reason step by step.
Self-consistency — sampling several reasoning chains at a nonzero temperature and choosing the most common final answer (a majority vote).
Test-time compute — computation spent when answering a question, mostly the number of generated reasoning tokens or samples.
Reasoning model — a model trained, usually with reinforcement learning on checkable tasks, to produce long reasoning before its answer.
Process reward model — a model that scores each step of a reasoning chain, not only the final answer.
Faithfulness — whether the written reasoning is the real cause of the answer, and not a story made up after the fact.
Thinking budget — a limit on the number of reasoning tokens the model may use for a request.
history: Before language models did it, people showed that intermediate text helps neural networks compute. Nye et al. (Google, 2021) trained transformers to write a "scratchpad" of intermediate steps and found large gains on multi-digit addition and program execution. Cobbe et al. (OpenAI, 2021) built GSM8K, a set of grade-school maths word problems, and trained verifiers to pick the best of many sampled solutions.
Wei et al. (Google Brain, January 2022) showed that you do not have to train for this. A few examples in the prompt, each with the reasoning written out, were enough to unlock multi-step reasoning in large models. They also found that the effect appeared only at large scale: models below roughly 100 billion parameters wrote fluent but illogical chains and did not improve. Kojima et al. (2022) found the zero-shot trigger "Let's think step by step". Wang et al. (2022) added self-consistency, which gave about 18 points of improvement on GSM8K for PaLM 540B on top of CoT. Zelikman et al. (2022, STaR) trained a model on its own correct reasoning chains. Yao et al. (2023) generalised the idea to search over trees of thoughts.
The next question was how to supervise reasoning. Lightman et al. (OpenAI, 2023, "Let's Verify Step by Step") showed that scoring each step (process supervision) beat scoring only the final answer for selecting good solutions. Theory followed: Feng et al. (2023) and Li et al. (2024) proved that (under standard complexity assumptions) transformers of constant depth cannot solve certain serial problems alone but can solve them when allowed enough chain-of-thought tokens.
In September 2024 OpenAI released o1, which was trained with reinforcement learning to produce long hidden reasoning, and reported that accuracy improved smoothly with both training compute and thinking time. Snell et al. (2024) studied how to spend test-time compute best. DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) showed in an open paper that reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards alone, using the GRPO algorithm, can make a base model produce long chains in which it checks its work, backtracks and tries other ideas. Muennighoff et al. (2025, "s1") showed that even a small fine-tuned model can be made to think longer by simply forcing it to continue (appending "Wait") and that accuracy rises with length. Today most major labs offer a reasoning mode with an adjustable budget.
how: A transformer produces one token per forward pass. The pass has a fixed depth (the number of layers, for example 80) and a fixed cost, roughly 2 × N floating-point operations for N parameters. In one pass, information can flow from earlier positions to the current one through attention, but the number of sequential steps of computation is limited by the number of layers. Problems that need more sequential steps than that (for example, following a chain of 10 dependent additions) cannot be solved reliably in a single token.
Chain of thought changes the structure. The model writes a token, and that token becomes an input to the next pass. So the output text is a tape on which the model can store partial results. If each generated token can do a bounded amount of work, then T tokens give T times the sequential depth. Theory makes this precise: with a polynomial number of chain-of-thought tokens, a constant-depth transformer can simulate a general sequential computation, while without them it is limited to a smaller class of problems (circuits of constant depth, TC⁰). This is where learning theory and complexity enter: the capacity of a fixed network is bounded, and the sequence length is the dial that raises the bound.
There are three ways to get a chain. In few-shot prompting you place worked examples in the prompt, and the model imitates the format. In zero-shot prompting you add an instruction such as "think step by step". In reasoning models, the habit is trained in. The model first goes through supervised fine-tuning on examples with reasoning, and then reinforcement learning on tasks with checkable answers (maths with a known result, code with unit tests). The reward is 1 for a correct final answer and 0 otherwise, sometimes with small terms for format. The policy-gradient update raises the probability of whole chains that ended in correct answers. Over thousands of steps, behaviours that help correctness grow: longer reasoning, checking an answer by a second method, and returning to an earlier step after finding an error.
Probability enters through sampling. At a temperature above zero, each sampled chain is a different path, and the final answers form a distribution. Self-consistency draws k chains and returns the most frequent answer. If each chain is correct with probability p = 0.6, and wrong answers are spread over many values, the majority is correct with a higher probability: with 5 chains about 68%, with 11 about 75%, with 21 about 83%. This is the same argument as for an ensemble of independent voters. The gain shrinks when the chains make the same mistake (correlated errors), which is common, because they come from one model. Variants replace the vote with a verifier or a reward model that picks the best chain (best-of-n), or with a search over partial chains guided by a process reward model.
Test-time search goes one step further. Instead of sampling whole chains independently, a system can expand partial chains: at each step it samples a few candidate next steps, scores them with a process reward model, keeps the best few, and continues (a beam or tree search over thoughts). This spends compute where the chain is uncertain. It needs a reliable step scorer, which is hard to train, and many current reasoning models skip explicit search and rely on one long learned chain instead.
A reasoning model's compute at inference is therefore the number of generated tokens times the per-token cost. A long answer of 20,000 reasoning tokens costs 20,000 forward passes, plus the memory for the key-value cache that holds all the earlier tokens. It scales latency and money linearly with length. Providers expose this as a thinking-token budget or an "effort" level, and bill reasoning tokens like output tokens. The quality curve is roughly logarithmic in the number of tokens: each doubling of the thinking budget gives a smaller gain, and for easy questions the gain is zero.
Finally, the written chain does not have to be the real mechanism. Studies (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023) showed that models can give a plausible explanation that omits the true factor, for example a hint in the prompt that changed the answer. Chains help because the tokens carry computation, but what the text says and what the network computes are not guaranteed to match.

example: A word problem: "A shop sells 3 notebooks at ₹45 each and 2 pens at ₹12 each. You pay with ₹200. How much change do you get?" A model asked for the number directly has to compute 200 − (3 × 45 + 2 × 12) inside one pass. With a chain of thought it writes: "Notebooks: 3 × 45 = 135. Pens: 2 × 12 = 24. Total: 135 + 24 = 159. Change: 200 − 159 = 41." Each line is an operation that a single pass can do reliably, and each result is now in the text for later lines to read.
Compute cost. Take a 70-billion-parameter model, which uses about 2 × 70 × 10⁹ = 140 GFLOPs per generated token. The direct answer is about 3 tokens: roughly 0.4 TFLOPs. The chain above has about 60 tokens: about 8.4 TFLOPs, which is 20 times more compute for the same question. For a hard competition-maths problem, a reasoning model may write 20,000 tokens: 2.8 PFLOPs, which is about 7,000 times the direct answer. At a decoding speed of 50 tokens per second, that takes 400 seconds, nearly 7 minutes. So the budget is a real product decision.
Voting arithmetic. Each chain is right with probability 0.6, and wrong answers are scattered. With 5 chains, the majority is correct if at least 3 are right: P = 0.6⁵ + 5 × 0.6⁴ × 0.4 + 10 × 0.6³ × 0.4² = 0.078 + 0.259 + 0.346 = 0.683. With 11 chains it is 0.753, and with 21 chains 0.826. The cost is 5, 11 or 21 times the compute, so the gain per extra sample falls quickly. If the model has a favourite wrong answer that appears in 40% of chains while the right answer appears in 35%, the vote prefers the wrong answer more and more reliably as the number of chains grows. Voting amplifies whichever answer is most likely, right or wrong.
Latency and price sketch. A request with a 2,000-token reasoning chain at 50 tokens per second takes 40 seconds before the answer starts to stream, unless the provider streams a summary. If output tokens cost, say, 15 dollars per million, 2,000 tokens cost 3 cents. At 100,000 such requests per day that is 3,000 dollars per day, which is the sort of number to compare with the value of the accuracy gain.
intuition: Show your working: writing intermediate steps lets the model use its own output as scratch paper, and every extra line of scratch work is extra thinking time.
A person cannot multiply two five-digit numbers in their head in one glance, but can do it on paper in a minute. The paper holds partial results, and each small step is easy. A transformer has the same limit: one glance (one pass) is shallow, but the tokens it writes are a paper that it can read back. Letting it write more tokens is a way to give a fixed network more serial time. Reinforcement learning then teaches it how to use the time: break the problem down, check each part, and try again when something looks wrong.
mistakes: People often think the model writes down its real thought process. In fact the text is generated by the same next-token process as any other text. It often does carry the computation, but studies show cases where the stated reasons omit what really changed the answer, such as a hidden hint, or where the model reaches the answer first and writes a story after. Treat the chain as useful evidence, not as a guaranteed explanation.
People often think more thinking is always better. In fact there are diminishing returns, and very long chains can talk the model out of a correct first answer, repeat themselves, or drift. On easy questions extra reasoning costs money and time with no gain. Good systems adjust the budget to the difficulty.
People often think chain of thought gives a small model the ability of a large one. In fact the early results showed that small models gain little or become worse, because they cannot produce sound steps. Reasoning needs a base model with enough knowledge, and reasoning training builds on it. Smaller models can be taught to reason by distilling chains from a larger one, but the ceiling stays lower.
People often think self-consistency fixes any error by voting. In fact voting helps when errors are independent and the right answer is the most common one. When the model has a systematic misconception, most chains agree on the wrong answer, and more samples only make the wrong answer look more certain.
friction: Cost and latency come first. Reasoning tokens are generated one by one, and they are billed. A system that answers in 1 second without reasoning may take 30 seconds to several minutes with it, and its key-value cache and compute grow with the length. For interactive products you must decide when to reason, and show progress to the user.
The benefit is concentrated in tasks with a clear structure: maths, code, logic, planning, and multi-step analysis. On tasks that depend on recalling a fact, or on style, extra reasoning adds little, and sometimes lowers quality. Rewards for reasoning training exist where an automatic checker exists, so the skill transfers less well to open-ended tasks with no checker, where the reward model is the weak point.
Faithfulness and safety are open problems. If the chain is used to monitor a model's intentions, it is useful only as long as it reflects what the model is doing, and training pressure on the chain itself (to look good) can reduce that. Hidden chains also cannot be audited by users. Further problems are overthinking (spending thousands of tokens on trivial questions), sensitivity to prompt wording in few-shot CoT, sampling cost for self-consistency, and evaluation contamination: public benchmark problems may appear in training data, so scores can overstate real ability.
practice: For a standard model without built-in reasoning, ask it to reason before the final answer, and put the answer in a fixed, parseable format at the end, for example a line "Answer: 41". Give two or three worked examples for tasks with a special structure. Place the reasoning first and the answer last; if you ask for the answer first, the model commits before it has thought, and the explanation becomes a justification.
For reasoning models, do not tell the model how to think step by step; state the task clearly, give the needed context, and set the effort or budget. Start with a low or medium budget and raise it only if accuracy on your evaluation set improves enough to justify the cost. Measure accuracy, latency and cost per request at several budgets and pick the knee of the curve. Use cheaper non-reasoning models for easy traffic and route only hard cases to the reasoning mode.
Use verification where you can. For code, run the tests; for maths, check the result by substitution; for extraction, validate against a schema. Best-of-n with a real checker beats a vote. When you use sampling with voting, use a temperature around 0.7 to 1.0 for diversity, and 5 to 20 samples are usually where most of the gain is. Do not rely on the text of the reasoning as an audit trail for decisions that matter; log it, but test the answer. And if you are building an agent, keep the reasoning short per step and let tools do the exact computation.
tradeoffs: Choose zero-shot or few-shot CoT on a standard model when you need better accuracy on multi-step tasks and can afford 5 to 30 times more output tokens. You give up latency and cost per request, and you depend on prompt wording.
Choose a dedicated reasoning model when tasks are hard (competition maths, difficult code, scientific questions) and have checkable answers. You get the largest gains, but you pay for thousands of reasoning tokens per request and for slower responses. For simple lookups, extraction or chat, a non-reasoning model is cheaper and as accurate.
Choose self-consistency or best-of-n when you can run many requests in parallel and want to trade money for accuracy without changing the model. It adds a factor k in cost, but not in latency if parallel. Choose a single longer chain when the problem requires a deep sequential argument, where parallel samples do not help much, and when voting is hard because answers are free-form text.
Choose a bigger model over more thinking when the task depends on knowledge the small model lacks; choose more thinking over a bigger model when the knowledge is there but the problem needs several steps. Snell et al. found that on easy and medium prompts, extra test-time compute on a small model can beat a model 14 times larger, while on the hardest prompts a larger model is better.
Choose to show or hide the reasoning depending on the product. Showing it builds trust and helps debugging but exposes content you may not want to expose, and users may take it as a true explanation. Hiding it protects the product but loses transparency.

value: A language model does not output text. At each step it outputs a probability for every token in its vocabulary (32,000 to 256,000 entries): "the next token is 'the' with 3%, 'a' with 2%, ..." Something outside the network has to pick one token, append it to the text, and run the model again. That outside step is decoding. It is a small piece of code, but it decides whether the answer is precise or creative, repetitive or varied, valid JSON or broken JSON.
The same trained model gives very different behaviour under different decoding settings. Greedy decoding always takes the most likely token and gives stable but sometimes dull and looping text. Sampling with temperature 1 gives varied text but now and then picks a bad token, and one bad token early in an answer can derail everything after it. Top-p, top-k and min-p cut off the unlikely tail. Beam search keeps several candidate sequences and is standard in translation. Constrained decoding goes further: at each step it removes every token that would break a grammar, so the output is guaranteed to parse.
For the layer above, every product built on an LLM relies on this step: chat assistants (sampling), code completion (low temperature), extraction pipelines (structured output), agents (tool calls that must be valid JSON), and reasoning models (sampling many chains). Two lower-layer ideas make decoding precise. Probability gives the objects: the softmax distribution over tokens, and the operations on it (rescaling, truncating, renormalizing, sampling). Information theory gives the language to describe the effect: temperature changes the entropy of the distribution, which is the number of bits of surprise per token, and entropy tells you how random the output will be.
terms: Logits — the raw scores the model outputs for each token before the softmax. Softmax turns them into probabilities that sum to 1.
Greedy decoding — always choose the token with the highest probability.
Temperature T — a number that divides the logits before the softmax. \( T < 1 \) sharpens the distribution; \( T > 1 \) flattens it; \( T \to 0 \) is greedy.
Top-k — keep only the k most likely tokens, set the rest to zero probability, renormalize, then sample.
Top-p (nucleus) — keep the smallest set of top tokens whose probabilities add up to at least p, renormalize, then sample.
Min-p — keep tokens whose probability is at least a fraction (for example 0.05) of the top token's probability.
Beam search — keep the B best partial sequences by total log-probability at each step, and expand each of them.
Repetition penalty — a change to the logits that lowers the score of tokens that already appeared.
Constrained (guided) decoding — setting the probability of every token that violates a grammar or schema to zero at each step.
Entropy — the average surprise of a distribution in bits; \( H = -\sum p \log_2 p \). It is high when many tokens are plausible.
history: Decoding began in speech recognition and machine translation, where the goal was to find the single most probable output. Exact search over all sequences is impossible (the number of sequences grows as vocabulary size to the power of length), so systems used beam search, a pruned breadth-first search. Sutskever et al. (2014) used beam search with a small beam, as few as 1 to 2 hypotheses, for neural translation, and the Transformer paper (Vaswani et al., 2017) used beam size 4 with a length penalty of 0.6.
Open-ended text brought a different problem. Fan, Lewis and Dauphin (2018) found that for story generation, beam search made text bland and repetitive, and sampling from the full distribution made it incoherent; they proposed top-k sampling. Holtzman et al. (2019, published at ICLR 2020), in "The Curious Case of Neural Text Degeneration", showed that the highest-probability text is not human-like: people do not always say the most likely thing. They introduced nucleus (top-p) sampling, which adapts the cutoff to the shape of the distribution: few tokens when the model is confident, many when it is not. Meister et al. (2022) proposed typical sampling, based on information theory, which prefers tokens whose surprise is close to the distribution's entropy. Nguyen et al. (2024) proposed min-p, which scales the cutoff by the top probability.
In parallel, engineers made decoding faster and more controlled. Leviathan et al. (Google, 2022) and Chen et al. (DeepMind, 2023) introduced speculative decoding: a small model drafts several tokens and the large model verifies them in one pass, with an acceptance rule that keeps the output distribution exactly the same as sampling from the large model. Grammar-constrained decoding appeared in llama.cpp (GBNF grammars, 2023) and in the Outlines library (Willard and Louf, 2023), which compiles a regular expression or JSON schema into a finite-state machine and builds the token masks in advance. XGrammar (2024) extended this to context-free grammars with pushdown automata and low overhead. Hosted APIs added "structured outputs" modes from 2023 to 2024.
how: At step t, the model reads the prompt and the tokens generated so far and outputs a vector of logits z with one entry per vocabulary token. Decoding turns z into one chosen token and repeats. The common pipeline is: (1) apply penalties and masks to the logits, (2) divide by the temperature, (3) convert to probabilities with the softmax, (4) truncate with top-k, top-p or min-p and renormalize, (5) sample one token, (6) append it and go to step 1. Libraries differ in the exact order of the steps, which matters for the result.
Temperature is the main dial. The softmax with temperature is \( p_i = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)} \). As T falls toward 0, the largest logit dominates and the distribution approaches a one-hot vector, so sampling approaches greedy decoding. At T = 1 you sample from the model's own distribution. As T rises, the distribution flattens toward uniform. In information terms, the entropy of the distribution grows with T: low entropy means predictable text, high entropy means varied and eventually random text. This is the same form as the Boltzmann distribution of physics, with the logits playing the role of negative energies.
Truncation fixes the tail problem. A vocabulary of 128,000 tokens has a long tail of tokens that are individually very unlikely, but together they may hold 2% to 5% of the probability at a confident step. With plain sampling, each step has that chance of picking a tail token, and over a 500-token answer you expect several such tokens, any of which can derail the sentence. Top-k removes all but the k best, but a fixed k is wrong at both ends: when the model is certain, k = 50 still allows 49 bad choices; when it is uncertain (the start of a story), k = 50 may cut good options. Top-p solves this by working on probability mass: if the top token has 0.95, the nucleus for p = 0.9 is that single token; if the mass is spread out, it keeps dozens. Min-p works similarly by using the top probability as a reference, and behaves well at high temperatures, where the distribution has been flattened.
Greedy decoding and beam search aim at the most probable sequence. Greedy is a local choice; the best word now may lead to a poor sentence. Beam search with width B keeps B partial sequences, each ranked by the sum of log-probabilities, extends every one by every token, and keeps the best B. Because longer sequences have lower total probability, a length penalty is needed. Beam search suits tasks with a nearly unique right answer, such as translation or summarisation of short texts. For open-ended generation it tends to produce generic, repetitive text.
Speculative decoding is a speed technique that keeps the sampling distribution. A small draft model q proposes k tokens. The large target model p scores all k positions in one forward pass, which costs about the same as one normal step because decoding is limited by memory bandwidth, not compute. For each drafted token, accept it with probability min(1, p(x)/q(x)); at the first rejection, sample a replacement from the normalized positive part of p − q and stop. This procedure produces tokens that are exactly distributed as if the large model had sampled them, with a speedup of typically 2 to 3 times when the draft is good.
Constrained decoding adds a correctness guarantee. Take a JSON schema. A compiler turns it into an automaton (a state machine) that knows, in each state, which characters may come next. Because the model emits tokens, which may contain several characters, the compiler maps each automaton state to the set of vocabulary tokens that keep the output valid. At each step the decoder sets the logits of all other tokens to \( -\infty \), so their probability after the softmax is exactly 0, and then samples as usual. The model still chooses among the valid tokens. The cost is a table lookup per step, plus a one-time compile (which can take from milliseconds to seconds for large grammars and vocabularies).
Probability is what makes all of these steps legal. Truncation followed by renormalization defines a new distribution; a mask is a conditional distribution given the grammar; speculative sampling is a rejection-sampling scheme. Information theory is the guide to what each change does: lowering the temperature and truncating the tail reduce entropy, trading diversity for reliability.

example: Suppose the model is completing "Today the weather is ___" and gives these logits to six candidate tokens: sunny 4.0, cloudy 3.0, rainy 1.0, hot 0.5, nice 0.0, odd −1.0. Take T = 1 first. The exponentials are 54.6, 20.1, 2.72, 1.65, 1.00, 0.37; their sum is 80.4. So the probabilities are 0.679, 0.250, 0.034, 0.021, 0.012 and 0.005. The entropy is about 1.27 bits.
Now change the temperature. At T = 0.5 the logits double to 8, 6, 2, 1, 0, −2, and the probabilities become 0.878, 0.119, 0.002, 0.001, 0.000, 0.000. The top choice is now almost certain, and the entropy drops to 0.56 bits. At T = 2 the logits halve and the probabilities become 0.450, 0.273, 0.100, 0.078, 0.061, 0.037. The entropy rises to 2.07 bits, and "odd" is picked about 4 times in 100.
Next, truncation at T = 1. Top-k with k = 3 keeps sunny, cloudy and rainy, with total 0.963, and after renormalization they have 0.705, 0.260 and 0.035. Top-p with p = 0.9: sunny alone has 0.679, and adding cloudy gives 0.929, which is at least 0.9, so the nucleus is {sunny, cloudy}; renormalized they get 0.731 and 0.269. Min-p with 0.1: the cutoff is 0.1 × 0.679 = 0.068, and only sunny and cloudy pass. In this example top-p and min-p give the same result. When the distribution is flat, for example when the top token has 0.10, top-p keeps many tokens and min-p keeps all those above 0.01.
Beam search on a tiny case. With beam width 2, step 1 keeps "sunny" (log 0.679 = −0.39) and "cloudy" (log 0.250 = −1.39). At step 2 each is extended. Suppose "and" has probability 0.30 after "sunny", while after "cloudy" the model is nearly sure of "and" with probability 0.95. Scores: sunny-and = −0.39 + (−1.20) = −1.59; cloudy-and = −1.39 + (−0.05) = −1.44. Greedy decoding would have taken "sunny" at step 1 and never seen the better total for "cloudy". The beam kept both and finds the sequence with the higher total probability. This is the kind of case where beam search beats greedy.
Cost of constraints. A 128,000-token vocabulary needs a mask of 128,000 bits, 16 KB, per automaton state. With precomputed masks, applying one costs microseconds. Compiling a large JSON schema grammar over the full vocabulary used to take seconds with naive methods, which is why libraries cache the result and why grammar engines compute masks lazily.
intuition: The model proposes a ranked list of next words with odds; decoding is the rule for choosing, like deciding whether to always order the most popular dish or to roll a die weighted by popularity.
A model is a map of what could come next and how likely each option is. Choosing the single most likely option every time sounds safe but locks the text into the most average path, and it is not even the best path overall. Choosing by the raw odds follows the model faithfully but allows the rare bad pick. Temperature and truncation are two simple controls over how much risk to take, and a grammar mask is a hard rule about what is allowed at all.
mistakes: People often think temperature 0 makes the output deterministic. In fact it removes the randomness of sampling, but real servers can still return different text for the same prompt. The reason found in practice is that the numerical result of a computation depends on the batch size and on how the kernels are scheduled, and the load on the server changes the batch. Careful implementations with batch-invariant kernels make the output reproducible, but standard serving usually does not.
People often think a higher temperature makes the model smarter or more creative. In fact it only moves probability from likely tokens to unlikely ones. A bit of it helps variety; too much causes mistakes. For tasks with one correct answer (maths, code, extraction), low temperature is usually better, and diversity is obtained by sampling several answers and choosing or voting.
People often think top-k and top-p do the same thing. In fact top-k has a fixed number of candidates, while top-p has a fixed probability mass and so adapts to how confident the model is. When the model is sure, top-p can leave a single token, whereas top-k will still allow k options.
People often think constrained decoding only costs time. In fact it can also change quality. A mask forces the model to follow a format even if it would have chosen a token outside the format, and if the schema asks for fields the model lacks information about, it will make up values. Putting a field for reasoning before the answer in the schema helps.
People sometimes think beam search always finds a better answer than sampling. In fact "more probable" is not "better" for open-ended text, and wide beams can give short or repetitive outputs. Beam search helps only when the task has a nearly unique target.
friction: Randomness makes testing hard. Two runs of the same prompt differ, so evaluations need many samples or fixed seeds, and even then different hardware or batch sizes can change results. Many hosted reasoning models restrict or fix some sampling parameters, so your control may be limited by the provider.
Low temperature and greedy decoding can fall into loops, repeating a phrase until the length limit, because after a repeated phrase the most probable next token is a repeat. Repetition penalties help but also punish legitimate repeats (names, code identifiers, numbers). High temperature or no truncation raises hallucination and breaks formats. The settings interact: a high temperature with top-p near 1 is very different from a high temperature with min-p.
Constraints limit what the model can say and can reduce accuracy when the format conflicts with how the model wants to reason; they also need a compile step and a good tokenizer match, because a grammar over characters must be mapped to multi-character tokens, and some tokenizations are awkward. Speculative decoding gives gains that depend on how often the draft is accepted, and it adds memory and complexity. Finally, sampling hides problems: a model with a wrong top choice can look better under sampling because the right answer occasionally appears, so measure with the settings you will use in production.
practice: For tasks with one right answer (extraction, classification, code, maths without voting), use temperature 0 or a low value such as 0.2, and do not use a high top-k. For chat and writing, a typical starting point is temperature 0.7 to 1.0 with top-p around 0.9 to 0.95, or min-p around 0.05 with a higher temperature. Change one parameter at a time; most providers advise adjusting temperature or top-p, not both.
When the output must be machine-readable, use the provider's structured-output mode or a grammar-constrained engine (Outlines, XGrammar, llama.cpp grammars, vLLM guided decoding), not a request in the prompt alone plus a parser and a retry. Keep schemas simple, put a free-text reasoning field before the final fields when accuracy matters, and still validate the result with a parser.
For testing, record the model version and all settings, run each test case several times, and report rates, not single outputs. If you need reproducibility, set a seed where supported, use temperature 0, and know that exact equality is still not guaranteed on shared servers. For quality gains on hard problems, sample several answers and vote or rank, rather than lowering temperature to zero.
For serving cost, use speculative decoding or multi-token prediction heads when latency matters and the output is predictable (code, structured text). For long outputs, add a maximum length and a stop sequence. Log the temperature and top-p with each request, because a change in defaults is a common hidden reason for a change in behaviour after a model upgrade.
tradeoffs: Choose greedy or temperature near 0 when you need repeatable, precise answers and can accept some dullness and the risk of loops. You give up diversity and the ability to get a second opinion by sampling again.
Choose sampling with top-p or min-p when you want natural, varied text, as in chat or creative tasks. You give up exact repeatability and accept a small rate of poor choices. Choose min-p over top-p when you use high temperatures (above 1), as it keeps coherence better; choose top-p when you want a setting that most providers and libraries support.
Choose beam search when the output is short and nearly unique (translation, speech recognition, summarisation of a known text) and you can pay B times the compute per step. Choose sampling for open-ended generation, where beams produce repetitive text.
Choose constrained decoding when a downstream parser or API call must not fail, and when failure costs more than a few percent of latency. Choose prompting plus validation and retry when the schema is large or changes often, or when you cannot control the decoder (a closed API). You give up some quality under hard constraints, or you pay retries in the other case.
Choose speculative decoding when you serve a large model at low batch sizes and output is predictable; you pay with memory for the draft model and an engineering effort. At high batch sizes the hardware is already busy and the gain shrinks.
interview: What does temperature do mathematically, and what happens at the extremes? || It divides the logits by T before the softmax: \( p_i \propto \exp(z_i/T) \). As \( T \to 0 \) the largest logit takes all the probability, which is greedy decoding. At T = 1 you sample from the model's distribution. As \( T \to \infty \) the distribution becomes uniform. Entropy rises with T.
Why is top-p usually better than top-k? || Top-k uses a fixed number of candidates regardless of the model's confidence: too many when the model is sure, too few when it is unsure. Top-p chooses the smallest set holding a given probability mass, so it adapts: one token when the distribution is peaked, dozens when it is flat. It cuts the low-probability tail without removing valid options.
Explain how speculative decoding keeps the output distribution unchanged. || A draft model q proposes tokens; the target model p scores them in one pass. Each draft token x is accepted with probability min(1, p(x)/q(x)). At the first rejection, sample from the normalized max(0, p − q) and discard the remaining draft. This is rejection sampling, which makes the accepted tokens exactly distributed as p. Speed comes from verifying several tokens per target pass.
How does constrained decoding guarantee valid JSON, and what does it cost? || A schema is compiled to an automaton. At each state, the set of vocabulary tokens that keep the text valid is known, and the decoder sets all other logits to \( -\infty \) before sampling. Validity holds by construction. Costs are a compile step, a mask lookup per token, possible quality loss if the format forces unnatural tokens, and the need to handle multi-character tokens correctly.
Your pipeline gives different outputs for the same prompt at temperature 0. How do you investigate? || Check that the provider really uses greedy decoding and that seed and top-p are not interfering. Then consider numerical nondeterminism: results depend on batch size and kernel choices, and server load changes the batch. Test with a single request on an idle server; use batch-invariant kernels or a local deployment if you need bitwise repeatability, or make the pipeline robust to small variations.
quiz: In what order does a typical sampler apply temperature, truncation and sampling? || Apply penalties and masks to the logits, divide by the temperature, compute the softmax, truncate with top-k, top-p or min-p and renormalize, then sample one token. Library orders can differ slightly.
Why does lowering the temperature make the output more repetitive? || It sharpens the distribution toward the top token and lowers the entropy. At T near 0 the model always picks the most probable token, and after a repeated phrase the most probable next token is often another repeat, so loops can form.
Why is the nucleus (top-p) cutoff adaptive? || It keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p. When the model is confident that set is tiny, and when the model is unsure the set is large, so the number of candidates follows the shape of the distribution.
With logits [4, 3, 1, 0.5, 0, −1] at T = 1, what are the top-p (p = 0.9) candidates and their renormalized probabilities? || The probabilities are 0.679, 0.250, 0.034, ...; the first token alone is 0.679 < 0.9, and the first two sum to 0.929 ≥ 0.9. So the nucleus is the top two tokens, with probabilities 0.679/0.929 = 0.731 and 0.250/0.929 = 0.269.
You need an LLM to call an API with strictly valid JSON, and latency is important. What do you do? || Use constrained decoding with the JSON schema (a provider's structured output mode, or Outlines or XGrammar on your own server), with low temperature, and compile and cache the grammar once. Keep the schema small, put a reasoning field first if accuracy matters, and still validate the output as a safety net.

@@ contrastive
value: Many of the most useful datasets come without labels, but with natural pairs: an image and its caption, a search query and the page the user clicked, two crops of the same photo, a question and its answer. Contrastive learning turns such pairs into a training signal without any manual labelling. It trains an encoder to map each item to a vector, so that the two items in a pair get vectors that point in similar directions, and items from different pairs get vectors that do not. After training, "similar meaning" becomes "small distance", and you can search, classify or compare by geometry alone.
The best-known result is CLIP (OpenAI, 2021). It trained an image encoder and a text encoder on 400 million image-caption pairs from the web. Without being trained on any ImageNet label, it classified ImageNet images by comparing the image vector with the vectors of the sentences "a photo of a {class}", and reached 76.2% top-1 accuracy, equal to the supervised ResNet-50 that was the standard baseline. The same recipe now produces text embedding models for search and retrieval-augmented generation, the vision encoders in multimodal LLMs, and the two-tower models that rank items in recommendation and ad systems.
For the layer above, contrastive training is how modalities are connected (multimodal models, computer vision) and how retrieval systems get their embeddings. Two lower-layer ideas matter. Geometry and metric spaces (L4) give the target: a space with a notion of distance or angle, usually the unit sphere with cosine similarity, in which "closer" means "more related". Information theory (L4) gives the loss: InfoNCE is a lower bound on the mutual information between the two views of a pair, so the training objective has a precise meaning, not just a heuristic one.
terms: Embedding — a vector of a few hundred to a few thousand numbers that represents an item (image, sentence, user) so that similar items have nearby vectors.
Positive pair — two items that should be close, such as an image and its own caption.
Negative — an item that should be far from the anchor. In most setups the other items in the same batch serve as negatives.
Cosine similarity — the dot product of two vectors after scaling each to length 1; it equals the cosine of the angle between them, from −1 to 1.
InfoNCE loss — a cross-entropy loss that asks the model to identify the true partner of an anchor among N candidates (one positive and N − 1 negatives).
Temperature τ — a scale applied to similarities before the softmax; a small τ makes the loss focus on the hardest negatives.
Two-tower model — two separate encoders (for example image and text, or user and item) trained so that their outputs can be compared by dot product.
Hard negative — a wrong item that looks similar to the right one and so gives a strong learning signal.
False negative — an item treated as a negative that is in fact related to the anchor; it pushes the model in the wrong direction.
Zero-shot classification — classifying without task-specific training, by comparing an input's embedding with embeddings of the class descriptions.
history: The first contrastive losses came from metric learning. Hadsell, Chopra and LeCun (2006) trained a network so that pairs of similar inputs were pulled together and dissimilar ones pushed beyond a margin. FaceNet (Schroff et al., 2015) used a triplet loss: an anchor face, a positive of the same person and a negative of another, with the positive required to be closer by a margin. Choosing good triplets was hard, which limited it. In language, word2vec (2013) trained word vectors with negative sampling, a close relative: raise the score of true word-context pairs, lower the score of random ones.
Sohn (2016) proposed the N-pair loss, using all other examples in a batch as negatives, and van den Oord, Li and Vinyals (DeepMind, 2018) introduced the InfoNCE loss with Contrastive Predictive Coding and showed that it is a lower bound on mutual information. This linked contrastive learning to the information theory view. Between 2019 and 2020, self-supervised vision took off: MoCo (He et al.) kept a queue of recent embeddings as negatives and a slowly moving copy of the encoder; SimCLR (Chen et al., Google, 2020) showed that strong augmentations, a nonlinear projection head and very large batches (4,096 to 8,192) were enough. BYOL (2020) and SimSiam showed that you can even drop the negatives with other tricks to avoid collapse.
For language and retrieval, Karpukhin et al. (2020, DPR) trained dual encoders for passage retrieval with in-batch negatives and a few hard negatives from BM25, beating BM25 itself on open-domain question answering. Then CLIP (Radford et al., January 2021) and ALIGN (Jia et al., Google, 2021, 1.8 billion noisy pairs) applied the idea to image-text pairs at web scale. CLIP's training used a batch size of 32,768 pairs. After CLIP, nearly every vision-language system used a contrastively trained encoder. Zhai et al. (Google, 2023) proposed SigLIP, which replaced the batch-wide softmax by an independent sigmoid loss for each pair, which made training with very large batches simpler. Text embedding models (E5, BGE, GTE and commercial APIs) are trained in a similar way on large sets of text pairs.
how: Start with a dataset of N pairs (x_i, y_i) per batch, for example images and their captions. Two encoders produce vectors: \( u_i = f(x_i) \) and \( v_i = g(y_i) \). Both are scaled to length 1 (L2 normalization), so that the dot product of two vectors is their cosine similarity. The geometry here is the unit sphere: all embeddings lie on it, and distance is the angle between points.
Compute the \( N \times N \) matrix of similarities \( S_{ij} = u_i \cdot v_j \). The diagonal holds the true pairs; the off-diagonal entries are the \( N^2 - N \) mismatched pairs, which are all treated as negatives. Divide each entry by the temperature τ. For image i, the loss is the cross-entropy of a softmax across row i, with the label being the diagonal entry: \( L_i = -\log \frac{\exp(S_{ii} / \tau)}{\sum_j \exp(S_{ij} / \tau)} \). That is a classification problem with N classes, where the correct class is the partner. CLIP also does the same for columns (find the right image for each caption) and averages the two losses. This is the InfoNCE loss.
Why this is related to information. If the pair (x, y) share information, then identifying the right partner among N candidates is possible only if the embeddings keep that shared information. van den Oord et al. showed that \( I(x; y) \ge \log N - L_{\text{InfoNCE}} \). So minimizing the loss maximizes a lower bound on the mutual information. The bound can never exceed \( \log N \): with N = 32,768 it saturates at ln 32768 ≈ 10.4 nats (15 bits). This explains why larger batches help: they raise the ceiling of what the bound can measure and supply more, and harder, negatives.
The temperature controls what the model pays attention to. With a large τ, all negatives count roughly equally and the model learns coarse structure. With a small τ, the softmax is dominated by the most similar negatives, so the loss focuses on hard negatives and learns fine differences, but it also becomes sensitive to false negatives and noise. CLIP initializes τ = 0.07 and learns it as a parameter. The loss also has a useful geometric interpretation (Wang and Isola, 2020): it encourages alignment (positives are close) and uniformity (all embeddings spread over the sphere, not collapsed to a point).
Negatives are the cost driver. The similarity matrix for a batch of 32,768 has 1.07 billion entries. CLIP computes it in a distributed way: each GPU computes the embeddings for its own slice of the batch, the embeddings (small, for example 32,768 × 512 × 2 bytes = 32 MB) are gathered across GPUs, and each GPU computes only its own rows of the matrix, so the large matrix is never stored in one place. SigLIP's sigmoid loss treats each entry of the matrix as an independent yes-or-no question, with a label of +1 on the diagonal and −1 elsewhere, and so needs no global normalization across the batch. MoCo's alternative is a queue of thousands of past embeddings from a momentum encoder, giving many negatives without a large batch.
When the data has only one modality, the pair is built by augmentation. SimCLR takes one image and makes two random views of it (a random crop, colour change, blur). The two views are positives, and views of other images in the batch are negatives. The augmentations define what the model should ignore: if colour is randomly changed, the embedding learns to be invariant to colour. A small extra network, the projection head, sits between the encoder and the loss, and is discarded after training; the loss works on its output, but the features before the head transfer better to other tasks.
After training, the encoders are used without the loss. For retrieval, you embed all items offline into an index, embed a query online, and find nearest neighbours by cosine similarity (using an approximate nearest-neighbour index such as HNSW). For zero-shot classification, you embed class names in a template sentence and take the class with the highest similarity to the image. For transfer, you freeze the encoder and train a small linear classifier on top (a linear probe), or use the encoder as the vision part of a larger multimodal model. In recommendation and ads, two-tower models embed users and items separately and score by dot product, so that item embeddings can be indexed in advance; the in-batch softmax is used with a correction for the fact that popular items appear more often as negatives.

Row 2: logits [1.1, 9.9, 8.0], exponentials 3.0, 19,930, 2,981, probability 19,930 / 22,914 = 0.870, loss 0.14. The information bound: log 3 = 1.10 nats, so \( I(x; y) \ge 1.10 - 0.126 \approx 0.97 \) nats for this batch. With the same quality of match but N = 32,768, the ceiling would be 10.4 nats. Effect of temperature. Use τ = 1.0 instead of 0.1 on row 3: the logits are [0.78, 0.78, 0.99]. The exponentials are 2.18, 2.18, 2.69, so the probability of the right caption is 2.69 / 7.05 = 0.38, and the loss is 0.96. The same embeddings are now penalised much more, and the gradient is spread over the two negatives equally. With τ = 0.1 the loss for the same row was 0.22, because the margin of 0.21 in cosine similarity became 2.1 in logits. A smaller τ makes margins matter more. Too small a τ, such as 0.01, would make the loss for any mislabelled pair huge.
Scale check for a retrieval system. A corpus of 100 million passages with 768-dimensional embeddings in 16-bit precision takes 100 × 10⁶ × 768 × 2 bytes = 154 GB. A brute-force search against one query costs 100 million dot products of length 768, about 77 billion multiply-adds: fine on a GPU, too slow for a CPU at high query rates. So the index uses approximate nearest-neighbour search (HNSW or IVF with product quantization), which cuts the work by 100 to 1,000 times with a small loss in recall, and can compress vectors 8 to 32 times.
intuition: Learn what matches by seeing many things that do not, like learning to recognise your friend's face by being surrounded by a crowd of strangers.
Two items that belong together share some information that other items lack. To make an embedding keep exactly that information, the simplest test is a matching game: given a thing, pick its partner from a lineup. The only way to win reliably is to encode what makes the partner unique. Bigger and harder lineups force finer distinctions. Putting everything on a sphere with an angle as the distance means that the learned space has a shape you can use directly: nearest-neighbour search, clustering and linear classifiers all become simple geometry.
mistakes: People often think contrastive learning needs labels. In fact it needs only a definition of which items belong together, and that can come from data structure: captions next to images, two crops of one image, a query and its click. The cost moves from labelling to designing or finding good pairs.
People often think the embedding space captures meaning in general. In fact it captures what the pairs taught it. If captions rarely mention colour or counting, CLIP-style models are weak at them. Research on the "modality gap" (Liang et al., 2022) shows that image and text vectors from CLIP sit in two separate regions of the sphere, rather than mixed together; cosine ranking works inside that structure, but absolute similarity values are not comparable across tasks.
People often think more negatives are always better. In fact, more random negatives help up to a point, but the gain comes from hard negatives. Too hard negatives may be false negatives: another photo of the same dog, or a different caption that also describes the image. They push the model to separate things that should be close. Careful systems filter or down-weight such pairs.
People often think the temperature is a minor constant. In fact it changes what is learned. Small τ concentrates the gradient on the hardest negatives; large τ spreads it. CLIP learns it, and many text embedding recipes tune it carefully (values near 0.01 to 0.05 are common).
People sometimes think cosine similarity of two embeddings gives a probability or a calibrated score. In fact it is a ranking signal. A similarity of 0.8 means different things for different models and domains, so thresholds must be set on your own data.
friction: Contrastive training depends on batch size and negatives. Getting thousands of negatives needs many accelerators with all-gather communication, a queue with a momentum encoder, or a sigmoid loss. The quality of pairs sets the ceiling: web alt-text is noisy, so CLIP-scale systems invest heavily in data filtering, and biases in the pairs (stereotypes, missing groups, language imbalance) are learned and reproduced in downstream use.
Embeddings are opaque and lose detail. A single vector per image or passage cannot hold everything; fine-grained attributes, counts, spatial relations and negation ("a dog not on a sofa") are poorly captured. Text embedding models can match words about a topic but fail at exact identifiers, numbers or rare names, so many search systems combine dense vectors with keyword search. The space is also tied to the model: embeddings from two different models or two versions of one model cannot be compared, so a model upgrade means re-embedding the entire corpus (a heavy cost with billions of items).
Other issues are dimensional collapse (embeddings occupy a small subspace if the loss or augmentations are poorly chosen), domain shift (a model trained on web photos works worse on medical images or ad creatives), and long-tail items with few examples. Vector indexes add their own costs: memory (hundreds of GB for hundreds of millions of items), and recall that falls as you push for lower latency.
practice: If you need embeddings for search, recommendation or classification, first try a strong pre-trained model (an open text embedding model, or CLIP/SigLIP for images) and measure recall on your own data. Fine-tune with contrastive loss only if the off-the-shelf model falls short, using pairs from your own logs: query and clicked item, item and co-purchased item, question and the document that answered it.
For fine-tuning, use in-batch negatives with the largest batch your memory allows (gradient caching helps), add a few mined hard negatives per example, and watch out for false negatives, for example by removing candidates that rank above the positive by a wide margin. Start with τ near 0.05, or learn it. Normalize embeddings, and evaluate with recall@k and nDCG on a held-out set that matches real queries, not only the training loss.
For the serving side, pick the index by scale: exact search up to about a million vectors, HNSW for tens of millions, IVF with product quantization when memory is the limit. Use lower-dimensional or quantized vectors where the quality loss is small (Matryoshka-trained embeddings allow cutting the dimension). Combine dense and keyword search with a reranker for high precision. Version the embedding model with the index, and plan re-embedding as part of every model upgrade.
In ad tech and recommendation, a two-tower model trained with an in-batch softmax gives user and item embeddings for fast candidate retrieval; correct for the popularity bias of in-batch negatives (log-frequency correction), and keep fresh items represented by content embeddings so that they are not ignored.
tradeoffs: Choose contrastive learning when you have naturally paired data and no labels, and you need embeddings for retrieval, matching or zero-shot transfer. You give up the fine control of a supervised task, and you depend on pair quality and negative sampling.
Choose a dual encoder (two towers) when you need to search millions of items in milliseconds: items are embedded offline and only the query is encoded online. Choose a cross-encoder (query and item processed together) when you need the best ranking quality on a short list of 20 to 200 candidates. The cross-encoder is much more accurate, but costs one model pass per candidate and cannot be indexed.
Choose the softmax InfoNCE loss with large batches when you have many accelerators and want the best-known recipe; choose a queue with a momentum encoder (MoCo) or the sigmoid loss (SigLIP) when memory or batch size is limited. SigLIP gives stable training and works well at both small and large batch sizes; InfoNCE gives a direct mutual-information interpretation.
Choose a small temperature (0.01 to 0.05) when the data is clean and you want fine separation; choose a larger one (0.1 or more) when pairs are noisy or have many false negatives. Choose higher dimensions (768 to 1536) for quality, lower (128 to 256) or quantized vectors when index memory and latency dominate; you lose a few points of recall.
Choose a general pre-trained embedding with fine-tuning on your pairs when the domain is specialised (legal, medical, ad creatives); choose an off-the-shelf model unchanged when you have little data and the domain is general.
interview: Explain the InfoNCE loss and what the negatives do. || For an anchor, you compute similarities to its true partner and to N − 1 other items, scale by \( 1/\tau \), and apply softmax cross-entropy with the partner as the label. The negatives define what the embedding must distinguish; with few or easy negatives the task is trivial and the embedding learns little. The loss gives a lower bound \( I(x;y) \ge \log N - L \), so larger N makes the bound tighter and the task harder.
Why does CLIP need a large batch, and what are alternatives when you cannot afford it? || The batch supplies the negatives, and more negatives, especially hard ones, force finer distinctions and raise the information bound log N. Alternatives are a memory queue with a momentum encoder (MoCo), gradient caching to enlarge the effective batch, mined hard negatives, or the sigmoid loss of SigLIP, which evaluates pairs independently.
What does the temperature do, and what happens if it is too small or too large? || It scales similarities before the softmax. A small τ concentrates the gradient on hard negatives, giving fine separation but sensitivity to false negatives and noise. A large τ treats all negatives alike, leading to coarse structure and weak separation. It can be learned or tuned, typically 0.01 to 0.1.
Your two-tower retrieval model trained with in-batch negatives under-recommends popular items. Why, and what do you do? || In-batch negatives are drawn in proportion to item popularity, so popular items appear as negatives far more often than they would under uniform sampling, and the model learns to push them down. Fix this by subtracting the log of each item's sampling probability from its logit (logQ correction), mixing in uniformly sampled negatives, and monitoring recall by popularity bucket.
When would you choose a cross-encoder over a bi-encoder? || When the candidate set is small (the top 50 to 200 from a first-stage retrieval) and ranking quality matters more than cost. A cross-encoder reads the query and item jointly and is much more accurate, but it needs one forward pass per pair and cannot be precomputed. The standard pattern is a bi-encoder for retrieval followed by a cross-encoder reranker.
quiz: What do positives and negatives mean in a CLIP batch? || A positive is an image with its own caption (the diagonal of the similarity matrix); every other caption (or image) in the batch is a negative for it.
Why are embeddings normalized to unit length in contrastive learning? || Then the dot product equals the cosine similarity, which depends only on the angle. All points lie on a sphere, so the model cannot reduce the loss by changing the length of vectors, and distance has a simple geometric meaning.
Why does the InfoNCE loss relate to mutual information, and what limits the bound? || It satisfies \( I(x;y) \ge \log N - L \), so minimizing L maximizes a lower bound on the mutual information of the pair. The bound cannot be larger than \( \log N \), so a small batch limits what can be measured and learned.

value: A variational autoencoder (VAE) is a neural network that learns two things at once: a compact code for each data item (an image, an audio clip, a molecule), and a way to produce realistic new items from such codes. An ordinary autoencoder also compresses and reconstructs, but its code space has holes: a random code usually decodes to garbage. The VAE adds a probabilistic twist. The encoder does not output one code, it outputs a small distribution of codes, and training pushes all those distributions toward a simple shared one (a standard Gaussian). As a result, any point sampled from that simple distribution decodes to something plausible, and nearby codes decode to similar items.
Direct image generators based on VAEs are rarely used now, because their samples tend to be blurry. The VAE found a more important role as a compressor. Latent diffusion models such as Stable Diffusion use a VAE to turn a 512 × 512 × 3 image (786,432 numbers) into a 64 × 64 × 4 latent (16,384 numbers), 48 times smaller. The expensive diffusion process then runs on the small latent, and the VAE decoder turns the result back into pixels. The same pattern is used for video, audio and other modalities. The discrete variant, VQ-VAE, turns images into a grid of integer tokens, which a transformer can model like text.
Three lower-layer ideas make the VAE work. Probability gives the model: a latent-variable model \( p(x) = \int p(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz \), and the notion of an approximate posterior \( q(z \mid x) \). Convexity and Jensen's inequality (L4) give the training objective: the integral is intractable, but Jensen's inequality turns \( \log p(x) \) into a lower bound that can be computed. Backpropagation (L2) trains the networks, and the reparameterization trick makes it possible to pass gradients through the random sampling step.
terms: Latent variable z — a hidden, low-dimensional description of the data (for example 64 numbers describing an image) that is not observed but inferred.
Encoder \( q(z \mid x) \) — a network that maps an input x to the parameters of a distribution over z, usually a mean μ and a standard deviation σ for each latent dimension.
Decoder p(x|z) — a network that maps a latent z back to a distribution over data x.
Prior p(z) — the simple distribution assumed for z before seeing data, usually a standard Gaussian N(0, I).
ELBO — the evidence lower bound: a quantity that is always below log p(x) and that we maximize in training. It equals reconstruction quality minus a KL term.
KL divergence — a measure of how different one distribution is from another; here, between the encoder's distribution and the prior.
Reparameterization trick — writing a sample as \( z = \mu + \sigma \times \varepsilon \), with \( \varepsilon \) drawn from \( \mathcal{N}(0, I) \), so that gradients can flow through μ and σ.
Posterior collapse — a failure in which the encoder ignores the input and outputs the prior, and the decoder works without the latent.
VQ-VAE — a VAE variant whose latent is a choice from a learned codebook of vectors, giving discrete tokens.
history: Autoencoders (compress then reconstruct) go back to the 1980s, and Hinton and Salakhutdinov (2006) used deep ones for dimensionality reduction. They were not generative models. The probabilistic view came from variational inference, an older method in Bayesian statistics, where an intractable posterior is approximated by a simpler distribution fitted by maximizing a bound.
In December 2013 Kingma and Welling (University of Amsterdam), in "Auto-Encoding Variational Bayes", combined the two: the approximate posterior is a neural network (the encoder), the model is another network (the decoder), and the bound is optimized by stochastic gradient descent, using the reparameterization trick to get low-variance gradients. Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra (DeepMind) published the same idea independently in January 2014. The paper was presented at ICLR 2014, and Kingma and Welling later wrote an accessible introduction (2019).
Many improvements followed. Burda et al. (2015) proposed importance-weighted autoencoders with a tighter bound. Bowman et al. (2015) applied VAEs to text and described posterior collapse. Higgins et al. (2017) proposed β-VAE, which weights the KL term to encourage disentangled latent factors. Sønderby et al. (2016) added KL annealing. Hierarchical VAEs (Ladder VAE, NVAE in 2020, very deep VAEs in 2020) stacked several levels of latents and reached strong likelihoods.
The discrete line started with van den Oord, Vinyals and Kavukcuoglu (DeepMind, 2017), who proposed VQ-VAE, and VQ-VAE-2 in 2019. DALL-E (OpenAI, 2021) used a discrete VAE that turned each 256 × 256 image into a 32 × 32 grid of tokens from a vocabulary of 8,192, so a transformer could model images and text as one sequence. VQGAN (Esser et al., 2021) added a perceptual loss and an adversarial loss to make the reconstructions sharp. Rombach et al. (2022) then built latent diffusion on a KL-regularized autoencoder trained in this way, which became Stable Diffusion. Newer image and video generators continue to use learned autoencoders with a larger latent (Stable Diffusion 3 uses 16 channels instead of 4) to keep fine detail.
how: The model has three parts: an encoder network, a decoder network, and a fixed prior. For an input x (say an image), the encoder outputs two vectors of the same length d, the means μ and the log-variances \( \log \sigma^2 \). These define a diagonal Gaussian \( q(z \mid x) = \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2) \). A latent z is sampled from it, and the decoder maps z to the parameters of a distribution over x, for example the mean of a Gaussian over the pixels. The prior \( p(z) \) is \( \mathcal{N}(0, I) \).
We would like to maximize the log-likelihood \( \log p(x) \) of the training data. But \( p(x) = \int p(x \mid z)\, p(z)\, dz \) is an integral over all latents, which cannot be computed. The trick is to introduce q(z|x) and use Jensen's inequality:
\[ \log p(x) = \log \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[ \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z \mid x)} \right] \ge \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[ \log p(x, z) - \log q(z \mid x) \right] \]
The log function is concave, so the log of an average is at least the average of the logs. This lower bound is the ELBO. Rearranged, \( \mathrm{ELBO} = \mathbb{E}_q[ \log p(x \mid z) ] - \mathrm{KL}( q(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z) ) \). The first term is the reconstruction term: how well the decoder explains x from a z sampled from the encoder. The second term is a regularizer: it keeps the encoder's distributions close to the prior. The gap between \( \log p(x) \) and the ELBO equals \( \mathrm{KL}( q(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z \mid x) ) \), the error of the approximate posterior, so a better encoder gives a tighter bound.
For Gaussians the KL term has a closed form: \( \mathrm{KL} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_i \left( \mu_i^2 + \sigma_i^2 - \log \sigma_i^2 - 1 \right) \). It is zero when \( \mu = 0 \) and \( \sigma = 1 \), and grows if the encoder moves its mean away from 0 or makes its variance small. The reconstruction term needs a sample. With a Gaussian decoder of fixed variance, it is proportional to the squared error \( \lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2 \), so the loss looks like "mean squared error plus a KL penalty". For binary data (black and white images), it is the cross-entropy.
The sampling step blocks gradients: a random draw has no derivative with respect to μ and σ. The reparameterization trick moves the randomness outside the network: draw \( \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \), then compute \( z = \mu + \sigma \odot \varepsilon \). Now z is a differentiable function of μ and σ for fixed ε, and backpropagation (the chain rule through the decoder, the sample and the encoder) trains everything together. This gives gradient estimates with far lower variance than the older score-function (REINFORCE) estimator.
One design choice deserves a name: amortized inference. Classical variational inference fits a separate approximate posterior for every data point, which is slow. The VAE uses one encoder network for all points, so inferring the latent of a new item costs a single forward pass. The price is the amortization gap: a shared network cannot be as exact for each item as a per-item optimization, which is one reason the bound is not tight.
Why the KL term gives a usable generator: it forces the regions of latent space that the encoder uses to overlap and to be centred around the origin with spread near 1. If two nearby points decode to different things, a point between them will also decode to something sensible, because the encoder's distributions overlap. After training you generate by sampling z ~ N(0, I) and decoding. The same structure supports interpolation (decode points along a line between two latents) and anomaly detection (items that have a low ELBO).
The VQ-VAE replaces the Gaussian by a codebook. The encoder outputs a vector, which is replaced by the nearest of K codebook vectors (for example K = 512 or 8,192). The decoder sees only the chosen code. The nearest-neighbour step has no gradient, so the gradient is copied from the decoder input to the encoder output (the straight-through estimator), and extra loss terms move the codebook vectors toward the encoder outputs and the encoder toward the codes (a commitment loss). The result is a grid of integers, such as 32 × 32 = 1,024 tokens for an image, which a transformer can model autoregressively.
For latent diffusion, the autoencoder is trained first with reconstruction, perceptual and adversarial losses and a very small KL weight, so it is more of a good compressor than a probabilistic model. Then the diffusion model is trained only on the latents. The VAE's quality sets a ceiling: fine text, faces and small details lost in the autoencoder cannot be recovered by the diffusion model.

example: Take a toy model with a 1-dimensional latent. The input is a single number x = 3.0. The encoder outputs μ = 1.0 and σ = 0.5. The decoder is \( \hat{x} = 2z \).
KL term. \( \mathrm{KL}( \mathcal{N}(1.0, 0.5^2) \,\|\, \mathcal{N}(0, 1) ) = \frac{1}{2} ( \mu^2 + \sigma^2 - \ln \sigma^2 - 1 ) \) = ½ ( 1.0 + 0.25 − ln 0.25 − 1 ) = ½ ( 0.25 + 1.386 ) = 0.818 nats. Information-theoretically, this is the extra cost of describing this latent with the prior as the code instead of with the encoder's own distribution.
Sampling. Draw ε = 0.8. Then \( z = \mu + \sigma \varepsilon \) = 1.0 + 0.5 × 0.8 = 1.4. The decoder gives x̂ = 2.8. With a Gaussian decoder of variance 1, the reconstruction term is −½ ( 3.0 − 2.8 )² = −0.02 (ignoring constants). The ELBO for this sample is −0.02 − 0.818 = −0.838. Training maximizes the average of this over data and noise.
Gradients. Since \( z = \mu + \sigma \varepsilon \), we have \( \partial z/\partial \mu = 1 \) and \( \partial z/\partial \sigma = \varepsilon = 0.8 \). The reconstruction error is x − x̂ = 0.2, and \( \partial \hat{x}/\partial z = 2 \), so the gradient of the reconstruction term with respect to μ is 0.2 × 2 × 1 = 0.4. The reconstruction pushes μ up (toward 1.5, where 2z = 3). The KL gradient with respect to μ is μ = 1.0, pulling it down toward 0. The balance sets the final μ. Without the reparameterization, there would be no \( \partial z/\partial \mu \).
Compression ratio for latent diffusion. Image: 512 × 512 × 3 = 786,432 values. Latent with downsampling factor 8 and 4 channels: 64 × 64 × 4 = 16,384 values. The ratio is 48. A diffusion U-Net or transformer works on 4,096 spatial positions instead of 262,144, and self-attention cost grows with the square of the position count, so attention is about 4,096 times cheaper. This is the source of the large saving.
Token grid for VQ-VAE. A 256 × 256 image encoded with downsampling factor 8 gives 32 × 32 = 1,024 tokens. With a codebook of 8,192 entries, each token carries 13 bits, so the image takes about 13,300 bits, about 1.7 KB, against 196,608 bytes for raw 8-bit pixels, about 118 times smaller (lossy).
intuition: Learn to compress to a small, well-organized code and to imagine from it; if the codes are packed neatly around one centre, then any point near the centre is a valid description of something.
A plain autoencoder is like a filing system where each document has a private, arbitrary label; a random label means nothing. The VAE forces the labels to share a common, smooth coordinate system. Adding noise to the code during training (the σ) makes the decoder robust to small moves, so nearby codes must mean similar things. The KL term keeps the noise from shrinking to zero, which would turn the model back into an ordinary autoencoder with holes. The balance between the two terms is the central tension of the method.
mistakes: People often think the VAE decoder produces blurry images because VAEs are weak models. In fact the blur comes mostly from the simple likelihood. With a Gaussian of fixed variance, the best prediction under uncertainty is the average of all plausible images, which looks blurry. Better likelihoods, a perceptual loss or an adversarial loss (as in VQGAN and the Stable Diffusion autoencoder) give sharp results.
People often think the KL term is just a regularizer to be tuned away. In fact it is half of the objective, and it is what makes the latent space usable for sampling. Setting its weight too low gives an ordinary autoencoder with holes; setting it too high gives posterior collapse.
People often think the ELBO equals the log-likelihood. In fact it is a lower bound, and the gap is the KL between the approximate and true posterior. A weak encoder can give a loose bound, so the ELBO can understate how well the decoder models the data.
People often think a bigger latent is always better. In fact a latent that is too large lets the decoder memorise, and the KL cost grows. In latent diffusion there is a different trade-off: a smaller latent saves compute in diffusion but loses detail, so the compression factor (usually 8) and channel count are chosen together.
People sometimes think the encoder outputs one code. In fact it outputs a distribution, and the code is a random sample from it. At test time for reconstruction, many systems use the mean μ and skip the noise.
friction: Posterior collapse is the best-known failure. When the decoder is strong (an autoregressive text decoder, for example), it can model the data on its own, and the cheapest way to lower the KL term is to make q(z|x) equal to the prior for all inputs, so the latent carries no information. Fixes include KL annealing (start with weight near 0 and increase), free bits (no penalty below a threshold per dimension), a weaker decoder, and constraining the KL.
Sample quality is lower than for diffusion models or GANs unless extra losses are added. The ELBO is a bound, not an exact likelihood, and a tight bound needs a rich posterior family, such as flows or hierarchical latents. Choosing the KL weight is a tuning problem: β-VAE gives disentanglement in simple data, but there is no guarantee that individual latent dimensions mean something, and unsupervised disentanglement is in general not identifiable without extra assumptions (Locatello et al., 2019).
In latent diffusion the autoencoder is a hidden bottleneck. Reconstruction errors on small text, faces, hands or thin lines limit the final image quality whatever the diffusion model does, and changing the autoencoder means retraining everything downstream. Discrete codes bring codebook collapse: only a few codebook entries are used, wasting capacity. Fixes include codebook resets, lower-dimensional lookups, and finite scalar quantization.
practice: Use a VAE when you need a compact, smooth representation with a decoder: as the first stage of a latent generative model, for anomaly detection (large reconstruction error or low ELBO on unusual data), for data compression research, or when you need to sample and interpolate in a small latent space. If you want high-quality samples directly, prefer diffusion or autoregressive models and use the VAE only as a compressor.
When training one, monitor the reconstruction loss and the KL separately, and also the per-dimension KL to detect collapsed dimensions (dimensions with KL near 0 are unused). Start with a small KL weight and raise it slowly (annealing). Use a decoder likelihood suited to the data (cross-entropy for binary, discretized logistic or Gaussian for pixels), and add perceptual and adversarial losses if sharpness matters. For text, weaken the decoder or use word dropout to force it to use the latent.
For latent diffusion pipelines, take a pre-trained autoencoder from the model family you build on (changing it means retraining the diffusion model), test its reconstructions on your own content, especially text, faces and fine patterns, and cache latents if you train many diffusion variants on the same data. For discrete tokens, check codebook utilisation: if fewer than half the entries are used, use resets or a different quantizer. Libraries such as Hugging Face diffusers include standard autoencoders; Kingma and Welling's 2019 introduction and Stanford's CS236 notes are the best starting texts.
tradeoffs: Choose a VAE when you need fast, single-pass encoding and decoding, a smooth latent space, and an explicit likelihood bound. You give up sample sharpness unless you add extra losses, and you must manage the reconstruction and KL balance.
Choose a deterministic autoencoder (with a small regularizer) when you only need compression or features and will never sample from the latent space; the KL term costs reconstruction quality. Choose a VAE when you will sample, interpolate or use the latent as the space for another generative model.
Choose a continuous latent (Gaussian) when the next model is a diffusion or flow model; choose a discrete latent (VQ-VAE) when the next model is an autoregressive transformer, because it needs a finite vocabulary. Discrete tokens make joint text-and-image modelling easy; they lose detail when the codebook is small and can suffer codebook collapse.
Choose a stronger compression (factor 16 or more) to cut diffusion cost for high resolutions and video, and accept loss of fine detail; choose a milder one (factor 8 with more channels) when text, faces or small objects matter. Each doubling of the downsampling factor cuts the number of positions by four, and attention cost by up to sixteen.
Choose a low KL weight (as in image autoencoders for diffusion) when reconstruction quality matters more than a clean prior; choose a larger weight (\( \beta > 1 \)) when you want a regular latent space or some disentanglement, and accept blurrier reconstructions.
interview: Derive the ELBO and say what each term does. || Start from \( \log p(x) = \log \int q(z \mid x) \frac{p(x, z)}{q(z \mid x)}\, dz \). Jensen's inequality (log is concave) gives \( \log p(x) \ge \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x \mid z)] - \mathrm{KL}(q(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z)) \). The first term rewards reconstruction; the second keeps the encoder close to the prior so that sampling from the prior gives valid codes. The gap is \( \mathrm{KL}(q(z \mid x) \,\|\, p(z \mid x)) \).
What problem does the reparameterization trick solve? || Sampling z from a distribution that depends on the encoder's parameters has no gradient. Writing \( z = \mu + \sigma \varepsilon \) with \( \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \) moves the randomness into ε, so z is a differentiable function of μ and σ and backpropagation works. It also has much lower variance than the score-function (REINFORCE) gradient estimator.
Why are VAE samples blurry, and how do modern systems fix it? || A fixed-variance Gaussian likelihood is a squared-error loss, whose optimum under uncertainty is the mean of plausible outputs, which is blurry. Systems add perceptual and adversarial losses to the autoencoder (VQGAN, the Stable Diffusion VAE), or they use the VAE only as a compressor and let a diffusion model produce the detail in the latent space.
Why does latent diffusion use a VAE, and what are the risks? || Diffusion in pixel space is expensive, and a 48× smaller latent makes training and sampling much cheaper, especially since attention cost grows with the square of the number of positions. The risks are that the autoencoder's reconstruction error sets a quality ceiling (text, faces, small details) and that the whole pipeline is tied to that specific autoencoder.
What is posterior collapse, how do you detect it, and what do you do? || The encoder outputs the prior for every input and the decoder ignores z, usually when the decoder is powerful. value: Most generative models can produce samples but cannot tell you how probable a given sample is. GANs have no likelihood at all. VAEs give only a lower bound. Autoregressive models give exact likelihoods but generate one element at a time. A normalizing flow gives both at once: it turns simple random noise into data with a single invertible function, so it can generate in one pass and also compute the exact probability density of any data point by running the function backwards.
The method relies on a fact from calculus. If you transform a random variable with an invertible, differentiable function, the new density equals the old density at the matching point, multiplied by a factor that measures how much the function stretches or squeezes space locally. That factor is the absolute value of the determinant of the Jacobian matrix. A flow is a stack of simple invertible layers, each chosen so that this determinant is cheap to compute. Chained together, the layers can reshape a standard Gaussian into a very complicated distribution, while the log-density stays exact: it is the Gaussian log-density of the inverse image, plus the sum of the log-determinants of the layers.
Flows have a smaller place than diffusion models in today's products, but their ideas came back as the basis of flow matching and rectified flows, which are used to train recent image and video generators (for example Stable Diffusion 3 and similar systems) and need fewer sampling steps than classic diffusion. Flows are also used where exact density matters: variational inference, anomaly detection, scientific sampling, and fast speech vocoders.
The layers below add three things. Calculus (L4) supplies the change-of-variables formula and the chain rule that lets log-determinants of layers add up. Linear algebra (L4) shows how to make determinants cheap: the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal. Backpropagation (L2) trains the layer parameters by gradient ascent on the exact log-likelihood.
terms: Normalizing flow — an invertible, differentiable map from a simple distribution to a complex one, built by composing many simple layers.
Base distribution — the simple starting distribution, usually a standard Gaussian N(0, I).
Change of variables — the rule \( p_X(x) = p_Z(z) \times \left| \det \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \right| \) for \( z = f^{-1}(x) \), which links the densities before and after an invertible map.
Jacobian — the matrix of all partial derivatives of the outputs of a function with respect to its inputs.
Log-determinant — log |det J|, which measures the local change of volume. For a chain of layers it is the sum of the layers' log-determinants.
Coupling layer — a layer that leaves half of the dimensions unchanged and transforms the other half by a function of the unchanged half; it is easy to invert and has a triangular Jacobian.
Autoregressive flow — a flow in which output dimension i depends only on input dimensions up to i, again giving a triangular Jacobian.
Continuous normalizing flow (CNF) — a flow defined by an ordinary differential equation \( dx/dt = v(x, t) \), where v is a neural network (a velocity field).
Flow matching — a way to train a CNF by regressing the velocity field onto target velocities along simple paths between noise and data, without simulating the ODE during training.
Dequantization — adding small random noise to discrete data (such as 8-bit pixels) so that a continuous density is meaningful.
history: Change of variables is old statistics, but its use in deep learning started with Tabak and Vanden-Eijnden's "density estimation by dual ascent" (2010) and Tabak and Turner (2013), where the name "normalizing flow" appears. Dinh, Krueger and Bengio (2014) introduced NICE, the first deep flow with additive coupling layers, whose Jacobian determinant equals 1. Rezende and Mohamed (2015) used flows to make variational approximations in VAEs richer, and popularized the term in deep learning.
Dinh, Sohl-Dickstein and Bengio (2016) added scaling in RealNVP (affine coupling layers, multi-scale structure) and generated recognizable images. Kingma and Dhariwal (OpenAI, 2018) published Glow, with invertible 1 × 1 convolutions and activation normalization, and showed flow samples of 256 × 256 faces with realistic attribute edits. Papamakarios et al. (2017) developed masked autoregressive flows, and Kingma et al. (2016) the inverse autoregressive flow, which is fast to sample from. This property was used in Parallel WaveNet (DeepMind, 2017), a flow-based speech generator deployed in a production voice assistant, and in WaveGlow (NVIDIA, 2018).
Chen, Rubanova, Bettencourt and Duvenaud (2018) introduced neural ODEs and continuous normalizing flows: instead of discrete layers, a network defines a velocity field and the density changes by the trace of its Jacobian, not the full determinant. FFJORD (Grathwohl et al., 2018) made the trace cheap with a stochastic estimator. Training still required solving the ODE at every step, which was slow.
In 2022 three groups independently removed that cost. Lipman et al. ("Flow Matching for Generative Modeling"), Liu, Gong and Liu ("Flow Straight and Fast", rectified flow) and Albergo and Vanden-Eijnden (stochastic interpolants) showed that one can train a velocity field by simple regression, without simulation. Esser et al. (Stability AI, 2024) scaled rectified flow with a transformer and used it in Stable Diffusion 3. Meanwhile Song et al. (2021) had shown that every diffusion model has a deterministic "probability flow" ODE, so diffusion models are a kind of continuous flow too. Zhai et al. (Apple, 2024) revived discrete flows with transformers (TarFlow) and showed that they are competitive with other likelihood models on image density estimation.
how: A flow is a function f that maps latent noise \( z \sim p_Z \) (for example \( \mathcal{N}(0, I) \)) to data \( x = f(z) \). The function is a composition \( f = f_K \circ \cdots \circ f_1 \) of invertible layers. Because each layer is invertible, there is also a reverse map \( z = f^{-1}(x) = f_1^{-1} \circ \cdots \circ f_K^{-1}(x) \). Dimensions are preserved: the latent has as many numbers as the data. This is a strong restriction (and a difference from VAEs and GANs, where the latent is smaller).
The change-of-variables formula gives the exact density: \( \log p_X(x) = \log p_Z(z) + \sum_k \log |\det J_k| \), where \( z = f^{-1}(x) \) and J_k is the Jacobian of layer k's inverse at the point it was applied. Sampling is one pass: draw z, apply f. Evaluating the likelihood is one pass the other way: apply f⁻¹, sum the log-determinants, add the Gaussian log-density of z. Training maximizes the average log p_X(x) over the data by gradient ascent; there is no bound, no sampling noise in the objective and no adversary.
A general \( D \times D \) determinant costs \( O(D^3) \). For an image of 32 × 32 × 3 = 3,072 values this is about 2.9 × 10¹⁰ operations per layer per example, and for 256 × 256 × 3 = 196,608 values it is about 7.6 × 10¹⁵. That is impossible, so the whole design of flows is about layers whose Jacobian is triangular, where the determinant is the product of the diagonal and costs \( O(D) \). Linear algebra provides this fact, and it dictates the architecture.
A coupling layer is the standard building block. Split the input into two halves (x_a, x_b). Leave x_a unchanged: \( y_a = x_a \). Transform x_b with a scale and shift computed from x_a by any neural network: \( y_b = x_b \odot \exp(s(x_a)) + t(x_a) \). The Jacobian has the block form \( \begin{bmatrix} I & 0 \\ \partial y_b/\partial x_a & \operatorname{diag}(\exp(s)) \end{bmatrix} \), which is triangular, and the log-determinant is the sum of the entries of s(x_a). The inverse is just as cheap: \( x_b = (y_b - t(y_a)) \odot \exp(-s(y_a)) \). The networks s and t can be arbitrarily complex (deep convolutional nets or transformers) because they never need to be inverted or differentiated analytically. Successive layers swap the roles of the halves (with masks or permutations) so that every dimension is transformed.
Other designs follow the same recipe. Autoregressive flows make output i depend on inputs 1 … i, which is triangular; they are fast in one direction and slow in the other. Glow adds invertible 1 × 1 convolutions that mix channels: a c × c matrix W has log-determinant log |det W|, which is made cheap by an LU decomposition, giving O(c) per pixel. Multi-scale architectures factor out half of the dimensions at intervals, to save computation. Image data are dequantized: integer pixel values 0 to 255 get uniform noise added, and results are reported as bits per dimension.
Continuous flows take the number of layers to the limit. A neural network v_θ(x, t) defines how a point moves: \( dx/dt = v_\theta(x, t) \). Integrating this ODE from t = 0 to 1 maps noise to data, and it is invertible by construction (run it backwards). The log-density changes at the rate \( d \log p/dt = -\operatorname{tr}(\partial v/\partial x) \), the trace of the Jacobian, which is cheaper than a determinant and can be estimated stochastically. The network is unconstrained, which is the advantage. The old way to train, by maximum likelihood, needed an ODE solver in the training loop.
Flow matching removes that. Pick a simple path between a noise sample x₀ and a data sample x₁, for example the straight line \( x_t = (1 - t) x_0 + t x_1 \). Its velocity is \( x_1 - x_0 \). Train the network to predict that velocity at random points on the path with a squared-error loss: \( L = \mathbb{E}\, \lVert v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0) \rVert^2 \). It can be shown that this regression learns a velocity field whose flow turns the noise distribution into the data distribution. To sample, draw noise and integrate the ODE with a few steps of an ordinary solver (for example 20 to 50 Euler steps). Rectified flow repeats the training on pairs produced by the model itself ("reflow") to straighten the paths, so that very few steps are enough.

example: A one-dimensional flow. Let the base be \( z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \) and the flow \( x = f(z) = 2z + 1 \). Then the inverse is \( z = (x - 1)/2 \), with derivative 1/2. For the data point x = 3: z = 1. The base log-density is log N(1; 0, 1) = −½ ln(2π) − ½ = −0.919 − 0.5 = −1.419. The log-determinant is ln(1/2) = −0.693. So log p_X(3) = −1.419 − 0.693 = −2.112. Check: x is N(1, 4), with density 1/(2√(2π)) × exp(−(3 − 1)²/8) = 0.1995 × 0.6065 = 0.1210, and ln 0.1210 = −2.112. The formula works. The −0.693 term says that because f stretches space by 2, the density is spread out: it is halved.
An affine coupling layer on 2 numbers. Use \( s(a) = 0.5a \) and \( t(a) = a \) (toy functions). Forward from latent to data: input (x_a, x_b) = (2, 1). Then s = 1.0 and t = 2.0, so y_a = 2 and y_b = 1 × e^1.0 + 2.0 = 4.718. The layer's log-determinant (forward) is s = 1.0. Inverse: given (2, 4.718), x_a = 2; x_b = (4.718 − 2.0) × e^(−1.0) = 2.718 × 0.368 = 1.0. Recovered. The log-density of the data point (2, 4.718) under a standard Gaussian base: z = (2, 1), log N(z; 0, I) = −½ (4 + 1) − ln(2π) = −2.5 − 1.838 = −4.338. The data-side density subtracts the forward log-determinant: −4.338 − 1.0 = −5.338.
Cost of the determinant. With D = 3,072 (a 32 × 32 colour image), a general determinant needs about D³ ≈ 2.9 × 10¹⁰ operations; the triangular coupling Jacobian needs D = 3,072. That is a saving of about 10⁷ times, per layer, per training example.
A flow matching step. Take a two-dimensional case with noise x₀ = (0.5, −1.0) and a data point x₁ = (2.0, 1.0). Pick t = 0.3. Then x_t = 0.7 × x₀ + 0.3 × x₁ = (0.35 + 0.6, −0.7 + 0.3) = (0.95, −0.4). The target velocity is x₁ − x₀ = (1.5, 2.0). If the network predicts (1.0, 1.5), the loss is (0.5)² + (0.5)² = 0.5. The gradient moves the prediction toward the target. Since many different (x₀, x₁) pairs pass through nearby points, the network learns the average velocity at each place and time, and that average is what transports the whole distribution. Sampling with 20 Euler steps costs 20 network evaluations, against hundreds for early diffusion samplers.
Bits per dimension. A model with 3.35 bits per dimension on CIFAR-10 (the figure reported for Glow) spends 3.35 × 3,072 = 10,291 bits, about 1.3 KB, to encode one image with ideal coding, against 3,072 bytes raw. Likelihood-based models report this number, and it is a metric that GANs cannot provide.
intuition: Knead a simple ball of dough into the shape of the data, keeping track of exactly how much each region was stretched or squeezed.
If you know the density of the dough before kneading, and you know how much each spot was stretched, you know the density after kneading: stretched regions become thinner, squeezed regions become denser. That is the whole change-of-variables formula. The design question is only how to knead with moves that are easy to undo and whose stretch can be measured cheaply. Coupling layers do this by changing half of the numbers using the other half, which leaves a simple triangular stretch. Flow matching takes the continuous version: do not design the moves, just learn a wind field that carries every grain of dough along straight lines from the ball to the shape.
mistakes: People often think exact likelihood means flows make the best samples. In fact likelihood and sample quality are different things. Classic flows had worse sample quality than GANs and diffusion models, because the invertibility constraint limits what the network can express, and a model can have a good likelihood while its samples look poor.
People often think a flow with a high likelihood on its training set will give a low likelihood to unfamiliar data. In fact Nalisnick et al. (2019) found that flows trained on CIFAR-10 gave higher likelihood to SVHN house-number images, which are a different and simpler kind of image. Likelihood mostly reflects local pixel statistics, so using it for out-of-distribution detection is unreliable.
People often think flow matching is a new, unrelated idea. In fact it is a continuous normalizing flow trained in a smarter way. The velocity field and the ODE are the same objects as in CNFs; what changed is the training objective (a regression, with no ODE solve) and the choice of simple paths.
People often think flows need the latent to be a small code. In fact the latent has exactly the same dimension as the data, since the map must be invertible. This is why flows do not compress, and why high-resolution flows are large.
People sometimes think diffusion and flows are separate families. In fact the probability flow ODE of a diffusion model is a continuous normalizing flow with the same marginals, and flow matching with Gaussian paths includes diffusion-style training as a special case.
friction: Invertibility and cheap Jacobians restrict the architecture. A coupling layer changes only half of the dimensions at a time, so a flow needs many layers (Glow has roughly 100 to 200 coupling steps) to mix information, and every layer must store activations for backpropagation. Compared with an unconstrained network of the same size, a flow is less expressive. Because the latent has the full dimension, memory and compute for high resolutions are large, which is one reason diffusion in a compressed latent space won.
Topology is another limit. A smooth invertible map cannot split a single blob into separate pieces without extreme stretching, so a Gaussian base is hard to map to multimodal data with well-separated clusters. Numerical issues arise too: log-determinants can explode, inverses can be unstable when scales are large, and discrete data must be dequantized, which adds noise to the likelihood. Likelihood does not guarantee good samples or good out-of-distribution detection.
Continuous flows need ODE solvers. With maximum-likelihood training, each step involves an expensive integration; with flow matching, training is cheap but sampling still needs several network evaluations, and few-step sampling needs rectification or distillation. Paths that look straight for each pair can be curved after averaging over all pairs, which is why more steps give better quality. Finally, exact likelihoods of images are dominated by imperceptible detail, so they are less useful as a quality measure than they sound.
practice: Use a normalizing flow when you need an exact, fast, differentiable density together with sampling: variational inference (a flexible posterior), density estimation on tabular or low-dimensional data, sampling from physical systems with known energy functions (Boltzmann generators), and anomaly scoring on structured data. For tabular data with up to a few hundred dimensions, masked autoregressive flows or neural spline flows are strong baselines, and libraries such as nflows, normflows and Zuko in PyTorch provide them.
For high-dimensional media generation, use flow matching or rectified flow, not classic discrete flows. The training loop is short: sample a time t, noise and a data point, form the interpolation, regress the velocity. Use a transformer or U-Net backbone, put the model in a latent space of a pre-trained autoencoder for images and video, and sample with 20 to 50 steps of an ODE solver, adjusting step count against quality. A common choice is to sample t from a distribution that emphasises the middle of the path (a logit-normal distribution, as in Stable Diffusion 3) and to condition on text or other inputs.
When evaluating, report bits per dimension for likelihood claims and FID or human preference for sample quality, and do not mix them. Check the invertibility numerically (reconstruction error of f⁻¹(f(z))), watch the log-determinant for explosion, and use gradient clipping and careful initialisation (the last layer of each coupling network is initialised to zero so the layer starts as the identity). For reading, Papamakarios et al.'s review (2021) is the standard survey, and the CS236 notes explain the change-of-variables derivations.
tradeoffs: Choose a classic normalizing flow when you need exact density and cheap sampling in moderate dimensions, for example as a variational posterior or for anomaly scores on tabular data. You give up expressiveness per parameter, and you must design layers with tractable Jacobians.
Choose flow matching or rectified flow over classic diffusion when you want simple regression training, a deterministic ODE sampler and good quality in 20 to 50 steps. Choose classic diffusion training when you rely on existing pipelines and samplers; the two are close, and the choice is mostly about the training objective and the noise schedule.
Choose a VAE or latent-space model over a flow when you want a compressed code (the flow's latent is as large as the data). Choose a flow when you need exact likelihood and the data dimension is modest. Choose an autoregressive model when exact likelihood and the best compression matter and slow sequential sampling is acceptable.
Choose continuous flows (with an ODE) over discrete coupling flows when you want unrestricted network architectures; you pay with solver steps at sampling time. Choose discrete flows when you want a fixed, small, deterministic cost in both directions.
Choose few-step distillation of a flow or diffusion model (1 to 4 steps) for interactive applications, accepting a small quality loss and an extra training stage; choose the full 20 to 50 step solver when quality is paramount and latency is not.

value: To generate realistic images, a model needs a way to judge "does this look real?" Writing such a judge by hand is impossible, and the usual training losses (such as squared error against a target) reward blurry averages. A generative adversarial network (GAN) solves this by learning the judge at the same time as the generator. Two networks play a game. The generator turns random noise into a sample. The discriminator looks at samples, some real and some produced by the generator, and tries to tell which is which. The discriminator is trained to win; the generator is trained to fool it. As the discriminator gets better at spotting flaws, the generator is forced to remove them.
The result, around 2018 to 2020, was the first synthetic images that people could not easily tell from photographs. StyleGAN produced 1024 × 1024 faces of people who do not exist, with control over pose, hair and age. GANs were also fast: one forward pass of the generator makes an image, in milliseconds, where diffusion models need tens of passes. By 2021 diffusion models overtook GANs in quality and diversity on large, varied datasets, and became the main method for text-to-image generation. Yet the adversarial idea has remained: it is used as an extra loss in the autoencoders of latent diffusion (so decoded images are sharp), in neural audio codecs and speech vocoders (HiFi-GAN), in super-resolution, in image-to-image translation, and in fast distilled diffusion models that make an image in 1 to 4 steps.
Two lower-layer ideas matter. Game theory (L4) provides the frame: training is a two-player zero-sum game, and the solution is an equilibrium where neither player can improve alone. The theory also explains why the training is hard, since finding a saddle point with simultaneous gradient steps is not the same as minimizing a loss. Backpropagation (L2) provides the mechanism: the generator never sees real data directly; it learns from gradients that flow through the discriminator, which say in which direction each generated pixel should move to look more real.
terms: Generator G — a network that maps a random vector z (the noise) to a sample, such as an image.
Discriminator D — a network that outputs the probability that its input is real and not generated.
Minimax game — a game in which one player minimizes the quantity that the other maximizes. Here D maximizes the value function V and G minimizes it.
Nash equilibrium — a state in which neither player can gain by changing strategy alone. For a GAN it is G reproducing the data distribution and D outputting 1/2 everywhere.
Non-saturating loss — the practical generator loss \( -\log D(G(z)) \), used instead of \( \log(1 - D(G(z))) \) because it gives stronger gradients early in training.
Mode collapse — the generator produces only a few kinds of samples (or one) because they fool D, instead of the full diversity of the data.
Jensen–Shannon divergence — a symmetric measure of difference between two distributions; the original GAN loss, at the optimal discriminator, equals twice this divergence minus log 4.
Wasserstein distance — the minimum "work" to move one distribution into another; WGAN uses it, which gives meaningful gradients even when the distributions do not overlap.
FID (Fréchet Inception Distance) — a score that compares statistics of image features from real and generated sets; lower is better.
Truncation trick — sampling the noise z from a narrower range at generation time to trade diversity for quality.
history: Goodfellow and colleagues (Université de Montréal) published "Generative Adversarial Nets" in 2014. The core idea came from asking how to train a generator without computing a likelihood. The first results were small digit and face images. Radford, Metz and Chintala (2015) published DCGAN, with convolutional architectures and a set of practices (batch normalization, strided convolutions, Adam with learning rate 2 × 10⁻⁴ and β₁ = 0.5) that made training work reliably enough to be used by many people.
Arjovsky and Bottou (2017) analysed why the original loss fails: when real and generated distributions occupy different low-dimensional regions, the Jensen–Shannon divergence is constant and gives no gradient. Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou proposed WGAN (2017), based on the Wasserstein distance, and Gulrajani et al. added the gradient penalty (WGAN-GP). Miyato et al. (2018) proposed spectral normalization. Heusel et al. (2017) introduced FID and the two time-scale update rule, still the standard for measuring and tuning GANs.
Quality then rose quickly. Karras et al. (NVIDIA) published ProGAN (2017), which grows the networks from 4 × 4 to 1024 × 1024 during training, and StyleGAN (2018), which maps the noise through a mapping network to a style vector that controls each layer; StyleGAN2 (2019) and StyleGAN3 (2021) fixed artifacts. BigGAN (Brock et al., DeepMind, 2018) scaled class-conditional generation to 512 × 512 ImageNet images with very large batches. Applications spread: pix2pix and CycleGAN translated images between domains (horse to zebra, sketch to photo), SRGAN and ESRGAN sharpened low-resolution photos, and CTGAN (2019) synthesized tabular data.
In 2021 Dhariwal and Nichol showed that a diffusion model with guidance beat BigGAN on ImageNet, and the field moved. GANs kept some ground: GigaGAN (Kang et al., 2023) scaled to a billion-parameter text-to-image model with fast sampling; Adversarial Diffusion Distillation (Sauer et al., 2023; SDXL-Turbo) used a discriminator to distil a diffusion model into a 1 to 4 step generator; and Huang et al. (2024, "The GAN is dead; long live the GAN!") argued that a well-regularized modern GAN baseline matches strong diffusion results on several benchmarks, reducing the tricks that older GANs needed.
how: Draw a noise vector z from a simple distribution (for example 512 independent standard Gaussians). The generator G maps it to a sample \( \tilde{x} = G(z) \), such as a 64 × 64 × 3 image. The discriminator D takes an image and outputs a number between 0 and 1: its belief that the image is real. The training data are real images x. The two networks are trained on the value function \( V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_x[ \log D(x) ] + \mathbb{E}_z[ \log(1 - D(G(z))) ] \). D tries to maximize V (give high scores to real, low scores to fake); G tries to minimize it.
Training alternates. In a D step: take a batch of real images and a batch of generated images; compute V; update D's weights to increase it by gradient ascent. In a G step: sample new noise, generate images, pass them through D, and update G's weights so that D gives them higher scores. The generator's gradient reaches its weights by backpropagation through D: the chain rule carries "how should this pixel change to look more real" back through the discriminator's layers into the generator's weights. Common settings are the Adam optimizer, one D step per G step (WGAN uses five), batch sizes from 64 to 2,048 and, in the early DCGAN recipe, a learning rate of 2 × 10⁻⁴.
Why this should work. For a fixed generator with sample density p_g, the best possible discriminator is \( D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)} \). Putting D* into V gives \( -\log 4 + 2 \times \mathrm{JSD}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_g) \), where JSD is the Jensen–Shannon divergence. This is minimized, with value \( -\log 4 \), exactly when \( p_g = p_{\text{data}} \). At that point \( D^* = 1/2 \) everywhere: the discriminator cannot do better than guessing. So if both players are perfectly optimized, the generator reproduces the data distribution. Probability and information theory give this result; game theory describes it as the Nash equilibrium.
Why the practice is harder. Theory assumes that D is optimal at each step and that G can represent any distribution. In practice the two networks are updated by simultaneous gradient steps, and a minimax problem is not a minimization. Consider the simplest game, min over x and max over y of \( x \times y \), with equilibrium at (0, 0). Gradient descent on x and ascent on y gives \( x \leftarrow x - \eta y \) and \( y \leftarrow y + \eta x \), which rotates around the origin and, with finite step size, spirals outward. GAN training can circle in the same way instead of converging. In addition, early in training the discriminator wins easily, \( D(G(z)) \) is near 0, and the term \( \log(1 - D(G(z))) \) is flat, so the generator receives almost no gradient. The practical fix is the non-saturating loss: G maximizes \( \log D(G(z)) \) instead, which has a large gradient exactly where the old one vanished.
Many techniques stabilise the game. WGAN replaces D by a critic that estimates the Wasserstein distance, which needs a Lipschitz constraint (weight clipping, gradient penalty or spectral normalization). The R1 penalty (Mescheder et al., 2018) punishes large gradients of D on real data and gives local convergence. Updating D and G with different learning rates (TTUR) helps. Architecture choices matter: StyleGAN's mapping network and per-layer style modulation, progressive growing, and exponential moving averages of the generator's weights used at test time. Differentiable augmentation (ADA) lets GANs train on small datasets of a few thousand images without the discriminator memorising them.
Discriminators come in different shapes. An image-level discriminator outputs one score per image. A patch discriminator (PatchGAN, used in pix2pix and in the VQGAN autoencoder) outputs a grid of scores, one for each local patch of, say, 70 × 70 pixels, so it judges local texture and needs fewer parameters; it cannot judge global structure, which the reconstruction loss and the rest of the model must supply. Each design decides what kind of error the generator is punished for.
Conditioning gives control: feed a class label or a text embedding to both networks (conditional GANs), or use a paired input, as in pix2pix, where D sees the input and the output together. Evaluation uses FID, which fits Gaussians to Inception-network features of real and generated sets and computes the Fréchet distance: \( \mathrm{FID} = \lVert \mu_1 - \mu_2 \rVert^2 + \operatorname{Tr}\big(\Sigma_1 + \Sigma_2 - 2(\Sigma_1 \Sigma_2)^{1/2}\big) \). Because GANs have no likelihood, FID and sample inspection are the main evidence of progress.

example: A one-dimensional world. Real data follow N(4, 1). The generator currently produces N(0, 1), so p_data(x) and p_g(x) are two bell curves four units apart. The best discriminator is \( D^*(x) = p_{\text{data}} / (p_{\text{data}} + p_g) \). At x = 0: p_data = 0.00013, p_g = 0.399, so D* = 0.0003 ("fake"). At x = 2, the midpoint: both are 0.054, so D* = 0.5. At x = 3: p_data = 0.242, p_g = 0.0044, D* = 0.982. At x = 4: p_data = 0.399, p_g = 0.00013, D* = 0.9997 ("real"). So the discriminator is a smooth step at x = 2, and its slope at the generator's samples tells the generator to move its samples to the right. The generator updates its mean toward 4. When the generator reaches N(4, 1), D* = 0.5 everywhere and the value is \( V = -\log 4 = -1.386 \).
The non-saturating gradient. Suppose D gives a generated sample the score D(G(z)) = 0.0003. The original generator objective \( \log(1 - D) \) has value log 0.9997 = −0.0003 and its derivative with respect to D is \( -1/(1 - D) \) = −1.0003, but D itself is nearly flat in the logit (the slope of the sigmoid is \( D(1 - D) \) = 0.0003), so the gradient to the logit is about −0.0003. The non-saturating objective \( -\log D \) has value 8.1 and a gradient to the logit of \( -(1 - D) \) = −0.9997: about 3,000 times larger. That is why the non-saturating form is used.
A spiral. Take the game \( x \times y \) with a step size of 0.1, starting from (1, 1). One step: x = 1 − 0.1 × 1 = 0.9, y = 1 + 0.1 × 1 = 1.1. Second step: x = 0.9 − 0.11 = 0.79, y = 1.1 + 0.09 = 1.19. The distance from the origin starts at 1.414 and is multiplied by √(1 + 0.01) = 1.005 every step, giving 1.486 after 10 steps. It never reaches (0, 0). Real GAN training has the same rotational tendency, which methods such as optimistic gradients, a gradient penalty and smaller learning rates try to damp.
Cost scale. A StyleGAN2 model for 1024 × 1024 faces has about 30 million parameters in the generator. It was trained on the FFHQ set of 70,000 face images, and generates an image in a single forward pass, in tens of milliseconds on one GPU. A comparable diffusion model needs 20 to 50 passes, unless distilled. This speed is why GAN-based decoders, vocoders and super-resolution models remain common in production.
intuition: A forger and a detective train each other: the forgeries get better only because the detective gets better, and the detective gets better only because the forgeries get better.
The idea is natural because it replaces a hard task, writing down what "realistic" means, with an easier one: telling real from fake. A classifier can learn what makes a face look wrong (the skin texture, the shape of an ear) from examples. The generator does not need to be told what is wrong; it only needs the direction that makes the classifier less sure. Each player's difficulty increases with the other's skill, so the game keeps raising the standard. The same feature makes the game unstable: if one player gets ahead, the other has nothing to learn from.
mistakes: People often think GANs are just two networks minimizing a combined loss. In fact it is a minimax game, and gradient descent on a minimax problem does not behave like descent on a loss. The loss values do not tell you if training is progressing: a falling generator loss may mean the discriminator is failing, not that images are improving. This is why people watch samples and FID.
People often think the discriminator should be as strong as possible. In fact a discriminator that wins too easily gives the generator no useful gradient (it is confident that everything is fake, so there is no direction to improve). The balance between the two matters, which is why learning rates, regularization and the number of D steps are tuned.
People often think mode collapse means the generator is bad at images. In fact the images can be excellent; the problem is that the generator covers only part of the data distribution. The objective does not punish missing modes much: the discriminator sees one sample at a time, and if the generator gives one good image, the discriminator may not notice that all the images look the same. Minibatch discrimination and diversity-aware losses address this.
People often think diffusion models made GANs obsolete. In fact diffusion models dominate large, diverse text-to-image generation, but GANs are still used where speed matters (real-time upscaling, vocoders), as a sharpening loss in autoencoders, and as the discriminator in distilled few-step generators.
People sometimes think a GAN learns the data distribution exactly. In fact the guarantee holds only for an ideal D and unlimited capacity. In practice the generator often has a distribution close in some respects (good samples) but not in others (missing rare modes), and there is no direct likelihood to check.
friction: Training instability is the main cost. Results depend on learning rates, architecture details, normalization and random seeds, and runs can diverge after a long stable period. Mode collapse and partial coverage of the data are frequent. There is no loss curve that reliably shows quality, so evaluation relies on FID (which has its own biases and depends on the Inception network), precision and recall measures, and human inspection.
Without a likelihood, GANs cannot say how probable a sample is, so they are poor for density estimation or anomaly detection, and inverting a GAN (finding z for a given image) needs extra optimization or an encoder. Conditioning on rich inputs such as free text was harder for GANs than for diffusion models, which scale more smoothly with data and compute, and have more stable training. Large GANs also need huge batch sizes (BigGAN used 2,048), costly hardware, and carefully engineered tricks.
There are societal risks as well. Realistic synthetic faces and voices lower the cost of fraud and disinformation (deepfakes), and detection becomes an arms race, because a detector can itself be used as a discriminator to train a better fake. Privacy is another issue: generators can reproduce training examples when the data set is small.
practice: If you need a generative model today, start with a diffusion or flow-matching model for image quality, and consider GANs for specific roles. Good roles for a GAN are: a fast decoder or upsampler where one forward pass is needed; a perceptual-sharpness loss alongside a reconstruction loss in an autoencoder (patch-based discriminators are standard); speech vocoders such as HiFi-GAN; image-to-image translation with paired data (pix2pix-style, with an L1 loss plus an adversarial loss); and distilling a slow generator into a one-step one, with a discriminator loss that pushes the one-step outputs toward the real image distribution.
When you train a GAN, begin from a proven recipe and codebase (StyleGAN2-ADA for images, or the R3GAN baseline), not from scratch. Use the non-saturating loss with R1 regularization, or a WGAN-GP; Adam with β₁ = 0 to 0.5; an exponential moving average of the generator weights; and differentiable augmentation when you have fewer than about 100,000 images. Monitor FID on a fixed set every few thousand steps, save sample grids, and keep checkpoints, since quality can peak and then degrade.
For synthetic tabular data (for example to share a privacy-safe copy of a table), compare CTGAN-style GANs with simpler approaches such as copulas, Bayesian networks or diffusion-based tabular models; evaluate with train-on-synthetic and test-on-real accuracy, and check privacy by looking for near copies of real rows. Always label synthetic media, and do not use GAN output as evidence of real events.
tradeoffs: Choose a GAN when you need a single-pass generator with very low latency, for example real-time super-resolution, a speech vocoder, or an on-device image filter, and you have a dataset with limited diversity (faces, rooms, one object class). You give up training stability and the ability to cover diverse data and complex text conditions.
Choose a diffusion or flow-matching model when you need diversity, text control and reliable training, and you can afford 20 to 50 steps or a distillation stage. You give up latency, unless you distil.
Choose adversarial distillation (a discriminator on top of a diffusion teacher) when you want 1 to 4 step generation of a diffusion model's quality; choose consistency or progressive distillation without a discriminator when you prefer a simpler and more stable objective with slightly lower sharpness.
Choose WGAN-GP or R1-regularized objectives when a standard GAN fails to converge; you pay extra compute for gradient penalties (an extra backward pass) and may lose a little sharpness. Choose the plain non-saturating loss with spectral normalization when you need the cheapest training step.
Choose a VAE or flow over a GAN if you need a likelihood or an encoder to map data into a latent space; a GAN gives no density and no encoder.
interview: Write the GAN objective and explain what happens at the optimal discriminator. || \( \min_G \max_D \mathbb{E}_x[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_z[\log(1 - D(G(z)))] \). For a fixed G the best D is \( p_{\text{data}}/(p_{\text{data}} + p_g) \). Substituting gives \( -\log 4 + 2\,\mathrm{JSD}(p_{\text{data}} \,\|\, p_g) \), which is minimized when \( p_g = p_{\text{data}} \), where \( D = 1/2 \) everywhere. So the generator is trained to minimize the Jensen–Shannon divergence, given an ideal discriminator.
Why is GAN training unstable? || It is a minimax game solved by simultaneous gradient steps, which can rotate or oscillate rather than converge. If the discriminator becomes too strong, the generator's gradient vanishes (or the JSD is constant when supports do not overlap). The generator can also collapse to a few modes. Remedies: non-saturating loss, WGAN with a Lipschitz constraint, R1 penalty, TTUR, spectral normalization, augmentation.
What is mode collapse and how would you detect and reduce it? || The generator covers only part of the data distribution, such as a few classes or a single face. Detect it with sample grids, recall metrics, class-wise counts using a classifier, and a rising FID despite sharp images. Reduce it with minibatch discrimination, WGAN-style losses, larger or more diverse batches, unrolled objectives and regularization of the generator.
Why did diffusion models overtake GANs, and where do GANs still win? || Diffusion training is a stable regression with no adversary, it covers the data distribution better, and it scales with data and compute and conditions on text easily. GANs still win on single-pass speed, and adversarial losses are used in autoencoders, vocoders, super-resolution and few-step distilled generators.
How would you evaluate a GAN without a likelihood? || Use FID or a similar distribution distance on features (and KID for small sets), precision and recall for fidelity and coverage, nearest-neighbour checks against the training set for memorization, class-conditional accuracy with a pre-trained classifier, and human studies. Report the sample count and feature network, since FID depends on both.
quiz: What do the generator and the discriminator each try to do? || The generator maps noise to samples and tries to make the discriminator classify them as real. The discriminator receives real and generated samples and tries to classify them correctly.
What is the optimal discriminator for a fixed generator, and what is the value at the equilibrium? || \( D^*(x) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(x)}{p_{\text{data}}(x) + p_g(x)} \). At the equilibrium \( p_g = p_{\text{data}} \), so \( D^* = 1/2 \) everywhere and \( V = -\log 4 \approx -1.386 \).
Why is the non-saturating generator loss used? || Early in training the discriminator rejects fakes with \( D(G(z)) \) near 0, and \( \log(1 - D(G(z))) \) is almost flat there, so the generator gets a tiny gradient. Maximizing \( \log D(G(z)) \) has a strong gradient in the same situation.

You need to upscale video frames in real time on a phone. Do you use a GAN or a diffusion model, and why? || A GAN-trained (or adversarially trained) upscaler, because it needs one forward pass of a small network and low latency, while diffusion needs many passes. value: A trained neural network is a pile of billions of numbers that produces useful behaviour, and nobody wrote down how. When a model gives a wrong answer, refuses a safe request, or appears to follow a hidden goal, the weights do not tell us why. Interpretability is the research field that tries to open the box: to find out what information a network represents, which internal parts compute which behaviour, and whether a stated explanation matches what really happened inside.
The motive is practical. Engineers want to debug failures. Safety researchers want to check whether a model is deceiving its users or is hiding a dangerous capability, which cannot be settled from outputs alone, since a model can say what you want to hear. Regulators and users want reasons for decisions. And scientists want to know how a system that nobody programmed does arithmetic, translates, or plans the end of a rhyme. There is a direct line from interpretability to tools in use today: probes that read a model's internal state to detect harmful intent or hallucination, steering vectors that change a model's behaviour by editing its internal activations, and audits that look for hidden objectives.
The field works because networks are unusually open to study. Every activation can be recorded, edited, and replayed exactly, which is impossible for a brain. Three lower-layer ideas give the method. Linear algebra (L4) supplies the central view that a feature is a direction in activation space, so reading a feature is a dot product and editing one is vector addition, and that a network can store more features than it has dimensions by using nearly orthogonal directions (superposition). Residual connections (L2) give the stream: each layer reads from and adds to a shared vector, so the final output is a sum of contributions that can be attributed layer by layer. Self-attention (L2) provides the components of circuits, such as the induction heads that implement in-context copying.
terms: Activation — the vector of numbers computed by a layer for a given input. The residual stream is the running sum of all layers' outputs at each token position.
Feature — a human-meaningful property of the input (for example "the text is in French" or "the Golden Gate Bridge") that the network represents, usually as a direction in activation space.
Circuit — a set of connected features and components (attention heads, MLP neurons) that together compute a specific behaviour.
Superposition — a network storing more features than it has dimensions by assigning them nearly orthogonal, overlapping directions; it works because most features are rarely active at the same time.
Probe — a small classifier (often linear) trained on a network's activations to test whether some information is present.
Sparse autoencoder (SAE) — a network trained to rebuild a model's activations from a much larger set of features, of which only a few are active at a time; the active ones are often interpretable.
Activation patching — replacing the activation of one component in a run with its activation from another run, to test whether that component causes a difference in output.
Induction head — an attention head, working with a previous-token head, that finds an earlier occurrence of the current token and predicts the token that followed it.
Steering — adding or removing a feature direction in the activations to change the model's behaviour.
Attribution — a method that assigns credit for an output to inputs or to internal components.
history: Early interpretability looked at inputs. Simonyan et al. (2013) used gradients of the output with respect to the pixels to draw saliency maps; Ribeiro et al. (2016, LIME), Lundberg and Lee (2017, SHAP) and Sundararajan et al. (2017, integrated gradients) gave methods that assign importance to features. Adebayo et al. (2018) showed that some saliency maps look the same for a trained and a randomly initialised network, so looking reasonable is not evidence of being right. In parallel, Alain and Bengio (2016) introduced linear probes, and Hewitt and Liang (2019) showed that probes need control tasks, since a flexible probe can learn the task on its own.
The mechanistic approach, which tries to reverse-engineer the algorithm inside a network, grew from Chris Olah's work on vision models (feature visualization, 2017) and the Distill "Circuits" thread (Olah et al., 2020), which proposed that networks consist of features connected by weights into circuits. For transformers, Elhage et al. (Anthropic, 2021) gave "A Mathematical Framework for Transformer Circuits", describing attention heads as independent components that read and write the residual stream. Olsson et al. (2022) found induction heads and argued that they are a main source of in-context learning, appearing in a sudden "phase change" during training.
The problem was superposition. Neurons in language models often respond to several unrelated things, so they are not the right unit. Elhage et al. (2022, "Toy Models of Superposition") showed in small models how a network packs many sparse features into fewer dimensions. Cunningham et al. (2023) and Bricken et al. (Anthropic, October 2023, "Towards Monosemanticity") trained sparse autoencoders on activations and found many interpretable features. Templeton et al. (May 2024) scaled this to Claude 3 Sonnet with up to 34 million features and showed that clamping a feature, for example one for the Golden Gate Bridge, changes behaviour. Gao et al. (OpenAI, 2024) scaled SAEs to 16 million latents on GPT-4 activations with a TopK activation, and Google DeepMind released Gemma Scope (2024), a set of open SAEs for Gemma 2.
In 2025, Anthropic published "Circuit Tracing" and "On the Biology of a Large Language Model" (March 2025). They built attribution graphs using cross-layer transcoders (a replacement of the MLPs by interpretable features) for Claude 3.5 Haiku and traced, for example, how the model represents "Texas" internally on the way from "the capital of the state containing Dallas" to "Austin", and how it picks rhyme words before writing a line of a poem. Also in 2024, Arditi et al. found that refusal in several chat models is mediated by a single direction in activation space. Sharkey et al. (2025) published a survey of open problems.
how: The methods fall in a ladder from correlation to causation. At the first level, you ask what information is present. A probe is trained on recorded activations to predict a property (is the sentence true, what is the part of speech, is the user a minor). If a linear probe on layer 20 reaches 95% accuracy, the information is linearly readable there. Linear algebra is the reason this is meaningful: if the property were not encoded as a direction, a linear classifier could not read it. But presence is not use. The model may not use the information, so probes need controls and causal tests.
The geometry of high dimensions explains why a directions-based view is possible at all. Two random unit vectors in d dimensions have a cosine with standard deviation of about \( 1/\sqrt{d} \), which is 0.016 for d = 4,096. So a very large number of directions can be almost orthogonal at the same time, far more than d, and a layer can assign a separate direction to each of many rare features. Reading one feature then picks up only a small amount of noise from the others.
The second level asks which input parts or components mattered. Attribution methods such as gradient times input or integrated gradients give a score per input token. Component attribution uses the residual stream: because every layer adds its output to a shared vector, the final logits are a sum of direct contributions from each head and MLP (plus indirect effects). The "logit lens" projects the residual stream at an intermediate layer through the output layer to see what the model would predict at that stage, and shows predictions forming across layers.
The third level is causal. In activation patching, you run the model on a clean prompt ("The Eiffel Tower is in" → Paris) and on a corrupted prompt (a different landmark), then patch an activation from one run into the other and see if the output changes. A component whose patch restores the clean answer is part of the circuit. The cost is one forward pass per component and position: for 32 layers, 32 heads and 50 token positions, that is about 51,000 runs; attribution patching approximates it using a gradient in two passes. Ablation (setting a component to zero or its average) and steering (adding a direction) are the same idea. With these, researchers decoded circuits such as the indirect-object identification circuit in GPT-2 small, which uses about 26 attention heads in several functional groups.
The fourth level deals with superposition. Individual neurons are polysemantic, so one asks for the right basis: features. A sparse autoencoder takes an activation x of dimension d (for example 4,096) and learns an encoder \( f = \mathrm{ReLU}(W_{\text{enc}} x + b_{\text{enc}}) \) with \( m \gg d \) features (typically 8 to several hundred times more), and a decoder \( \hat{x} = W_{\text{dec}} f + b_{\text{dec}} \). The training loss is the reconstruction error \( \lVert x - \hat{x} \rVert^2 \) plus a penalty \( \lambda \lVert f \rVert_1 \) (or a TopK constraint) that makes the code sparse. The columns of \( W_{\text{dec}} \) are the learned feature directions. Each is then interpreted by looking at the inputs where it fires most, and by testing what steering it does. The rationale: if the true features are sparse and nearly orthogonal, a sparse code in an overcomplete basis can recover them.
The fifth level traces features through the network. Transcoders and cross-layer transcoders replace the MLP blocks by sparse feature layers that approximate them. With these, one builds an attribution graph for one prompt: nodes are active features, edges are the direct causal effects of one feature on another (computed on a frozen, linearised copy of the model, with attention patterns held fixed). Researchers then prune the graph to the part that matters for the output, group features into named steps, and verify the story by intervening on the real model: suppress a feature and see whether the output changes as predicted.
A canonical mechanism shows how the parts combine. Consider a sequence "… Harry Potter … Harry". A previous-token head at layer 0 copies, at each position, a signal "the previous token was X" into the residual stream. An induction head in a later layer, at the current "Harry", forms a query that matches keys carrying "the previous token was Harry". This composition of two heads (K-composition) makes the head attend to the "Potter" position, and its output-value path copies the token identity of "Potter" into the logits. The circuit explains in-context copying, and the loss curve of the model shows it appearing suddenly.

  induction circuit:  ... Harry Potter ... Harry  -> predicts "Potter"
    prev-token head (L0): "Potter" slot stores "previous token = Harry"
    induction head (L1+): query "Harry" matches it, copies "Potter"
example: Superposition with numbers. Let a 2-dimensional layer store 3 features with directions d1 = (1, 0), d2 = (−0.5, 0.866), d3 = (−0.5, −0.866), 120° apart. If only feature 1 is active (strength 1), the activation is x = (1, 0). To read the features, take dot products with each direction: d1·x = 1, d2·x = −0.5, d3·x = −0.5. A ReLU with threshold at 0 zeroes the negative values, so the readout is (1, 0, 0): correct. Three features are stored in two dimensions. Now suppose features 1 and 2 are both active: x = d1 + d2 = (0.5, 0.866). The readouts are d1·x = 0.5, d2·x = 0.5, d3·x = −1.0. Both are detected, but at half strength: they interfere. So superposition works when features are rarely active together. With features that are active 1% of the time, collisions are rare; with 50% they are common.
A sparse autoencoder on the same data. Use the three directions as the encoder rows. For x = (1, 0), the pre-activations are (1, −0.5, −0.5) and f = (1, 0, 0). The decoder gives 1 × d1 = (1, 0): perfect reconstruction with an L1 penalty of 1. For x = (0.5, 0.866), f = (0.5, 0.5, 0), the reconstruction is 0.5 d1 + 0.5 d2 = (0.25, 0.433), and the squared error is 0.0625 + 0.1875 = 0.25. A trained SAE would adjust its weights to reduce such errors for the data it sees, balanced against the sparsity penalty.
Scale of a real SAE. A layer with d = 4,096 and a dictionary of 16 times that, m = 65,536 features, has 2 × 4,096 × 65,536 = 537 million weights, more than the layer it describes. It is trained on billions of activation vectors. Each feature is described by looking at the 20 to 50 inputs where it activates most; automated labelling with a language model reads those examples and writes a description, and a second pass checks it by predicting activations on new text.
Steering. A feature with decoder direction d_f is found to fire on text about a bridge. At some layer, add \( \alpha d_f \) to the residual stream at every position, with α set to a multiple of the feature's typical activation. If the model starts to mention the bridge in unrelated answers, that supports the claim that the direction causally controls the concept. Clamping this feature to a high value in a large model produced a version that kept bringing up the Golden Gate Bridge. This is also a test of the explanation: a feature label that predicts the effect of steering is more credible than one based on top examples alone.
Patching cost. Finding which of 1,024 heads (32 layers × 32 heads) matters for one behaviour by patching each head at each of 50 positions needs 51,200 forward passes, each about 0.2 seconds, so about 3 hours on one GPU. With a gradient-based approximation, the same map costs two forward and one backward pass.
intuition: Neuroscience for artificial networks, with the advantage that every neuron can be recorded, replayed and edited without harm.
A network represents the world as directions in a high-dimensional space, because a dot product is the cheapest way for later layers to read a fact, and a sum is the cheapest way to combine facts. If so, the way to understand the network is to find the directions that mean something, see how they feed each other, and push on them to test the story. The hard part is that the network has no reason to give each concept its own neuron. It packs thousands of rare concepts into overlapping directions, and our tools must undo that packing before the structure becomes readable.
mistakes: People often think a neuron is a concept. In fact many neurons are polysemantic: one neuron may respond to quotation marks, to a type of legal text and to a part of a URL. Concepts live in directions that cut across neurons, which is why sparse dictionaries were developed.
People often think that if a probe can read some information from a layer, the model uses that information. In fact a probe shows only that the information is present and linearly accessible. The model may never use it, or a flexible probe may learn the task itself (Hewitt and Liang, 2019). Causal tests such as patching and steering are needed.
People often think a good-looking explanation, such as a saliency map or a feature's top examples, is a correct one. In fact Adebayo et al. (2018) found saliency maps that did not depend on the trained weights, and top-activating examples can mislead about what a feature really responds to. Explanations should be tested by interventions, which predict outputs on new inputs.
People often think a model's written explanation (chain of thought) tells us what it did. In fact studies on faithfulness show models can give reasons that omit the true factor. Interpretability methods that look at internal states can check such stories, and sometimes disagree with them.
People sometimes think interpretability will soon give a full, human-readable program for a frontier model. In fact current methods explain parts of behaviour on chosen prompts, with significant reconstruction error and manual effort. A complete account of a large model is an open goal.
friction: The methods have clear limits. An SAE does not rebuild activations exactly; substituting its reconstruction into the model raises the language-modelling loss by a noticeable amount, so some of what the model uses is missing from the features, and the part left out is often called "dark matter". Features split into finer ones as the dictionary grows, so there is no single right number of features. Many features are not easily nameable. SAE training costs a noticeable fraction of the compute used to train the model itself, and each layer needs its own SAE.
Attribution graphs explain one prompt at a time, rely on a replacement model that approximates the real model, and need hours of human work to interpret. Anthropic reports that for many prompts the graphs give only partial insight. Findings can fail to generalise from a toy or small model to a large one, and circuits for the same behaviour can differ between models. Different methods can disagree.
There are deeper questions. We lack a way to know if we have found "the" features or just one useful description, and there is a risk of finding what we expected. Interpretability results rarely come with a measurable guarantee, so using them for safety (for example to certify the absence of deception) is hard: the absence of evidence from a method with poor recall is weak. Finally, models change fast, and an analysis of one version may not carry over to the next.
practice: For debugging, start with the cheap tools. Look at the attention patterns and the logit lens for a failing prompt. Train a linear probe on the layer you suspect, with a control task, and see whether the information is present at all. Then do a causal check: patch or ablate the component, or steer along the probe direction, and see whether the behaviour changes. TransformerLens, nnsight and Neuronpedia are common tools for open models, and Gemma Scope and similar open SAEs let you browse features without training one.
If you work with hosted models, use behavioural versions of the same ideas: counterfactual prompts (change one fact and see if the answer changes accordingly), consistency checks between the stated reasoning and the answer, and held-out evaluations for features such as sycophancy or refusal. Probes on open-weight models are used in practice for hallucination detection and for flagging unsafe content, and are cheap enough to run at inference.
When you claim a mechanism, test it: predict the effect of an intervention before doing it, run it on prompts that were not used to find the mechanism, and report how much of the behaviour it explains (for example the fraction of the logit difference recovered by the circuit). Keep a record of the model version, the layer and the SAE used. For studying the field, read the Anthropic Transformer Circuits thread in order (framework, induction heads, toy models, monosemanticity, scaling, biology) and work through the ARENA mechanistic interpretability exercises.
tradeoffs: Choose black-box behavioural testing when you only have API access, when behaviours are easy to specify, and when you need results this week. You give up knowledge of the cause, so you cannot be sure a fix generalises.
Choose probes when you have open weights, labelled examples, and want a fast, cheap detector of a property such as harmful intent or truthfulness. They are easy to run and scale. You give up causal certainty: a probe may track a correlate, and probes can break when the input distribution shifts.
Choose activation patching and ablation when you need to know which components cause a specific behaviour on a defined task. The cost grows with the number of components and positions. Choose attribution patching when you need a quick map of a larger model and accept an approximation.
Choose sparse autoencoders when you want a broad, unsupervised catalogue of features at a layer, to find concepts you did not know to look for. You pay for training (and the SAE's reconstruction error); use pre-trained SAEs if they exist for your model. Choose supervised probes or contrast-based steering vectors when you already know the concept and have examples; they are cheaper and more targeted.
Choose full circuit tracing only for high-value questions about a small number of behaviours; it is manual and slow. Choose steering when you need to change behaviour at inference without retraining, accepting that it can degrade fluency if pushed too hard.
interview: What is superposition, and why does it make interpretation hard? || A network represents more features than it has dimensions by assigning them nearly orthogonal directions, which works because most features are rarely active at once. So single neurons respond to many unrelated things (polysemantic), and the natural unit of analysis is a direction in activation space, not a neuron. Interpretation then needs a method to find those directions, such as sparse autoencoders.
How does a sparse autoencoder work, and how do you know its features are meaningful? || It encodes an activation into many more features with a sparsity penalty (L1 or TopK) and decodes back, minimizing reconstruction error. Features are judged by their top-activating examples, by automated labelling that predicts activations on held-out text, and above all by causal tests: steering the feature changes behaviour as predicted, and ablating it removes the behaviour. You also report reconstruction loss and the model's loss when the SAE output replaces the activation.
Explain how activation patching finds a circuit. What are its limits? || Run a clean and a corrupted prompt; replace one component's activation in the corrupted run with its clean value and measure how much of the clean output returns (for example a logit difference). Components that restore it are causally involved. Limits are the cost (one pass per component and position), redundancy (backup components hide effects when ablated), and out-of-distribution activations created by patching.
A linear probe on layer 12 predicts "the statement is true" with 93% accuracy. Can you conclude the model uses truth to answer? || No. It shows the information is linearly available, not that it is used. The probe might read a correlate, such as topic or style, or learn the task itself. Check against a control task, test generalisation to other datasets, and run a causal test: steer or ablate along the probe direction and see if the model's answers change.
How would you use interpretability to check whether a model is hiding a goal? || Use several methods that do not rely on the model's own statements: probes and SAE features for concepts like deception, evaluated on cases with known ground truth; steering and ablation to see if those features change behaviour; attribution graphs on suspicious outputs; and comparison between the stated reasoning and the internal computation. Treat findings as evidence, not proof, since recall is low and a negative result does not clear the model.
quiz: What is a feature in the linear view of a network? || A human-meaningful property of the input that is represented as a direction in activation space, so that it can be read with a dot product and edited by vector addition.
Why are neurons often not the right unit of analysis? || Because of superposition, a network packs many sparse features into overlapping directions, so a single neuron responds to several unrelated things (polysemantic) and a feature is spread across many neurons.
What does a sparse autoencoder minimize? || The reconstruction error between the original activation and its decoded version, plus a sparsity penalty (an L1 term on the feature activations, or a TopK constraint), using many more features than the activation has dimensions.
Three features at 120° in 2D: features 1 and 2 are active with strength 1. What do the readouts d1·x, d2·x and d3·x give? || x = d1 + d2 = (0.5, 0.866). The readouts are 0.5, 0.5 and −1.0 (the last is cut to 0 by the ReLU): the two active features are found but at half strength, which is interference.
You find a direction that a probe can read, and steering along it changes the model's behaviour as predicted. How strong is this evidence and what else would you test? || It is good evidence of a causal role because the intervention predicted the effect. Also test on held-out prompts, check side effects on unrelated behaviour, try ablating the direction, and compare with random directions of the same norm to rule out a generic perturbation.
