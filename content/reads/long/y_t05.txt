tradeoffs: H100/H200, Blackwell, or a different accelerator. Choose Hopper (H100/H200) when you have working FP8/BF16 code and the model fits in 80 to 141 GB per GPU, because supply is better, rental prices are lower (often a third to half of Blackwell per hour) and the software is mature. Choose Blackwell (B200, GB200 NVL72) when the model is large enough that a 72-GPU NVLink domain removes cross-node tensor-parallel traffic, or when inference cost per token matters more than the hourly price: FP4 and 192 GB of HBM3e at about 8 TB/s can cut cost per token by a large factor on memory-bound decoding. You give up, by going newer, price per hour, power (over 1 kW per GPU, racks above 100 kW with liquid cooling that many data centres cannot host) and kernels that are less tuned in the first months.
NVIDIA or TPU/Trainium/AMD. Choose NVIDIA when the team uses PyTorch with custom kernels, needs to hire people who know CUDA, or runs many different model shapes, because the CUDA stack and NCCL are the most complete and a rented H100 is available from almost every cloud. Choose a TPU when you train or serve a few large, stable architectures on Google Cloud and can accept JAX/XLA, because systolic arrays and a 3D torus network are cheap per FLOP at very large scale. Choose AMD Instinct when memory capacity per dollar matters (192 to 288 GB per GPU) and you have engineers to debug ROCm. You give up, off NVIDIA, the ecosystem moat and the default of "it just works"; on NVIDIA you give up price (a high margin) and supply certainty.
Buy, reserve, or rent on demand. A rule of thumb: an H100 costs roughly 25 to 40 thousand US dollars to buy, plus a similar amount for the server, network and power share; renting is about 2 to 4 dollars per GPU-hour. If utilization is above about 60 to 70% for a year or more, owning or a 1 to 3 year reservation wins; for bursty research with 20 to 30% utilization, on-demand or spot wins even at a higher hourly rate. You give up flexibility when you reserve, and you take hardware failure, power and ops risk when you own.
Training precision: BF16, FP8, or FP4. BF16 is the safe default with a wide range and almost no tuning. FP8 doubles tensor-core throughput on Hopper and Blackwell and is worth the work for runs above roughly 10^23 FLOPs, where a 1.3x speedup saves weeks, but it needs per-tensor or per-block scale factors and monitoring for loss spikes. FP4 (NVFP4) is mainly proven for inference and weights; for training it is still research-grade. You give up numerical headroom and debuggability for each halving of bits.
Scale up (more GPUs per NVLink domain) or scale out (more nodes over InfiniBand). Tensor parallelism and expert parallelism need hundreds of GB/s per GPU, so keep them inside one NVLink domain (8 GPUs, or 72 in NVL72); data and pipeline parallelism tolerate the 50 GB/s of a 400 Gbit/s NIC. If your model needs tensor parallelism of 16 or more, an NVL72 rack avoids a 15x bandwidth drop, but you pay for a rack-scale system and a single failure domain of 72 GPUs.
interview: Your team has a fixed budget. Do you buy H100s or B200s? || I compare cost per useful token or per training run, not price per GPU. I estimate model memory (weights, KV cache, optimizer state) to see if it fits, estimate the FLOPs from 6ND for training, and use measured utilization (30 to 50% for training) for both. If inference is memory-bound, B200's 8 TB/s and FP4 give more tokens per dollar even at a higher price; if the model fits easily on H100 and supply or power is limited, H100 may be cheaper in total.
Why does a kernel reach only 20% of peak TFLOP/s? || Probably it is below the ridge point (about 295 FLOP per byte on H100 for dense BF16), so HBM bandwidth limits it, or warps stall on memory latency, or divergence and poor occupancy waste lanes. I would profile with Nsight Compute, check achieved bandwidth against 3.35 TB/s, then raise arithmetic intensity with tiling, fusion or FlashAttention-style kernels, or a larger batch.
Why is tensor parallelism kept inside a server or rack? || Each layer needs an all-reduce of activations twice per forward and backward, on the critical path. NVLink gives 900 GB/s (Hopper) to 1.8 TB/s (Blackwell) per GPU against about 50 GB/s from a NIC, so crossing nodes would leave GPUs idle. Data parallel gradient all-reduce can overlap with backward compute, so it can go over InfiniBand.
Datasheet says 4 PFLOPS FP8 on one GPU, but your run measures far less. What do you check? || The headline may assume 2:4 sparsity (the dense number is half). Then I check the actual precision used by the kernels, MFU (usually 30 to 50% at best), data loading, communication that does not overlap with compute, and thermal or power throttling.
A training job on 4,000 GPUs fails every few hours. How do you design for it? || At this scale a failure of some GPU, NIC or link every few hours is normal. I would checkpoint asynchronously every 10 to 30 minutes to fast storage, keep spare nodes for quick replacement, detect stragglers and slow links with health checks, and make restart automatic. The checkpoint cost must be small compared with the loss of work between checkpoints.
@@+ commitlog
tradeoffs: A log or a queue or a database table. Choose a log (Kafka, Pulsar, Redpanda, Kinesis) when several independent consumers need the same ordered data, when you want to replay (reprocess last week after a bug fix), or when the rate is above roughly 10 thousand records per second and you want sequential disk I/O. Choose a classic queue (SQS, RabbitMQ) when each message is a task done by one worker, you need per-message acknowledgement, retries with delay, or dead-letter handling, and replay is not needed. Choose a database table as a queue only below about a few hundred messages per second, where one transactional system is worth more than the extra service. You give up, with a log, per-message acks and priorities (a slow record blocks its partition) and you take on partition planning; with a queue, replay and fan-out to many readers.
Partition count. More partitions give more parallelism (one consumer per partition at most) and more throughput, since one partition takes about 10 to 50 MB/s of writes. But each partition costs file handles, replication traffic, longer leader election after a broker failure and more end-to-end latency, and you can add partitions later only by breaking per-key order for existing keys. A practical rule: size for peak throughput divided by about 10 MB/s per partition, then multiply by 1.5 to 2 for growth; most topics need 6 to 50, and a topic needing thousands is a sign to split it. Fewer partitions give up parallelism; more give up operability.
Durability: acks and replication. With replication factor 3, min.insync.replicas=2 and acks=all, a write survives the loss of one broker with no acknowledged data lost, at the price of the latency of the second-slowest replica (a few ms in one zone, 1 to 3 ms more across zones, plus cross-zone network cost). acks=1 is faster but a leader crash can lose acknowledged records; use it for metrics and logs where a loss of 0.01% is fine. Stretching across regions (more than 50 ms round trip) breaks the latency of acks=all, so use asynchronous mirroring and accept a recovery point objective of seconds.
Where to keep old data: disk retention, tiered storage, compaction, or a lakehouse. Local retention of 7 days at 100 MB/s is about 60 TB times replication 3; tiered storage keeps hours locally and the rest in object storage at about a tenth of the cost, with slower reads (tens to hundreds of ms for the first byte). Compaction fits state (latest value per key, such as a user profile), not events (a purchase must not be merged away). For long history and analytics, land the stream in Delta or Iceberg and keep the log short. Each choice gives up something: compaction loses history, tiering loses read latency, a long log loses cheap brokers.
Which implementation. Kafka is the default for ecosystem and tooling (Connect, Streams, Schema Registry). Redpanda suits teams that want a Kafka API with fewer moving parts and lower tail latency on fewer, bigger machines; Pulsar suits many tenants and topics with separated compute and storage; Kinesis suits an AWS team that wants no brokers and accepts limits of 1 MB/s or 1,000 records per second per shard. Object-storage-native logs trade 200 to 500 ms of latency for much lower cross-zone cost.
interview: When would you not use a commit log? || When each item is a job for one worker needing retries, delays or priorities (use a queue), when the system needs low-latency random reads or updates by key (use a database or key-value store), or when volume is tiny and one more cluster costs more than it returns. A log is the right tool for ordered fan-out and replay, not for everything.
How do you pick a partition key and what goes wrong? || The key should be the entity whose events must stay in order (user ID, order ID) with enough distinct values to spread the load. A skewed key (one huge customer, or null keys) creates a hot partition whose consumer lags while others idle. I would measure bytes per partition, salt the very hot keys if their order does not matter, and avoid changing partition count for keyed topics because it remaps keys.
A leader broker dies. What exactly is lost and what is the recovery time? || With acks=all and min ISR 2, no acknowledged record is lost, because a new leader is chosen from the in-sync set. Clients see errors for the election time (seconds with KRaft; longer if thousands of partitions were led by that broker), then retry; idempotent producers prevent duplicates from retries. With acks=1 or unclean leader election, the tail of the log can be lost.
Consumers read a topic and write to a database. How do you get no duplicates? || The log gives at-least-once delivery to the consumer after retries or rebalances. I would make the write idempotent (upsert keyed by topic, partition, offset or business key) or store the offset in the same database transaction as the result. Kafka transactions give exactly-once only for log-to-log pipelines, not for an external database.
Sizing: 50,000 records per second of 1 KB, retention 7 days, replication 3. What cluster? || Ingest is 50 MB/s, so about 5 to 10 partitions per topic suffices for write throughput, but pick more for consumer parallelism. Storage is 50 MB/s × 604,800 s ≈ 30 TB, times 3 replicas ≈ 90 TB, so for example 6 brokers with 20 TB disks at 75% fill; network is 50 MB/s × 3 for replication plus consumers, which is easy. Tiered storage would reduce the local disk need to hours.
@@+ streamproc
tradeoffs: Stream or batch. Choose streaming when the answer loses value within minutes (fraud scoring, frequency caps, alerts, live features) and the cost of a late answer is higher than the cost of running a cluster all day. Choose batch (hourly or daily) when a freshness of 15 minutes to a day is acceptable, because a batch job uses machines only while it runs, is simpler to rerun and test, and a 1-hour batch costs often a fifth to a half of an always-on stream for the same logic. A middle path is micro-batch or incremental refresh (Spark Structured Streaming with a 1 to 5 minute trigger, Delta Live Tables, materialized views), which gives minute-level freshness without the operating burden of a low-latency engine. You give up, with streaming, cheap reruns and simple debugging; with batch, freshness.
Which engine. Choose Flink when you need sub-second latency, large keyed state (hundreds of GB in RocksDB), event-time windows with fine control, or complex event patterns. Choose Spark Structured Streaming when the team already runs Spark or Databricks, latency of 1 to 10 seconds is fine, and you want the same code and cluster for batch and streaming. Choose Kafka Streams (or ksqlDB) when the logic is a service-sized job reading and writing Kafka, you want no separate cluster, and state is below tens of GB per instance. You give up, with Flink, a second platform to run (checkpoints, savepoints, upgrades); with Spark, p99 latency below about a second; with Kafka Streams, flexible scaling and non-Kafka sources.
Delivery guarantee. At-least-once with idempotent writes (upsert by key) is the simplest and cheapest, and covers most cases. Effectively-once through checkpoints and a transactional sink adds latency equal to the checkpoint interval (commit on checkpoint, so 1 to 10 seconds of delay for visible output) and needs a sink that supports two-phase commit or atomic files. Exactly-once is not worth paying for when the result is an approximate aggregate or when downstream deduplicates anyway; it is worth it for money and counters that cannot be rebuilt cheaply.
Lateness and state size. A longer allowed lateness and watermark delay makes results more complete but holds state longer and delays the first result by the same time: with a 5-minute watermark delay every window emits 5 minutes late. A rule: measure the delay distribution of your sources, set the watermark to cover the 95th to 99th percentile, and send the rest to a side output for a batch correction. For keyed state, estimate keys × bytes per key; state above memory means RocksDB, and above about 1 TB per job means rethinking the design (shorter TTL, a different key, or a store like Pinot or a database for the history).
Parallelism and checkpoint interval. Parallelism is set by the busiest key group or source partition, so more tasks than Kafka partitions do nothing for a source. A short checkpoint interval (10 to 30 seconds) means quick recovery and low sink latency but uses more I/O; a long one (5 to 10 minutes) is cheaper but a failure replays more data and a large state takes longer to restore. Choose the interval so that replay time after a failure is within your freshness target.
interview: Your Flink job is lagging by 30 minutes and growing. How do you find the cause? || I check backpressure to find the slowest operator, usually the one just before the first fully busy-then-blocked stage. Then I look for a hot key (one task at 100% while others idle), slow state access in RocksDB, an external call without batching or async I/O, GC pauses, or a sink throttling. If the load simply grew, I raise parallelism within the Kafka partition count and check that checkpoints still finish in time.
How do you choose the watermark delay? || From the measured lateness of events: compute the distribution of processing time minus event time per source, set the delay near the 99th percentile, and handle the remaining late events with allowed lateness or a side output. A longer delay raises completeness but adds the same latency to every result, and idle partitions must be marked idle or they hold the watermark back.
How would you do a 30-day unique-user count over a stream with 500 million users? || Exact distinct state would be 500 million × about 16 bytes = 8 GB per window slice, and it would need many slices. I would use HyperLogLog sketches (about 12 KB each at 1% error) per day and merge 30 of them at read time, which makes the state tiny and the merge cheap. If an exact count is legally needed, compute it in a daily batch job.
Why can you not just reprocess the stream after a bug fix? || You can only if the source is replayable: Kafka retention must cover the period, or the data must exist in a lake. Replay also needs a deterministic job (event time, not processing time) and idempotent sinks, otherwise old and new results mix. For long reprocessing I would run the same logic as a batch backfill on the lake and then switch the stream to the right offset.
Streaming or micro-batch for 2-second latency? || Micro-batch on Spark gives 1 to 5 second end-to-end with good throughput and simple operations; a record-at-a-time engine like Flink is better for 100 ms to 1 s targets and for large timers and state. Below that, a plain service with a log consumer may do. I would decide from the p99 requirement and team skills rather than from fashion.
@@+ batchproc
tradeoffs: Batch or stream or incremental. Choose batch when data is complete before you need it (daily billing, month-end, model training sets), when correctness at one fixed point matters more than minute-level freshness, or when the logic is a big join or sort over history. A 4 TB scan at 500 MB/s per node on 200 nodes finishes in a minute or so of scan time plus shuffle, and the cluster is released afterwards. Choose streaming when results older than a few minutes lose value. Choose incremental processing (process only new partitions or changed rows with MERGE, Delta change feed, or materialized views) when the full recompute is hours long but only 1 to 5% of the data changes per day. You give up, with batch, freshness; with incremental, simplicity and sometimes exactness on late updates.
Engine choice: Spark, SQL warehouse, or something lighter. Choose Spark (or Databricks) for jobs above roughly 100 GB to several TB, for complex Python/Scala logic and for ML preparation. Choose a SQL warehouse or lakehouse SQL engine when the logic is joins and aggregations that SQL expresses well, because the optimizer and vectorized engines (Photon, Snowflake, BigQuery) often beat hand-written code and need less tuning. Choose DuckDB, Polars or a single large machine for data below about 100 to 500 GB, where a 64-core machine avoids shuffle and cluster startup entirely and can be 3 to 10 times cheaper. You give up, with a cluster, startup time (minutes) and shuffle cost; with one machine, the ability to scale beyond its memory and disk.
Cluster size and partitions. More machines shorten the scan but not the serial parts (planning, listing millions of files, the biggest skewed task, the final commit), so by Amdahl's law the gain flattens: if 5% of the job is serial, 100 nodes give at most about 17x, not 100x. A practical target is 100 to 200 MB per partition and 2 to 4 waves of tasks per stage; too few partitions means spills and memory errors, too many (millions of tiny tasks) means scheduler overhead. Autoscaling saves money on jobs with a heavy early stage and a light later stage, but costs a few minutes at each scale-up.
Join strategy. Broadcast the small side when it is below a few hundred MB, because it avoids shuffling the large table entirely. Use a shuffle sort-merge join when both are large, and fix skew with adaptive query execution, key salting, or a separate path for the few hot keys. Pre-partitioned or bucketed tables remove the shuffle for repeated joins on the same key at the cost of rigid layout and write-time work.
Fail-and-retry or checkpoint. Lineage-based retry is cheap for jobs under a few hours; for a 10-hour multi-stage pipeline, write intermediate results to a table at stage boundaries so a failure costs one stage, not the whole run. Use spot instances for batch when tasks are retried safely (30 to 70% cheaper), and keep the driver and shuffle service on stable nodes. You give up extra storage and a slightly longer run for restartability.
interview: A nightly job that took 2 hours now takes 6. What do you check? || First the Spark UI for the slowest stage and its task time distribution: one task far above the median means skew from a hot key or a new null-heavy partition. Then input growth (file count, small files, partition pruning no longer working), shuffle spill, a join that changed from broadcast to sort-merge because a table crossed the threshold, and resource changes such as fewer executors or spot loss.
How do you fix a skewed join? || Confirm skew from the task time and shuffle read size distribution. Use adaptive skew-join handling if available; otherwise salt the hot key (split it into N sub-keys on the big side and replicate the small side N times), broadcast the small side, or handle the few hot keys separately and union the results. Filtering null or default keys before the join is often the whole problem.
When is Spark the wrong tool? || When data fits on one machine (a few hundred GB), where DuckDB or Polars is faster and cheaper; when the job is a lookup or point query workload; or when latency in seconds is needed. Also when the logic is plain SQL that a warehouse engine runs better. Spark's strengths are large data, flexible code and ML pipelines.
How do you make a batch job safe to rerun? || Make each run a pure function of its input partition and write output with an overwrite of one partition or an atomic MERGE keyed by business key, not an append. Avoid using the current time inside the logic; pass the run date as a parameter. Then a retry or backfill produces the same table, and a duplicate trigger does no harm.
Estimate the cost and time to process 20 TB with a shuffle-heavy job. || At about 128 MB per partition that is about 160,000 tasks; on 2,000 cores that is about 80 waves. If each task takes 60 seconds, the stage is around 80 minutes, and shuffle might double it, so roughly 2 to 3 hours on 2,000 cores, about 5,000 core-hours. At 0.05 dollars per core-hour on spot this is about 250 dollars; I would validate with a 1% sample run and check shuffle size before scaling.
@@+ lambdakappa
tradeoffs: Lambda, Kappa, or a lakehouse hybrid. Choose Lambda only when the real-time path and the batch path truly differ in what they need: for example the speed layer gives an approximate count in 2 seconds and the batch layer a fully reconciled, billing-grade number 6 hours later, and the business accepts that the number changes. Choose Kappa when the logic is the same in both paths, the log can hold the full history you need to replay (for example 30 days of Kafka, or years with tiered storage), and one team can own one code path. Choose the hybrid (stream into bronze, incremental silver and gold, batch backfill with the same code) when you already have a lakehouse; this is the common answer in 2026. You give up, with Lambda, one code path and consistent numbers; with Kappa, cheap scans of years of history and the freedom to use SQL batch engines for reprocessing; with the hybrid, pure simplicity, since both modes must stay equivalent.
Cost of reprocessing. In Kappa, a replay must read the whole retained log through a stream engine at a speed limited by partitions and state. Replaying 90 days of 50 MB/s is about 390 TB; at 1 GB/s that takes over 4 days, whereas a batch engine reading Parquet on 500 nodes finishes in under an hour. So choose log replay when history is under a few TB or a few days of work, and a batch backfill over lake files when it is larger. The cost you accept is two execution modes that must give the same results, and a test that proves it (run both on a day of data and diff).
Where correctness comes from. If the stream has exactly-once state, event-time windows and idempotent sinks, a speed layer is no longer inaccurate and Lambda's second code path buys little. If the source data itself is revised (late corrections, a vendor restating last week), you need a periodic batch reconciliation anyway, whichever name you give the design. Choose a nightly reconciliation job that overwrites partitions when more than about 0.1 to 1% of events arrive after your streaming lateness limit.
Serving. Lambda's serving layer merges a batch view and a real-time view at query time, which means a more complex query path and a visible jump when batch replaces speed numbers. Kappa-style serving reads one table. For sub-second dashboards at high concurrency, serve from an OLAP store such as Pinot or Druid fed by the stream; for analyst queries, serve from lakehouse tables. You give up, with an OLAP store, a second copy of data and its cost.
Retention policy. A Kappa log kept 7 days protects against a bug fixed within a week but not a defect found a quarter later; keep the raw events in the lakehouse (bronze) forever at about 20 to 25 dollars per TB-month in object storage, and keep the log short. Long Kafka retention on local disk of 3 replicas costs roughly 10 times more per TB; tiered storage narrows that gap.
interview: Is Lambda dead? || Not the idea, only the strict two-codebase form. Teams still keep a fast path and a slow, corrected path, but now they share logic through one engine (Spark or Flink in both modes), one table format, and a batch backfill that overwrites partitions. The reason is not fashion: late and corrected data exist, and some numbers must be exact.
Your streaming numbers differ from the nightly batch numbers by 0.5%. How do you find out why? || Compare the same day's partitions by key and find which keys differ. Common causes are late events past the watermark, different deduplication rules, different time zones or event-time versus processing-time assignment, filters applied in one path only, and non-idempotent sink retries. Then decide whether to fix the stream, widen the lateness, or declare the batch result as the official one and publish the stream number as provisional.
How would you backfill 6 months of data with a new logic after a bug? || Run the same transformation in batch mode over the bronze lake tables, write into a new table version or overwrite partitions atomically, validate against the old output on a sample, then swap readers. In the meantime, the stream keeps running, so I must make the handover point precise (an offset or a timestamp) and avoid double counting at the seam.
What does Kappa require from the log and the stream engine? || A log with enough retention and partitions to replay in a useful time, order within a key, and replayable offsets; an engine with checkpointed state, event-time semantics and idempotent or transactional sinks; and capacity to run the old and new job versions side by side during the switch. Without these, reprocessing is risky.
Why not stream everything? || Streaming costs always-on compute, state management, on-call burden and harder testing. If users read the number once a day, an hourly or daily batch is 3 to 10 times cheaper and easier. I would stream only where freshness has a measured business value, and use incremental batch elsewhere.
@@+ cdc
tradeoffs: Log-based CDC, query-based polling, triggers, or an outbox. Choose log-based CDC (Debezium on the MySQL binlog or PostgreSQL logical slot, or a managed DMS/Datastream) when you need every change including deletes, in commit order, with seconds of latency and no extra query load; the price is access to the replication log, a connector to operate and a strict watch on WAL growth. Choose polling on an updated_at column only when deletes are rare or soft, a delay of minutes is fine, the column is indexed and reliably set, and the table is under a few hundred million rows. Avoid triggers except on legacy databases without log access, because they add write latency (often 10 to 30%) inside every transaction. Choose the outbox pattern when consumers need business events (OrderPlaced) rather than row changes, because it makes the event contract explicit and decouples consumers from your table schema.
Row-change events or business events. Raw CDC exposes your private table layout, so every column rename becomes a breaking change for all consumers. Publishing row changes is fine for replication into a lake or search index owned by data teams; it is a poor public API between services. Choose an outbox or an event published by the service for cross-team contracts; choose CDC for copies of state. You give up, with CDC, schema independence; with outbox, a second write path and some table overhead.
Initial snapshot and ongoing load. A snapshot of a 2 TB table can take hours and hold a long transaction that blocks vacuum (PostgreSQL) or fills undo (MySQL). Choose incremental snapshots (the Debezium watermark-based method) or take the snapshot from a read replica to keep load off the primary. For very large tables, load the bulk from a database backup or export into the lake and start CDC from the recorded position; the cost is care at the seam.
Delivery guarantees. CDC delivers at least once, so choose idempotent sinks: upsert by primary key, ordered by LSN or commit timestamp, with the last-write-wins rule. For a lakehouse, MERGE INTO from a batch of changes with a deduplication on the latest LSN per key, in micro-batches of 1 to 5 minutes; smaller batches create many small files, larger batches add latency. Deletes need tombstones or an is_deleted flag, and a retention long enough for consumers to see them.
Replication slot risk and capacity. An idle or slow consumer makes PostgreSQL keep WAL on disk without bound; at 20 MB/s of WAL, a slot stalled for a day holds about 1.7 TB and can take the primary down. Set alerts on slot lag and max_slot_wal_keep_size, accept that the slot may be dropped (with a new snapshot as the recovery), and size throughput: one connector task handles tens of thousands of row changes per second, and a single table is not split across tasks, so a hot table limits throughput.
interview: Why CDC rather than a nightly dump or dual writes from the application? || A dump is stale and misses deletes unless compared in full, and polling puts load on the database. Dual writes can leave systems inconsistent when one write fails, with no atomicity across them. CDC reads the committed log, so the order and content equal what the database committed, and the application only writes once.
What happens to CDC during a schema change or a primary failover? || A column add or drop must reach consumers through a schema registry with compatibility rules, or the pipeline breaks; I would test additive changes first and use a staged rollout for breaking ones. On failover, the connector must resume at the right position on the new primary (GTIDs in MySQL; slots on the new primary in PostgreSQL need a version and setup that preserve them), and otherwise I take a new snapshot and deduplicate.
How do you keep the order of changes to one row? || Key the Kafka messages by primary key so all changes land in one partition, and keep a single connector reading the log. Consumers must not reorder with parallel threads for the same key. Across tables, the transaction boundary is available through transaction metadata events, but order between partitions is not guaranteed.
The downstream table is missing 0.01% of rows. Where do you look? || Check the seam between snapshot and stream (rows changed during the snapshot), connector restarts with a wrong offset, filtered tables or columns, deletes dropped by the sink, and failed MERGE batches. I would reconcile with counts and a checksum per key range between source and target, then replay from a known LSN.
How would you handle a GDPR delete in a CDC pipeline into a lakehouse? || The delete event must propagate as a row delete through the MERGE, and then physical removal needs VACUUM or compaction after the retention window, plus removal from the Kafka topics (tombstone and compaction, or topic retention), backups, and derived tables. I would keep a user-ID index so I can find all copies and prove deletion within the legal deadline.
@@+ lakehouse
tradeoffs: Lakehouse or warehouse. Choose a lakehouse (Delta, Iceberg or Hudi on object storage) when data is large (tens of TB and up), varied (logs, JSON, images, features), used by several engines, and when you want to own the files; storage costs about 20 to 25 dollars per TB-month versus several times that in a managed warehouse. Choose a warehouse (Snowflake, BigQuery, Redshift) when most users write SQL, you want little operations work, and workloads need strong concurrency and sub-second to seconds response with automatic tuning. Modern warehouses can read open tables too, so the question is increasingly who runs the engine. You give up, with a lakehouse, some tuning convenience (small files, clustering and compaction are yours or your vendor's job); with a warehouse, portability and some price control.
Delta, Iceberg, or Hudi. Choose Delta when the platform is Databricks or Spark-heavy and you want the simplest log-based design with deletion vectors, liquid clustering and tight integration. Choose Iceberg when several engines (Trino, Flink, Snowflake, Spark) must write, you want a vendor-neutral REST catalog, hidden partitioning and partition evolution without rewriting data. Choose Hudi when record-level upserts and incremental pulls on a CDC feed are the main workload. UniForm and cross-format readers reduce the penalty of picking wrong, but write support stays strongest in each format's home engine.
Copy-on-write or merge-on-read. Copy-on-write rewrites each file with a changed row: reads are fast and simple, writes are expensive. If an update touches 1% of rows scattered across 100% of files, copy-on-write rewrites nearly the whole table. Merge-on-read (or deletion vectors) writes small delete markers and delta files so updates are 5 to 20 times cheaper, but reads must merge them until compaction runs. Choose copy-on-write for read-heavy tables updated in batches; choose merge-on-read or deletion vectors for frequent small updates, such as CDC upserts and GDPR deletes.
File size, partitioning and clustering. Target 128 MB to 1 GB files; streaming writes produce many small files that slow planning and scans (listing and opening 100,000 files adds minutes). Partition by a low-cardinality column such as date only when the partition holds at least 1 GB; over-partitioning (by user ID) creates millions of tiny files. Prefer clustering (Z-order, liquid clustering) on high-cardinality filter columns, which gives data skipping at a cost of rewrite work. You give up write speed and compute for read speed.
Concurrency and latency limits. Commits are optimistic, so a table accepts only a few commits per second and concurrent writers touching the same files conflict and retry. Choose fewer, larger commits (micro-batches of 30 seconds to minutes), separate tables or partitions for independent writers, and a different store (Pinot, a database) for sub-second freshness or point lookups. Lakehouse reads on cold object storage have first-byte latency of tens of milliseconds, so interactive dashboards need caching or a serving layer.
interview: A table has thousands of small files and queries slowed. What do you do and why did it happen? || Streaming or frequent micro-batch writes create small files; I would run compaction (OPTIMIZE or rewrite_data_files) to about 512 MB to 1 GB, enable optimized writes or auto compaction, lengthen the trigger interval, and vacuum old files. Then I would check partitioning, because too fine a partition is a common root cause.
Why can two engines safely write to the same Iceberg table but not to a plain Parquet directory? || A table format makes a commit a single atomic pointer swap in a catalog, with a conflict check, so readers see either the old or the new snapshot. In a bare directory there is no atomic multi-file visibility, so readers see half-written outputs and writers overwrite one another.
Delta or Iceberg for a company using Spark, Trino and Snowflake? || I would lean to Iceberg with a REST catalog for the multi-engine write needs and governance; if Databricks is the main engine and other engines only read, Delta with UniForm is simpler. I would weigh the write features each engine really supports, catalog and governance integration, and how much migration costs, not the format's marketing.
How do you serve a dashboard with 500 concurrent users and 1-second response from a lakehouse? || Probably not directly from raw tables: use a warehouse or serverless SQL with caching and result reuse, aggregated gold tables, or push a rollup to an OLAP engine such as Pinot or ClickHouse. I would measure the p95 under the real concurrency, since scan-based engines slow under load.
How do you delete a user's data and also keep time travel? || Delete the rows (with deletion vectors or rewrite), then physically remove old files with VACUUM after a retention window that satisfies the legal deadline, which limits time travel to that window. I would document the policy and keep protected derived tables free of personal data.
@@+ medallion
tradeoffs: Three layers or fewer or more. Choose the full bronze, silver, gold when several teams consume the data, sources are messy (JSON, changing schemas, duplicates) and you need replay and audit; each extra hop costs storage (often 1.5 to 3 times the raw size across layers), compute and pipeline latency (a few minutes per hop for streaming, one scheduling slot for batch). Choose two layers (raw and curated) for a small team with one or two sources where a third copy is mostly duplication. Add layers (a platinum or feature layer, or domain-specific marts) only when a consumer needs a different contract, such as a feature store with point-in-time correctness. You give up, with fewer layers, a clean place to fix mistakes; with more, simplicity and ownership clarity.
What belongs in bronze. Store source data as received (raw payload, ingestion time, source offset or file name), with schema-on-read or minimal parsing, so that a later parsing bug is fixable by recompute. Parsing in bronze (explode JSON into columns) saves compute later and gives data skipping, but a bad parse then loses data; a common compromise is to keep the raw string column and add parsed columns, and to send rows that fail parsing to a quarantine table rather than dropping them. Retention: keep bronze as long as the legal and recompute needs justify, because it is the cheapest copy to hold in object storage (about 20 to 25 dollars per TB-month) and the most expensive to lose.
Where to deduplicate and enforce quality. Deduplicate in silver by business key and event time, using MERGE or a window with a bounded lookback (for example 7 days), because unbounded dedup keeps state or scans forever. Choose expectations that drop bad rows only for fields that make a row meaningless (null primary key); choose quarantine for fields that are merely suspicious. Hard failure of the pipeline on any violation protects accuracy but turns one bad source file into a full outage; the right level depends on the consumer, and for money data failing is correct.
Streaming, incremental or full refresh. Choose streaming or incremental (Delta Live Tables, Structured Streaming with availableNow, materialized views) when sources are append-only and you want minutes of freshness at a cost proportional to new data. Choose full refresh of gold when the logic includes non-incremental calculations (a percentile over a year, a rank across all rows) and the table is small enough to rebuild in the available window. Gold tables with late-arriving data need a partition rewrite for the last N days: choose N from your measured lateness (often 3 to 7 days).
Gold: one wide table or many marts. A wide denormalized gold table gives analysts simple fast queries and easy data skipping, at the cost of duplication and a rebuild when a dimension changes. Star schemas keep the dimensions single-sourced and cheaper to update but need joins at query time. For BI dashboards with hundreds of users, build gold tables per use case with a documented owner and freshness SLA; for exploration, give silver access with governance. Too many gold tables with overlapping metrics is the main failure, so keep metric definitions in one semantic layer.
interview: Why not go straight from the source to gold? || A direct path means every logic bug or source change requires going back to the source, which may not keep history or may be rate-limited; there is also no shared clean entity for other teams, so each builds its own cleaning and gets different numbers. Bronze gives replay and audit, silver gives a reusable contract, and gold gives performance and stable business definitions.
Where do you handle late and duplicate events? || Duplicates: in silver, by business key or event ID with a bounded window, written as an idempotent MERGE. Late data: bronze simply appends, silver upserts by key, and gold partitions for the last N days are recomputed or merged. N comes from measured lateness. I would also track the share of events outside N and send it to a correction job.
A bad transformation went to production for 3 days. How do you recover? || Because bronze is intact, I fix the logic, then recompute silver and gold for the affected partitions with the same code in batch mode, writing atomically (overwrite partitions or MERGE) so readers see the old or the fixed data, not a mix. I would use time travel to compare before and after, notify consumers of the changed numbers, and add a data test that would have caught it.
How do you design data quality checks across the layers? || Bronze: file or message count, freshness and schema drift checks, with no business rules. Silver: key uniqueness, not-null on keys, type and range checks, referential checks with quarantine tables. Gold: reconciliation against a source of truth (totals against billing), and metric sanity bands. Failed checks should block publishing to the next layer only for critical rules.
How do you control cost in a medallion pipeline? || Process incrementally rather than recompute, partition and cluster by the columns consumers filter on, compact small files, vacuum old versions with a retention that suits audit needs, and tier bronze history to colder storage. @@+ olap
tradeoffs: Which engine for analytical queries. Choose a cloud warehouse or lakehouse SQL engine (Snowflake, BigQuery, Databricks SQL) for internal analysts: seconds-level queries, tens of concurrent users, flexible joins, nothing to run. Choose ClickHouse when you need very fast scans and aggregations on a few big tables (billions of rows in 100 ms to a few seconds), can accept limited joins and manage a cluster. Choose Pinot or Druid for user-facing analytics with hundreds to thousands of queries per second at p99 below 100 to 500 ms on fresh data. Choose DuckDB or Postgres with a columnar extension for data under about 100 GB to 1 TB. You give up, with the specialised stores, ad hoc SQL freedom (joins, window functions) and a second copy of the data; with the warehouse, tail latency under high concurrency and cost per query at scale.
Pre-compute or compute at query time. Raw scans with vectorized columnar execution handle roughly 1 to 10 GB/s per core, so a 1 TB table touching two columns (about 50 GB) scans in a few seconds on a cluster of 50 cores. If 1,000 users issue the same group-by every minute, scanning is wasteful: choose rollups, materialized views or a star-tree index, which cut a query from seconds to milliseconds. You give up flexibility (the pre-aggregation answers only the dimensions you chose), storage (often 2 to 5 times with several rollups) and freshness, since rollups lag. Use rollups when the set of dimensions is known and stable.
Star schema, wide table, or normalized. A wide denormalized table avoids joins and works best on columnar engines (the repeated values compress well, often 10 to 50 times, thanks to dictionary and run-length encoding), but dimension changes require rewriting facts. A star schema keeps dimensions small and consistent; with broadcast joins of small dimensions it costs little. Choose wide tables for engines with weak joins (Pinot, Druid, older ClickHouse); choose star for warehouses with a good optimizer. Normalized third-normal-form schemas belong in OLTP, not here.
Exact or approximate aggregates. Distinct counts on a billion rows need memory per group; HyperLogLog sketches (about 12 KB per sketch, 1 to 2% error) or theta sketches (that also support set operations) cut cost by a factor of 10 to 100 and can be merged across segments and time. Approximate percentiles (t-digest, KLL) behave the same. Choose exact counts for billing and finance, and sketches for dashboards, where a 1% difference changes no decision. State the error in the UI to avoid disputes.
Partitioning and sort order. Partition by time (day or hour) so old data can be dropped or skipped, and sort or cluster by the column used in most filters; a good sort order can reduce data read by 10 to 100 times through min/max pruning. Over-partitioning creates small files and metadata overhead; under-partitioning forces wide scans. Choose the sort key from the actual query log, and revisit it when queries change.
interview: A dashboard query over 2 TB takes 40 seconds. How do you make it 1 second? || Read the plan: which columns and how much data are scanned. Make sure the filter prunes partitions or files (sort or cluster on the filter column), select only needed columns, and replace the heavy group-by with a materialized view or rollup if the dimensions are fixed. If concurrency is high, move to a store built for it (Pinot, ClickHouse) or cache results. Estimate the floor first: scanned bytes divided by cluster scan bandwidth.
Why do columnar formats help OLAP? || Queries touch few columns, so a column store reads only those, compresses similar values well (dictionary, run-length, delta), allows vectorized processing and min/max skipping. A row store reads whole rows, so a query on 3 of 100 columns reads about 30 times more data than needed. The cost is slow single-row writes and point updates.
When would you pre-aggregate and when not? || Pre-aggregate when query patterns are repeated and dimensions are known, concurrency is high and latency is tight. Do not when users slice by arbitrary dimensions, the cardinality of the cross product is huge (the rollup is nearly as large as the raw data), or the metric needs exact distinct counts that cannot be merged without sketches.
How do you handle a slowly changing dimension in OLAP? || Choose by need: overwrite (type 1) when history is not needed, versioned rows with valid-from and valid-to (type 2) when reports must show the value at the time of the event, and a snapshot join at event time when facts carry the dimension version. Type 2 costs more storage and more complex joins but gives correct history.
Why can OLAP not replace the OLTP database? || OLAP engines are optimized for large scans and batch loads, with immutable segments, weak or no row-level transactions and poor point-update performance. OLTP needs low-latency single-row reads and writes with ACID guarantees and constraints. The usual design is OLTP for the application, with CDC feeding OLAP for analysis.

tradeoffs: Real-time OLAP store or a warehouse with fast ingestion. Choose Pinot or Druid when the queries serve end users (thousands of queries per second, p99 below 200 ms) and data must be visible within 1 to 10 seconds; choose ClickHouse for similar speed with simpler operations and stronger SQL, where you can run a few big nodes; choose a warehouse or lakehouse (Snowflake dynamic tables, Databricks serverless SQL with streaming tables) when freshness of 1 to 5 minutes and concurrency of tens of queries is enough, since you avoid a separate system. How much freshness to buy. Each step of freshness costs: seconds-level visibility needs an always-on stream consumer, small segments and frequent flushes (which hurt compression and query speed until merged); minute-level can use micro-batch files; hourly can use plain batch loads. Ask what decision changes between a 5-second and a 5-minute lag; for most dashboards the answer is nothing, and a 1-minute target costs about a quarter of the second-level one. Spend the freshness budget on alerting and fraud, not on reports.
Pre-aggregate at ingest or store raw. Rolling up at ingest (Druid rollup, Pinot star-tree, ClickHouse AggregatingMergeTree) can reduce stored rows by 10 to 100 times and make queries milliseconds, but you lose raw event drill-down and cannot change the grain later without a replay. Storing raw events keeps flexibility at a cost of more storage and CPU per query. Choose rollup for metrics with fixed dimensions and high volume (ad impressions by campaign and minute); choose raw for debugging and investigative queries, or keep both, with raw in short retention and rollup for long.
Upserts and late data. Choose append-only events with a "latest" view computed at query time if updates are few; choose upsert tables when the latest state is the common query. Late events beyond the segment window may require reloading a segment from batch; plan a daily batch correction.

tradeoffs: Which MPP engine. Choose Snowflake, BigQuery or Databricks SQL when you want no cluster to run, elastic compute and storage separated, and queries of seconds to a minute for analysts. Choose Trino (or Starburst/Athena) when the data sits in many sources and a lake and you want federated interactive SQL on open tables, accepting that you manage coordinators, memory limits and no mid-query fault tolerance by default (a worker loss fails the query unless fault-tolerant execution is on). Choose Spark SQL when queries are long (minutes to hours), need fault tolerance through retries per stage, or mix SQL with Python and ML. Choose Redshift or a shared-nothing cluster when the workload is steady, tuned, and you can accept resizing work. You give up, with the managed warehouses, control and some price transparency; with Trino, resilience for long queries; with Spark, interactive latency below a few seconds.
Broadcast or shuffle join. Broadcast when one side is small enough (below roughly 100 MB to a few GB, and below about 10 to 20% of worker memory), because it sends N copies of the small table but moves nothing of the big one. Shuffle (partitioned) join when both sides are large: it moves both tables once across the network, so a 1 TB × 1 TB join moves about 2 TB, which on 50 nodes with 10 Gbit/s links (about 1.2 GB/s each) takes roughly 30 to 60 seconds of network time. Co-located or bucketed joins avoid the move if both tables are distributed on the join key, at the cost of a rigid layout. Wrong statistics make the optimizer choose broadcast for a table that is not small and run out of memory.
Scale out or scale up. More workers cut scan time almost linearly until a stage is limited by the shuffle, a skewed key, or the coordinator. Doubling workers from 50 to 100 on a 400 GB scan lowers scan time by half but doubles the number of shuffle connections (N × N), and a single hot key still lands on one worker. Choose more, smaller workers for scan-heavy queries and fewer, larger workers (more memory per core) for join- and aggregation-heavy ones. Cost scales with node-seconds, so a query that is 2x faster on 2x nodes costs the same; if it is less than 2x faster, you pay more.
Compute-storage coupling. Shared-nothing (local disks, Redshift classic) gives fast local scans and no object-store latency, but resizing means moving data and storage and compute are bought together. Disaggregated (object storage, Snowflake, BigQuery, Trino on S3) lets you scale compute to zero and share one copy of the data, at the cost of first-byte latency (10 to 100 ms per request), so engines add local SSD caches and large reads. Choose disaggregated for spiky workloads and many teams; choose coupled for steady, latency-sensitive workloads with predictable size.
Concurrency and workload isolation. One cluster executes tens of concurrent queries well; hundreds of dashboards on the same cluster as heavy ETL give unpredictable latency. Choose separate warehouses or resource groups for ETL, BI and ad hoc use; accept that idle capacity costs money. Admission control with queues and per-query memory limits protects the cluster at the price of waiting time.
interview: A query that joined two big tables used to take 30 seconds and now takes 20 minutes. What happened? || Likely the plan changed: statistics became stale so the optimizer chose a different join order or a broadcast of a large table (spilling), a skewed key appeared, partition pruning stopped (a function on the filter column), or the cluster is shared and queued. I would compare the old and new query plans and the stage-level metrics: bytes shuffled, spill, the slowest task.
How does a distributed join work and what does it cost? || Both inputs are repartitioned by hash of the join key so matching rows meet on one worker, then each worker builds a hash table of one side and probes with the other. Cost is moving both tables across the network once, plus memory for the hash table; a broadcast join replaces the shuffle of the big side with copies of the small side.
How do you deal with data skew in a join? || Detect it by one task running far longer, then use the engine's skew handling, broadcast the small side, salt the hot key, or process the hot keys in a separate branch. Null keys should be removed or handled before the join. Increasing the number of workers does not help because one key cannot be split by hash.
Why does Trino fail long queries and Spark not? || Trino streams data between stages in memory by default, so losing a worker loses part of the pipeline state and the query restarts; Spark writes shuffle output to disk and can re-run only the failed tasks from lineage. Trino has a fault-tolerant execution mode that spools intermediate data to storage, at the cost of speed.
How would you size a cluster for 200 dashboards queries per minute scanning 20 GB each? || That is about 4 TB of scan per minute, roughly 67 GB/s. If each core scans around 200 to 500 MB/s of compressed columnar data, I need on the order of 150 to 350 cores, plus headroom, and more for joins. Caching, result reuse, pre-aggregation or Pinot-like serving would cut it by an order of magnitude, so I would check the dashboards first.
@@+ coordination
tradeoffs: Which service. Choose etcd when you run Kubernetes or Go services and need a small key-value store with strong consistency, watches and leases (a metadata set under about 2 to 8 GB, a few thousand writes per second). Choose ZooKeeper when a system you already run needs it (older Kafka, Hadoop, HBase, Solr) or you want its well-understood recipes and sessions. Choose Consul when service discovery, health checks and a service mesh are the main need, across data centres. Choose a managed offering or a cloud primitive (DynamoDB conditional writes, a cloud lock service) when you only need a lock or a lease, because it removes the cluster from your on-call. You give up, with a managed primitive, watches and portability; with self-hosting, operations time.
Do you need it at all. Since Kafka's KRaft mode (production-ready since 3.3) and ClickHouse Keeper, Redpanda and CockroachDB embed Raft, many systems no longer require an external ensemble; a separate coordination service is justified when many independent applications share it. Adding one means a new critical dependency, so choose it when you need linearizable agreement across processes (leader election, distributed locks, config). Do not use it for data: a lock service holding application records is slow and fragile.
Ensemble size. Three nodes tolerate one failure, five tolerate two; seven adds little. A write needs a majority acknowledgement, so more nodes make writes slower (more round trips to wait on) and add no throughput, since all writes go through the leader. Choose 3 in a single data centre with fast replacement, 5 when maintenance windows overlap with failures or when you run across three zones and want to survive a zone loss plus one node. Across regions, writes pay the inter-region round trip (tens to 100+ ms), so keep the ensemble within one region and use observers or separate clusters per region.
Locks and leader election: leases with fencing. A lock in a coordination service is a lease, and the holder can lose it (a GC pause of 20 seconds, a network partition) while still believing it holds it. Choose a fencing token (a monotonically increasing number checked by the resource) when the lock protects a write to shared data; choose a simple lease with a short TTL (5 to 30 seconds) when a duplicate action is harmless. The trade is TTL length: short TTLs recover fast but flap during pauses; long ones are stable but leave you leaderless for longer after a crash.
Load and watch patterns. The service is built for read-mostly small data (kilobytes per node) and a modest write rate. Thousands of clients watching one key create a herd after each change; choose watching a parent or using sequential nodes, so each client watches only its predecessor. Heartbeats from tens of thousands of clients add load; choose longer session timeouts (10 to 30 seconds) or hierarchical proxies. Disk fsync latency on the leader sets write latency, so use fast SSDs and a separate disk for the log.
interview: Why do we need an odd number of nodes in an ensemble? || A majority quorum needs more than half of the nodes. With 4 nodes you need 3, so you still tolerate only 1 failure, the same as with 3 nodes, but with more machines and more chances for a split. With 5 nodes you need 3 and tolerate 2. Even sizes add cost without adding fault tolerance.
How do you implement a distributed lock safely? || With a lease in the coordination service (an ephemeral node or lease key) and a fencing token that increases on each acquisition; the protected resource rejects writes with an older token. Without fencing, a paused client whose lease expired can still write and corrupt state, so a TTL alone is not safe.
What happens when the leader of the coordination service fails? || The followers detect the missing heartbeat after an election timeout (hundreds of ms to a few seconds), a new leader is elected with the most up-to-date log by majority vote, and writes resume. During that time writes block and clients may see session or lease issues; clients with longer timeouts than the election survive without losing sessions.
Why is a coordination service not a good database? || It keeps its whole dataset in memory with a replicated log, caps value sizes (about 1 MB), and serializes writes through one leader, so throughput is limited to thousands of writes per second and the data must stay small. Large or hot data would slow consensus for every critical use.
Can the coordination service lie to you about being the leader or holding a lock? || The service is linearizable, but a client's view is delayed: a client might be told it holds a lease that has already expired by the time it acts, because of pauses and clock differences. That is why safety must rely on fencing tokens at the resource, and why timeouts must reflect worst-case pauses.
@@+ cachelayer
tradeoffs: Cache-aside, read-through, write-through, or write-back. Choose cache-aside (the app reads the cache, on a miss reads the database and fills the cache) as the default: it is simple, tolerant to cache failure and works with any store. Choose read-through (the cache library loads on a miss) when you want one place for loading logic and request coalescing. Choose write-through (write both before acknowledging) when stale reads after writes are unacceptable and write rates are low; you pay double write latency. Choose write-back (write to cache, flush later) only for data you can afford to lose or rebuild, such as counters and view counts, because a cache node failure loses unflushed writes. You give up, with cache-aside, a window of stale data and a miss penalty; with write-back, durability.
TTL versus explicit invalidation. A TTL of 30 to 300 seconds bounds staleness with no coupling to writers and is right for product pages, feeds and config. Explicit invalidation on write (delete the key after the database commit, or consume CDC events to invalidate) gives near-fresh reads, but races exist: a reader can repopulate the cache with an old value between the write and the delete. Choose TTL plus jitter (plus or minus 10 to 20%) for most reads; add invalidation through CDC for data where staleness over a few seconds is costly, and keep a TTL as a safety net.
Where to put the cache. An in-process cache (Caffeine, a local LRU) answers in about 100 ns to microseconds and needs no network, but each instance has its own copy, so the hit rate falls with the number of instances and invalidation is hard; limit it to small, hot, rarely changing data. A shared remote cache (Redis, Memcached) costs 0.2 to 1 ms per call, holds one copy, and scales with sharding, but is another service to run and size. A CDN suits public, cacheable HTTP responses. Often the best design is two levels: a small local cache with a short TTL (1 to 5 seconds) in front of Redis.
Size and eviction. For skewed traffic (Zipf), a cache holding 5 to 10% of the keys often serves 80 to 95% of requests, but adding more memory has diminishing returns: going from a 90% to a 99% hit rate cuts backend load by 10 times, and may need many times the memory. Measure the miss ratio curve from a request trace before buying memory. LRU is a good default; LFU or TinyLFU (as in Caffeine) resists scans and one-time keys better; TTL-only suits time-bounded data. Memcached gives simple multi-threaded key-value storage; Redis adds data structures and persistence, at some CPU and memory overhead.
Failure behaviour: stampede, penetration, and cold start. When a hot key expires, thousands of requests can hit the database at once; choose request coalescing (single flight), a lock on refill, probabilistic early refresh, or serving stale data while one request refreshes. Requests for keys that do not exist bypass the cache every time; cache negative results briefly (30 to 60 seconds) or use a Bloom filter. After a cache restart the hit rate is zero and the database may take the full load, so size the database for a cold cache or warm up gradually. You give up, with these protections, some complexity and a little staleness.
interview: Hit rate is 95%. The database still struggles. Why? || A 95% hit rate means 5% of the traffic still reaches the database, which at 100,000 requests per second is 5,000 per second; the misses may be the expensive ones (large or uncacheable queries), or concentrated on hot keys and cold-start periods. I would look at the miss distribution by key, cost per miss, and whether misses come in bursts (stampedes); raising the rate from 95 to 99% cuts load by five times.
How do you keep the cache consistent with the database? || You can only bound inconsistency: use TTLs for a staleness limit, delete the key after the transaction commits (not before), and for stronger freshness invalidate from the CDC stream, which orders invalidations with commits. Writing the cache directly from two places creates races, so I would let the database be the only source of truth and the cache a derived copy.
What is a cache stampede and how do you stop it? || When a popular key expires or the cache is cold, many requests miss at once and all query the database. I would use single-flight request coalescing so one request loads while others wait, add jitter to TTLs, refresh early before expiry, or serve stale-while-revalidate. For known hot keys, never expire them but refresh in the background.
Redis or Memcached? || Memcached for a plain, large-scale, multi-threaded string cache with simple operations and predictable memory use. Redis when you need data structures (sorted sets, hashes, counters), atomic scripts, replication and optional persistence, accepting more memory overhead and a single-threaded command loop per shard. I would choose from the operations needed, not from habit.
How do you shard a cache and what happens when a node dies? || Use consistent hashing (or hash slots as in Redis Cluster) so adding or losing a node remaps only about 1/N of the keys. When a node dies, its keys miss and the database takes that share of the load, so I would add replicas for hot shards, limit the retry rate, and make sure the database can absorb 1/N extra traffic at peak.
