@@ rnn
value: A recurrent neural network reads a sequence one element at a time and keeps a small vector, the hidden state, that summarizes everything it has seen so far. At each step it combines the new input with the old state using the same weights, and produces a new state. Because the weights are shared across time, one small network can handle a sentence of 5 words or an audio clip of 5,000 frames, and the number of parameters does not grow with the length of the input.
This solves a real problem. A plain multilayer network (the perceptron concept) has a fixed input size and no memory. Language, speech, sensor streams, click sequences and time series are different: the meaning of the current element depends on what came before, and the length varies. Recurrence gives the model a memory with a fixed size. It also gives constant cost per new token at inference time: to produce the next output you need only the current state and the new input, not the whole history.
For the layer above, recurrent networks were the main sequence model from roughly 2010 to 2017. LSTMs drove speech recognition, machine translation and early language models, and attention (the next concept in the stack) was invented in 2014 to fix a weakness of recurrent translators. Transformers then took over because they train in parallel over the whole sequence. Since 2023 recurrence has come back in a new form: state-space models such as S4 and Mamba, and linear-recurrence models such as RWKV, are recurrent networks designed so that training can also be parallel. Their appeal is the original one: a fixed-size state and a cost per token that does not grow with context length.
The layers below make this work. Linear algebra (L4) supplies the matrix multiplications that update the state, and its eigenvalues explain why the state either fades or explodes over many steps. Calculus and the chain rule (L4) give the gradient of the loss with respect to weights used many times. Backpropagation (L2) is applied to the unrolled network, which is called backpropagation through time. The multilayer network (L2) is the small function applied at every step.
terms: Hidden state — a vector \( h_t \) that the network carries from step t to step t+1; it is the model's whole memory.
Recurrence — computing the new state from the old state and the new input, \( h_t = f(h_{t-1}, x_t) \), with the same weights at every step.
Unrolling — drawing the loop as a chain of T copies of the same network, one per time step, so that ordinary backpropagation can be applied.
Backpropagation through time (BPTT) — backpropagation on the unrolled chain; the gradient for each shared weight is the sum of its contributions from all steps.
Vanishing and exploding gradients — the gradient signal is multiplied by a similar matrix at every step, so over many steps it shrinks toward zero or grows without bound.
Gate — a vector of values between 0 and 1 (from a sigmoid) that multiplies another vector element by element, deciding how much of it passes.
LSTM — long short-term memory; a recurrent cell with a separate cell state and three gates (forget, input, output) that lets information and gradients survive many steps.
State-space model (SSM) — a model whose state evolves by a linear rule, \( h_t = \bar A h_{t-1} + \bar B x_t \), with outputs read from the state; Mamba is a recent version.
Associative scan — a way to compute all the prefixes of a sequence of linear recurrences in parallel, in \( O(\log T) \) depth.
history: The idea of a network with a loop is old. Hopfield (1982) studied networks with feedback as memories. Jordan (1986) and Elman (1990, "Finding Structure in Time") proposed the simple recurrent network that is still the textbook example: the hidden state from the previous step is fed back as extra input. Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams (1986) had already shown backpropagation for feed-forward networks, and Werbos described its application through time. Elman's networks learned simple grammatical structure, but they could not learn dependencies longer than about ten steps.
The reason was found by Sepp Hochreiter in his 1991 diploma thesis, supervised by Jürgen Schmidhuber: gradients passed back through many steps shrink or grow exponentially. Bengio, Simard and Frasconi (1994) published the same finding in English. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber answered with the LSTM in 1997. Its key idea is a cell state that is updated by addition, so that a gradient can flow back along it without being multiplied by a weight matrix at every step. Gers, Schmidhuber and Cummins (2000) added the forget gate, which made the LSTM as it is used today. Cho and colleagues (2014) simplified it into the GRU, with two gates and no separate cell state.
Computing power caught up around 2010: Mikolov showed recurrent language models beating n-gram models, Sutskever, Vinyals and Le (2014) built translation from two LSTMs, and Google's neural translation system (2016) ran LSTMs in production. The decline came from two limits. A recurrent network cannot be trained in parallel across time, because step t needs step t−1. And one fixed vector must carry the whole past. Attention (Bahdanau and others, 2014) let the decoder look back at all encoder states, and the Transformer (Vaswani and others, 2017) removed the recurrence entirely. The return began with Gu, Goel and Ré's S4 (2021), which modeled long sequences with a structured linear state-space layer. Gu and Dao's Mamba (December 2023) made the state-space parameters depend on the input and trained it with a parallel scan. RWKV (2023) and xLSTM (Beck, Hochreiter and others, 2024) took related paths, and production hybrid models (for example AI21's Jamba, 2024) mix attention layers with Mamba layers.
how: The simplest recurrent cell has two weight matrices and one bias. At step t it takes the input vector \( x_t \) and the previous state \( h_{t-1} \):
\[ h_t = \tanh(W_h h_{t-1} + W_x x_t + b) \]
The output at that step is read from the state, for example \( \hat y_t = \text{softmax}(W_y h_t) \) for a language model that predicts the next token. The same \( W_h, W_x, b \) are used at every step. The matrix \( W_h \) is square, of size d × d for a state of dimension d, so it is the part that carries the past forward. The multilayer network concept is visible here: each step is a one-layer network whose input is the pair (old state, new input).
Training uses backpropagation through time. The network is unrolled over T steps into a chain of T copies of the cell, the loss is computed from the outputs, and ordinary backpropagation runs from the last step to the first. Because the weights are shared, the gradient of \( W_h \) is the sum of its gradients at every step. The chain rule from calculus tells us the gradient that flows from step T back to step t is a product of T − t Jacobian matrices:
\[ \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_t} = \prod_{k=t+1}^{T} \text{diag}\!\left(1 - h_k^2\right) W_h \]
Linear algebra explains what that product does. If the largest singular value of the repeated matrix is below 1, the product shrinks exponentially with distance, and the loss at step T learns nothing about inputs at step t. If it is above 1, the product explodes. The tanh derivative is at most 1, so it can only make shrinking worse. This is why a simple recurrent network struggles with dependencies longer than 10 to 20 steps. Exploding gradients have a cheap fix: clip the global gradient norm to a threshold. Vanishing gradients need a change in the cell.
The LSTM changes the cell. It keeps two vectors: the cell state \( c_t \) (the long-term memory) and the hidden state \( h_t \) (the output). Three gates, each a sigmoid of a linear function of \( [h_{t-1}, x_t] \), control them. The forget gate \( f_t \) decides what to erase, the input gate \( i_t \) decides what to write, and the output gate \( o_t \) decides what to expose:
\[ c_t = f_t \odot c_{t-1} + i_t \odot \tilde c_t, \qquad h_t = o_t \odot \tanh(c_t) \]
Here \( \tilde c_t = \tanh(W_c [h_{t-1}, x_t] + b_c) \) is the candidate new content and \( \odot \) is element-wise multiplication. The important line is the first one. The cell state is updated by a gated sum. The gradient along it is just the product of forget gates, \( \partial c_t / \partial c_{t-1} = f_t \) element-wise, with no weight matrix and no tanh in the way. If the model learns \( f_t \approx 1 \) for some channel, that channel keeps its value and its gradient across hundreds of steps. A common practice is to initialize the forget-gate bias to 1 so that remembering is the default. The cost is four weight blocks instead of one: an LSTM with state 512 and input 256 has \( 4 \times (512 \times 768 + 512) \approx 1.57 \) million parameters in one layer.
Inference is where recurrence is attractive. To generate the next token you hold the current state, apply one cell update, and read out. The cost per token is constant, about \( 2 \times \) the number of parameters in floating-point operations, and the memory is one state. A Transformer instead keeps a key-value cache that grows with every token. For a model with 32 layers, 8 key-value heads of size 128 and 16-bit numbers, the cache is \( 2 \times 32 \times 8 \times 128 \times 2 \) bytes, about 128 KiB per token, or roughly 13 GB at 100,000 tokens. A recurrent state of a few megabytes does not grow at all.
Training is where recurrence hurts. Step t waits for step t−1, so a sequence of length T needs T sequential steps and a GPU sits mostly idle in each. Modern state-space models remove this limit by making the recurrence linear. For \( h_t = a_t h_{t-1} + b_t \), combining two consecutive steps gives another step of the same form: \( (a_1, b_1) \circ (a_2, b_2) = (a_1 a_2,\; a_2 b_1 + b_2) \). Because this combination is associative, all T states can be computed with a parallel scan in \( O(\log T) \) depth. Linearity is the price: there is no tanh between steps, and the nonlinearity lives in the layers around the recurrence.
An S4-style layer starts from a continuous system \( h'(t) = A h(t) + B x(t) \), \( y(t) = C h(t) \), and discretizes it with a step size \( \Delta \): \( \bar A = \exp(\Delta A) \), and \( \bar B \) from a matching formula. With A structured (S4 uses a special initialization called HiPPO, built from orthogonal polynomials so that the state remembers a compressed history), the layer can be trained as a long convolution and run as a recurrence. Mamba's change is selectivity: \( \Delta, B, C \) are computed from the current input. A large \( \Delta \) makes the model overwrite the state with the new token, a small \( \Delta \) makes it ignore the token. This is the same job as the LSTM's gates, written in a linear form that a parallel scan can handle.

example: Take the smallest possible recurrence: one number as the state, \( h_t = w \, h_{t-1} + x_t \), with no nonlinearity, and a loss that depends on the last state \( h_T \). The gradient of \( h_T \) with respect to \( h_t \) is \( w^{T-t} \).
Step 1: set w = 0.9 and look back 50 steps. The factor is \( 0.9^{50} \approx 0.005 \). An input 50 steps ago changes the loss 200 times less than the latest input. With 100 steps it is about \( 2.7 \times 10^{-5} \). The network cannot learn from distant inputs, because their gradient is smaller than the noise.
Step 2: set w = 1.1. The factor is \( 1.1^{50} \approx 117 \) and \( 1.1^{100} \approx 13{,}800 \). One update would throw the weights far away. Clipping fixes this: if the gradient norm is 13,800 and the threshold is 1, scale the whole gradient by 1/13,800. The direction is kept and the size is bounded.
Step 3: now write the LSTM cell state in the same scalar form, \( c_t = f \, c_{t-1} + i \, \tilde c \). The gradient to a step 50 earlier is \( f^{50} \). With a forget gate of 0.99 it is \( 0.99^{50} \approx 0.6 \), and \( 0.99^{200} \approx 0.13 \). The same 200-step distance that gave \( 10^{-9} \) for w = 0.9 (about \( 7 \times 10^{-10} \)) is now a usable signal. The gate is also learned per channel and per step, so the model can set f near 1 for a channel that stores "we are inside a quote" and near 0 for a channel that tracks the current word.
Step 4: compare memory at inference. Suppose a 1-billion-parameter LSTM-style model has 24 layers and a state of 2,048 numbers per layer in 16-bit form: the whole memory is \( 24 \times 2048 \times 2 \approx 98 \) KB, whether it has read 10 tokens or 10 million. A Transformer with the cache from the previous section stores 128 KiB per token, so after 1,000 tokens the cache is about 128 MB, more than a thousand times that recurrent state, and at 100,000 tokens it is about 13 GB. This is the trade the state-space revival is built on.
intuition: A recurrent network is a person reading a book while keeping only a notepad of fixed size: after each sentence they rewrite the notepad, and whatever is not on it is gone.
The design is natural because many real processes are exactly this: a small amount of state that is updated by each new event. A running average, a parser stack, a user session and a physical system with a position and a velocity all work this way, and they work with any input length. Repeating one function over time is the same idea as a loop in a program. The difficulty is the same as in a program too. A loop that multiplies by 0.9 each time forgets, and one that multiplies by 1.1 diverges, so memory over long spans needs the multiplier to stay near 1. The LSTM gate and the structured state-space matrix are two ways to place that multiplier near 1 on purpose, and to let the input choose when to forget.
mistakes: People often think an LSTM removes the vanishing gradient completely. In fact it only gives the gradient a short path through the cell state. The path exists only while the forget gate stays near 1. Gradients still vanish through the other connections, and LSTMs in practice reliably use context of a few hundred steps, not thousands.
People often think recurrent models are obsolete because Transformers won. In fact they lost on training parallelism and on recall of exact earlier tokens, not on inference cost. A Transformer's cache and its attention cost grow with context length, a recurrent state does not, and this is why state-space and hybrid models are used for long-context and on-device work.
People often think a state-space model is a different thing from an RNN. In fact a Mamba layer is a recurrent network: it has a state, updated one token at a time, with a constant cost per token. What changed is that the update is linear in the state, which makes it possible to compute all steps at once with a scan during training.
People often think the hidden state "stores the sentence". In fact it stores whatever features helped to predict the training targets. It is lossy by design, and a fact that was never useful for the loss is not kept.
friction: The main limit is the fixed-size state. All information that the model may need later must fit in d numbers, and a compression that is good for the average task loses exact detail. Studies of state-space models show that they trail Transformers on tasks that require copying a long span or retrieving one exact item from far back (for example the "needle in a haystack" test), unless the model is large or is combined with a few attention layers. This is the main reason that strong production designs are hybrids.
Training is the second limit. A classic LSTM has a sequential dependency, so GPU utilization is poor, and training on 4,000-token sequences needs 4,000 dependent steps per layer. Memory is also high: BPTT must keep every intermediate state for the backward pass, so memory grows with T unless you use truncated BPTT (cut the chain every 100 to 200 steps) or recompute the states during the backward pass. Truncation limits the span over which gradients can teach the model anything.
tradeoffs: Choose an LSTM or GRU when you have a small to medium sequence problem (sensor data, a streaming classifier, an on-device model with a budget of milliseconds and a few MB) and you need constant memory per step. Choose a Transformer when you have enough data and parallel hardware, and the task needs exact recall or sequences of a few thousand tokens or less. You give up inference scaling with length for training speed and recall.
Choose a state-space model such as Mamba when the context is very long (hundreds of thousands of steps in audio, genomics or logs), when serving cost per token and cache memory matter more than exact retrieval, or when you stream data indefinitely. Choose a hybrid (a few attention layers among many state-space layers) when you need both long context and reliable retrieval. A common ratio in published hybrids is far more state-space layers than attention layers. You give up simplicity and some kernel maturity.
Choose truncated BPTT with a window of 100 to 200 steps when memory is the limit and dependencies are short. Choose full BPTT with checkpointing (store every k-th state, recompute the rest) when long dependencies matter and you can pay about one extra forward pass in compute. In both cases you trade compute and memory against the span the gradient can reach.
practice: Start from a baseline. For a streaming or small-data sequence task, a one- or two-layer GRU or LSTM with hidden size 128 to 512 is cheap to train and often hard to beat. Clip the global gradient norm (a value near 1.0 is common), initialize the LSTM forget-gate bias to 1, use the same dropout mask at all time steps, and pack variable-length batches so that padded positions do not update the state.
Evaluate the span you care about. Build a synthetic test that matches your need, such as "recall a value from k steps back", and measure accuracy as k grows. If accuracy falls at k = 200 and you need 5,000, no tuning of an LSTM will fix it; change the architecture. For long contexts, test retrieval specifically, because perplexity can look fine while exact recall is poor.
For inference, treat the state as a first-class object. In serving, keep the state per session (a few KB to MB) in memory, process a new chunk by feeding it into the saved state, and you get cheap incremental processing, such as streaming speech recognition on a phone. This is the strongest practical reason to choose recurrence today. With state-space models, use the maintained kernels from the authors' repositories rather than writing the scan yourself.
interview: Why do gradients vanish in a simple RNN, and what exactly does the LSTM change? || The gradient across T steps is a product of T Jacobians that each contain the same matrix \( W_h \) and a tanh derivative; if the largest singular value is below 1 the product decays exponentially. The LSTM adds a cell state updated by addition, so the gradient along it is a product of forget gates, with no weight matrix, which can stay near 1. It reduces but does not remove the problem.
What does truncated backpropagation through time give up? || It cuts the unrolled chain every k steps and does not propagate gradients across the cut, so the model cannot learn dependencies longer than k from gradient signal, though the state still carries forward at inference. It saves memory proportional to T/k and allows stateful training on streams.
Why can a Mamba layer be trained in parallel when an LSTM cannot? || The Mamba recurrence is linear in the state, so each step is an affine map and composing maps is associative. A parallel scan then computes all states in \( O(\log T) \) depth. An LSTM has gates and tanh that depend on the previous hidden state, so step t cannot start before step t−1 finishes.
You need a model to read 1 million tokens of logs and answer questions about specific lines. RNN, Transformer or hybrid? || A pure Transformer pays for a very large cache and quadratic attention unless sparse or sliding methods are added. A pure state-space model is cheap but may lose exact lines. A hybrid, or retrieval over chunks followed by a smaller context, is the practical answer; I would test exact-recall accuracy at length before choosing.
How would you serve a streaming speech model for 10,000 simultaneous users? || Recurrence suits it: each session needs one fixed-size state, so memory is users times state size (for example 10,000 × 1 MB = 10 GB) and each audio chunk costs the same compute. I would batch chunks from different sessions into one matrix multiply, keep states in GPU memory, and evict idle sessions.
What are the failure modes of selectivity in Mamba-style models? || The model must decide at write time what to keep, without knowing which later question will need it. Anything it discarded cannot be recovered, which explains weakness at exact copying and retrieval. Selectivity also makes the model input-dependent, so it needs a scan kernel and cannot use a fixed convolution.
quiz: What is the role of the forget gate in an LSTM? || It multiplies the previous cell state element by element by a value between 0 and 1. Values near 1 keep the memory and let gradients pass along the cell state, and values near 0 erase it.
Why does backpropagation through time produce vanishing gradients when the largest singular value of \( W_h \) is below 1? || The gradient from step T to step t is a product of T−t Jacobians that each include \( W_h \). A product of many matrices with norm below 1 shrinks exponentially with the distance T−t.
Why does a recurrent model have constant memory per token at inference but a Transformer does not? || A recurrent model compresses the past into a fixed-size state. A Transformer keeps keys and values of every previous token in its cache, so memory grows linearly with the context length.
A simple RNN has state 1 and w = 0.8. About how large is the gradient factor over 30 steps, and over 100 steps? || \( 0.8^{30} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-3} \) and \( 0.8^{100} \approx 2 \times 10^{-10} \), so long-range inputs have almost no influence on learning.
When would you pick a hybrid of attention and state-space layers over a pure Transformer or a pure state-space model? || When you need long context at low serving cost and also exact retrieval of earlier details. The state-space layers keep the cache small, and a few attention layers recover exact lookup.

@@ gnn
value: A graph neural network (GNN) is a neural network whose input is a graph: nodes with feature vectors, and edges between them. Each layer lets every node collect information from its neighbours, transform it, and combine it with its own state. After k layers, the vector of a node (its embedding) depends on everything within k hops. These embeddings can then be used to classify nodes, to predict properties of the whole graph, or, in link prediction, to score a pair of nodes and decide whether an edge between them is missing or will appear.
Plain networks do not fit graphs. A multilayer network wants a fixed-size vector, and a convolutional or recurrent network wants a grid or a line. A graph has no fixed size, no order of nodes, and each node has a different number of neighbours. Flattening the adjacency matrix gives a different input for every relabeling of the same graph. A GNN is built so that it gives the same answer however the nodes are numbered, and so that it uses the same small set of weights for every node, in the same way a convolution shares weights across image positions.
For the layer above, GNNs turn the structure that products already store into signal. Recommenders such as Pinterest's PinSage learn from the graph of pins and boards. Social and commerce platforms predict "people you may know" or "items bought together" as link prediction. Identity resolution can score whether two device or account nodes belong to one person. AlphaFold-style models reason over a graph of residue pairs. Graph theory (L4) supplies the object and its adjacency matrix, linear algebra (L4) shows that one message-passing layer is a normalized matrix multiplication, embeddings (L2) give the idea that a node becomes a vector, and backpropagation (L2) trains everything end to end.
terms: Node feature — the input vector attached to a node, such as a user's profile or a pin's image embedding.
Message passing — one round in which each node receives vectors from its neighbours, combines them, and updates its own vector.
Aggregation — the permutation-invariant function (sum, mean, max) that merges the neighbour messages into one vector.
Receptive field — the set of nodes that can influence a node's embedding after k layers; the nodes within k hops.
Adjacency matrix — the matrix \( A \) with \( A_{ij} = 1 \) if there is an edge from i to j, and 0 otherwise.
Link prediction — predicting whether an edge exists, or will appear, between two nodes, usually by scoring the pair of their embeddings.
Negative sampling — building training examples for "no edge" by choosing random node pairs that are not connected.
Over-smoothing — with many layers the embeddings of all nodes in a connected region become nearly equal and lose their differences.
Inductive vs transductive — an inductive model can embed nodes it never saw in training; a transductive model learns a fixed embedding for each training node.
history: Early work on neural networks for graph-structured data came from Gori, Monfardini and Scarselli (2005) and Scarselli and colleagues (2009, "The Graph Neural Network Model"). Their model repeated a local update until the node states settled to a fixed point. It worked but was slow to train. In parallel, spectral graph theory offered another route: Bruna and colleagues (2014) defined convolutions through the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian, and Defferrard, Bresson and Vandergheynst (2016) made them cheap with polynomial filters.
The simplification that made GNNs popular was Kipf and Welling's graph convolutional network (GCN), posted in 2016 and published at ICLR 2017. They reduced the spectral filter to a first-order form: each layer multiplies the node features by a degree-normalized adjacency matrix with self-loops, and then by a weight matrix. It was a few lines of code and gave strong results on citation-network benchmarks. The same authors also proposed the variational graph autoencoder (2016), which predicts links by taking dot products of node embeddings.
In 2017 the field widened quickly. Hamilton, Ying and Leskovec's GraphSAGE showed how to learn an inductive function by sampling a fixed number of neighbours, which made large graphs and unseen nodes possible. Veličković and colleagues' graph attention network (GAT) learned weights for each neighbour. Gilmer and colleagues' message passing neural network paper (2017) unified many variants for molecules. Xu and colleagues (2018, "How Powerful are Graph Neural Networks?") proved that message-passing GNNs are at most as discriminating as the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph test, and designed the GIN layer to reach that limit.
Industrial scale arrived with PinSage (Ying and colleagues, KDD 2018), trained at Pinterest on a graph with about 3 billion nodes and 18 billion edges. Zhang and Chen's SEAL (2018) showed that link prediction improves when the model looks at the small subgraph around the candidate pair. Later work studied the limits (over-smoothing, Li and colleagues 2018; over-squashing, Alon and Yahav 2020) and mixed GNNs with attention in graph transformers.
how: Start with a graph of N nodes. Each node i has a feature vector \( x_i \), and the graph has an adjacency matrix \( A \). A GNN layer does the same three steps for every node. Step 1: compute a message from each neighbour, usually by multiplying the neighbour's vector by a weight matrix. Step 2: aggregate the messages with a function that does not depend on their order. Step 3: combine the result with the node's own vector and pass it through a nonlinearity. The Kipf and Welling layer is the simplest example, and it can be written as one matrix expression:
\[ H^{(l+1)} = \sigma\!\left(\tilde D^{-1/2}\, \tilde A\, \tilde D^{-1/2}\, H^{(l)} W^{(l)}\right), \qquad \tilde A = A + I \]
Here \( H^{(l)} \) is the N × d matrix of node vectors at layer l, \( W^{(l)} \) is a learned weight matrix, \( \tilde D \) is the diagonal degree matrix of \( \tilde A \), and \( \sigma \) is a function such as ReLU. This is where linear algebra and graph theory carry the weight. Multiplying by \( A \) sums the vectors of the neighbours of each node. Adding the identity \( I \) lets a node keep its own vector. The factor \( \tilde D^{-1/2} \) on both sides divides each message by \( \sqrt{d_i d_j} \), so a node with 10,000 neighbours does not produce a vector 10,000 times larger than a node with 1 neighbour. Multiplying by \( W \) is the small neural network from the multilayer perceptron concept, shared by all nodes. Because the sum does not depend on the order of the neighbours, relabeling the nodes only permutes the rows of the output; the model is permutation equivariant.
Each layer widens the receptive field by one hop. After two layers, node i has a vector that depends on its neighbours' neighbours. This is why the number of layers is usually small (2 to 4): the receptive field grows exponentially in a graph with high degree, and a few hops can already reach a large part of the graph.
Training works like any network. For node classification the final vector goes through a softmax and a cross-entropy loss against labels. For link prediction, take the final embeddings \( z_u \) and \( z_v \) of two nodes and compute a score, most simply the dot product, turned into a probability: \( p(u,v) = \sigma(z_u \cdot z_v) \). The loss rewards high scores for real edges and low scores for sampled non-edges (negative sampling). Backpropagation sends gradients through the scoring function, through every layer, and into the shared weights. Nothing is hand-designed per node: the weights learn what is useful for predicting edges. Better decoders replace the dot product by a small neural network over \( [z_u, z_v] \), or use relation-specific scoring in a graph with several edge types. The set of positive edges used for training must be hidden from message passing when computing the loss for that edge, or the model sees the answer.
Large graphs need sampling, because the full matrix \( \tilde A \) and all intermediate \( H \) do not fit in the memory of one GPU. GraphSAGE changes the aggregation: for each target node, sample a fixed number of neighbours at each layer (the paper used 25 for the first hop and 10 for the second), then average or pool their transformed vectors and concatenate them with the node's own vector. Cost per target node is then 25 × 10 = 250 sampled nodes, not the whole graph, and it is the same for every node. PinSage adds random-walk-based neighbour sampling, so the neighbours are the ones with the most visits and not just direct edges, and it trains with minibatches on a graph stored on CPU memory. The sampling also makes the model inductive: it learns a function from neighbourhoods to vectors, so a new node can be embedded as soon as it has neighbours.
Link prediction on real systems is usually a two-stage job. The GNN produces embeddings offline or in a nightly batch. At serving time, candidate generation uses an approximate nearest neighbour index over the embeddings, and a ranker scores the shortlist with extra features. This is why a dot-product decoder is popular: it turns "find the best new links for u" into a maximum inner product search.

example: Take four nodes with edges A–B, A–C, B–C and C–D. Give them one-number features: A = 1, B = 2, C = 3, D = 4. Use the GCN rule with W = 1 and ignore the nonlinearity, so we can see the aggregation clearly.
Step 1: add self-loops and count degrees. A has neighbours B, C and itself, so its degree is 3. B is the same, degree 3. C has A, B, D and itself, so degree 4. D has C and itself, so degree 2.
Step 2: for each node compute the sum over j of \( x_j / \sqrt{d_i d_j} \). For A: from A itself, \( 1/\sqrt{3 \cdot 3} = 0.333 \); from B, \( 2/3 = 0.667 \); from C, \( 3/\sqrt{12} = 0.866 \). The total is 1.866. B gives the same total, 1.866, because its neighbours have the same degrees. For C: A gives \( 1/\sqrt{12} = 0.289 \), B gives \( 2/\sqrt{12} = 0.577 \), C itself gives \( 3/4 = 0.75 \), and D gives \( 4/\sqrt{8} = 1.414 \). The total is 3.030. For D: from C, \( 3/\sqrt{8} = 1.061 \); from itself, \( 4/2 = 2.0 \). The total is 3.061.
Step 3: after one layer, A and B have the same value (1.866) although they started with 1 and 2. Their neighbourhoods are almost identical, so they are pulled together. D started at 4 and is now 3.06, pulled toward its only neighbour C. After a second layer, D's value would depend on A and B too, which are two hops away. This is the receptive field growing, and also the first sign of over-smoothing: values move toward each other.
Step 4: link prediction. Suppose training has given 2-dimensional embeddings \( z_A = (1, 0) \), \( z_B = (0.8, 0.6) \), \( z_D = (-0.5, 0.9) \). The dot products are A·B = 0.8, A·D = −0.5 and B·D = −0.4 + 0.54 = 0.14. After the sigmoid the probabilities are 0.69, 0.38 and 0.53. The pair A–B is an existing edge, so its loss is \( -\ln 0.69 = 0.37 \). The pair A–D is a sampled negative, so its loss is \( -\ln(1 - 0.38) = 0.47 \). Gradient descent will move \( z_A \) and \( z_D \) apart and \( z_A \) and \( z_B \) together.
Step 5: the cost of sampling. With fan-outs of 25 and 10, one target node needs at most 250 second-hop samples plus 25 first-hop ones, about 275 vectors. A batch of 1,024 target nodes then needs roughly 280,000 vector reads, regardless of whether the graph has a million or 3 billion nodes. Without sampling, a node near a hub with 100,000 neighbours would pull in 100,000 vectors at the first layer alone.
intuition: A GNN is a group of people who each repeatedly ask their friends what they think, mix that with their own opinion, and ask again; after k rounds, each person's view reflects the whole group within k steps.
The design is natural because the useful signal in a graph is local. Your interests are correlated with those of your neighbours (homophily), and a missing edge is likely between two nodes that share many neighbours. A GNN learns how to combine neighbour information, instead of an engineer writing "count common neighbours" by hand. The fixed set of weights shared by all nodes is the same idea as the shared filter of a convolution: the rule for combining neighbours is the same everywhere, so one model works for a graph of any size.
mistakes: People often think a GNN needs the whole graph to be small enough for one machine. In fact the layer depends only on a k-hop neighbourhood. With neighbour sampling, as in GraphSAGE and PinSage, training cost per node is fixed, and graphs with billions of edges are trained on in minibatches.
People often think more layers always help, as in image models. In fact repeated multiplication by the normalized adjacency matrix pushes all node vectors toward the same dominant eigenvector, so after many layers nodes in a connected region look alike (over-smoothing). Most useful GNNs have 2 to 4 layers, or add residual connections.
People often think link prediction is the same as ranking by common neighbours. In fact common neighbours and similar scores are strong baselines, and a GNN beats them mainly by using node features and longer paths. On graphs where structure alone explains the edges, the simple heuristic can be as good, so always compare to it.
People often think the test edges can be left in the graph during training. In fact that is data leakage: if the edge u–v is in the graph while the model learns to predict it, message passing carries the answer between the two nodes. Split edges first, and build the message-passing graph only from the training edges.
friction: Neighbour explosion is the first limit. A k-layer GNN with average degree d touches \( d^k \) nodes per target, and real graphs have hubs with millions of neighbours. Sampling bounds the cost but adds variance and a bias toward the sampled nodes, and building the sampled subgraphs is often slower than the matrix multiplications that follow, so the data pipeline can dominate training time.
Expressiveness is the second limit. Message-passing GNNs cannot tell apart some graphs that the Weisfeiler-Lehman test cannot distinguish, for example they cannot count triangles reliably without extra structural features. Over-smoothing and over-squashing limit depth. Over-squashing means that information from many distant nodes must pass through the fixed-size vector of a bottleneck node, so long-range signals are compressed away. Graph transformers and added positional features address this at a high cost.
Practical issues come next. Real graphs change every minute, so embeddings go stale, and an inductive model still needs fresh neighbours at serving time. Graphs are often heterogeneous (users, items, devices, with different edge types), and the model must handle each type. Labels for edges are biased by what the product has shown to users: an edge that was never observed may be a non-edge or just unseen. Negative sampling choices change what the model learns, and offline metrics such as AUC can be high while the top of the ranking is poor.
tradeoffs: Choose a GNN when the structure carries information that node features do not, and relations are many-to-many (social graphs, item co-purchase graphs, device graphs). Choose a plain model on node features, or a gradient-boosted tree model with graph features such as degree, common neighbours and PageRank, when the labeled data is small or when you need a model you can explain and serve cheaply. You give up the learned multi-hop signal but gain simplicity; on many tabular problems the engineered-feature baseline is within a few points of the GNN.
Choose a full-batch GCN when the graph has fewer than about a million nodes and fits in GPU memory: it is simple, exact and fast. Choose neighbour sampling (GraphSAGE style) when the graph is larger or when you need an inductive model for new nodes. You give up exactness and a little accuracy for a bounded cost per node.
Choose a dot-product decoder when candidates must come from a nearest-neighbour index among millions of items; choose a learned pair decoder (a small network over both embeddings and pair features) for reranking a shortlist. The first is fast and slightly less accurate, the second the opposite.
Choose precomputed embeddings (nightly batch) when freshness of a day is acceptable; choose online sampling at request time only when new edges must count within seconds, and expect a latency budget of tens of milliseconds to be dominated by neighbour fetches.
practice: First build the baselines: common neighbours, Adamic-Adar, PageRank features and a gradient-boosted model. Report the GNN's gain over them, not over random. Split edges by time, not at random, if the product predicts future links, since random splits leak.
Use a library. PyTorch Geometric and the Deep Graph Library provide GCN, SAGE, GAT layers, samplers and link-prediction examples; for web-scale graphs, look at distributed samplers and graph stores. Start with two layers, hidden size 64 to 256, mean aggregation, and add residual connections before adding depth. Sample negatives that are harder than uniform (for example nodes within two hops, or popular items) after the first model works, and evaluate with ranking metrics such as recall at k and hits at k on a held-out set of edges, not accuracy.
For production, separate the embedding job from serving. Compute embeddings in batch, write them to a vector index, and handle cold start with a model on node features alone. Monitor drift: the distribution of embedding norms and nearest-neighbour overlap between today and yesterday are cheap signals that a pipeline broke. Be careful with sensitive attributes, because the graph can reveal them through neighbours even when you remove the column.
interview: Walk through exactly what one GCN layer computes, and why the normalization matters. || Each node sums its neighbours' (and its own) vectors, scaled by \( 1/\sqrt{d_i d_j} \), multiplies by a shared weight matrix and applies a nonlinearity. Without the normalization, high-degree nodes get huge vectors and low-degree nodes tiny ones, which destabilizes training; the symmetric form also keeps the operator's eigenvalues in a stable range.
Why can a GNN embed a node it never saw in training, and a classic node-embedding table cannot? || A GNN learns a function from a node's features and its neighbours' features to a vector. A lookup table such as DeepWalk learns one free vector per training node, with nothing to compute for a new one.
Your link-prediction AUC is 0.97 offline but the product impact is small. What do you check? || First leakage (test edges present in message passing, random split on temporal data), then whether negatives are too easy, then whether the ranking at top k matches the product need. AUC averages over easy negatives and says little about the top of a ranked list; I would report recall at k against candidates the product would really show.
How do you serve GNN recommendations at 10 ms latency? || Do the graph computation offline: batch-compute embeddings, put them in an approximate nearest-neighbour index, and serve by a vector lookup plus a light ranker. Online neighbour sampling needs several remote reads and rarely fits the budget, so only use it when freshness requires it.
What can message-passing GNNs not do, and what would you change? || They are bounded by the Weisfeiler-Lehman test, so they miss some structure, such as counting cycles, and they struggle with long-range dependencies through over-squashing. Options are structural or positional features, higher-order GNNs, adding virtual nodes, or a graph transformer, each at extra cost.
quiz: What does a k-layer message-passing GNN let a node's embedding depend on? || Exactly the nodes within k hops of it (its k-hop neighbourhood), because each layer brings in information from one more hop.
Why is the sum over neighbours used with normalization, and what goes wrong without it? || The sum is independent of neighbour order, which makes the layer permutation-equivariant. Without dividing by degrees, nodes with many neighbours get very large vectors and training becomes unstable.
Why does stacking many GCN layers produce over-smoothing? || Each layer multiplies by the normalized adjacency matrix, and repeated powers of that matrix converge to its dominant eigenvector, so the vectors of nodes in a connected region become almost the same.
With sampling fan-outs of 20, 10 and 5 for three layers, how many nodes does one target node need at most? || The sampled counts multiply: 20 + 20 × 10 + 20 × 10 × 5 = 20 + 200 + 1,000 = 1,220 neighbours, plus the target itself.
When would you prefer a gradient-boosted model with graph features to a GNN? || When labeled data is small, when explainability and cheap serving matter, or when structure is well captured by a few features such as degree and common-neighbour counts; the GNN's extra accuracy may not justify its pipeline cost.

value: Object detection answers "what is in this image, and where?". The output is a list of boxes, each with a class label and a confidence score. Segmentation goes one step further and labels pixels: semantic segmentation gives every pixel a class, instance segmentation gives each separate object its own mask, and panoptic segmentation does both. Classification, in contrast, gives one label per image and says nothing about position or count.
This matters wherever software has to act on a scene and not just describe it. A car must know where the pedestrians are, a factory camera must locate a cracked part, a phone must separate a person from the background, a medical tool must outline a tumour, and an ad or content system must find the logos and faces in a video frame. The difficulty is that the number of objects is unknown, their sizes differ by a factor of 100, and they can overlap. A fixed-size output vector cannot describe that.
Detectors solve it in a common way. A backbone network turns the image into feature maps. At many positions and scales, a small head predicts "is there an object centred here, what class, and how should the box be adjusted". This produces thousands of overlapping candidate boxes, and a post-processing step called non-maximum suppression (NMS) keeps the best box for each object. The layers below supply the pieces. Convolution (L2) gives the backbone that shares weights across positions, so that the same detector works anywhere in the picture. Linear algebra (L4) turns convolutions into large matrix multiplications that GPUs run fast, and provides the geometry of boxes. Optimization (L4) trains the network with a combined loss for class, box position and, in segmentation, mask shape. For the layer above, computer vision uses detectors and segmenters as basic building blocks for tracking, counting, autonomous driving, retail and video analysis.
terms: Bounding box — a rectangle around an object, written as (x1, y1, x2, y2) or as centre, width and height.
IoU (intersection over union) — the area where two boxes overlap divided by the area they cover together; 1 means identical, 0 means no overlap.
Anchor — a reference box of a chosen size and shape placed at every position of a feature map; the network predicts a small correction to it.
Backbone, neck, head — the backbone extracts features, the neck (for example a feature pyramid) combines scales, and the head makes the box and class predictions.
Non-maximum suppression (NMS) — a procedure that sorts boxes by score and removes any box that overlaps a better one by more than a set IoU.
Feature pyramid — feature maps at several resolutions, so that large objects are found on coarse maps and small objects on fine maps.
mAP (mean average precision) — the area under the precision-recall curve, averaged over classes, and for COCO also over IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95.
Semantic, instance, panoptic segmentation — per-pixel classes; a separate mask per object; and both together.
Mask — a per-pixel yes/no map of which pixels belong to one object.
history: Before deep learning, detection used hand-made features and a sliding window. Viola and Jones (2001) made real-time face detection possible with simple Haar features and a cascade of classifiers that rejected most windows early. Dalal and Triggs (2005) used histograms of oriented gradients with a linear SVM for pedestrians, and Felzenszwalb and colleagues' deformable part model (2008) dominated the PASCAL VOC benchmark for years.
Girshick and colleagues' R-CNN (2014) changed the field. It proposed about 2,000 regions per image with an older algorithm (selective search), ran a convolutional network on each region and classified it. It was accurate but slow, because the network ran 2,000 times per image. Fast R-CNN (Girshick, 2015) ran the backbone once and cut features for each region from the shared map. Faster R-CNN (Ren, He, Girshick and Sun, 2015) replaced selective search with a learned region proposal network that uses anchors, and it made the whole detector one network trained end to end.
A second family dropped the proposal stage. YOLO (Redmon and colleagues, 2015) divided the image into a grid and predicted boxes and classes directly in one pass, reaching real-time speeds of about 45 frames per second in its first version. SSD (Liu and colleagues, 2015) predicted from several feature maps at different scales. These single-stage detectors were fast but initially less accurate. Lin and colleagues' RetinaNet (2017) explained why: the huge number of easy background boxes dominated the loss. Their focal loss reduced the weight of easy examples, and single-stage detectors caught up. The feature pyramid network (Lin and colleagues, 2017) became the standard way to handle scale.
For segmentation, Long, Shelhamer and Darrell's fully convolutional networks (2014) produced a class map by making every layer convolutional, and U-Net (Ronneberger and colleagues, 2015) became the standard for medical images. Mask R-CNN (He and colleagues, 2017) added a mask branch to Faster R-CNN. Carion and colleagues' DETR (2020) used a transformer and matched predictions to objects one to one, so that no NMS was needed. In 2023 Meta's Segment Anything Model (SAM) was trained on a dataset of over a billion masks to segment objects from prompts such as a point or a box, and SAM 2 (2024) extended it to video.
how: A modern detector has three parts. The backbone is a convolutional network (or a vision transformer) that turns an image of, say, 640 × 640 pixels into feature maps at strides 8, 16 and 32, so the maps are 80 × 80, 40 × 40 and 20 × 20. This is the convolution concept at work: filters with shared weights slide across the image, so a feature that detects a wheel works at every position, and deeper layers see larger regions. The neck, usually a feature pyramid, passes information from the coarse maps down to the fine maps so that every scale has both detail and meaning. The head is a small convolution applied at every position of every map, with outputs for class scores and box coordinates.
Each position is responsible for objects near it. In an anchor-based detector, every position has several anchors, for example 3 sizes times 3 aspect ratios, so 9 anchors per position. Faster R-CNN on a 1000 × 600 image with stride 16 has about 60 × 40 × 9 ≈ 21,600 anchors. For each anchor the head predicts a class score and four numbers that move and resize the anchor:
\[ t_x = \frac{x - x_a}{w_a}, \quad t_y = \frac{y - y_a}{h_a}, \quad t_w = \ln\frac{w}{w_a}, \quad t_h = \ln\frac{h}{h_a} \]
The offsets are relative to the anchor size, so one set of weights works for small and large boxes, and the logarithm keeps widths positive. Anchor-free detectors (FCOS, CenterNet, recent YOLO versions) skip the preset shapes and predict distances from the position to the four box sides, or the centre of the object, directly.
Training must decide which predictions are responsible for which ground-truth box. A common rule marks an anchor as positive if its IoU with a ground-truth box is above 0.5 to 0.7, negative if it is below 0.3 to 0.4, and ignores the rest. Newer methods assign by a dynamic cost that combines classification and box quality. The loss then has two parts: a classification loss for every anchor, and a box regression loss (smooth L1 or an IoU-based loss) only for positives. This is where optimization enters: the two terms have different scales, and the sum is minimized by stochastic gradient descent. Most anchors are background, often 99.9% of them, so the class loss must handle imbalance. Faster R-CNN samples a balanced batch of anchors. RetinaNet uses focal loss, \( \text{FL}(p_t) = -(1 - p_t)^{\gamma} \ln p_t \) with \( \gamma = 2 \), which multiplies the loss of a confident correct prediction (for example \( p_t = 0.9 \)) by \( 0.1^2 = 0.01 \), so the many easy negatives stop dominating.
At inference the head produces tens of thousands of scored boxes, and many of them cover the same object. Non-maximum suppression cleans this up in a loop: sort all boxes of a class by score; take the highest, keep it, delete every remaining box whose IoU with it is above a threshold (commonly 0.5 to 0.7); repeat with the next highest remaining box. It is a greedy algorithm and runs in \( O(n^2) \) in the worst case, which is acceptable after a score filter has reduced n to a few hundred.
Two-stage detectors add a second step. The proposal network suggests about 300 to 2,000 regions, and for each one the network pools a fixed-size feature (RoI pooling or the more precise RoIAlign, which uses bilinear interpolation instead of rounding coordinates) and refines the class and box. This costs more time but tends to be more accurate for small or crowded objects. For instance segmentation, Mask R-CNN adds a small convolutional branch that predicts a 28 × 28 mask per region for its class, and the mask is resized into the box. Semantic segmentation networks such as U-Net or DeepLab instead produce a class score for every pixel, through an encoder that shrinks the image and a decoder that upsamples it, with skip connections that carry fine detail across. The loss is per-pixel cross-entropy, often combined with a Dice or IoU loss to cope with small objects.
DETR changes the post-processing. A transformer decoder outputs a fixed set of, say, 100 predictions. During training, the Hungarian algorithm finds the best one-to-one match between predictions and ground-truth boxes, and only matched predictions receive box losses, while the rest must predict "no object". Because the model learns not to duplicate, NMS is not needed at inference. The price was slow convergence, which later variants (Deformable DETR, RT-DETR) reduced.

example: Four detections arrive for one class. Boxes are written (x1, y1, x2, y2).
Box A = (10, 10, 110, 110), score 0.90. Box C = (20, 20, 120, 120), score 0.85. Box B = (30, 30, 130, 130), score 0.60. Box D = (300, 300, 380, 380), score 0.70.
Step 1: compute IoU between A and C. Each box has area 100 × 100 = 10,000. The overlap is the square from (20, 20) to (110, 110), so 90 × 90 = 8,100. The union is 10,000 + 10,000 − 8,100 = 11,900. IoU = 8,100 / 11,900 = 0.68.
Step 2: compute IoU between A and B. The overlap is from (30, 30) to (110, 110), so 80 × 80 = 6,400. The union is 20,000 − 6,400 = 13,600. IoU = 6,400 / 13,600 = 0.47.
Step 3: run NMS with threshold 0.5. Sort by score: A 0.90, C 0.85, D 0.70, B 0.60. Keep A. C has IoU 0.68 with A, which is above 0.5, so delete it. D does not overlap A, so it stays. Take D next and keep it. B has IoU 0.47 with A, below 0.5, so it also survives, and B has no overlap with D. Final result: A, D and B. If the three boxes A, C and B were all the same person, we now have a duplicate, B.
Step 4: lower the threshold to 0.4. Now B is deleted because 0.47 is above 0.4. The cost appears in a crowd. If B were a different person standing right behind A with 0.47 overlap, the lower threshold would delete a real object. This is why the threshold depends on the data, and why Soft-NMS (which lowers the score of an overlapping box by a factor that grows with IoU, instead of deleting it) and DETR-style one-to-one matching exist.
Step 5: evaluate. A prediction counts as correct if its class matches and its IoU with a ground-truth box is at least 0.5. Precision is correct predictions divided by all predictions, and recall is correct predictions divided by all ground-truth boxes. Sweeping the confidence threshold gives a precision-recall curve whose area is the average precision. COCO averages this over IoU thresholds 0.50, 0.55, …, 0.95, so a detector must also place boxes tightly to score well.
intuition: A detector is a very large set of tiny guards, each watching one spot at one size, who all shout "object here" at once, followed by an editor who keeps only the loudest shout for each object.
The design is natural because an image has no fixed number of objects, but it has a fixed number of positions. Turning "find all objects" into "for every position and size, answer one local question" makes the output a fixed-size grid that a convolutional network can produce, and weight sharing makes the answer the same everywhere. The cost of this trick is duplicates, since several neighbouring positions will see the same object, and the editor (NMS, or a one-to-one training rule) removes them.
mistakes: People often think a detector looks at the image many times with a sliding window. In fact modern detectors run the backbone once and read all positions from the shared feature map. The old sliding window with a separate network call per window is what R-CNN did, and it is why R-CNN took seconds per image.
People often think a higher mAP at IoU 0.5 means the boxes are good. In fact mAP@0.5 accepts a box that overlaps only half. COCO mAP averages up to 0.95, and a detector can score well at 0.5 and poorly at 0.9. If you need accurate boxes, as for measuring or robotic grasping, look at the strict thresholds.
People often think NMS is a harmless cleaning step. In fact it is a hand-tuned rule that decides recall in crowds, and it is not differentiable, so the network is never trained for it. A threshold that is right for cars on a road is wrong for people in a stadium.
People often think segmentation is detection with more detail. In fact semantic segmentation cannot count: two touching cars have the same class label and form one connected region. Instance or panoptic segmentation is needed when you must separate individual objects.
People often think a model trained on a public dataset will work on your cameras. In fact detectors depend heavily on viewpoint, lighting, object size and image quality. A model trained on COCO photos, where objects are large and centred, typically loses a large share of its accuracy on small objects in a wide-angle surveillance or drone view.
friction: Small objects are the persistent weakness. An object of 16 × 16 pixels covers one cell of a stride-16 map, so its features are mixed with the background after several downsampling steps. Higher input resolution, finer pyramid levels and tiling help, and each costs compute that grows with the number of pixels. Occlusion and crowds break the one-box-per-object assumption, and unusual views, rare classes and the long tail of categories remain hard.
Labels are expensive. A box takes seconds and a tight polygon mask takes close to a minute per object, and labelers disagree at object edges, so the ground truth has noise. Class imbalance and annotation bias limit what the model can learn. Foundation models like SAM reduce the cost by producing masks from prompts, but they do not know which objects matter for your task, and their masks still need review.
Speed and size are a constant trade. Real-time video at 30 frames per second leaves about 33 ms per frame for the whole pipeline, including decode, resize, inference and NMS. Post-processing on the CPU can take a significant share of that, and the models that look best in papers may not fit an edge device. Finally, detectors are brittle outside their training distribution: they can be fooled by small changes, produce confident false positives on textures, and give scores that are poorly calibrated as probabilities.
tradeoffs: Choose a two-stage detector (Faster R-CNN family) when accuracy on small or crowded objects matters more than speed, you run offline or on a strong GPU, and you can afford tens to a few hundred milliseconds per image. Choose a single-stage detector (YOLO, SSD, RetinaNet) when you need real-time rates of 30 to 100 or more frames per second or an edge deployment. You give up a few points of accuracy on the hardest cases and gain a simpler, faster pipeline.
Choose a DETR-style detector when NMS is a problem, as in dense scenes, or when you want a simple end-to-end pipeline and have enough data and training budget. Choose a convolutional single-stage model when you have limited data or compute, since transformer detectors usually need more training and data to converge.
Choose semantic segmentation when you need regions, not objects (road, sky, tissue); choose instance segmentation when you must count or track individual objects; choose a promptable model (SAM) when you need masks quickly for labeling or for classes you did not train on, and fine-tune a task-specific model when you need reliable results at scale.
practice: Start with a pre-trained detector and fine-tune it on a few thousand images from your own cameras. Libraries such as Torchvision, Detectron2, MMDetection and Ultralytics provide trained models and training loops. Check the licence of a model before you use it commercially, since some popular detectors are released under copyleft licences.
Evaluate what the product needs. Report per-class average precision, a precision-recall curve at your operating threshold, and error buckets by object size and by scene type (day and night, near and far). Look at false positives and misses in pictures; the pattern, such as "misses everything under 30 pixels", points to the fix (higher resolution or tiling). Tune the confidence threshold and the NMS IoU on a validation set from your own domain, not on COCO.
For deployment, export to an optimized runtime (TensorRT, ONNX Runtime, Core ML), use 16-bit or 8-bit quantization after checking accuracy, and batch frames when latency allows. Put NMS into the exported graph or on the GPU so that it does not become the bottleneck. For video, add a tracker on top of the detector so that detections link across frames and so that you can smooth out flicker.
interview: Why do detectors produce duplicate boxes, and how do you remove them? || Every nearby position and anchor can respond to the same object, so many boxes cover it. NMS keeps the best-scored box and removes boxes with IoU above a threshold; Soft-NMS decays scores instead; DETR-style training with one-to-one matching removes the need for NMS.
Why was focal loss needed for single-stage detectors? || A single-stage detector scores about 100,000 candidate locations and nearly all are easy background. Their summed loss swamps the gradient from the few real objects. Focal loss multiplies each example's loss by \( (1-p_t)^\gamma \), so confident correct predictions contribute almost nothing and training focuses on hard ones.
Your detector has good mAP@0.5 but customers complain that boxes are loose. What do you do? || Look at mAP at 0.75 and above and at the average IoU of matched boxes. Use a loss that optimizes overlap directly (GIoU, DIoU, CIoU), better box refinement (a cascade of heads), higher resolution features, and check annotation quality, since inconsistent labels cap achievable tightness.
How would you detect small objects in 4K images at 30 frames per second? || Running a full 4K image through a large model is too costly. Use tiles or a region of interest from motion or a first coarse pass, a finer pyramid level, and a light model; batch tiles on the GPU and run NMS across tile borders. I would measure recall by object size to see which choice pays.
What is the difference between semantic, instance and panoptic segmentation, and when does it matter? || Semantic assigns a class per pixel and cannot separate two touching objects of the same class; instance gives each object a mask but ignores background regions; panoptic combines both. It matters when you need counts or per-object tracking, which need instances.
quiz: What does IoU measure, and what is the IoU of two identical boxes? || It is the area of overlap divided by the area of union of two boxes. Identical boxes have IoU 1.
Why does non-maximum suppression make crowded scenes harder? || It deletes every box that overlaps a higher-scoring box above a threshold, so a real object standing close to another one can be removed along with the duplicates.
Why do anchors predict offsets relative to the anchor size instead of absolute coordinates? || Offsets normalized by anchor width and height make the targets similar in scale for small and large objects, so shared weights work for all sizes, and the log for width and height keeps sizes positive.
A detector has a stride-16 feature map for a 640 × 640 image with 9 anchors per position. How many anchors does it have at that level? || The map is 40 × 40 = 1,600 positions, and 1,600 × 9 = 14,400 anchors.
When would you choose a single-stage detector over a two-stage detector, and what do you give up? || When you need real-time speed or must run on an edge device. You may lose some accuracy on small or crowded objects, which two-stage detectors with region refinement usually handle better.

@@ anomaly
value: Anomaly detection finds events that do not look like the rest: a card payment from a new country at 3 a.m., a sudden fall in daily impressions, a device that clicks 40 ads a minute, a server whose latency moves away from its usual pattern. Fraud detection is the same problem with a financial or adversarial edge, where someone is trying to look normal. The main difficulty is that you rarely have good examples of the thing you want to find. Fraud may be 0.1% of events, new attacks look different from old ones, and labels arrive weeks late through chargebacks or manual review.
For that reason the standard approach is to describe what normal looks like and to measure how far each event is from it. The description can be a statistical baseline (mean and spread by hour of week), a tree-based isolation score, a distance to the nearest cluster, a reconstruction error from an autoencoder, or features taken from a graph of shared devices and accounts. A score above a threshold creates an alert, and rules and human review confirm it. Where some labeled fraud exists, a supervised classifier is added, because labeled examples are the strongest signal there is.
The layer above uses this everywhere. Data quality and observability systems run volume and distribution monitors on pipelines, which are anomaly detectors on counts. Alerting watches metrics for unusual values. Security teams look for intrusions and account takeover. In programmatic advertising, invalid-traffic detection filters bots, click farms and spoofed apps before an advertiser pays. The layers below supply the tools. Probability and statistics (L4) define "unusual" as a small probability under a model, give tail bounds, and show how many false alerts a threshold will create. Decision trees and boosting (L2) give isolation forests and supervised fraud models. Clustering (L2) gives distance-to-cluster and mixture-model scores.
terms: Baseline — the model of normal behaviour, such as an expected value and a spread for each hour of the week.
Anomaly score — a number that grows as an event looks less normal; an alert is a score above a threshold.
Point, contextual and collective anomaly — one strange value; a value strange only in its context (high traffic at 4 a.m.); a group of events that is strange only together (a burst of small payments).
Z-score — the number of standard deviations a value is from the mean, \( z = (x - \mu)/\sigma \).
Isolation forest — an ensemble of random trees; points that are separated from the rest in few random splits are anomalies.
Reconstruction error — the difference between an input and what an autoencoder rebuilds from its compressed form; high error means the input is unlike the training data.
Base rate — the share of events that are truly anomalous; it controls how many alerts are real.
Precision at k — the share of the top k highest-scored events that are truly anomalous.
Concept drift — the slow change of what is normal, which makes an old baseline wrong.
history: The statistics of outliers is old. Chauvenet (1863) and Peirce (1852) proposed rules to reject observations that are too far from the mean, and Grubbs (1950, 1969) gave the test that is still taught. Shewhart (1920s) invented the control chart at Bell Labs: plot a process measurement over time with limits at three standard deviations, and investigate points outside. These tools assume one roughly normal quantity at a time.
Fraud detection moved to machine learning in the 1990s as banks collected card data. Neural networks for card fraud were deployed in the early 1990s, and large processors have used models since. Breunig, Kriegel, Ng and Sander's local outlier factor (2000) compared the density around a point to the density around its neighbours, which handles clusters of different density. Schölkopf and colleagues' one-class support vector machine (2001) learned a boundary around normal data. Chandola, Banerjee and Kumar's survey (2009) organized the field into statistical, nearest-neighbour, clustering and classification techniques.
Liu, Ting and Zhou's isolation forest (2008) made a conceptual change: do not model normal and measure distance, just count how easy it is to separate a point with random splits. It is fast, has few parameters and works in many dimensions, and became a default. Deep methods followed: autoencoders for reconstruction error, variational and adversarial models, and self-supervised methods (Pang and colleagues' 2021 survey covers them). For time series, Twitter's S-H-ESD method (Hochenbaum, Vallis and Kejariwal, 2017) combined seasonal decomposition with a robust test and became the pattern for metric monitoring. In fraud, the last decade added graph features, because organized fraud shares devices, addresses and payment instruments, and gradient-boosted trees on engineered features became the strong supervised baseline.
how: Every detector has three steps: build a model of normal, score each event by its distance from the model, and decide a threshold. The methods differ in step one.
Statistical baselines are the simplest. For a metric such as hourly impressions, keep a baseline per hour of week, because traffic has strong daily and weekly cycles. A robust choice uses the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD) instead of the mean and standard deviation, since the outliers you want to catch would otherwise distort the baseline. The score is \( z = (x - \text{median}) / (1.4826 \times \text{MAD}) \); the constant makes the MAD comparable to a standard deviation for normal data. Probability tells us what a z-score means. For normal data, \( P(|Z| > 3) \approx 0.0027 \). Without any assumption about the distribution, Chebyshev's inequality still gives \( P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le 1/k^2 \), so a deviation of five standard deviations has at most a 4% probability, a weak but safe bound. For time series, remove trend and seasonality first (STL decomposition or a forecast model) and score the residual.
Isolation forest uses trees (the decision-tree concept, but with random splits and no labels). Build 100 trees. Each tree is grown on a random sample of 256 points. At each node choose a random feature and a random split value between that feature's minimum and maximum in the node, and continue until each point is alone or a depth limit is reached. A point far from the others is separated after very few splits, while a point inside a dense region needs many. The anomaly score of a point uses the average path length \( E[h(x)] \) over all trees:
\[ s(x) = 2^{-E[h(x)]/c(n)}, \qquad c(n) = 2H(n-1) - \frac{2(n-1)}{n} \]
where \( H(i) \approx \ln i + 0.5772 \) is the harmonic number and \( c(n) \) is the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree, used to normalize. Scores near 1 are anomalies, and scores below about 0.5 are normal. Building needs no labels and no distance computations, and the cost is linear in the sample size.
Clustering gives distance-based scores. Fit k-means or a Gaussian mixture model to normal data, and score an event by its distance to the nearest centre, or by the negative log-likelihood under the mixture (which accounts for the spread of each cluster). Local outlier factor goes further and compares local densities, so a point that is sparse relative to its neighbours scores high even if the whole region is sparse. Autoencoders learn to compress and rebuild normal inputs. Anything that the network cannot rebuild well, because it differs from the training data, has high reconstruction error.
When labels exist, use supervised learning on the labeled part. Gradient-boosted trees on features such as amount, time since last transaction, count of events per device in the last hour, and mismatch between billing and shipping country are the strong baseline in fraud. Class imbalance is handled by sample weights or undersampling the majority class (and then correcting the probabilities), and the model is evaluated by precision-recall curves, not accuracy. Unsupervised scores are often fed in as features of the supervised model, so the two approaches combine.
Graph features catch what single events cannot show. Compute, for each account, the number of distinct devices it used, the number of accounts that share its device, the degree and the connected-component size, and the age of the neighbours. A cluster of 40 new accounts sharing 3 devices is a ring, although each account looks normal. These features are cheap to compute in a batch job and powerful in practice.
Deployment completes the system. Scores are thresholded using the cost of each error: if reviewing an alert costs 5 minutes and a missed fraud costs 200 dollars, the threshold is the score at which the expected loss equals the review cost. A rules layer adds business knowledge, and a review queue is ordered by score. Confirmed outcomes flow back as labels, and the baseline is refreshed so that it follows drift. The pipeline stays under human control because attackers change behaviour once they learn what triggers alerts.

example: Take two checks, one statistical and one base-rate calculation.
Step 1: a metric. Daily impressions of a campaign have a baseline mean of 10.0 million and a standard deviation of 0.5 million for this weekday. Today the number is 8.2 million. The z-score is (8.2 − 10.0) / 0.5 = −3.6. For normal data the two-sided probability of a deviation this large is about 0.0003, so this is unusual. But it is not proof: a one-in-3,000 event will happen to some campaign every few days if you watch thousands of them.
Step 2: multiple checks. Suppose you evaluate each metric once per minute with an alert at |z| > 3, which has a false alarm probability of 0.0027 per check for normal data. Each metric makes 1,440 checks a day, so you expect 1,440 × 0.0027 ≈ 3.9 false alerts per metric per day. With 500 metrics that is almost 2,000 false alerts a day. This is why monitors need longer windows, persistence rules ("three minutes in a row"), or a higher threshold, and why alert fatigue is the real cost.
Step 3: base rate. A card processor sees 1,000,000 transactions, of which 0.1% (1,000) are fraud. A detector catches 90% of fraud (900 cases) and wrongly flags 1% of normal transactions (9,990 cases). The alerts total 900 + 9,990 = 10,890, so precision is 900 / 10,890 = 8.3%. Only about 1 alert in 12 is real, although the detector looks excellent (90% recall, 99% specificity). If an analyst reviews an alert in 3 minutes, the team spends 10,890 × 3 = 32,670 minutes, about 545 hours, to find 900 frauds.
Step 4: improve precision. Cut the false positive rate to 0.2% at the same 90% recall: false alerts become 1,998, alerts total 2,898, precision becomes 31%. The review time is 145 hours. A 5-times reduction in false positives is worth more than a few points of recall, which is why the metric to track is precision at the volume your team can review, say the top 500 alerts a day.
Step 5: isolation score. With a sample of n = 256, \( H(255) \approx \ln 255 + 0.5772 = 6.118 \), so \( c(256) = 2 \times 6.118 - 2 \times 255/256 \approx 10.24 \). A point in a dense region with an average path length of 10 gets \( s = 2^{-10/10.24} \approx 0.51 \). A far point isolated after an average of 4 splits gets \( s = 2^{-4/10.24} \approx 0.76 \). You then choose a threshold such as the top 0.5% of scores, not a fixed number such as 0.76, because the score scale depends on data.
intuition: An anomaly detector is a security guard who knows every regular face in the building: no list of criminals is needed, and the guard stops anyone who does not fit.
The design is natural because the unusual is defined against the usual, and the usual is plentiful and well-labelled by default. Rare events can never be fully described in advance, since attackers choose them to be new, so describing the majority and flagging the exception covers cases nobody has seen. The price is the same as for the guard: strangers who are innocent are stopped too, and an intruder who dresses like a regular is not. Everything else in the field is about reducing that price with context, with better features, and with human review.
mistakes: People often think a detector with 99% accuracy is good for fraud. In fact if 99.9% of events are normal, a model that never flags anything has 99.9% accuracy and catches nothing. With a base rate this low, even a 1% false positive rate buries real cases, as the 8.3% precision example shows. Use precision-recall measures at the review volume you can afford.
People often think an unusual event is a bad event. In fact an anomaly only means rare under the model. A marketing campaign, a holiday or a product launch produces large, legitimate changes. Models need context features (calendar, deploys, campaigns), and alerts should say how the event differs, not only that it does.
People often think more complex models are better detectors. In fact on tabular event data with some labels, gradient-boosted trees with well-chosen features usually beat deep models, and a median-and-MAD baseline per hour of week catches most operational incidents. Choose the simple method first and ask what it misses.
People often think the model is finished once it is trained. In fact fraud is adversarial: when fraudsters see which behaviour is blocked, they change it. What is normal also drifts. A static model decays, so you need monitoring of score distributions, label feedback and regular retraining.
friction: Labels are the biggest problem. They are rare, delayed, biased and sometimes wrong. A chargeback arrives 30 to 90 days after a payment, so the most recent data has no labels yet, and events that were blocked have no outcome at all, so the model never learns whether the block was right. This selection effect means that training only on reviewed cases teaches the model to reproduce past decisions.
Adversaries and drift add moving targets. Attackers probe thresholds, spread activity across many devices and use real device fingerprints. Legitimate behaviour changes with product releases, so a model must separate "the world changed" from "something is wrong". Interpretation is another problem. An alert with only a score cannot be acted upon; reviewers need the reason (which features contributed), and deep models give this poorly. Finally, the cost of false positives is not only review time. In fraud, blocking a good customer loses revenue and trust, and in advertising, wrongly filtering real traffic makes a publisher lose money.
tradeoffs: Choose a simple statistical baseline (median and MAD per hour of week, plus persistence rules) for operational metrics and pipeline monitors: it is cheap, explainable and works with no labels. Choose a learned model when behaviour depends on many features together and the baseline produces too many false alerts. You give up transparency for coverage.
Choose a supervised classifier when you have at least a few thousand confirmed positives and labels arrive within weeks; choose unsupervised or semi-supervised detection when positives are very few, the threat is new, or labels are late. In practice combine them: unsupervised scores and graph features as inputs to a supervised model, trained on recent data with time-based splits.
Choose isolation forest as the first unsupervised model on mixed numeric features with thousands to millions of rows: it scales linearly and needs little tuning. Choose local outlier factor or a mixture model when density varies by region and you have fewer than about 100,000 points. Choose an autoencoder when inputs are high-dimensional and structured (images, long sequences) and you have a lot of normal data. Each of the last two costs more tuning and is harder to explain.
Choose a threshold from costs: set it where the expected cost of review equals the expected loss prevented. A tighter threshold raises precision and lowers recall, and the right balance depends on review capacity and the loss per missed case. Choose real-time scoring (milliseconds, features from a fast store) for payments and bid requests, and batch scoring (hourly or daily) for account rings and pipeline checks, accepting that batch detection reacts later.
practice: Define the cost of each error before building anything. Ask how many alerts a day the team can review and what a missed case costs. Then choose the metric: precision at the reviewable volume, recall at that precision, and time to detect. Keep an unbiased audit sample: let a small random share of traffic through without blocking and review it, so you can estimate the true rate and measure the model on cases it did not decide.
Build features that capture velocity and context: counts and sums over 1 minute, 1 hour and 1 day per device, account and IP; the ratio to the entity's own history; the time since the previous event; and mismatches between fields. Compute the same features in the batch training job and in the real-time path from one definition, or training and serving will disagree. Split train and test by time. For metrics, use seasonal baselines by hour of week, check the baseline against holidays, and require persistence or a minimum volume to avoid alerts on tiny counts.
Close the loop. Send alerts to a queue with the reason codes, record the reviewer's decision, retrain with those labels, and watch the alert rate and score distribution for drift. Combine layers: hard rules for known bad patterns, a model for ranking, and human review for the top of the list. In ad traffic, follow industry standards for invalid traffic (the Media Rating Council guidelines separate general invalid traffic, found by lists and simple rules, from sophisticated invalid traffic, found by analysis) and keep both kinds of filtering auditable.
interview: A fraud model has 99.5% accuracy. Is it good? What do you ask? || Accuracy is meaningless at a low base rate. I would ask for the base rate, then precision and recall at the volume the review team can handle, a precision-recall curve, and the cost per false positive and per missed case. Then I would check how labels were created and whether the test set is time-split.
You have 500 metrics checked every minute with a 3-sigma rule, and the on-call team is flooded. How do you fix it? || The expected false alerts are 500 × 1,440 × 0.0027 ≈ 1,900 a day even if nothing is wrong. I would use seasonal baselines, require persistence over several minutes, aggregate related alerts, use a robust score, and set thresholds from the on-call capacity. I would also review which alerts people actually acted on.
How do you build a fraud detector when you have almost no labels? || Start with rules and an unsupervised score (isolation forest or per-entity baselines) plus graph features. Send the top-scored cases to review to create labels, and keep a random audit sample to estimate the base rate. Once there are enough labels, train a boosted-tree model and use the unsupervised scores as features.
Why can training on only reviewed or blocked cases hurt? || The label depends on what the old system chose to review, so the model learns the old system's behaviour (selection bias) and cannot find fraud the old system missed. Randomized sampling of traffic for review, and counterfactual or audit data, give an unbiased view.
Fraudsters adapt to your model within weeks. What do you change? || Monitor score distributions and the fraud rate by segment, retrain on recent data often, use features that are hard to fake (behaviour over time, network structure), keep some rules and models secret and diverse, and add layers so that evading one does not evade all. Expect an arms race, not a final fix.
quiz: What is the base rate and why does it matter for anomaly detection? || It is the share of events that are truly anomalous. When it is very low, even a small false positive rate produces many more false alerts than real detections, so precision is low.
Why does an isolation forest give anomalies short average path lengths? || Random splits separate an isolated point from the others very quickly, in a few splits, while points inside a dense region need many splits to be separated.
Why use the median and MAD instead of the mean and standard deviation for a baseline? || The mean and standard deviation are pulled by the very outliers you want to detect, which inflates the spread and hides them. The median and MAD are robust to a few extreme values.
A detector flags 1% of 2,000,000 normal events and catches 95% of 2,000 frauds. What is its precision? || False alerts are 1% of 1,998,000 ≈ 19,980. True alerts are 0.95 × 2,000 = 1,900. Precision is 1,900 / (1,900 + 19,980) ≈ 8.7%.
When would you prefer a supervised model over an unsupervised detector, and what is the risk? || When you have enough confirmed labels that are reasonably recent. The risk is that it learns only known patterns and the selection bias of past reviews, so new attacks pass it.

@@ ot
value: Operational transformation (OT) lets several people edit the same document at the same time and still end with identical text. Each user sees their own edits immediately, with no waiting for a server. The problem is that edits are made against different versions of the document. If Alice inserts a character at position 1 while Bob deletes the character at position 2, then applying Bob's "delete at 2" after Alice's insert deletes the wrong character, because everything after position 1 has moved by one. OT fixes this by rewriting each incoming operation so that it means the same thing in the document state where it is applied.
This is the technique behind Google Docs, and earlier behind Google Wave and Etherpad. It gives three things at once: low latency (local edits apply at once), convergence (everyone ends with the same document) and intention preservation (an edit lands where its author meant it, even if text moved). The alternative of locking a paragraph while someone types prevents concurrent editing. The alternative of "last writer wins" loses edits.
For the layer above, real-time collaboration (live cursors, shared documents, co-editing in browsers) needs a way to merge concurrent edits without conflicts that users must resolve. Real-time update channels such as WebSockets carry the operations to every client, and OT decides what each client does with them. The layers below make the design possible. Consistency models (L2) define what "everyone sees the same document" means when updates arrive in different orders. Lamport clocks and causal ordering (L2) tell us which operations were concurrent and so need transformation, and which came one after another and do not. Order theory (L4) describes the document histories as a partial order whose concurrent branches must meet again. Proofs and induction (L4) are how the transformation functions are shown correct, which is the hardest part.
terms: Operation — a small description of a change, such as insert("X", position 1) or delete(position 2), instead of the full new text.
Concurrent operations — two operations made without either user having seen the other; neither happened before the other.
Transformation function — a function T(a, b) that rewrites operation a so it can be applied after operation b has already been applied.
Convergence — all replicas end in the same document state after they have applied the same set of operations.
Intention preservation — each operation has the effect its author wanted, in the state where it is finally applied.
Revision number — the count of operations the server has accepted; a client says which revision its operation was based on.
TP1 — the property that two concurrent operations a and b, transformed against each other, reach the same state in either order.
Tombstone — a marker that keeps a deleted item's slot; CRDTs use it, and classic OT does not.
history: OT was introduced by Clarence Ellis and Simon Gibbs in "Concurrency Control in Groupware Systems" (SIGMOD 1989), in the GROVE collaborative outliner. They saw that locking was too slow for people typing together, and proposed that operations execute at once and are transformed when they meet others. Their algorithm, dOPT, worked for the cases they tested, but Ressel, Nitsche-Ruhland and Gunzenhäuser (1996) found a case where replicas diverged, now called the dOPT puzzle, and proposed the adOPTed algorithm with a formal framework.
Nichols, Curtis, Dixon and Lamping's Jupiter system (UIST 1995, at Xerox PARC) made a decisive simplification: use a central server. Each client talks to the server only, so there are only two sites to reconcile at any time. Jupiter is the ancestor of the designs used in production today. Sun, Jia, Zhang, Yang and Chen (1998) defined the goals of convergence, causality preservation and intention preservation for the peer-to-peer case, with more complex algorithms. Many peer-to-peer algorithms were published, and many were later shown wrong. Imine, Molli, Oster and Rusinowitch (2003) used automated verification to find counterexamples in published transformation functions. This history is the reason people say OT is hard.
OT reached users on a large scale in Google Docs and in Google Wave (2009), whose protocol was a client-server OT design described in public white papers. Etherpad used OT, was acquired by Google in 2009, and was later released as open source. The open source library ShareJS, later ShareDB, by Joseph Gentle, brought OT to many JavaScript applications, and Quill's Delta format gives a modern, compact operation type for rich text. Meanwhile Oster and colleagues (2006) published WOOT, an early algorithm that avoids transformation altogether, and this started the line of CRDTs that is the main alternative today.
how: The basic setting of a production OT system is client-server, in the style of Jupiter. The server holds the authoritative document and a numbered history of accepted operations. Each client holds a copy of the document and a revision number: the count of server operations it has applied. When the user types, the client applies the operation to its local copy at once and sends it to the server, tagged with the revision it was based on.
The central problem appears when an operation arrives at the server based on revision r, but the server is already at revision r + k. The k operations that were accepted in between are concurrent with the new one: its author had not seen them. The server transforms the new operation against those k operations, one by one, producing a version that applies to the current document. It then appends the result as revision r + k + 1, applies it, and broadcasts it. The server order is the total order of the document, which is what makes the system simple: it gives a single history that everyone agrees on.
The transformation function is the core. For text with single-character operations it is a small table. Let ins(p, c) insert character c at position p and del(p) delete the character at position p. To transform operation a against operation b (already applied), adjust a's position to account for b:
Step 1: if b is ins(pb, c) and a is ins(pa, d): if pa < pb, a is unchanged; if pa > pb, a moves to pa + 1; if pa = pb, a tie-break rule is needed (for example the lower client id goes first), and it must be the same on every site.
Step 2: if b is ins(pb, c) and a is del(pa): if pa < pb unchanged, otherwise a moves to pa + 1.
Step 3: if b is del(pb) and a is ins(pa, d): if pa ≤ pb unchanged, otherwise a moves to pa − 1.
Step 4: if b is del(pb) and a is del(pa): if pa < pb unchanged; if pa > pb, pa − 1; if they are equal, both users deleted the same character and a becomes a no-op.
The client side adds the second half of the protocol. A client may have sent an operation and not yet received the acknowledgement. In the Jupiter and Google Wave designs, the client has at most one operation in flight and buffers the rest, composing them into one pending operation. While it waits, server operations from other users arrive. The client transforms each incoming server operation against its in-flight and buffered operations, and also transforms those against the incoming one. Both sides apply the same transformation pairs, so they reach the same state. Because there are only two parties at each step (client and server), the number of transformation cases stays small. The cost for one operation is about one transform per concurrent operation, so a client that is k revisions behind costs O(k) transforms.
The correctness condition is called TP1. For two concurrent operations a and b that start from the same state S, applying a and then the transformed b must give the same state as applying b and then the transformed a:
\[ S \circ a \circ T(b, a) \;=\; S \circ b \circ T(a, b) \]
This is a "diamond": two paths from the same start that end at one point. Here order theory and proofs enter. Document states reached by different orderings of concurrent operations form a lattice, and TP1 says that the squares of this lattice commute. For client-server OT, TP1 is enough, because the server's total order means that you never need to transform one operation against two sites' views at once. Peer-to-peer OT needs the stronger property TP2 (transformation order independence for three concurrent operations), which is very hard to get right and was the source of most published bugs. Proving TP1 for a given pair of operation types is a case analysis over their combinations, and for richer data (rich text, JSON trees) the number of cases grows, which is why correctness is checked by both proofs and exhaustive or randomized testing.
Causality still matters. The revision number is a simple causal clock: it tells the server exactly which operations the client had seen when it made its edit, so the server transforms against precisely the concurrent ones and not more. In a peer-to-peer system, a Lamport timestamp or a vector clock fills that role. The result is a system with strong eventual consistency: all replicas that have applied the same operations hold the same document, and with a central server they pass through one agreed history.

example: Walk through the diagram above with numbers, then one harder case.
Step 1: start with the text "ABC" at revision 0 on the server and on both clients. Positions start at 0, so A is at 0, B at 1, C at 2.
Step 2: Alice types X between A and B, which is ins(1, "X"). Her screen shows "AXBC" at once. At the same time Bob deletes C, which is del(2). His screen shows "AB". Neither has seen the other's change.
Step 3: the server receives Alice's operation first and it is based on revision 0, which equals the current revision, so no transformation is needed. The document becomes "AXBC" and the revision becomes 1. It is sent to Bob and acknowledged to Alice.
Step 4: the server receives Bob's del(2), based on revision 0. The server is at revision 1, so one operation has happened in between. Transform del(2) against ins(1, "X"): the insert is at position 1, which is not after position 2, so everything at or after 1 shifted right, and the delete becomes del(3). The server applies it to "AXBC", which deletes C and gives "AXB" at revision 2.
Step 5: Alice's client receives del(3) as revision 2. It has no pending operations of its own (her operation was acknowledged), so it applies del(3) to "AXBC" and gets "AXB". Bob's client receives Alice's ins(1, "X") as revision 1. It has one pending operation, del(2), already applied. It transforms the incoming insert against the pending delete: position 1 is before position 2, so it is unchanged. Bob applies it to "AB" and gets "AXB". The third message for Bob is the acknowledgement of his own delete. All three copies show "AXB".
Step 6: a tie case. Both Alice and Bob insert at position 1 of "AB": Alice inserts "X", Bob inserts "Y". The server takes Alice first, giving "AXB". Bob's ins(1, "Y") transforms against ins(1, "X"): positions are equal, so a rule is needed. If the rule is "the operation that arrives later goes to the right", Bob's insert becomes ins(2, "Y") and the result is "AXYB". Bob's own client already shows "AYB". When it receives Alice's insert it must produce "AXYB" as well, so it inserts X at position 1, to the left of its own Y. The tie rule has to be consistent on both sides, or the documents diverge: "AXYB" on one and "AYXB" on the other.
intuition: Operational transformation is like two people editing a printed page by phone: when Bob says "delete the third word", Alice, who has just added a word at the start, translates it to "the fourth word" before she acts.
The design is natural because an edit is relative to the state it was made in. Instead of forcing everyone to wait for the same state (locking), OT lets each person act on their own state and then corrects the meaning of the edit when it travels. The server only has to choose one order. After that, each pair of operations is a small geometry problem: if the two paths in the diamond meet at the same state, any combination of paths does too. This is the commutation of the diamond, and it is why the transformation functions are small even though the number of possible histories is huge.
mistakes: People often think OT sends the whole document or a diff of it. In fact it sends tiny operations such as insert and delete with a position, and the server stores a log of them. This keeps messages small, usually a few dozen bytes, and makes transformation possible, since there are no document snapshots to merge.
People often think OT needs no server. In fact the algorithms that scale in production use a central server for ordering, because that removes the need for TP2. Fully peer-to-peer OT exists but is complex, and its published algorithms had many errors. Without a central coordinator, CRDTs are the usual choice.
People often think OT is a conflict-resolution trick that picks a winner. In fact OT merges both edits: nothing is dropped. The tie rule only decides the order of two inserts at the same place. The only information lost is in cases where two operations are truly contradictory, such as two users changing the same word to different spellings, where one change necessarily comes after the other.
People often think that if the text converges, the result is what the users meant. In fact convergence only means that all replicas are equal. Two users who each fix the same sentence differently can converge to a garbled sentence. Intention preservation works at the level of positions and ranges, not meaning.
friction: Correctness is the hardest part. Every pair of operation types needs a transformation function, so the number of cases grows with the square of the number of operation types. Rich documents have many: insert, delete, format a range, move, split a paragraph, edit a table cell, comment anchors. Each new operation needs transforms against all existing ones, and each must satisfy TP1. Many published algorithms were later proven incorrect, and production teams rely on randomized tests that generate random operation pairs and check convergence.
The server is a bottleneck and a single point of failure for a document. All operations for a document must pass through one ordering point, so a document is sharded by its id, and a failure or a network partition stops collaboration for that document. Offline editing is limited: a client that was offline for an hour has a long list of pending operations, and the server must transform them against everything that happened in the meantime. Transform cost grows with the length of the gap, and merges can produce surprising results.
tradeoffs: Choose OT with a central server when you have a web application with an authoritative backend, mostly online users, and a document model of text or a tree of modest size. The ordering point makes the system simple and gives a single history for audit, permissions and undo. You give up offline editing, peer-to-peer operation and independence from the server.
Choose a CRDT (Yjs, Automerge, Loro) when users edit offline for long periods, devices sync peer-to-peer, or the server should not need to understand the data. A CRDT carries per-item metadata (identifiers, sometimes tombstones), so memory and document size grow, but merging needs no transformation functions and no ordering point. Libraries have narrowed the overhead to a small multiple of the text size, and the cost is a design that must stay compatible with the CRDT's data model.
Choose operation-based sync with a simple rule, such as last-writer-wins per field, when conflicts are rare and structured, like form fields or settings. Choose OT or CRDTs only when many people edit the same text or list at the same time. For these simple cases, transformation is more machinery than the problem needs.
practice: Do not invent your own transformation functions for production data. Use an existing library: ShareDB with its JSON0 or rich-text types, the Quill Delta library for rich text, or a CRDT library such as Yjs if your architecture fits. If you must write transforms, make the operation set as small as possible, define it with the full TP1 checks, and write a randomized test that creates thousands of pairs and sequences of concurrent operations on a few clients, plays them through your server in random orders, and asserts that all documents are equal at the end.
Design the protocol around one operation in flight per client. Tag each operation with the base revision, have the server acknowledge it, and let the client buffer. Make operations idempotent at the transport level (a client retry must not apply twice) using client ids and sequence numbers. Keep the operation log, with periodic snapshots, so that new clients load the latest snapshot and then the later operations.
interview: Explain why applying a delete at position 2 after a concurrent insert at position 1 breaks the document, and how OT fixes it. || The insert moved every later character one place to the right, so position 2 now names a different character. OT transforms the delete against the insert: because the insert is at or before the delete's position, the delete shifts to position 3. The operation then deletes the character its author saw.
Why is a central server such a big simplification? || It supplies a total order, so only two parties (the client and the server) ever need to be reconciled, and TP1 is enough. Peer-to-peer OT must also satisfy TP2 for three or more concurrent operations, which was the source of most incorrect algorithms.
What are TP1 and TP2? || TP1 says that for two concurrent operations a and b, applying a then T(b, a) equals applying b then T(a, b). TP2 says the transformed forms of a third operation c are the same regardless of the order in which a and b were transformed against it. TP2 is needed when there is no single server ordering.
How does the client handle operations while waiting for the server's acknowledgement? || It keeps one operation in flight and buffers further ones, composing them. Incoming server operations are transformed against the in-flight and buffered operations, and those operations are transformed against the incoming one, so the client's local view stays consistent and the next send is based on the latest acknowledged revision.
When would you choose a CRDT instead of OT? || When devices work offline for long periods, when peers sync directly, or when no central server should order operations. I accept metadata overhead and a data model fixed by the CRDT library. With a reliable central backend, OT is smaller in memory and gives a simple history.
How would you test an OT implementation? || Property-based tests: generate random document states and random concurrent operations, check TP1 for every pair, then simulate several clients and a server with random delays and reorderings and assert convergence. Also replay recorded production traces, and fuzz rich-text and structured operations separately.
quiz: What problem does operational transformation solve? || Concurrent edits are made against different document versions, so a position in one operation may point to the wrong place after another operation is applied. OT rewrites the operation so that it keeps its meaning in the new state and all copies converge.
Why does an insert transformed against a concurrent delete before its position move to the left by one? || The delete removed a character before that position, so every later character shifted left by one place; the insert must shift too to land at the same spot in the text.
Why must the tie-break for two inserts at the same position be the same on all sites? || If sites order the two characters differently, one ends with "XY" and the other with "YX", and the documents diverge. A deterministic rule, such as ordering by client id, gives one result everywhere.
A client based its operation on revision 40 and the server is at revision 47. How many transformations does the server do for this operation? || One against each of the 7 operations accepted since revision 40, so 7 transformations.
When is OT the better choice than a CRDT, and what do you give up? || When there is a reliable central server and users are mostly online. You get smaller documents, simple history and easier permissions. You give up offline and peer-to-peer editing and must write and prove transformation functions.

value: The browser rendering pipeline is the sequence of steps that turns bytes of HTML, CSS and JavaScript into pixels on the screen, and then keeps updating those pixels sixty or more times per second as the page changes. The steps are: parse the HTML into a tree of objects (the DOM) and the CSS into a style tree (the CSSOM); compute which style rules apply to each element; lay out the boxes (find the size and position of everything); paint the boxes into drawing instructions; and composite the painted layers into the final image, usually on the GPU.
Knowing this pipeline is what separates "my page is slow" from a diagnosis. Each step has a cost, each step depends on the previous one, and a change late in the chain is cheap while a change early in it repeats all the later steps. Changing the colour of a button re-runs paint and composite. Changing its width re-runs layout, which can move other elements, and then paint and composite. Changing its transform with a compositor-friendly animation may re-run only composite. Most front-end performance work is about moving changes later in the pipeline and avoiding work that is not needed.
The pipeline also has a hard limit: JavaScript, style, layout and paint share one main thread in the browser, and the event loop runs one task at a time. A script that runs for 200 ms blocks clicks, scrolling that depends on the main thread, and the next frame. For the layer above, browsers (Blink, Gecko, WebKit) implement the pipeline, React batches updates to avoid repeating layout, and web architecture choices such as server versus client rendering are choices about which of these steps happen on the server and which on the user's device. The layers below supply the mechanisms. Parsing and grammars (L3) turn HTML and CSS text into trees, with the error-tolerant rules that make every HTML page parse. Displays and scanout (L3) set the deadline: the screen reads a frame at a fixed rate, so the browser has about 16.7 ms per frame at 60 Hz. The event loop (L2) decides when scripts and rendering get to run.
terms: DOM — the Document Object Model, the tree of objects the parser builds from HTML; scripts read and change it.
CSSOM — the CSS Object Model, the tree of style rules built from all stylesheets.
Render tree / layout tree — the tree of visible boxes, made by combining the DOM with computed styles.
Layout (reflow) — computing the size and position of each box from styles, content and the viewport.
Paint — turning boxes into a list of drawing instructions (draw this rectangle, this text, this image).
Raster — converting drawing instructions into actual pixels of a layer, now often on the GPU.
Composite — combining raster layers in the right order, with transforms and opacity, into the frame the screen shows.
Main thread — the single thread that runs JavaScript, style, layout and (in many browsers) paint for a page.
Render-blocking resource — a stylesheet or synchronous script that must load before the browser can paint.
Long task — a task on the main thread that runs longer than 50 ms, which makes the page feel unresponsive.
history: The first browsers, such as Mosaic (1993) and Netscape Navigator (1994), loaded a document, laid it out once and displayed it. Pages were static, and "rendering" had little to optimize. CSS (proposed in 1994, first recommendation 1996) separated style from content, and JavaScript (1995) made pages dynamic. Once a script could change the DOM after it was shown, browsers needed incremental layout and repaint, and the idea of a pipeline with stages that can be re-run appeared.
Three engine families came out of this period. Gecko came from Netscape and Mozilla (1998). KHTML (KDE, 1998) was forked by Apple as WebKit (2001) for Safari, and Google used WebKit in Chrome in 2008, then forked it in 2013 as Blink. Chrome (2008) also made a lasting design choice: one process per tab and, over the next years, a separate compositor thread. With a compositor thread, scrolling and some animations can continue while the main thread is busy with JavaScript.
The HTML5 specification (2008 onward, now maintained by WHATWG as a living standard) defined parsing in exact detail, including how to recover from errors, so that all browsers build the same DOM from the same broken markup. It also described the event loop and the "update the rendering" step. Mobile and high-resolution screens pushed rendering onto the GPU in the 2010s. Mozilla's Servo research project fed Firefox Quantum (2017) with a parallel style engine (Stylo) and later WebRender, which draws pages as GPU scenes. Chrome's RenderingNG project (publicly described from 2021) rebuilt much of Blink's pipeline into clearly separated stages with a focus on running them on separate threads and processes. Google's Core Web Vitals (2020), including LCP, CLS and, from March 2024, INP in place of First Input Delay, made rendering costs a measured part of search ranking and engineering goals.
how: Step 1: the network delivers HTML bytes. The tokenizer and tree builder, defined as a state machine in the HTML standard, turn them into tokens (start tag, end tag, text) and then into DOM nodes. This is the parsing concept in action: a formal grammar with defined error recovery, so that a missing closing tag still gives a predictable tree. While the main parser is working, a preload scanner looks ahead through the raw text for resources such as stylesheets, scripts and images and starts downloading them early. Parsing is incremental, so the browser can start building the page before the whole file arrives.
Step 2: CSS is parsed into rules. Stylesheets are render-blocking: the browser will not paint until it has the CSS, because painting unstyled content and then restyling would flash. A synchronous `<script>` with no `defer` or `async` attribute is parser-blocking: the parser stops, waits for the script to download, and runs it, because the script may call `document.write` or inspect the DOM. If a stylesheet is still loading, the script also waits for it, because the script might ask for a computed style. This is why scripts at the end of the body, or with `defer`, are the common advice.
Step 3, style: for each element, the browser finds all matching rules and computes the final value of every property, following the cascade (origin, specificity, order) and inheritance. Selectors are matched from right to left, so `.menu li a` first finds anchors, and then checks the ancestors. The result is the computed style of each element.
Step 4, layout: starting from the viewport width, the engine walks the box tree and computes sizes and positions. Block boxes stack vertically, inline text flows into lines and breaks at the container width, and flexbox and grid solve constraint systems. Layout often depends on the parent and on the children, so a change to one element can move many others. This is the most expensive stage on large pages, and its cost is roughly linear in the number of affected boxes.
Step 5, paint: the engine produces a display list per layer, a record of drawing operations in order (background, borders, text, children). Elements are grouped into layers when they need independent movement or effects, for example elements with a 3D transform, `will-change`, a fixed position or a video. Paint does not make pixels yet.
Step 6, raster and composite: layers are split into tiles, and raster workers turn the display list of each tile into pixels, mostly on the GPU. The compositor thread then draws the tiles in the right order with each layer's transform and opacity. Because composite works on finished textures, changing a layer's transform or opacity does not need layout or paint and can run on the compositor thread even when the main thread is busy. This is the reason that animating `transform` and `opacity` is smooth, while animating `left` or `width` is not.
Step 7, frame timing: the display reads a new frame at a fixed rate through vertical synchronization (vsync), 60 times a second on a typical screen, or 120 on many phones and laptops. The browser tries to deliver one frame per vsync. The HTML event loop has a step called "update the rendering" that runs at these opportunities: it runs animation-frame callbacks (`requestAnimationFrame`), then style and layout, then paint. If the main thread is still running a long task when vsync arrives, the frame is late and the user sees a stutter. So the budget is about 16.7 ms at 60 Hz and 8.3 ms at 120 Hz for everything the main thread must do per frame.
Step 8, invalidation: the browser avoids repeating work by marking what is dirty. Changing a style marks the element for recalculation, and if the change affects geometry it marks layout dirty, then paint. The browser usually batches these changes until the next frame. There is one exception that causes serious trouble: if a script reads a layout-dependent value such as `offsetHeight` or `getBoundingClientRect()` after a write that made layout dirty, the browser must run layout immediately to give an accurate answer. This is called forced synchronous layout. Alternating reads and writes in a loop makes layout run once per iteration. React's design, which collects changes in a virtual tree and writes them to the DOM in a batch, is a response to exactly this cost.
```js
// Layout thrashing: each read forces a layout after the previous write
for (const el of items) {
  el.style.width = box.offsetWidth / 2 + 'px';   // read, then write
}

// Batched: all reads first, then all writes -> one layout
const w = box.offsetWidth / 2;
for (const el of items) {
  el.style.width = w + 'px';
}
```

example: Take a page with 1,000 list items. A script runs when the user clicks "Resize" and sets each item's width to half of the container's width. We count work for two versions of the code.
Step 1: the thrashing version, as in the first code block: inside the loop, each iteration writes a width (which makes layout dirty) and the next iteration reads `box.offsetWidth` (which needs a clean layout). So the browser runs layout 1,000 times. If one layout of this page takes 2 ms, the click handler takes about 2,000 ms, two full seconds with the page frozen. That is about 120 dropped frames at 60 Hz.
Step 2: the batched version: read the width once, then do 1,000 writes. All the writes only mark layout dirty. The browser runs layout once, at the next frame: 2 ms, plus a little for the style changes. The result is the same and the cost is about 1,000 times smaller.
Step 3: the frame budget. At 60 Hz the budget is 1000 / 60 ≈ 16.7 ms. Suppose the browser itself needs about 6 ms to do style, layout, paint and commit for this page. This leaves roughly 10 ms for your JavaScript per frame. A handler that takes 50 ms fills three frames and also counts as a long task. Input events queue behind it, so the click on the next button reacts only after the handler is done. This is the quantity that Interaction to Next Paint measures: the time from an input to the next frame that shows the result, where under 200 ms is considered good.
Step 4: an animation. To move a panel 300 px to the right in 300 ms at 60 Hz you need 18 frames. With `left`, each frame changes geometry, so each frame needs layout, paint and composite on the main thread; if the page is complex, that can take 12 ms, and any extra JavaScript will cause dropped frames. With `transform: translateX(...)`, the panel is on its own layer, the compositor only changes its matrix each frame, and the main thread can be completely idle.
intuition: The pipeline is an assembly line in a print shop: text is typeset into a layout, the layout is printed onto transparent sheets, and the sheets are stacked and moved under a light, so changing the stack is cheap and re-typesetting is expensive.
Each stage depends on the output of the one before, and later stages have less information to compute. This is natural: you cannot paint a box before you know its size, and you cannot size it before you know its style. The browser's task is to avoid repeating the early, expensive stages. Splitting the picture into layers lets it move finished sheets without re-typesetting them, which is the idea behind compositing.
mistakes: People often think rendering starts only after the whole HTML file has been downloaded. In fact the parser works on the bytes as they arrive, and the browser can paint content before the end of the document. What blocks first paint are render-blocking CSS and parser-blocking scripts, not the document size.
People often think `async` and `defer` do the same thing. In fact both download the script without blocking the parser, but `defer` runs scripts in order after parsing is finished, and `async` runs each one as soon as it arrives, in any order, possibly in the middle of parsing. Scripts that depend on each other or on the DOM need `defer`.
People often think JavaScript animation is slow compared to CSS. In fact what matters is which properties change. A JavaScript animation of `transform` and `opacity` can run on the compositor; a CSS animation of `width` still needs layout every frame. The trick is to animate properties that only need composite, whichever API you use.
People often think a fast server means a fast page. In fact the time after the HTML arrives can be larger than the network time: parsing and executing 1 MB of JavaScript on a mid-range phone can take well over a second of main-thread time, and that time is invisible in the server metrics.
friction: The main thread is the bottleneck. JavaScript, style, layout and much of paint share it, so a single long script, a large DOM or an expensive selector delays everything, including input. Huge DOMs are costly because style and layout scale with the number of nodes; pages with tens of thousands of nodes are slow to update, and tools like `content-visibility: auto`, virtualized lists and CSS containment (`contain`) exist to limit how much work each change causes.
Measuring is hard. Lab tools such as Lighthouse use a simulated device, while real users have different CPUs and networks, so field data (for example the Chrome User Experience Report) is needed. Metrics also change: First Input Delay was replaced by INP in March 2024, and the thresholds are tuned over time. Finally, third-party scripts (ads, tags, widgets) run on your main thread and you often do not control their cost, which is a particular problem for ad-supported pages.
tradeoffs: Choose server-side rendering or static generation when the first view must appear fast and be indexed (content pages, shops), because the server sends ready HTML and the browser can paint before any application code runs. Choose client-side rendering for highly interactive applications behind a login, where the cost of a JavaScript bundle is paid once and later navigation is local. The cost of client rendering is a blank page until the bundle downloads, parses and runs; the cost of server rendering is server work and then a second step to attach event handlers (hydration) that can duplicate work on the main thread.
Choose to inline the critical CSS (a few KB for the first screen) when first paint time matters and your stylesheet is large, so the browser needs no second round trip. Choose a normal external file when the CSS is small or shared across pages, since it is cached after the first visit. You trade a faster first view against a larger HTML file that is not cached separately.
Choose `transform` and `opacity` for animation, and accept that this limits what you can animate. Choose layout properties only for one-time changes. Choose `will-change` for an element that is about to animate, and remove it afterwards, because each extra layer takes GPU memory.
practice: Measure before changing anything. Open the Performance panel in Chrome DevTools, record a load or an interaction and look at the main thread: long tasks are marked in red, and the breakdown shows scripting, style, layout and paint time. Look for the purple "Layout" blocks repeated in a loop, which indicate forced synchronous layout. Check Core Web Vitals for real users in field data, because lab results can differ.
For load, make the critical path short. Put critical CSS first and keep it small, load other CSS without blocking where possible, use `defer` for scripts, preload the hero image or font with `<link rel="preload">`, set image width and height (or `aspect-ratio`) so the layout does not shift when images arrive, and split JavaScript so the first view does not ship code for later pages. Compress text with Brotli or gzip and use a CDN.
For updates, batch reads and writes, use `requestAnimationFrame` for visual updates, break long tasks into pieces smaller than 50 ms (yield to the event loop with `setTimeout`, `scheduler.yield()` where available, or move work to a Web Worker), and keep the DOM small. Prefer CSS animations on `transform` and `opacity`. In React, avoid re-rendering large subtrees on each keystroke, and use list virtualization for long lists. For ad and tag code, load it after the main content and watch its main-thread cost in your own traces.
interview: Walk me through what happens between receiving HTML and seeing pixels. || Parse HTML to the DOM (a preload scanner fetches resources early), parse CSS to the CSSOM, compute styles, build the layout tree and compute geometry, paint into display lists per layer, raster into tiles on the GPU, and composite the layers on the compositor thread. Stylesheets block painting; synchronous scripts block the parser.
Why is animating `left` worse than animating `transform`? || `left` changes geometry, so every frame needs style, layout, paint and composite on the main thread. `transform` on a promoted layer changes only the layer's matrix, which the compositor thread applies without layout or paint, even when the main thread is busy.
What is layout thrashing and how do you find and fix it? || Interleaving DOM writes with reads of layout values (`offsetHeight`, `getBoundingClientRect`) forces a synchronous layout in each iteration. In the profiler you see repeated layout blocks inside one task. The fix is to batch all reads first and then all writes, or to use `requestAnimationFrame`, or a library that schedules DOM work.
A page has a fast server but poor INP on mid-range phones. What do you investigate? || Long tasks on the main thread: large JavaScript bundles to parse and run, expensive event handlers, hydration work, big DOM updates and third-party scripts. I would record a trace on a throttled CPU, find the tasks over 50 ms around interactions, then split, defer or move work off the main thread.
How do server-side rendering and hydration change the pipeline? || With SSR, the browser parses ready HTML and can paint at once, so the first paint is early. Hydration then runs the same component code on the client to attach handlers, which costs main-thread time and can delay interactivity, so the page may look ready but not respond until it finishes.
Why can too many composited layers hurt? || Each layer needs texture memory (width × height × 4 bytes) and the compositor must draw all of them every frame. On a phone with limited GPU memory, many large layers cause memory pressure or slower compositing, so promote only elements that really animate.
quiz: Which stages of the pipeline does a change of `transform` on a composited layer re-run? || Only composite. Style, layout and paint are skipped because the layer's pixels do not change, only its matrix.
Why is a synchronous script in the head a problem for rendering? || The parser must stop, download the script, and run it before it continues, because the script could change the document. Nothing after it is parsed or shown until then, and the script itself also waits for pending stylesheets.
What is forced synchronous layout? || When a script reads a layout-dependent value after a style write has made layout dirty, the browser must run layout at once to give a correct answer instead of waiting for the next frame. Repeating this in a loop runs layout many times.
At 120 Hz, how much time does the main thread have per frame, and how many frames does a 60 ms task block? || The budget is 1000 / 120 ≈ 8.3 ms per frame. A 60 ms task blocks about 60 / 8.3 ≈ 7 frames.
When would you choose client-side rendering over server-side rendering? || For an interactive application behind a login, where search indexing and first-load speed matter less and later navigation benefits from local rendering. You pay with a larger initial bundle and a blank page until it runs.

@@ ddd
value: Domain-driven design (DDD) is a way to build software for a complicated business by making the code follow the language and structure of that business. Its most useful idea is the bounded context: a boundary inside which every word has one precise meaning and one model is consistent. The word "campaign" means a signed contract with a budget to the sales team, a set of targeted line items with a delivery schedule to the ad server, and a billable line to the finance system. Trying to build one Campaign class that serves all three produces a huge, fragile model that nobody understands. DDD says: let each context have its own model, and connect the contexts with explicit translations.
The problem DDD addresses is not technical. It is that large systems fail because people do not share a clear understanding of the business, and because every team depends on every other team's data model. A change to one table breaks five services. Developers and domain experts use different words, and the code drifts from what the business actually does. DDD gives a method to find the right boundaries, a shared vocabulary (the ubiquitous language) inside each boundary, and patterns for how contexts relate to each other.
For the layer above, microservices usually draw their service boundaries along bounded contexts, because a context is the unit that can be owned by one team and changed without coordinating with others. Data mesh applies the same idea to analytics: data products are owned by domains, and each publishes data in its own vocabulary as a contract. The layers below supply the reasoning. Software engineering principles (L3) give modularity and information hiding: a module hides its internal decisions behind a small interface, so changes stay local. Reductionism and abstraction (L5) explain why a model must leave things out to be useful, and why one model of everything is impossible. Architectural styles (L2) give the shape of the code inside a context; DDD is usually combined with hexagonal architecture, which keeps the domain model free from databases and frameworks.
terms: Domain — the area of business activity the software serves, such as ad delivery, payments or shipping.
Ubiquitous language — the vocabulary shared by developers and domain experts inside one context, used in conversation, documents and code alike.
Bounded context — the explicit boundary within which a particular model and its language apply consistently.
Subdomain — a part of the business domain: core (the competitive advantage), supporting (needed but not special) or generic (solved the same way by everyone, such as authentication).
Context map — a diagram and description of how bounded contexts relate: who depends on whom, and how they translate.
Aggregate — a cluster of objects treated as one unit for changes, with one root object, inside which the rules (invariants) are always true after each transaction.
Entity and value object — an entity has an identity that persists as its data changes (an order); a value object has no identity and is defined by its values (a money amount).
Domain event — a record that something meaningful happened in the domain, named in the past tense (CampaignBooked).
Anti-corruption layer — a translation layer that stops another context's model from leaking into yours.
history: Eric Evans published "Domain-Driven Design: Tackling Complexity in the Heart of Software" in 2003. He collected patterns from object-oriented projects in which teams had succeeded by keeping a rich model of the business in code. The book's roots are older. Parnas (1972) argued that modules should be divided by design decisions that are likely to change, and hide them. Conway (1968) observed that a system's structure copies the communication structure of the organization that builds it. Fowler's "Analysis Patterns" (1997) and Beck's and Cunningham's early object work on shared metaphors shaped the ideas.
For a decade, DDD was read mostly as a set of object-oriented building blocks: entities, value objects, repositories. The "strategic design" half of the book, with bounded contexts and context maps, was read less. Vaughn Vernon's "Implementing Domain-Driven Design" (2013) gave practical examples and made aggregates and events clearer. Alberto Brandolini introduced Event Storming (2013), a workshop in which experts and developers place domain events on a wall, which became the standard way to discover contexts and aggregates. Martin Fowler's short article on bounded contexts (2014) spread the idea widely.
The rise of microservices (2014 onward) brought DDD to the centre. Teams that split systems by technical layers or by guesses found tight coupling, and bounded contexts offered a rule to split by. Evans has said in later talks that the strategic part, and the bounded context in particular, deserved more emphasis than it got. Cockburn's hexagonal architecture (2005) and its relatives (onion and clean architecture) became the usual way to organize code in a context. Zhamak Dehghani's data mesh (2019) carried domain ownership into analytics. Today DDD is mostly used for its strategic part, and critics note that the tactical patterns are often overused in simple applications.
how: The method starts with language. Developers and domain experts work together, using real examples, until they agree on the terms the business uses. Each term must have one meaning inside a given context, and the code uses the same words: if the business says "book a campaign", the code has a `Campaign.book()` method, not `createRecord`. When the same word keeps meaning two things, that is the signal of a boundary. The word "segment" in an audience-building context means a rule that selects devices; in a measurement context, it means a group for reporting. Forcing one definition would blur both.
A bounded context is that boundary made explicit. Inside it, there is one model, one language, usually one team, and ideally one deployable unit with its own data store. This is information hiding applied to the business: the model is the hidden decision, and other contexts see only a small published interface. Contexts are discovered, not decreed, and they follow the structure of the business and of the organization (Conway's law). They are often found by Event Storming: lay out the events in time order, and notice where the vocabulary or the owner changes.
Subdomains guide investment. The core subdomain is where the company differs from competitors (for an ad platform, perhaps bidding and audience modelling), and it deserves the best people and the richest model. Supporting subdomains (campaign management screens) get simpler solutions. Generic subdomains (identity, email, payments) should be bought or taken from a library. This avoids the common error of spending months building a custom model for something that is not a competitive advantage.
Contexts must relate, and the context map names the relationship. In a customer-supplier relationship, the upstream context publishes what the downstream one needs, and they plan together. A conformist downstream accepts the upstream model as it is, which is cheap but couples the two. An anti-corruption layer lets the downstream keep its own model by translating on the boundary, and is the right choice when the upstream model is poor or foreign (a legacy system, an external vendor). An open-host service with a published language offers a stable, documented protocol for many consumers. A shared kernel is a small piece of model that two teams own together, and it must be kept very small. Separate ways means no integration at all. Integration usually happens through events or APIs, and not through a shared database: a shared database would make the two models one.
Inside a context, tactical patterns shape the model. An aggregate is the unit of consistency. All rules that must hold at the same time (for example "the sum of line budgets does not exceed the campaign budget") are enforced inside one aggregate, in one transaction, through its root. Other aggregates are referenced by identifier only, and changes across aggregates are made eventually consistent, with domain events. The practical rule is: one transaction changes one aggregate. This keeps aggregates small, which keeps lock contention low and makes them fit naturally into a document or a row group.
```python
class Campaign:                      # aggregate root
    def __init__(self, id, budget_cents):
        self.id, self.budget = id, budget_cents
        self.lines = []              # value objects inside
        self.events = []

    def add_line(self, line_id, cents):
        if sum(l.cents for l in self.lines) + cents > self.budget:
            raise ValueError("line budgets exceed campaign budget")
        self.lines.append(Line(line_id, cents))
        self.events.append(LineAdded(self.id, line_id, cents))
```
Domain events are how contexts stay loosely coupled. An event states a fact that already happened, in the language of the context that produced it: `CampaignBooked`, not `UpdateDeliveryTable`. The producer does not know who listens. Each consumer decides what the fact means for its own model, usually through its anti-corruption layer, and updates its own data. Delivery between contexts is at least once, so consumers must handle duplicates by checking an event id.
The domain model is kept free of infrastructure. In hexagonal architecture, the model sits in the centre. It talks to the outside through ports (interfaces such as `CampaignRepository`), and adapters (a SQL implementation, a Kafka publisher, an HTTP controller) are plugged in around it. Domain events recorded by the aggregate are published after the transaction commits, commonly through an outbox table, so other contexts can react. The result is code that reads like the business, a model that can be tested without a database, and boundaries that can become services when the organization needs it.

example: Take an ad platform, which has a clear problem of different meanings.
Step 1: collect the words. In an Event Storming session the team lists events in time order: ContractSigned, CampaignBooked, CreativeApproved, LineItemActivated, ImpressionServed, BudgetExhausted, InvoiceIssued. The experts say "campaign" in every context, but the sales lead means "what the advertiser bought for 500,000 dollars", the ad-ops person means "the 14 line items with targeting and frequency caps", and the finance person means "a line on the monthly invoice".
Step 2: split by language. The team draws three bounded contexts, Sales, Delivery and Billing, and a fourth, Measurement. In Sales, `Campaign` has a signed budget, an advertiser and a period. In Delivery, `Campaign` has line items, targeting and a pacing plan, and it has no price. In Billing, there is `InvoiceLine`, and the word campaign is only a reference.
Step 3: choose the relationships. Sales is upstream and publishes a `CampaignBooked` event with the advertiser id, the total budget in cents, and the dates. Delivery is downstream as a customer: it consumes the event through an anti-corruption layer which translates `total_budget_cents = 50_000_000` into a delivery `Budget` of 500,000 dollars to be paced over 30 days, about 16,667 dollars a day. Delivery never reads the Sales database.
Step 4: set the aggregate boundary. Delivery receives 20,000 impressions per second for one large campaign. If the whole campaign, with its 14 line items, were one aggregate that also held the spent amount, every impression would update the same row and 20,000 transactions a second would collide. So the team makes the spend counter a separate aggregate (a `Pacing` record per line item) updated asynchronously, and keeps the budget rule "line budgets do not exceed the campaign budget" in the small `Campaign` aggregate that changes only a few times a day. The price of this split is that spend is eventually consistent. If a counter is updated every 5 seconds, then at a rate of 20,000 impressions per second the system can overshoot by up to 100,000 impressions before it sees the limit. At a price of 2 dollars per thousand impressions, the potential overspend is 200 dollars, which the business must accept or reduce with a safety margin.
intuition: A bounded context is a country with its own language and its own law, and the context map is the treaty list: inside the border, people understand each other, and at the border, an interpreter translates.
The idea is natural because meaning depends on context in human language too. The same word is not a defect to be removed; it is the way people compress a lot of shared knowledge. Forcing one global definition costs more than translating at the edges. This is also why the structure works for software. A boundary around a consistent model means the model can be understood by one team, changed by one team, and tested without the rest of the company.
mistakes: People often think DDD means microservices. In fact DDD is a way of finding and describing boundaries; it works equally well inside a modular monolith. A bounded context can be a package or a module. It becomes a service only if independent deployment is worth the cost of a network and distributed data.
People often think one bounded context equals one service, or that every service needs its own context. In fact a context may contain several deployable parts, and a service that covers half of a context splits its model. The rule of thumb is: a service should not span contexts, and a context should have one owning team.
People often think DDD is entities, repositories and factories. In fact these tactical patterns are the lesser half. Teams that adopt them without the strategic design produce elaborate class structures around a simple database table.
People often think there should be one canonical enterprise data model that all contexts share. In fact a single shared model forces compromise among teams, and every change needs everyone's agreement. DDD replaces it by several models plus explicit translation, which is more work at the boundary and much less work everywhere else.
friction: The first problem is finding the right boundaries. They are a hypothesis about the business, and the first guess is often wrong. Wrong boundaries are expensive to fix after services, schemas and team structures have grown around them. Boundaries that follow the org chart instead of the domain, or that cut through one strongly consistent workflow, create chatty services that must change together.
DDD needs people. It depends on regular conversations with domain experts, who are usually busy, and on a team willing to learn the business. In organizations where requirements arrive through tickets with no access to experts, the benefits are small. The method also asks for judgment about where to invest: modeling everything richly is waste, and treating the core subdomain as a CRUD form is a mistake.
Integration between contexts brings distributed-system costs. Data is duplicated, because each context keeps its own copy of what it needs, and changes spread by events with delay. Cross-context transactions are no longer possible, so you design sagas or compensation steps and accept eventual consistency. Reports that need data from several contexts need a separate integration layer or a data platform.
tradeoffs: Choose DDD with explicit bounded contexts when the domain is complex, several teams work on it, and the business rules change often: ad delivery, payments, logistics, insurance. Choose a simple layered CRUD design when the rules are few and a small team owns the whole application. The cost of DDD is workshops, modelling time and translation code; the saving appears only if the domain is complicated enough to be worth it.
Choose a modular monolith with context boundaries enforced in code when you have fewer than about 20 to 30 engineers, deployments are not yet a bottleneck, and the boundaries are not yet certain. Choose separate services only when teams need independent release cycles or scaling, and the contexts are stable. You trade the simplicity of local calls and transactions for independence, and a wrong boundary is cheaper to move in a monolith.
Choose a small aggregate with eventual consistency across aggregates when throughput and contention matter (many writers). Choose a larger aggregate when a business rule truly needs strong consistency at once, like a hard budget limit, and writes are rare. The first gives scale and may allow brief violations of a rule; the second gives correctness and a lower write rate.
practice: Start with language and events, not classes. Run an Event Storming session with developers and domain experts, write the events in the past tense on a wall, mark the places where terminology or the responsible group changes, and draw candidate contexts. Write a short glossary for each context and keep it next to the code. Draw a context map with the relationship types, including the political ones: who is upstream, who has the power to change an interface.
Match teams to contexts so that the team structure and the architecture agree. Begin with a modular monolith, with each context in its own module that other modules reach only through a defined interface, and with its own schema or tables. Check dependencies in the build (a static check that fails when module A imports the internals of module B). Move a context to a separate service later, when you have evidence that it needs it.
For integration, publish domain events or stable APIs, not database access. Put an anti-corruption layer in front of legacy systems and vendors. Use the outbox pattern so that an event is recorded in the same transaction as the state change and published afterwards. Version event schemas, and treat them as contracts that consumers can test against. For analytics, publish each context's data as a documented data product rather than letting reports read its tables.
interview: What is a bounded context, and how do you find one? || It is an explicit boundary within which a model and its language are consistent. I look for places where the same word means different things, where different experts or teams own the concept, and where the rules and the rate of change differ, usually through Event Storming and interviews. A context is a hypothesis that I test against how often changes cross it.
Is a bounded context the same as a microservice? || No. A context is a modelling boundary, a service is a deployment boundary. A context can live in a module of a monolith, and a context may be served by more than one deployable. Services should not cross contexts, but contexts need not be services.
How do you decide the size of an aggregate? || I include only the objects that must satisfy an invariant together in one transaction. Smaller is better: it reduces contention, load size and cross-team coupling. Other aggregates are referenced by id, and cross-aggregate rules use events and eventual consistency, accepting possible short violations.
When do you use an anti-corruption layer instead of conforming? || When the upstream model is a poor fit, changes without notice, or comes from a legacy system or a vendor, and my context is core enough that its model must stay clean. When the upstream is a stable standard or my context is minor, conforming is cheaper.
Your teams own services that each need data from one another and release together. What is wrong, and what do you do? || The boundaries probably cut through one context, so the services are coupled by shared concepts. I would map the language and the change patterns, merge the services that always change together into one context (a module or service), and put explicit contracts at the remaining boundaries.
How does DDD relate to data mesh? || Data mesh applies the same domain ownership to analytics: each domain owns and publishes its data as a product in its own vocabulary, with a contract. The bounded contexts give the natural domain boundaries, and translation happens when analysts combine data from several domains.
quiz: What does a bounded context protect? || It protects the consistency of one model and one language. Inside the boundary each term has one meaning; across it, translation is explicit, so models do not leak into each other.
Why should one transaction change only one aggregate? || It keeps aggregates small, which reduces lock contention and coupling, and it makes each aggregate a clear consistency boundary. Consistency across aggregates is reached through events, eventually.
What is the role of the anti-corruption layer? || It translates between another context's model and your own at the boundary, so that a foreign or legacy model does not spread into your domain code and a change upstream affects only the layer.

When would you not use DDD? || For a simple CRUD application with few business rules and one small team, where modelling workshops and translation layers cost more than they return.

@@ ngram
value: An n-gram language model estimates how likely the next word is, given the previous n − 1 words, by counting. A bigram model (n = 2) asks: in my training text, how often did "tea" follow "like", out of all the times "like" appeared? A trigram model (n = 3) looks at the two previous words. Multiplying these conditional probabilities along a sentence gives the probability of the whole sentence. That number answers questions such as "which of these two transcriptions is more fluent?", "which word is the user probably typing?", and "does this text look like English?".
The idea is simple, and the difficulty is in the counts. Even with a billion words of training text, most possible word sequences never appear. If "tea with oat milk" was never seen, plain counting says its probability is exactly zero, and one zero makes the probability of a whole sentence zero. Smoothing is the set of methods that fix this: take a little probability away from the sequences that were seen and give it to those that were not, in a principled way. Good smoothing (Kneser-Ney) is the difference between a model that is useful and one that is not.
For the layer above, statistical NLP was built on n-gram models: speech recognition, machine translation, spelling correction and autocomplete all used them for decades, and tools such as KenLM still do, for example to filter web text by how natural it looks. An LLM is a next-token model of the same kind, and the evaluation measure, perplexity, comes straight from this era. The layers below supply the foundations. Markov chains (L4) justify the n-gram assumption that only the last n − 1 words matter. Information theory (L4) gives cross-entropy and perplexity as the measure of model quality, and Shannon's own 1948 experiments with approximations of English. Statistics and estimation (L4) explain that the counts are maximum-likelihood estimates, why they overfit, and what smoothing does as a prior or a correction.
terms: n-gram — a sequence of n consecutive words (or tokens); unigram, bigram, trigram are n = 1, 2, 3.
Markov assumption — the next word depends only on the previous n − 1 words, not on the rest of the history.
Maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE) — the probability that makes the training data most likely; for n-grams it is the count of the n-gram divided by the count of its context.
Sparsity — most possible n-grams never occur in any finite training set, so their counts are zero.
Smoothing — adjusting counts so that unseen n-grams get a small non-zero probability and seen ones get a little less.
Backoff and interpolation — using a shorter context (n − 1 words) when the longer one has too little data; backoff replaces the estimate, interpolation mixes both.
Perplexity — the exponential of the average negative log-probability per word on test text; the model is "as confused as" choosing among that many equally likely words.
Out-of-vocabulary (OOV) word — a word not in the model's vocabulary; usually mapped to a special token such as UNK.
Continuation count — the number of distinct words that precede a word; Kneser-Ney uses it in place of the word's raw frequency.
history: Andrey Markov studied dependence between consecutive letters in Pushkin's "Eugene Onegin" in 1913, counting vowels and consonants, which is the first example of what we now call a Markov chain. Claude Shannon's "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (1948) generated English-like text from letter and word frequencies of increasing order, and showed that a longer context gives a more English-looking result. This set up the idea that language has entropy that can be estimated, and that a model can be judged by how well it predicts.
The method became practical with speech recognition. At IBM in the 1970s and 80s, Frederick Jelinek's group framed recognition as picking the word sequence with the highest product of acoustic probability and language-model probability, and used trigram models. They also met sparsity at once. Good (1953), crediting Turing, had given a formula for the probability of unseen events based on how many things were seen exactly once. Jelinek and Mercer (1980) proposed interpolating models of different orders, and Katz (1987) introduced backoff. Kneser and Ney (1995) improved the lower-order estimate with continuation counts, and Chen and Goodman's large comparison (1996, expanded 1999) showed modified Kneser-Ney was consistently the best smoothing method.
Scale then became the main driver. Google released the Web 1T 5-gram data in 2006, built from about a trillion words, and Brants and colleagues (2007) showed that a simple "stupid backoff" approached the quality of Kneser-Ney as the training data grew, and that more data kept improving translation, which supported the view that data can matter more than cleverness. Heafield's KenLM (2011) made big models fast and small with efficient data structures, and it is still the standard tool. Neural models took over the main role: Bengio and colleagues (2003) proposed a neural n-gram model with learned word embeddings, recurrent models (Mikolov 2010) removed the fixed window, and Transformers followed. N-gram models did not disappear. They stayed as fast, cheap components, and in 2024 the Infini-gram work (Liu and colleagues) showed that an n-gram model with no fixed n, using a suffix array over trillions of tokens, gives useful predictions and can be used to analyse the training data of LLMs.
how: Start with the chain rule of probability, which is exact. The probability of a sentence is the product of the probability of each word given all the words before it:
\[ P(w_1, \dots, w_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}) \]
The conditional probabilities with long histories cannot be estimated, because almost every long history is unique. The Markov assumption cuts the history to the last n − 1 words:
\[ P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}) \approx P(w_t \mid w_{t-n+1}, \dots, w_{t-1}) \]
This is exactly an (n − 1)-order Markov chain over words. For a bigram model the state is the previous word. Sentence boundaries are handled with special tokens: add `<s>` at the start (so the first word has a context) and `</s>` at the end (so the model can say that a sentence is finished).
Training is counting. The maximum-likelihood estimate is
\[ P_{\text{MLE}}(w \mid v) = \frac{c(v\,w)}{c(v)} \]
where \( c(v\,w) \) is the number of times the word w followed v. A single pass over the text with a hash map of counts gives the model. For a trigram model the key is a pair of previous words, and the table holds all trigrams observed. This is the statistics layer at work: counting is the MLE of the model, and it is the best estimate for the data seen, but it assigns probability zero to everything unseen. With a vocabulary of V = 100,000 words there are \( V^3 = 10^{15} \) possible trigrams and \( 10^{25} \) five-grams, but a corpus of 10 billion words contains at most 10 billion distinct trigram instances. Most of what a user types next will be unseen, so smoothing is essential.
The simplest smoothing is add-k: pretend every n-gram was seen k extra times, \( P(w \mid v) = (c(vw) + k)/(c(v) + kV) \). Add-one (k = 1) moves far too much probability to unseen events when V is large, because there are V possible words after each context and most are impossible. It is a poor method for language and is useful mainly to understand the idea. Better methods use the shorter context. Interpolation mixes estimates of different orders with weights that sum to one:
\[ P_{\text{interp}}(w \mid v) = \lambda\, P_{\text{MLE}}(w \mid v) + (1 - \lambda)\, P(w) \]
The weights are tuned on held-out text. Backoff in Katz's form uses the long-context estimate when its count is positive, and otherwise falls back to the shorter context, scaled so that the total probability stays 1.
Kneser-Ney is the method that works best. It combines two ideas. The first is absolute discounting: subtract a fixed amount d (about 0.75) from every observed count, and give the freed mass to the shorter context in proportion to how many different words followed the context. The second is the key insight: the shorter-context estimate should not use raw frequency but continuation counts. The word "Francisco" is frequent, but it follows almost only "San". As a backoff for a new context, "Francisco" should get low probability, because it rarely appears after new words. The probability that a word is a good continuation is estimated by the number of distinct words that precede it:
\[ P_{\text{KN}}(w \mid v) = \frac{\max(c(vw) - d,\, 0)}{c(v)} + \lambda(v)\, P_{\text{cont}}(w), \qquad P_{\text{cont}}(w) = \frac{N_{1+}(\bullet\, w)}{N_{1+}(\bullet\,\bullet)} \]
where \( \lambda(v) = d \cdot N_{1+}(v\,\bullet) / c(v) \) is the mass freed by discounting, \( N_{1+}(\bullet\, w) \) is the number of distinct words preceding w and \( N_{1+}(\bullet\,\bullet) \) is the number of distinct bigram types. The model is evaluated by perplexity on held-out text:
\[ \text{PP} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \ln P(w_t \mid \text{context})\right) = 2^{H} \]
where H is the cross-entropy in bits per word, the information theory link. A perplexity of 100 means the model is, on average, as uncertain as if it chose uniformly among 100 words at each position. Test text must be kept apart from the training text, and unknown words are mapped to `<unk>` in both.
Large models need engineering. KenLM stores the counts in a trie or in a hash table with probing and quantizes the probabilities, so that a large 5-gram model fits in the memory of one machine and answers a query in microseconds. To get a language-model score for a sentence in speech recognition or translation, the decoder queries the model for each word and multiplies the results (or adds the logs) with the other scores.
```python
from collections import Counter
uni, bi = Counter(), Counter()
for sent in corpus:                       # each sent is a list of words
    toks = ["<s>"] + sent + ["</s>"]
    for a, b in zip(toks, toks[1:]):
        uni[a] += 1
        bi[(a, b)] += 1

def p_mle(w, v):
    return bi[(v, w)] / uni[v] if uni[v] else 0.0
```

example: Use a training corpus of three sentences: "i like tea", "i like coffee" and "you like tea". With boundary tokens each sentence has four events. Token counts, not counting `<s>`: c(i) = 2, c(you) = 1, c(like) = 3, c(tea) = 2, c(coffee) = 1, c(</s>) = 3, so 12 tokens in all.
Step 1: maximum-likelihood bigrams. P(i | <s>) = 2/3, P(you | <s>) = 1/3. P(like | i) = 2/2 = 1. P(like | you) = 1. P(tea | like) = 2/3 and P(coffee | like) = 1/3. P(</s> | tea) = 1 and P(</s> | coffee) = 1.
Step 2: score a sentence. "you like coffee" has probability P(you | <s>) × P(like | you) × P(coffee | like) × P(</s> | coffee) = 1/3 × 1 × 1/3 × 1 = 1/9 ≈ 0.111. Its perplexity over N = 4 events is \( 9^{1/4} \approx 1.73 \). Notice that this sentence never appeared in training, but each of its bigrams did.
Step 3: the zero problem. The test sentence "i tea" needs P(tea | i) = 0/2 = 0. Its whole probability is 0 and its perplexity is infinite, although it is only mildly odd.
Step 4: add-one smoothing. Take the six possible next words {i, like, tea, coffee, you, </s>}, so V = 6. Then P(tea | i) = (0 + 1)/(2 + 6) = 0.125, but also P(like | i) = (2 + 1)/(2 + 6) = 0.375, down from 1.0. The method gave 62.5% of the mass of this context to events that were never seen, which is far too generous.
Step 5: interpolation with the unigram, with λ = 0.8. The unigram probabilities are P(like) = 3/12 = 0.25 and P(tea) = 2/12 ≈ 0.167. Then P(tea | i) = 0.8 × 0 + 0.2 × 0.167 ≈ 0.033 and P(like | i) = 0.8 × 1 + 0.2 × 0.25 = 0.85. The seen event keeps most of its mass and the unseen one gets a small share.
Step 6: Kneser-Ney with d = 0.75. There are 8 distinct bigram types: (<s> i), (<s> you), (i like), (you like), (like tea), (like coffee), (tea </s>), (coffee </s>). Continuation probabilities: P_cont(like) = 2/8, since two different words (i, you) precede it, and P_cont(tea) = 1/8. For the context "i": c(i) = 2, one distinct follower, so λ(i) = 0.75 × 1/2 = 0.375. Then P_KN(like | i) = (2 − 0.75)/2 + 0.375 × 0.25 = 0.625 + 0.094 = 0.719, and P_KN(tea | i) = 0 + 0.375 × 0.125 ≈ 0.047. Check that the context sums to 1: the discounted part gives 0.625 and the backoff part gives 0.375 × (sum of all continuation probabilities = 1) = 0.375, so the total is 1.
intuition: An n-gram model is a very well-read autocomplete that remembers only the last few words and, for anything it has not read, guesses from shorter phrases it has read.
The design is natural because language is locally predictable: the next word depends strongly on the last two or three words and weakly on the rest. Counting is the most direct way to measure that. The only real difficulty is that language is made of rare events, and smoothing is the principled answer to what to believe about the events you have not seen. Kneser-Ney's insight adds an elegant point: when you back off, ask not "how common is this word?" but "in how many different contexts does it appear?", because a word that appears in many contexts is more likely to appear in a new one.
mistakes: People often think n-gram models are obsolete because LLMs exist. In fact they are cheap, fast, need no GPU, can be trained in minutes on a large corpus and can be updated by adding counts. They are still used in speech recognition, input methods, spelling correction, and data filtering (perplexity under a KenLM model is used to separate clean text from spam in web crawls).
People often think a higher n is always better. In fact the number of contexts grows exponentially with n and the counts per context shrink. Beyond 4 or 5 words, most contexts are unique, and the model needs backoff to shorter histories. The best n depends on the amount of data.
People often think smoothing is a minor technical fix. In fact the choice of smoothing changes perplexity by 10 percent or more, and it decides whether the model is usable. Good smoothing is a statistical model of the unseen, not an adjustment of numbers.
People often think perplexity can be compared across any two models. In fact it depends on the vocabulary and the tokenization. A model with a smaller vocabulary or one that treats rare words as `<unk>` has an easier task and a lower perplexity. Compare only on the same test text with the same tokens, or convert to bits per character.
friction: The fixed window is the basic limit. An n-gram model cannot use information further back than n − 1 words, so it cannot track agreement across a long clause, a topic, or a story. It also cannot generalize between similar words: seeing "the cat sat" tells it nothing about "the dog sat", because the two words are unrelated symbols. Neural models solve both with embeddings and long context, at much higher cost.
Smoothing choices make the models fragile in practice. Discounts and interpolation weights must be tuned on held-out text from the target domain, and a model trained on news performs poorly on chat. Probabilities are not calibrated for rare events, and the estimates for rare contexts are noisy. Finally, the models are only as good as the training text, and they reproduce its biases, duplicates and errors, since counting does not distinguish true from false.
tradeoffs: Choose an n-gram model when you need a fast, cheap, explainable score: filtering web text, rescoring candidates in speech or spelling, autocompletion on a device, or a baseline. Choose a neural language model when quality matters more than cost and you have GPUs: it captures long context and similar words. You give up a model that is trained in minutes and queried in microseconds for one that is trained in days and queried in milliseconds.
Choose modified Kneser-Ney with n = 3 to 5 when you have between 100 million and tens of billions of words. Choose stupid backoff (a fixed factor of about 0.4 for each backoff step, which gives scores, not true probabilities) when you have trillions of tokens and need simple distributed counting, since with enough data the smoothing method matters less. You give up normalized probabilities for scalability.
Choose a small n (2 or 3) when data is small (less than about 10 million words) or memory is tight; choose a larger n (5 or more) with pruning when data is large and the application benefits from exact phrases, such as transcription of names and set expressions. The deciding factor is the number of times a typical context was seen: if most contexts have fewer than about 5 examples, lower the order.
practice: Use KenLM. Build with `lmplz -o 5 < corpus.txt > model.arpa`, then convert to the binary format with `build_binary`, and query from Python or C++. Tokenize and normalize the training text exactly as you do at query time. Remove duplicates from the training text, because repeated documents inflate counts and make the model look better on them. Keep a held-out set from the target domain to tune pruning thresholds and to measure perplexity.
Match the training text to the use. For autocomplete, train on your own queries or messages and mix in a general model by interpolation, with the weight tuned on held-out data. For web-text filtering, train on a clean reference such as Wikipedia, score each document by perplexity per word, and inspect what falls at both ends: very low perplexity is often repeated boilerplate, and very high is often another language.
Evaluate sensibly. Report perplexity on held-out data with the same tokenization, but also the downstream metric (word error rate, translation quality, keystrokes saved). Lower perplexity does not always give a better downstream score. As a learning exercise, build a bigram model with add-k and with Kneser-Ney on a small corpus, and compare perplexities on held-out text; the gap between them shows what smoothing is worth.
interview: Why can't you use the maximum-likelihood estimate directly, and what does smoothing do? || MLE gives probability zero to any n-gram not in the training data, which makes the probability of a whole sentence zero and perplexity infinite. Smoothing takes some probability mass from observed n-grams and distributes it to unseen ones, usually by discounting counts and using shorter contexts.
Explain the idea behind Kneser-Ney. || When backing off to a lower-order model, the question should be how likely the word is to appear in a new context, not how frequent it is. It uses the number of distinct preceding words (continuation count) for the lower-order estimate and absolute discounting for the higher-order one. "Francisco" is frequent but follows only "San", so it gets a low continuation probability.
How is perplexity defined, and what does a value of 120 mean? || It is the exponential of the average negative log-probability per token on held-out text, which equals 2 to the cross-entropy in bits. A perplexity of 120 means the model is on average as uncertain as a uniform choice among 120 tokens at each position. It depends on the tokenization, so comparisons need the same tokens.
How would you build autocomplete for a product with 50 million past queries? || Count query n-grams in a trie, apply Kneser-Ney or simple interpolation, prune low counts to bound memory, and combine with prefix matching and popularity. Evaluate on held-out sessions by keystrokes saved. Update it by adding counts of recent queries and mixing with a general model.
Where do n-gram models sit relative to LLMs today, and what can you learn from them? || LLMs predict the next token from learned representations of long contexts. N-gram models are baselines, fast components for filtering and rescoring, and tools for analysing data. They show how much of language is predicted by short local context, and how much value the extra capacity of neural models adds beyond that.
Your language model's perplexity improved after you changed the tokenizer. Did the model get better? || Not necessarily. Perplexity per token depends on the number of tokens, so a tokenizer with longer tokens has higher per-token perplexity for the same quality of model. Compare bits per character, or the downstream metric on the same text.
quiz: What does the Markov assumption say in an n-gram model? || The next word depends only on the previous n − 1 words, and not on the earlier history.
Why does a trigram model with a 100,000-word vocabulary have a sparsity problem? || There are \( 10^{15} \) possible trigrams, far more than any corpus can contain, so most trigrams in new text were never seen in training and would get a zero count.
Why does Kneser-Ney use continuation counts instead of word frequencies for the backoff? || A word's total frequency is misleading if it occurs in only a few contexts, such as "Francisco" after "San". The number of different contexts it follows predicts better whether it will appear in a new context.
A model assigns probability 1/9 to a 4-token test sentence. What is its perplexity? || \( (1/9)^{-1/4} = 9^{1/4} \approx 1.73 \).
When would you choose stupid backoff instead of Kneser-Ney? || When the corpus has trillions of tokens and counting must be distributed simply. With that much data, careful smoothing matters less, and you accept scores that are not normalized probabilities.

@@ crawling
value: A web crawler is a program that discovers and downloads web pages by following links. It starts from a list of seed URLs, fetches each page, extracts the links in it, and adds the new ones to a queue of work to do. Repeating this billions of times builds a copy of a large part of the web. Search engines crawl to build their index, Common Crawl publishes crawls as open data, price and news aggregators crawl to collect fresh facts, and the training text of most large language models comes largely from crawled pages.
The idea is easy and the engineering is not. The web has hundreds of billions of pages and changes all the time. A crawler must fetch hundreds or thousands of pages per second, yet it must never overload any single website, so it needs politeness. It must not fetch the same URL twice, which means remembering billions of URLs. It must recognize that thousands of different URLs return the same or almost the same content, it must avoid traps that generate endless pages, and it must decide which pages to fetch first and when to come back.
For the layer above, a search engine is only as good as its crawl: pages that were never fetched cannot be found, and pages that are stale give wrong answers. An LLM's pretraining corpus is shaped by what crawlers could reach and what was filtered out. The layers below do the heavy lifting. HTTP (L3) is the protocol for fetching, including conditional requests that save bandwidth, status codes, redirects and the robots.txt convention. DNS (L3) resolves host names, and at crawl scale its latency dominates unless the results are cached. Queues and priority structures (L3) form the URL frontier. Bloom filters (L2) remember which URLs were seen in a small amount of memory. Locality-sensitive hashing (L2), with MinHash or SimHash, finds near-duplicate pages without comparing every pair.
terms: Seed URL — a starting address given to the crawler by hand or from an earlier crawl.
URL frontier — the data structure that holds the URLs waiting to be fetched, with their priorities and the per-host scheduling.
Politeness — the rule that a crawler limits the load it puts on any host, for example one request at a time and a pause between requests.
robots.txt — a text file at the root of a site that tells crawlers which paths they may fetch; now specified in RFC 9309.
URL-seen test — checking whether a URL has already been discovered, so that it is queued only once.
Content-seen test — checking whether the downloaded content duplicates a page already stored.
Canonicalization — rewriting a URL into one standard form (lowercase host, removed fragment, sorted parameters) so the same page does not appear under several names.
Near-duplicate — a page whose content is almost the same as another page, such as the same article with a different advertisement.
Spider trap — a set of pages that generates an unlimited number of URLs, like a calendar that links to the next month forever.
Freshness — the share of stored pages that still match the live web; re-crawl policies try to keep it high.
history: The first crawlers appeared in 1993 and 1994, soon after the web began: Matthew Gray's World Wide Web Wanderer measured the growth of the web, and Brian Pinkerton's WebCrawler (1994) was the first to index full text. Early crawlers sometimes overloaded small servers, and in 1994 Martijn Koster proposed the Robots Exclusion Protocol, a voluntary convention that stayed informal for 28 years until it was published as RFC 9309 in 2022.
The scale grew quickly. In "The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine" (1998), Brin and Page described Google's crawler: several distributed crawlers, each keeping about 300 connections open at once, fetching on the order of 100 pages per second at peak. The same year Cho and Garcia-Molina studied which URLs to crawl first (ordering by link counts or PageRank), and Najork and Wiener (2001) found that simple breadth-first order already downloads high-quality pages early. Heydon and Najork's Mercator (1999) became the reference design: a modular crawler with a two-level URL frontier for priority and politeness, fingerprint-based duplicate tests, and a design that could be written in Java and run on a cluster.
Duplicates were the next problem. Broder and colleagues (1997) introduced shingling and MinHash to estimate how similar two documents are, and they applied it to the web crawl of AltaVista. Manku, Jain and Das Sarma (2007) showed at Google that 64-bit SimHash fingerprints, compared by Hamming distance, detect near-duplicates in billions of pages. Cho and Garcia-Molina (2000, 2003) modelled page changes as random events and derived refresh policies that maximize freshness.
Common Crawl, a non-profit, began publishing monthly web crawls in 2008, and these became the main open source of text for language models, with later crawls holding billions of pages each. The 2010s added the problem of JavaScript: many pages show their content only after scripts run, so crawlers began to use headless browsers. In the 2020s publishers began to add rules for AI crawlers to robots.txt and to block or license access, so the crawling landscape is partly a question of permission and not only of engineering.
how: A crawler is a loop around four components: a frontier of URLs to fetch, fetchers that download pages, parsers that extract links, and filters that decide what to keep. The loop starts with seed URLs in the frontier. Take a URL, fetch it, parse out links, filter them, and put the new ones back in the frontier. The design questions are all about doing this at scale and politely.
The frontier is the core of the system. A simple FIFO queue is not enough, because it gives no priority and no politeness. The Mercator design uses two layers. Front queues order URLs by priority: pages from important sites, fresh news pages, or URLs that changed often rank higher. Back queues enforce politeness: there is one queue per host, and each host queue has a "next allowed time". A min-heap (the priority-queue concept) keyed by next allowed time tells the fetcher which host may be contacted now. When a fetch for host H finishes, the crawler sets H's next allowed time to the current time plus a delay, and a common rule is about 10 times the time the last fetch took, with a minimum of one second or the `Crawl-delay` the site asks for. A host never has more than one request in flight. Many hosts are fetched in parallel, so total throughput comes from the number of hosts and not from hammering one.
Before a host's pages are fetched, the crawler reads its robots.txt (cached for hours or a day) and checks every URL against the rules for its user agent. Next it needs the IP address of the host. DNS lookups can take tens to hundreds of milliseconds, and a crawler may touch hundreds of millions of distinct hosts, so it runs its own caching resolver with many parallel queries, since a generic resolver would become the bottleneck. Then the fetcher opens an HTTP connection (reusing it for the same host), sends the request with a clear user-agent string and contact address, and uses timeouts and size limits, because some servers answer slowly or send endless data. It follows redirects up to a limit, accepts compressed responses, and handles the status codes: 200 stores the page, 301 and 302 add the target URL, 404 and 410 mark the URL dead, and 429 or 503 mean slow down.
Parsing extracts links from the HTML (and from sitemaps if the site provides them). Each link is canonicalized: resolve relative paths against the base URL, lowercase the host, remove default ports and fragments, remove known tracking parameters, and sort query parameters. Without this, one page appears under many addresses. Then comes the URL-seen test, where the Bloom filter helps. A set of 10 billion URLs stored as 8-byte fingerprints needs 80 GB, but a Bloom filter at 10 bits per URL (about 1% false positives) needs about 12 GB. A false positive only means that a new URL is wrongly skipped, which is acceptable for most crawls; a rarely-skipped URL is found again from another link. Large crawlers combine a filter in memory with an exact structure on disk. The rule for the exact check is the same fingerprint set.
The content-seen test comes after fetching. An exact duplicate is found by hashing the page body (after removing boilerplate) and looking the hash up in a set. Near-duplicates need more. MinHash works on the set of overlapping word sequences (shingles) of each page: a short signature of, say, 128 minimum hash values is stored, and the fraction of equal positions in two signatures estimates the Jaccard similarity of the shingle sets. To avoid comparing every pair, locality-sensitive hashing splits the signature into b bands of r values each; two pages become candidates if any band matches exactly. The chance that two pages with similarity s become candidates is
\[ P(\text{candidate}) = 1 - \left(1 - s^{r}\right)^{b} \]
which is an S-shaped curve with a threshold near \( (1/b)^{1/r} \). SimHash is the alternative: compute a 64-bit fingerprint in which similar documents differ in few bits, and find all fingerprints within Hamming distance 3 using bit-permuted lookup tables. Duplicate pages are dropped before indexing, or marked so that the search engine shows only the best copy. The same step removes pages that are copies in a spider trap.
Finally the crawler decides when to return. Pages change at different rates, and a news page changes hourly while a manual changes yearly. If changes happen at random with rate \( \lambda \) per day, the chance a stored copy is still current after t days is \( e^{-\lambda t} \). A policy then spends the crawl budget where it improves freshness the most, and an interesting result of Cho and Garcia-Molina is that pages that change very fast should be crawled less, since the copy would be stale almost at once. Conditional requests (`If-None-Match` with an ETag, or `If-Modified-Since`) let a server answer 304 Not Modified with no body, which saves most of the bandwidth on revisits.
At scale, the crawl is distributed. URLs are assigned to crawler machines by a hash of the host name, so each host is handled by exactly one machine, which keeps politeness local and keeps the per-host queue in one place. Links to hosts owned by other machines are sent in batches. Pages are written to a distributed file system in large compressed files, and downstream jobs build the index.
```python
import heapq, time

class Frontier:
    def __init__(self):
        self.queues = {}          # host -> list of urls
        self.ready = []           # heap of (next_allowed_time, host)
        self.scheduled = set()    # hosts in the heap or being fetched

    def add(self, host, url):
        self.queues.setdefault(host, []).append(url)
        if host not in self.scheduled:
            self.scheduled.add(host)
            heapq.heappush(self.ready, (time.time(), host))

    def next(self):
        t, host = heapq.heappop(self.ready)
        time.sleep(max(0, t - time.time()))
        return host, self.queues[host].pop(0)

    def done(self, host, fetch_secs):
        delay = max(1.0, 10 * fetch_secs)
        if self.queues[host]:
            heapq.heappush(self.ready, (time.time() + delay, host))
        else:
            self.scheduled.discard(host)
```

example: Plan a crawl of 1 billion pages in 30 days and check what it needs.
Step 1: rate. 30 days is 30 × 86,400 = 2,592,000 seconds. One billion pages in that time is 1,000,000,000 / 2,592,000 ≈ 386 pages per second.
Step 2: concurrency. Each fetch takes about 300 ms in total (DNS cache hit, connection, response). By Little's law, the number of requests in flight is rate × time = 386 × 0.3 ≈ 116. In practice you run several hundred concurrent fetches to cover slow servers and timeouts.
Step 3: bandwidth and storage. If an average HTML page is 100 KB, the download rate is 386 × 100 KB ≈ 38.6 MB/s, about 310 Mbit/s. Storage is 1 billion × 100 KB = 100 TB raw. Compressed text is usually 4 to 5 times smaller, so about 20 to 25 TB.
Step 4: politeness. Suppose one large site has 1,000,000 pages and you allow one request every 2 seconds. Fetching it needs 2,000,000 seconds, which is 23 days. If you did only this site at that speed, the job would take most of the month. This is why the crawler must work on thousands of hosts in parallel, and why the frontier orders work by host: at 386 pages per second with a 2-second delay per host, you need at least 386 × 2 = 772 hosts active at any moment to keep the fetchers busy. It also shows a limit: a crawl cannot get a huge single site faster without asking permission to go faster.
Step 5: URL-seen memory. If the crawl discovers 10 billion distinct URLs (each page has many links), a Bloom filter with 9.6 bits per URL for a 1% error needs 10 × 10⁹ × 9.6 bits = 96 × 10⁹ bits = 12 GB. Exact storage of 8-byte fingerprints would take 80 GB. At a 1% false-positive rate, about 1 in 100 new URLs is wrongly skipped, but the pages that matter are usually linked from several places and are found anyway.
Step 6: near-duplicates. Each page has a MinHash signature of 128 values, split into 16 bands of 8 values. A pair with similarity 0.9 becomes a candidate with probability \( 1 - (1 - 0.9^8)^{16} \approx 0.9999 \). A pair with similarity 0.5 becomes a candidate with probability \( 1 - (1 - 0.5^8)^{16} \approx 0.06 \). The candidates are then verified by comparing the signatures, so only the pairs that really are similar are removed.
intuition: A crawler is a polite librarian's assistant sent to copy every book in a city: always walk to the next unvisited shelf, never stand at one shelf for too long, and keep a list of what you already copied so you do not copy the same book twice.
The structure follows from three limits. The web is a graph, so you must remember where you have been (the seen-set). The sites are independent machines with finite capacity, so you must spread the work across them (per-host queues). And the web is full of copies, so you must compare cheaply (hashing). Each component maps to one data structure: a queue for what is next, a Bloom filter for what was seen, and a locality-sensitive hash for what is similar.
mistakes: People often think a crawler is a simple loop that follows links. In fact the loop is a small part of the work. Politeness, deduplication, canonicalization, trap avoidance, retries, scheduling revisits and handling billions of URLs are the engineering. A naive crawler gets blocked within minutes or fills its disks with copies.
People often think robots.txt prevents access. In fact it is a request, not a security control. Well-behaved crawlers follow it, and others do not. Anything private must be protected with authentication, not by listing it in robots.txt, which also tells anyone where to look. Also, a page blocked by robots.txt can still appear in a search index through links to it, since blocking stops fetching, not indexing.
People often think duplicate detection is just comparing hashes. In fact exact hashes only find byte-identical pages, and most web duplicates differ slightly (timestamps, ads, session ids in links). Near-duplicate detection needs similarity measures such as SimHash and MinHash, with LSH to avoid comparing all pairs.
People often think the crawled web is the whole web. In fact a large part is not reachable by following links: pages behind forms and logins, pages generated by scripts, pages blocked by robots.txt, and pages nobody links to (the deep web). The crawl also over-represents what is easy to fetch and often what is in English.
friction: Freshness and coverage compete for a fixed budget. Re-crawling old pages costs the same as discovering new ones, and the web grows and changes faster than any crawler can follow, so every crawler is a sample with a delay. News and social pages need minutes of freshness; the long tail is crawled every few weeks or months.
JavaScript-heavy pages are costly. A simple HTTP fetch returns an empty shell, and getting the content needs a headless browser that runs scripts, which can cost 10 to 100 times more CPU and time per page than a plain fetch. Crawlers use it selectively, for example when the raw HTML is nearly empty. Anti-bot systems, rate limits, CAPTCHAs and legal conditions add more constraints, and terms of service and copyright rules differ across countries and are changing, especially for AI training data.
tradeoffs: Choose breadth-first crawling with simple host priorities when you want broad coverage and have no quality signal, since it finds popular pages early. Choose a priority-based crawl (by PageRank, traffic or change rate) when your budget is smaller than the web and the use case is specific, such as news or e-commerce prices. You give up neutral coverage for focus.
Choose a Bloom filter for the URL-seen test when the set is large (billions) and a small loss of coverage is acceptable. Choose an exact set on disk or in a key-value store when you cannot afford to miss URLs, such as for a small focused crawl, and use the Bloom filter only as a first-level filter in front of it.
Choose SimHash for large-scale near-duplicate detection when each page needs a compact 64-bit fingerprint and you look for very similar pages (Hamming distance 3 or less). Choose MinHash with LSH when you want to control the similarity threshold, for example 0.8 Jaccard, and can store a signature of 100 or more values. SimHash costs less space, and MinHash gives a tunable and well understood trade-off.
practice: Respect the sites you crawl. Identify yourself with a user-agent that includes a contact URL, obey robots.txt, limit to one connection per host with a delay of at least a second (more if the site asks or responds slowly), back off on 429 and 503 responses, and cache robots.txt. Use conditional requests on revisits. Keep per-host and global rate limits as configuration that operators can change at once.
Build in safety against traps: cap the number of URLs per host, cap the URL length and depth, detect repeated path patterns, and limit the number of query parameters. Canonicalize URLs carefully and log the rules, as errors there multiply across billions of pages. Treat each downloaded page as untrusted input: limit size, set time limits, and parse with a safe parser.
For a first project, use an existing framework such as Scrapy for a focused crawl, or the Common Crawl indexes and files for large-scale text work (they let you fetch specific pages with HTTP range requests). Store raw responses in WARC files with headers, so you can re-parse later without fetching again. Measure per-host fetch rate, error rates by status code, duplicate rate, frontier size and age of the oldest pending URL; these show problems early.
interview: Design a web crawler that fetches 1 billion pages a month. What are the main components and numbers? || About 386 pages per second, so roughly 120 to a few hundred concurrent fetches, 40 MB/s of bandwidth for 100 KB pages, and 100 TB raw storage. Components are a frontier with priority and per-host queues, fetchers with a DNS cache and robots.txt cache, parsers, a URL-seen filter, a content-seen test, a store, and a recrawl scheduler, partitioned by host hash.
How do you make the crawler polite without losing throughput? || One request at a time per host, a delay (for example 10 times the last fetch time or the site's Crawl-delay), and a heap of hosts by next allowed time. Throughput comes from fetching many hosts in parallel: with a 2-second delay and 386 pages per second you need about 772 active hosts.
How do you avoid fetching the same URL twice, and what are the costs? || Canonicalize URLs and test them against a seen-set. A Bloom filter uses about 10 bits per URL at 1% error, much less than storing fingerprints, but a false positive makes the crawler skip a new URL. For stricter guarantees, combine it with an exact store on disk.
How do you detect near-duplicate pages across billions of documents? || Compute a SimHash fingerprint or a MinHash signature per page, and use locality-sensitive hashing so that only pages sharing a band or a permuted fingerprint prefix are compared. Candidates are verified by exact similarity. All-pairs comparison would be quadratic and impossible.
How would you decide when to re-crawl a page? || Estimate each page's change rate from past visits, for example with a Poisson model. The probability that a copy is stale after t is \( 1 - e^{-\lambda t} \), so allocate visits to maximize expected freshness per fetch. Conditional requests make unchanged pages cheap to check, and important or fast-changing sites get shorter intervals.
quiz: Why does a crawler keep one queue per host? || To enforce politeness. Each host queue has a next allowed time, so the crawler sends only one request at a time to a host and waits between requests, while fetching many other hosts in parallel.
Why is a Bloom filter suitable for the URL-seen test? || It stores billions of URLs in about 10 bits each and answers "seen" quickly, and its only error is a rare false positive, which skips a URL; it never forgets a URL that was added.
What is the purpose of canonicalization? || It rewrites different spellings of the same URL into one standard form, so the page is fetched and stored once and the seen-set stays accurate.
A crawl must fetch 500 million pages in 10 days. What page rate is needed? || 10 days = 864,000 seconds. 500,000,000 / 864,000 ≈ 579 pages per second.
When would you use SimHash rather than MinHash with LSH? || When you need compact 64-bit fingerprints and only want to find pages that are very similar (a small Hamming distance). MinHash with LSH is better when you need a tunable similarity threshold.

value: Automated planning is the problem of finding a sequence of actions that takes a system from its current state to a state that satisfies a goal. A warehouse robot must decide the order of moves to deliver five items. A spacecraft must schedule observations, turns and data downloads so that power and time limits hold. A software agent must call a series of tools to reach a user's goal. In each case there is a start, a goal, and a set of actions with rules about when each action can be used and what it changes. Planning searches for a path through the space of states that these actions create.
What makes classical planning different from ordinary search is that the problem is described, not coded. You write the actions once in a formal language (PDDL, the Planning Domain Definition Language): each action has preconditions (what must be true to do it) and effects (what it adds and removes). A general-purpose planner then solves any problem written in that language, with no special code. The planner finds this by searching from the start state, guided by a heuristic that it computes automatically from the description, usually by solving a relaxed, easier version of the problem.
For the layer above, AI agents break a goal into steps and tool calls, which is planning in a loose form, and robotics uses task planners to sequence actions before motion planners handle geometry. Space agencies have used planners to operate spacecraft. The layers below make it work. Heuristic search (L3) supplies A* and greedy best-first search over the state graph. Propositional and first-order logic (L4) give the language for states and actions. SAT solvers (L3) offer a second route: write the plan as a logical formula and let the solver find a satisfying assignment. Complexity theory (L3) says how hard this is: classical planning is PSPACE-complete, so no planner is fast on every problem. Constraint satisfaction (L3) handles the scheduling part, such as time windows and resources.
terms: State — a complete description of the world at one moment, written as the set of facts that are true.
Action (operator) — a possible step with preconditions (facts required before) and effects (facts added and facts deleted).
Goal — a set of facts that must hold at the end.
Plan — a sequence of actions that, applied from the start state, gives a state satisfying the goal.
Closed-world assumption — any fact not listed as true is false, so a state is just the list of true facts.
Delete relaxation — a simplified problem in which actions never remove facts, so facts only accumulate; its solution cost is a lower-bound-style guide for the real problem.
Heuristic (h) — an estimate of the number of steps (or cost) still needed from a state to the goal.
Admissible heuristic — one that never overestimates the true remaining cost; A* with such a heuristic returns an optimal plan.
PDDL — the standard text language for describing planning domains (actions) and problems (start and goal), introduced for the planning competitions.
history: Planning began with the Shakey robot at SRI. Fikes and Nilsson (1971) introduced STRIPS, the Stanford Research Institute Problem Solver, in which an action is a precondition list plus an add list and a delete list. This compact form is still the base of nearly all classical planners. In the 1970s and 80s work focused on partial-order planning (do not decide the order of steps that do not interact), and on the frame problem: how to say that most facts stay unchanged after an action without writing it out.
Three changes in the 1990s made planning practical. Blum and Furst's Graphplan (1995) built a layered graph of facts and actions and searched it backward, and was far faster than earlier planners. Kautz and Selman (1992, 1996) showed that planning problems could be translated to SAT and solved with fast SAT solvers. Bonet and Geffner's HSP (1999) and Hoffmann and Nebel's FF (2001) computed heuristics automatically from the delete relaxation and used them in forward search, which changed the field: the planner became a heuristic search engine. Bylander (1994) had meanwhile proved that propositional STRIPS planning is PSPACE-complete.
Standardization followed. McDermott and colleagues defined PDDL (1998) for the first International Planning Competition, and the yearly competitions since then have driven rapid progress. Helmert's Fast Downward (2006) became the reference system, with a translation to multi-valued variables, landmark heuristics and later the LM-cut heuristic (Helmert and Domshlak, 2009). Real deployments include NASA's Remote Agent on Deep Space 1 (1999), the first on-board planner to run a spacecraft, and later mission planning tools for the Mars rovers.
Since 2022 large language models are used for plan-like reasoning in agents. Studies such as Valmeekam and colleagues (2022, 2023) showed that LLMs alone are poor at generating valid plans in classical benchmarks such as Blocksworld, which has led to hybrid designs: an LLM translates a task into PDDL and a classical planner solves it (for example LLM+P, 2023), or a planner or verifier checks the LLM's proposals (Kambhampati's "LLM-Modulo" view, 2024).
how: A planning problem has a domain and an instance. The domain lists the predicates (types of facts) and the actions. The instance gives the objects, the initial state (a set of true facts) and the goal (a set of facts that must be true). Take the standard blocks-world domain with facts `on(x, y)`, `ontable(x)`, `clear(x)`, `holding(x)` and `handempty`. An action such as `stack(x, y)` has the precondition `holding(x)` and `clear(y)`, the add list `on(x, y)`, `clear(x)`, `handempty`, and the delete list `holding(x)`, `clear(y)`. Logic is what makes this precise. A state is the set of facts that are true, with the closed-world assumption that everything else is false. An action is applicable in state s if its preconditions are a subset of s, and the result is the new state \( s' = (s \setminus \text{del}) \cup \text{add} \). The frame problem is solved by a convention: whatever is not mentioned in the effects stays as it was.
Planning is then graph search. States are nodes and applicable actions are edges. A forward planner starts at the initial state, expands applicable actions, and stops when a state contains the goal facts. Breadth-first search finds a shortest plan but explodes. In blocks world with n blocks, the number of states is 1, 3, 13, 73, 501, 4051 for n = 1 … 6, and about 59 million for n = 10. In general the number of states is exponential in the number of facts. Heuristic search (A* or greedy best-first search) is used with a function h(s) that estimates the cost to the goal. A* returns an optimal plan if h is admissible, and greedy search is much faster but gives no optimality guarantee.
The crucial question is where h comes from. In domain-independent planning, it must be computed from the problem description alone. The delete relaxation is the main idea. Remove the delete lists from all actions, so facts are never lost. Now the problem becomes easy to reason about: every action that has become applicable can be applied, and facts only accumulate. Finding some plan in the relaxed problem takes polynomial time. Several heuristics read the answer from it. The FF heuristic extracts an actual relaxed plan and counts its actions. The \( h_{\max} \) heuristic takes the longest chain of dependent facts and is admissible, but weak; \( h_{\text{add}} \) sums the costs of the goal facts and is informative but can overestimate. LM-cut and other landmark-based heuristics find facts that every plan must make true at some time (landmarks), such as "the key must be picked up", and count them. Because a relaxed plan is never longer than a real plan (the optimal relaxed cost \( h^+ \) is a lower bound), these heuristics guide the search well on many problems.
The second route is compilation to SAT. Fix a horizon T, the maximum number of steps. Make a Boolean variable for each fact at each time \( f_t \) and for each action at each time \( a_t \). Write clauses: the initial state holds at time 0; the goal holds at time T; an action at time t implies its preconditions at t and its effects at t + 1; a fact that changes between t and t + 1 must be explained by an action that causes the change (the frame axioms); and mutually exclusive actions cannot both occur. A SAT solver finds a satisfying assignment if a plan of length at most T exists. Then T is increased until a plan is found. This reuses decades of SAT solver engineering, and it is strong for problems that need many parallel steps. Its size grows with the horizon times the number of facts and actions. For 1,000 facts, 5,000 actions and a horizon of 30, there are \( (1000 + 5000) \times 30 = 180{,}000 \) variables before the frame clauses.
Scheduling and resources move planning toward constraint satisfaction. When actions have durations, time windows or shared resources, the problem is written as variables (start times, resource choices) and constraints, and solved by backtracking with propagation. Temporal planners, such as those used for spacecraft, join causal reasoning with such constraint networks.
Complexity explains the limits. Deciding whether a plan exists for a propositional STRIPS problem is PSPACE-complete (Bylander, 1994). Each state is small (polynomial in size), so a search needs only polynomial memory if it forgets visited states, but the number of states is exponential and a shortest plan can be exponentially long: in the Towers of Hanoi with n disks, the shortest plan has \( 2^n - 1 \) moves, so 20 disks need 1,048,575 moves. No planner can output that plan quickly, let alone find it by trying alternatives.

example: Solve a small blocks-world problem by hand. Blocks A and B are on the table, and C is on top of A. The goal is `on(A, B)`.
Step 1: write the start state as facts: `ontable(A)`, `ontable(B)`, `on(C, A)`, `clear(C)`, `clear(B)`, `handempty`. The goal needs `on(A, B)`. The only action that adds `on(A, B)` is `stack(A, B)`, which needs `holding(A)` and `clear(B)`. `clear(B)` is already true. `holding(A)` comes from `pickup(A)`, which needs `clear(A)`, `ontable(A)` and `handempty`. But `clear(A)` is false because C is on A.
Step 2: the relaxed plan, used to compute h. Ignore all delete lists. `unstack(C, A)` needs `on(C, A)`, `clear(C)` and `handempty`, all true. It adds `holding(C)` and `clear(A)`. In the relaxed world `handempty` is still true, since deletions are ignored. Then `pickup(A)` is applicable and adds `holding(A)`. Then `stack(A, B)` is applicable and adds `on(A, B)`. The relaxed plan has 3 actions, so h(start) = 3.
Step 3: the real search. A* expands the start state. Applicable actions are `unstack(C, A)` and `pickup(B)`. Their relaxed costs: after `unstack(C, A)`, the state has `holding(C)` and `clear(A)`, but no `handempty`. Relaxed plan from there: `putdown(C)` (to get `handempty` back), `pickup(A)`, `stack(A, B)`: h = 3, and g = 1, so f = 4. After `pickup(B)`, the hand holds B and A is still covered by C: the relaxed plan needs `putdown(B)`, `unstack(C, A)`, `pickup(A)`, `stack(A, B)`, so h = 4, g = 1, f = 5. A* picks the first (f = 4), and the tie-breaking details do not change the result here.
Step 4: continue. From the state holding C: `putdown(C)` gives g = 2, and h = 2 (`pickup(A)`, `stack(A, B)`), f = 4. Then `pickup(A)` gives g = 3, h = 1, f = 4. Then `stack(A, B)` gives g = 4 and h = 0. The plan has 4 actions and is optimal, because h never exceeded the true remaining cost at any step. The planner expanded 4 states on the plan path and a few side states, not the 13 possible configurations of three blocks, and the saving is far larger on bigger problems.
Step 5: scale. With 10 blocks there are about 59 million states, and a blind search may visit a large part of them. An informative heuristic steers the search toward the goal, so it usually expands only a small fraction. At 20 blocks the number of states is astronomically large, and only heuristic search has a chance.
Step 6: the SAT view of the same problem. With horizon T = 4 the solver gets variables like `holding_C_1` and `unstack_C_A_0`. With T = 3 the formula is unsatisfiable, since no 3-step plan exists. With T = 4 it is satisfiable, and the true assignment gives the same four actions. The solver proves optimality by the failure at T = 3.
intuition: Planning is finding a route on a map you have not seen yet: the actions are the roads, but you only learn the map from the rules, and a heuristic is a rough guess of the distance left, such as the straight-line distance.
The design works because the rules of the world are local and repeated. Each action affects a few facts, and the same few action patterns create an exponentially large map. By describing the actions instead of the states, one planner can solve any domain. The relaxation trick is natural because most of the difficulty of planning is in the interactions among actions (one action destroys what another needs). If you remove interactions, the problem becomes easy, and the cost of the easy problem is a good first estimate of the cost of the hard one.
mistakes: People often think planning is the same as pathfinding on a map. In fact the state graph is not given; it is defined implicitly by rules and is exponentially large. A path planner searches a graph that fits in memory, whereas a task planner must discover relevant parts of a graph with more states than atoms in the universe.
People often think a planner needs a program written for each domain. In fact domain-independent planners take the PDDL description and need nothing else. Domain-specific knowledge (HTN methods, hand-written heuristics) makes them faster, but it is optional.
People often think a better computer will solve any planning problem eventually. In fact the problem is PSPACE-complete, and plans can be exponentially long. What helps is structure: good heuristics, decomposition and pruning. Hardware helps only linearly against exponential growth.
People often think large language models have made planners obsolete. In fact LLMs generate plausible steps, but their plans are often invalid when checked against the preconditions and effects, and they get worse as the plan grows longer. Classical planners are sound, give guarantees (valid and sometimes optimal plans), and can verify an LLM's proposals. The strong designs use the LLM to understand the task and the planner to solve it.
friction: The main limit is the cost of writing a correct model. Someone must define predicates, actions and all their preconditions and effects, and a missing precondition produces plans that are valid in the model and absurd in the world. Models are brittle, and verifying them is as hard as testing a program. Learning models from data, or having an LLM write them and a human review them, is an active area but not solved.
The second limit is scale and expressiveness. Classical planning assumes a fully known, deterministic, static world with instant actions. Real problems have uncertainty (actions with random outcomes), partial observation, time, continuous quantities such as fuel and battery, and other agents. Extensions exist (probabilistic, temporal, numeric planning, POMDPs), and each is much harder to solve than the classical version, so practical systems often relax the problem or add hand-built structure.
tradeoffs: Choose a domain-independent classical planner (Fast Downward and similar) when the world is well understood and discrete, the rules change often, and you want guaranteed valid plans: logistics, assembly sequences, test generation, workflow synthesis. Choose a hand-built scheduler or a mathematical optimizer (integer programming, constraint programming) when the problem is mainly about resources, time and cost with fixed structure, since these exploit numeric structure that PDDL planners handle poorly. You give up the flexibility of declarative actions for performance.
Choose SAT or constraint-based encoding when the plan needs many parallel actions, the horizon is short and bounded, or you want to add arbitrary extra constraints; choose heuristic forward search when plans are long and the horizon is unknown. SAT gives proof of optimality for a given horizon and extensibility; forward search gives speed on most benchmark domains.
Choose satisficing search (greedy best-first with the FF heuristic) when any good plan will do: it often answers in seconds. Choose optimal search (A* with LM-cut) when plan cost is money or risk and the problem is small. The optimal planner can be hundreds of times slower for a plan only a few percent cheaper.
Choose an LLM-based planner for fuzzy, open-ended tasks with no formal model, and add a verifier or a classical planner when errors are costly.
practice: Begin by writing the model carefully. List the objects, predicates and actions. State each precondition explicitly and check effects, especially deletes. Test the model on tiny problems by hand before scaling up. Use a validator (such as VAL) to check that plans are valid, and use several planners on the same model, as a bug in the model usually appears as a surprising plan.
Use an existing planner and not your own: Fast Downward is the standard open-source system with many heuristics and search algorithms, and the International Planning Competition benchmarks provide test domains. For a first run, use a satisficing configuration (a lazy greedy search with the FF heuristic and preferred operators). If you need quality, use an anytime configuration that finds a fast plan and then improves it. For scheduling with time and resources, consider a CP-SAT or similar constraint solver.
For LLM agents, separate the roles. Let the LLM translate the user request to a structured goal (or to PDDL), let a symbolic planner or a checker generate or verify the plan, and execute step by step with checks on preconditions before each tool call. Log the state after each action, and re-plan when an observation differs from the expected effect. Limit plan length, as errors accumulate over steps.
interview: Describe a planning problem formally. What is a state, an action, and how is an action applied? || A state is a set of true facts under the closed-world assumption. An action has preconditions, an add list and a delete list. It is applicable when its preconditions are a subset of the state, and applying it gives (state minus delete list) plus add list. A plan is a sequence of actions that leads from the initial state to one containing the goal.

Finding a plan then takes polynomial time, and its length estimates the remaining work of the real problem. Optimal relaxed cost never exceeds the true cost, so it is a lower bound; practical heuristics such as FF use a non-optimal relaxed plan, which is informative but not always admissible.

PSPACE-completeness means there is no known polynomial algorithm, and planners succeed through heuristics and structure, not worst-case guarantees.

How would you use an LLM in an agent that has to produce correct multi-step plans? || The LLM interprets the request and proposes a plan or a PDDL model. A verifier or a symbolic planner checks preconditions and effects, rejects invalid steps and returns feedback, and execution is monitored with re-planning. This uses the LLM for flexibility and the planner for guarantees.
Your planner returns a plan that is valid in the model but wrong in reality. Where is the bug? || Most likely in the model: a missing precondition or effect, or an assumption such as a deterministic action. I would replay the plan against the real system or a simulator, find the first step where the real state differs from the model state, and fix the action definition.

value: Knowledge representation (KR) is the part of AI that asks how to write down what is known about the world so that a computer can use it, not only store it. The most common form today is the knowledge graph: facts are stored as triples of the form subject, predicate, object, such as (Bengaluru, locatedIn, India) or (Ada, worksFor, AcmeCo). Entities have types (Person, City), and a layer of rules called an ontology says what the types and relations mean: every Manager is an Employee, every Employee is a Person, and locatedIn is transitive. A reasoner can then derive facts that nobody wrote down, for example that AcmeCo is located in India.
This solves two problems that tables and text handle badly. First, the data is about many kinds of things connected in many ways, and the schema changes often. Adding a new kind of link is adding a new predicate, not altering a table. Second, systems must combine facts from many sources and answer questions that follow from them. A search engine that knows that Mount Everest is a mountain, in Nepal and China, and 8,849 metres high can answer a question directly, not return ten links. A model of the domain, with explicit meaning, is also something humans can read, check and correct.
For the layer above, graph databases store knowledge graphs, either as RDF triple stores or as property graphs, and retrieval-augmented generation increasingly uses a graph extracted from documents (GraphRAG) so that an LLM can follow relations across many documents. The layers below give KR its meaning. Propositional and first-order logic (L4) say what it means for a fact to follow from others; ontologies are fragments of first-order logic called description logics, chosen so that reasoning terminates. Logic programming and Datalog (L3) give a way to apply rules to facts until nothing new appears. Relations and functions (L4) show that a triple is a row in a three-column relation and that a path query is a join. Graph theory (L4) describes the whole as a labelled directed graph, where reachability and transitive closure answer many questions.
terms: Triple — one fact as (subject, predicate, object), such as (Ada, worksFor, AcmeCo).
Knowledge graph — a collection of triples viewed as a labelled directed graph whose nodes are entities and whose edges are relations.
Ontology — a formal description of the types of things and relations in a domain, with axioms such as "Manager is a subclass of Employee".
RDF — the Resource Description Framework, the W3C standard in which every entity and predicate is a global identifier (IRI) and data is a set of triples.
OWL — the Web Ontology Language, a W3C standard for ontologies based on description logics.
SPARQL — the query language for RDF; a query is a graph pattern with variables that is matched against the triples.
Reasoner — a program that derives new facts (entailments) from the stored facts and the ontology.
Open-world assumption — a fact that is not stated is unknown, not false; RDF and OWL reason this way, while databases assume the closed world.
Property graph — a graph model in which nodes and edges can carry key-value properties; used by Neo4j and similar systems.
Entity linking — deciding which entity in the graph a mention in text refers to, such as "Paris" the city or the person.
history: The roots are in the 1960s and 70s. Quillian's semantic networks (1968) drew concepts as nodes and relations as labelled links, with inheritance along "is-a" links. Minsky's frames (1974) grouped the properties of a concept into a record with default values. These were intuitive but had no precise meaning, and different systems gave the same diagram different semantics. Brachman's KL-ONE (1985) separated the structure of concepts from the facts about individuals and gave it a formal meaning, which started the line of description logics. Hayes and others pointed out that these notations could be read as first-order logic, so questions of what follows from what could be answered by logic.
Description logics balanced expressive power against decidability: how much can you say before reasoning becomes impossible or too slow. This work led to OWL (a W3C recommendation in 2004, OWL 2 in 2009). In parallel Lenat's Cyc project (from 1984) tried to hand-write common-sense knowledge, with millions of assertions, and showed both the potential and the cost of manual knowledge engineering. The Semantic Web vision (Berners-Lee, Hendler and Lassila, 2001) proposed putting machine-readable data on the web using RDF (a W3C standard since 1999), RDFS, OWL and, from 2008, the SPARQL query language. The Linked Data principles (Berners-Lee, 2006) simplified the message: give things URIs, make them dereferenceable, link them.
Practical knowledge graphs grew from crowds and from search. DBpedia (2007) extracted structured data from Wikipedia. Freebase (Metaweb, 2007; bought by Google in 2010) was a collaborative graph. In 2012 Google launched its Knowledge Graph under the slogan "things, not strings", and Wikidata started the same year as a shared, multilingual, editable knowledge base that now holds over 100 million items. Schema.org (2011) gave web publishers a shared vocabulary that Google, Microsoft, Yahoo and Yandex read. In machine learning, Bordes and colleagues' TransE (2013) learned vector embeddings of entities and relations to predict missing links. Property-graph databases (Neo4j, from 2007) grew in industry, and the ISO standard graph query language GQL was published in 2024. Since 2024, GraphRAG (Edge and colleagues at Microsoft) uses an LLM to extract entities and relations from documents into a graph, which is then used to answer questions about the whole collection.
how: Start with the data model. A knowledge graph is a set of triples. In RDF, each subject and predicate is an IRI, a global identifier such as `https://www.wikidata.org/entity/Q1355` for Bengaluru, so that data from different sources can be merged by identifier. The object is either another IRI or a literal value (a string, number or date). Because a triple is a row with three columns (this is the relations concept), the whole graph is one table with columns S, P and O, and a query that follows a path is a series of self-joins. Because the same data is a graph (graph theory), each triple is an edge from S to O labelled P, and a path query asks for a walk through the graph.
```text
:Ada      a            :Manager .
:Ada      :worksFor    :AcmeCo .
:AcmeCo   :locatedIn   :Bengaluru .
:Bengaluru :locatedIn  :India .
:Manager  rdfs:subClassOf  :Employee .
:Employee rdfs:subClassOf  :Person .
:locatedIn a owl:TransitiveProperty .
```
Storage turns names into integers. A dictionary maps every IRI and literal to a 64-bit identifier, and triples are stored as three identifiers (24 bytes). Several sorted indexes are kept, such as SPO, POS and OSP, so that any pattern with one or two bound positions is a range scan in one of them. For a graph with one billion triples, each index takes about 24 GB uncompressed, so three indexes take 72 GB before compression, which fits in the memory of a large server. Compression of sorted identifiers typically cuts this by two to four times.
Querying uses graph patterns. A SPARQL query is a set of triple patterns with variables, and the engine finds all bindings of the variables that match triples in the graph, joining the patterns on shared variables. Property paths add navigation: `:worksFor/:locatedIn+` means "works for something, then follow one or more locatedIn links".
```text
SELECT ?person WHERE {
  ?person a :Person ;
          :worksFor/:locatedIn+ :India .
}
```
The query planner chooses a join order using statistics, as in a relational database: it starts from the most selective pattern. This is why a triple store is a specialised relational engine, and why the performance questions (join order, index choice, cardinality estimates) are those of databases.
Reasoning is what separates a knowledge graph from a graph database. The ontology adds axioms, and a reasoner derives entailments. RDFS gives simple rules: if x has type C and C is a subclass of D, then x has type D; if a relation has a declared domain or range, the types of its subject and object follow. OWL adds more: transitive, symmetric and inverse properties, class definitions by restriction ("a Parent is a Person with at least one child"), and disjointness. These can be written as Datalog-style rules, for example `locatedIn(X, Z) :- locatedIn(X, Y), locatedIn(Y, Z)`, and evaluated bottom-up: apply every rule to the current facts, add the new conclusions, and repeat until nothing changes (a fixed point). Semi-naive evaluation, in each round, uses only the facts that were new in the previous round, so work is not repeated. This is the logic programming concept. Materialization stores the derived triples so queries are fast, at a cost in storage and update time. The other approach, query rewriting, expands the query at run time to include the subclasses and subproperties, which keeps the store small and the data fresh.
Logic decides how much can be said. Full first-order logic is undecidable: no program can always decide whether a conclusion follows. Description logics restrict the language so that reasoning terminates. OWL 2 DL is based on the logic SROIQ, with a worst-case reasoning cost that is very high (N2EXPTIME-complete), so OWL 2 also defines profiles: OWL 2 EL (polynomial time, used in large biomedical ontologies such as SNOMED CT), OWL 2 QL (queries rewritten to SQL) and OWL 2 RL (rules that run in a rule engine). Choosing a profile is choosing which reasoning cost you accept. Separately, SHACL (a W3C standard from 2017) validates data against shapes under a closed-world view, because the open-world reasoning of OWL does not report "required field missing" as an error.
Knowledge graphs are built from sources in several ways: mapping databases and spreadsheets to triples, extracting entities and relations from text with NLP or LLMs, and merging records that describe the same entity (entity resolution, then a single identifier). In embedding methods such as TransE, each entity and relation is a vector and a true triple (h, r, t) should satisfy \( h + r \approx t \); the model ranks candidate links, which complements logical reasoning with statistical guesses about missing facts.

example: Use the seven triples shown in the first code block of the "how" section and trace what the system can answer.
Step 1: ask a question with only the stored facts. "Which Persons work for a company in India?" Nothing says Ada is a Person: the data says only that Ada is a Manager. Nothing says AcmeCo is in India either, only that it is in Bengaluru. A table or a plain graph database would return no result.
Step 2: apply the rules, round 1. Rule A: if x has type C and C is a subclass of D, then x has type D. From (Ada type Manager) and (Manager subClassOf Employee) we derive (Ada type Employee). Rule B: transitivity of locatedIn. From (AcmeCo locatedIn Bengaluru) and (Bengaluru locatedIn India) we derive (AcmeCo locatedIn India). Also the subclass chain: (Manager subClassOf Person) by transitivity of subClassOf.
Step 3: round 2. Using only the new facts, Rule A applies to (Ada type Employee) and (Employee subClassOf Person) and derives (Ada type Person). Nothing else is new.
Step 4: round 3 derives nothing new, so we have a fixed point. Four facts were derived from seven. The query now matches: Ada is a Person, Ada worksFor AcmeCo, and AcmeCo locatedIn India, so the answer is Ada. Nobody wrote the fact that makes the answer.
Step 5: size of derived data. Suppose the containment hierarchy is a chain of 1,000 places, each located in the next (street, district, city, region, and so on). There are 999 stored locatedIn edges. With transitivity, every pair (place, an ancestor) is derived: 1,000 × 999 / 2 = 499,500 triples, about 500 times the stored data. In real hierarchies, with depth 6 to 10, the blow-up is a factor of 3 to 5. Materializing helps queries and costs storage. Query rewriting avoids the storage and costs time at query time, with a path query like `locatedIn+`.
intuition: A knowledge graph is a set of index cards, each with one fact, plus a rule book that says what the words mean, so that a clerk can answer questions the cards never mention.
The design is natural because facts about the world are many small statements about things and their relations, and a triple is the smallest unit that holds a relation. Meaning does not live in the cards but in the rules, so separating them lets many people add cards while a few people maintain the rules. Logic is the guarantee: if the cards and the rules are true, every answer that follows from them is true. This is the opposite of a statistical model, which gives a likely answer to anything. A knowledge graph gives a certain answer to what it knows, and says nothing about what it does not.
mistakes: People often think a knowledge graph is just a graph database. In fact a graph database stores and traverses the data. A knowledge graph adds shared meaning: identifiers, types and an ontology with a reasoner or rules, so facts can be derived and merged across sources. You can store a knowledge graph in a graph database, but a graph database without an ontology is not yet a knowledge graph.
People often think the absence of a fact means it is false. In fact RDF and OWL use the open-world assumption: not stated means unknown. Many data problems come from mixing this with SQL's closed world. Constraints such as "every Person has a birth date" must be checked with a validation language like SHACL, not left to OWL reasoning.
People often think a reasoner makes the graph correct. In fact reasoning only passes truth along. A wrong fact or a wrong axiom produces wrong conclusions, often many of them. A mistaken `subClassOf` link between two top-level classes can make thousands of entities share a wrong type.
People often think LLMs make knowledge graphs unnecessary. In fact an LLM gives fluent, probably right text, with no guarantee and no source. A knowledge graph gives exact, auditable facts with provenance, and is cheaper to update. The two are used together: LLMs build and query graphs, and graphs ground LLM answers (GraphRAG, entity linking).
friction: Knowledge acquisition is the oldest problem. Writing facts and rules by hand is slow and expensive, and extracting them from text is error-prone: an extractor with 90% precision on a graph of 100 million triples creates 10 million false facts. Entity resolution (deciding that two records are the same person) is hard, and a false merge is worse than a missed one because it joins unrelated facts.
Schema and ontology design is difficult. Teams disagree on categories, and edge cases are everywhere: is a company a legal entity, a brand or a place of work? Ontologies drift as the business changes, and changing a widely used class breaks consumers. Heavy ontologies are costly to maintain, and many successful graphs (Wikidata, schema.org) use light schemas with human curation.
Reasoning has limits in cost and in what it can say. Expressive description logics have very high worst-case complexity, and even moderate reasoners can be slow on graphs with hundreds of millions of triples. Materialization increases storage and makes deletes hard: when a base fact is removed, you must retract the derived facts that depended only on it, which needs truth maintenance. Logical reasoning also handles uncertainty poorly: facts have confidence, time and source, and RDF has no built-in place for them (RDF-star and named graphs add one).
tradeoffs: Choose an RDF knowledge graph with an ontology when you must integrate many sources with shared identifiers, need inference (hierarchies, equivalences), want open standards for exchange (SPARQL, OWL, SHACL), or publish data for others. Choose a property graph (Neo4j or similar, queried with Cypher or GQL) when the main need is fast traversal and application development, edges need properties without the extra machinery, and inference is not central. You give up built-in semantics and standards-based exchange for developer convenience, and the reverse.
Choose a light ontology (a few dozen classes, subclass and domain/range axioms, validation by SHACL) when you need a shared vocabulary and consistency checks. Choose a rich ontology with a reasoner (OWL 2 EL or RL profiles) only when the domain is stable and derived knowledge is valuable, as in biomedical terminologies. The richer the ontology, the higher the reasoning cost and the maintenance burden.
Choose materialization of inferred triples when reads dominate and the data changes rarely, because queries become simple lookups. Choose query-time reasoning (rewriting) when the data changes often or the closure would be huge. You trade storage and update cost against query latency.
practice: Start from questions. List the 20 questions the graph must answer, then design the minimum set of types and relations that answer them. Reuse existing vocabularies (schema.org, Dublin Core, SKOS for taxonomies, and domain standards such as the IAB content taxonomy in advertising) instead of inventing every term. Give every entity a stable, resolvable identifier that does not encode meaning that can change, and keep a mapping from source identifiers to it.
Treat data quality as engineering. Validate with SHACL shapes in the pipeline, keep provenance (source, date, confidence) on facts, and measure the precision of a sample of extracted triples before loading. Do entity resolution with blocking and scoring, and keep merge decisions reversible. Version the ontology, and make changes additive where you can. Test the reasoning on small examples and include them as regression tests.
For tools, use an RDF store such as Apache Jena Fuseki, GraphDB, Stardog, Virtuoso or Amazon Neptune when you need SPARQL and reasoning, a property graph database when an application needs traversals, and keep heavy analytics in your warehouse or lakehouse, with the graph as a serving layer. For LLM applications, extract entities and relations with a schema, check them against the ontology, store them with the source passage, and retrieve both the neighbourhood and the passage so that answers can cite sources.
interview: What is the difference between a knowledge graph and a graph database? || A graph database is a storage and query engine for nodes and edges. A knowledge graph is a data model with shared identifiers, types and an ontology, so facts can be integrated across sources and new facts inferred. Many knowledge graphs run on graph databases or triple stores, but the semantic layer is the difference.
Explain open-world versus closed-world reasoning with an example. || In a SQL database, a missing row means the fact is false, so "employees without a manager" returns Ada. In RDF/OWL, a missing triple means unknown, so the system cannot conclude that Ada has no manager. You choose by what the application needs, and use SHACL or rules when you need closed-world checks.
How would you evaluate inferred facts, and what can go wrong with materialization? || Test the rules on small known cases, sample derived triples and have people check them, and compare counts before and after to catch explosions. Materialization can multiply storage (a chain of n places gives n(n−1)/2 pairs), and deletions need truth maintenance to remove derived facts that lose their support.
Why is full first-order logic not used for ontologies, and what do description logics change? || First-order entailment is undecidable, so reasoning may never finish. Description logics restrict the constructs so that reasoning is decidable, and the profiles (EL, QL, RL) restrict them further to get polynomial time or SQL rewriting. You trade expressive power for guaranteed termination and speed.
You need to unify customer, device and household data from five systems. Would you use a knowledge graph? || Possibly, if relationships and entity resolution across sources are the core need. I would define the identifiers and a light ontology, do entity resolution with confidence scores and reversible merges, keep provenance, and use the graph as an integration and serving layer next to the warehouse. If the need is mostly aggregation, plain tables are simpler.
How do knowledge graphs help LLM applications, and where do they fall short? || They give structured, verifiable facts and multi-hop relations that retrieval over text misses, and they allow citations. Shortcomings are the cost of building and curating the graph, extraction errors that become false facts, and coverage: what is not in the graph is invisible, so it is combined with passage retrieval.
quiz: What is a triple, and why can a set of triples be seen both as a table and as a graph? || A triple is (subject, predicate, object), one fact. As a table it is a row in a three-column relation; as a graph it is a labelled edge from the subject node to the object node.
What does the open-world assumption mean? || A fact that is not stated is unknown, not false. A system cannot conclude "no" from the absence of data, unlike a SQL database, which treats missing rows as false.
Why do description logics restrict the logic language? || Full first-order logic is undecidable. Restricted description logics make reasoning decidable, and their profiles make it fast enough for large ontologies.
A chain of 2,000 places each located in the next has 1,999 stored edges. How many locatedIn triples exist after materializing the transitive closure? || 2,000 × 1,999 / 2 = 1,999,000 triples, about 1,000 times the stored edges.
When would you choose a property graph over an RDF knowledge graph? || When the main need is application-driven traversal and fast development, with properties on edges and little need for inference or standards-based data exchange.
