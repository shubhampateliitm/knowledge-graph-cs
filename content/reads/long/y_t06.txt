@@+ ml
tradeoffs: Gradient-boosted trees or a neural network. Choose boosted trees (boosting, XGBoost) for tabular data with up to a few hundred features and from 10 thousand to about 100 million rows: they train in minutes on a CPU, need little scaling or encoding work, handle missing values, and are hard to beat on click, fraud and credit data. Choose a neural network when the input is raw pixels, audio, text or very high-cardinality IDs (millions of users or items), because there the model must learn its own features and embeddings. You give up, with trees, any learning from unstructured input and good use of IDs; with networks, a fast baseline, easy debugging and often a few points of accuracy on tabular data.
Simple model or complex model. Start with logistic regression when you have under about 10 thousand labelled rows, when a regulator or a business owner needs to see each feature's weight, or when the whole pipeline must score in under a millisecond on one core. Move up only if the offline gain on a held-out set is larger than the noise of that set; with 100 thousand test rows and a 2% base rate, a change of 0.1% in AUC is usually noise, not gain. You give up interpretability and cheap retraining as you add complexity; you give up accuracy and sometimes revenue when you stay simple, so price both before you decide.
Retrain often or train once. Choose daily or hourly retraining when behaviour drifts quickly (ads, feeds, fraud) and the cost of a stale model is visible in the A/B metric; choose monthly retraining or a fixed model when the target is stable (OCR, speech) or labels arrive slowly (loan default takes months). More frequent retraining also means more pipeline code to break, more risk of training on a bad day of data, and a need for automatic checks before a new model is promoted.
Buy a pretrained model or train your own. Calling a hosted model or fine-tuning an open one (lora) wins when you have fewer than about 100 thousand labelled examples, the task is language or vision, and a per-call cost of a fraction of a cent is acceptable. Training your own wins when you own a large proprietary label stream (billions of impressions with clicks), latency must be under 10 ms, or data cannot leave your network. You give up control and unit cost with a hosted model; you give up time to first launch and need an ML platform team with your own model.
interview: A team proposes a deep model for a table with 200 columns and 2 million rows. What do you ask before agreeing? || I ask what a tuned XGBoost or LightGBM baseline scores, because on tabular data of this size it is the number to beat and it takes a day to get. I also ask about latency, features that need embeddings (raw text, long ID lists), and who will maintain a GPU training pipeline. If the deep model does not beat the baseline by more than the noise of the test set, I would ship the trees.
Offline AUC went up 2% but the A/B test shows no gain. Why? || The common causes are leakage (a feature that exists in the logs but not at serving time), a train and test split that is random while production is time-ordered, a metric that does not match the business goal (ranking quality versus calibrated probability for an auction), or a test too small to see the effect. I would check the split first, then compare feature values offline and online for the same requests, then check calibration and the power of the A/B test.
How do you tell data drift from a bug when a model's online metric drops? || A bug usually causes a sudden step at a deploy or a pipeline change, and feature distributions change in one place (a null rate jumps, a unit changes). Drift is gradual or follows a real event such as a holiday. I compare feature histograms and null rates against last week, check the model version and upstream jobs for that date, and replay yesterday's traffic through the old model; if the old model does the same, the world changed.
Why validate with a time-based split instead of a random one? || With a random split, rows from the same user or the same day appear on both sides, so the model is tested on near-copies and the score is too high. A time-based split trains on the past and tests on the future, which is what production does, and it exposes drift. You pay by having less recent data to train on, and by having to pick a test window long enough to cover weekly patterns.
When is more data better than a better model? || When the learning curve is still rising (validation error falls as you add data) and the error is mostly variance, more data helps, and 10 times more data often beats a new architecture. When training and validation error are both high and flat, the model is too simple or the features are missing the signal, and more rows will not help. I plot error against training size at 10%, 30% and 100% of the data to see which case I am in.
@@+ dl
tradeoffs: Train from scratch or start from a pretrained model. Fine-tune a pretrained network when you have under about 1 million labelled examples and the domain is images, text or audio, because it usually reaches the target accuracy with 10 to 100 times less data and compute. Train from scratch when the input is unlike any public data (sensor traces, logs, click sequences, chemistry), when you have billions of examples, or when licence terms forbid reusing weights. You give up, with fine-tuning, control over the architecture and training data and inherit the base model's biases; with scratch training, weeks of GPU time and the risk of an unstable run.
Small model or big model. A model with 10 to 100 million parameters fits on one GPU, trains in hours, and serves in a few milliseconds on a CPU or small GPU; choose it for ranking, vision on devices and anything with a latency budget under 50 ms. Choose billions of parameters only when the task needs broad knowledge or reasoning, and expect to add model parallelism, mixed precision (mixedprec) and about 16 bytes of training state per parameter with Adam, so 70 billion parameters needs more than 1 TB of GPU memory in total. Distillation (distill) and quantization (quant) can give a small model some of a big one's quality, at the price of an extra training stage.
Data parallel or model parallel. Choose plain data parallelism (the same model on every GPU, gradients averaged by all-reduce) while the model plus optimizer state fits in one GPU's memory, roughly under 10 billion parameters on an 80 GB card with sharding tricks; it scales to hundreds of GPUs with 80 to 95% efficiency on fast interconnect. Add tensor and pipeline parallelism only when memory forces you to, because each split adds communication that eats efficiency (tensor parallelism needs NVLink-class bandwidth inside one node) and makes the code and debugging harder.
Deep learning or a classical method. Use deep learning when you have unstructured inputs, more than about 100 thousand examples and a GPU budget. Use boosted trees or linear models (ml) on tabular data, a rule or a regular expression when the logic is simple and must be exact, and a search or lookup when the answer already exists. Deep models give up exact guarantees, easy explanations and cheap retraining; in return they remove hand-built features.
interview: Your training loss is flat from the first step. What do you check? || First I try to overfit one small batch of 32 examples; if the loss does not go to near zero, the bug is in the code (wrong labels, loss on logits twice, frozen parameters, learning rate far too small). If it does overfit, I check learning-rate warmup, gradient norms (zero or exploding), data normalization, and whether the data loader gives shuffled, correctly paired inputs and labels.
Why does the loss go to NaN halfway through training, and how do you stop it? || Usually a step with a large gradient (a bad batch, a learning rate too high, an overflow in fp16) pushes weights to a bad region. I log the gradient norm, use gradient clipping (for example at 1.0), use bf16 instead of fp16 so values do not overflow, add warmup, and keep a checkpoint from before the spike to restart from while skipping the bad batch.
Training on 64 GPUs gives only 20 times the speed of one GPU. Where does the time go? || Likely all-reduce time is not hidden behind the backward pass, the data loader cannot feed the GPUs, or batches per GPU are too small so compute per step is short compared with communication. I profile one step for idle GPU time, check network bandwidth against gradient size (a 1 billion parameter model sends about 2 GB of bf16 gradients per step), and use bigger per-GPU batches, gradient bucketing and overlapped communication.
How would you decide the batch size and learning rate when you add GPUs? || A larger global batch needs a larger learning rate, roughly linear up to a point, with warmup; beyond a critical batch size, extra examples per step give little gain, so more GPUs just waste money. I find that point with short runs at 2 or 3 batch sizes and watch steps to reach a target loss; efficient scaling means steps fall almost in proportion to batch size.
Your model is accurate offline but too slow to serve. What are your options? || Cheapest first: quantize to int8 or fp8 and batch requests, export to an optimized runtime (TensorRT, ONNX Runtime, torch.compile), then distill to a smaller student, prune or cache results for repeated inputs. Each step trades a small accuracy loss for 2 to 4 times lower latency, so I measure accuracy on the real metric after each one, not just the loss.
@@+ llm
tradeoffs: Big model, small model or fine-tuned small model. Use a frontier model through an API for low volume (under about 1 million calls a month), open-ended tasks and the fastest start; a call costs fractions of a cent to a few cents. Use a small model of 7 to 8 billion parameters, self-hosted on one GPU, when volume is high and the task is narrow (classification, extraction), where it often matches the large model after fine-tuning (lora) with 1 to 10 thousand examples. You give up, with the small model, broad reasoning and robustness to odd inputs and you take on running GPUs; with the API, your marginal cost, control over latency and the guarantee that the model will not change under you.
Dense or mixture of experts (moe). A dense model uses all its weights for each token, so quality per parameter is highest and serving is simple. A mixture-of-experts model, such as one with 400 billion total and about 40 billion active parameters, gives the quality of a much larger dense model at a lower compute cost per token, but you must hold all experts in memory, and routing makes batching and multi-GPU communication harder. Choose MoE when you serve at large scale with many GPUs and compute is the bottleneck; choose dense when you serve on one or two GPUs and memory is the bottleneck.
Long context, retrieval or fine-tuning for new knowledge. Put the text in the prompt (a long context of 128 thousand to 1 million tokens) when it is a single document and you ask a few questions; cost grows with length and quality on facts in the middle can drop. Use retrieval (rag) when the knowledge base is larger than a few hundred thousand tokens, changes daily, or needs citations. Fine-tune only to change style, format or skill, not to teach facts that change, since the weights cannot be updated cheaply or audited. Each choice gives up something: prompt cost, retrieval recall, or freshness.
Decoding settings. Use temperature 0 (or greedy) for extraction, classification and code that must be repeatable; use temperature 0.7 to 1.0 with top-p around 0.9 for writing and brainstorming; sample several answers and vote (self-consistency) when a wrong answer is costly and you can afford 5 times the tokens. You give up variety and sometimes quality at low temperature, and you give up cost and latency when you sample many times.
interview: Why is the cost of a long prompt not linear in its length? || Attention compares every token with every earlier token, so the compute for the prompt grows with the square of its length, and the KV cache grows linearly and must be held for the whole generation. In practice, up to a few thousand tokens the cost looks linear because the feed-forward layers dominate; beyond tens of thousands attention dominates, and memory for the cache limits how many requests share a GPU. Prompt caching helps when the prefix repeats.
A model that scores well on a benchmark performs poorly on your task. Why, and what do you do? || The benchmark may be contaminated (the model saw the questions in training), measure a different skill, or use a different prompt format than yours. I build a small evaluation set from real traffic (200 to 500 examples with a clear grading rule), measure the model on that, and track it across model versions. A public score is a filter for candidates, not a decision.
How do you reduce hallucinations in a product that answers factual questions? || Give the model the source text (rag) and require answers to cite it, let it say "I do not know" when retrieval is weak, use temperature 0, and check answers against sources with a second pass or a rule. Measure the rate on a labelled set, since each fix costs recall or latency. Fine-tuning on facts is not a fix, because the model still produces plausible text when it is unsure.
What makes the first token slow and later tokens fast, or the reverse? || Prefill processes the whole prompt in parallel and is compute-bound, so time to first token grows with prompt length. Decoding produces one token at a time and is memory-bound, because each step reads all the weights and the KV cache; per-token time is roughly model bytes divided by memory bandwidth, so an 8 billion parameter model in fp16 on a 3 TB/s GPU gives around 5 ms per token at batch size 1.
Why does a model sometimes get arithmetic or counting letters wrong? || It sees tokens, not characters or digits (bpe splits words and numbers into pieces), and it predicts the next token from patterns rather than running an exact algorithm. Giving it a calculator or code tool, or asking for step-by-step working (cot), fixes most cases; the right fix is to use a tool for exact work and the model for language.
@@+ llmserving
tradeoffs: Hosted API or self-hosted. Use a hosted API when volume is below roughly 1 to 5 million requests a month or traffic is spiky, because you pay only for tokens and need no GPU team. Self-host (vllm, sglang) when traffic is steady enough to keep GPUs above about 50 to 60% busy, when you need a fine-tuned or small model, or when data cannot leave your network; one H100 at about 3 to 4 dollars an hour serving an 8 billion parameter model can produce several thousand tokens a second, which is far below API prices at high utilization. You give up, when self-hosting, elasticity and operations time (on-call, upgrades, capacity); with an API, per-token cost at scale and control over latency and model versions.
Throughput or latency. A bigger batch (continuous batching) lifts tokens per second per GPU, often 5 to 20 times over batch size 1, but every request then waits behind more work, so inter-token time grows. Choose large batches and a high concurrency cap for offline jobs and summaries, where cost per token is the metric; choose a smaller cap, a priority queue and chunked prefill for chat, where time to first token under about 500 ms and inter-token time under 50 ms are the targets. Set the target from a service level objective, then size the fleet: you cannot have the lowest cost and the lowest p99 latency from the same GPUs.
Quantize or keep full precision. Quantizing weights to int8 or fp8 halves weight memory and about doubles decode speed at small batch, with usually under 1% loss on benchmarks; int4 (AWQ, GPTQ) quarters the memory, which lets a 70 billion parameter model fit on one 80 GB card with room for the cache, with a loss that is larger on reasoning and code. Quantizing the KV cache to fp8 doubles the number of concurrent requests. Choose fp8 by default on H100 and later; test int4 against your own evaluation set before using it; keep bf16 for the smallest models where memory is not the limit.
Speculative decoding and prefix caching. Speculative decoding (specdecode) gives 1.5 to 3 times faster decoding at low batch sizes when a small draft model agrees with the large one most of the time (code, structured output); at high batch sizes the GPU is already busy and the gain falls toward zero or negative. Prefix caching (promptcache) pays off when many requests share a long prefix (a system prompt, a document, an agent history), cutting prefill cost by 80 to 90% on a hit; it does nothing for unique prompts and uses memory that could hold more requests. Disaggregating prefill and decode onto separate GPU pools helps when prompts are long (over 4 thousand tokens) and the two phases fight; it adds KV transfer over the network and more moving parts.
interview: How would you size a GPU fleet for 2,000 requests per second of chat with 500 input and 300 output tokens? || Output dominates: 2,000 times 300 is 600 thousand output tokens a second. If one GPU does about 3 thousand decode tokens a second at the batch size that meets the latency target (for a 8 billion parameter model), that is about 200 GPUs, plus prefill (1 million input tokens a second, compute-bound) and 30 to 40% headroom for peaks and failures. I would measure real throughput at the latency target with a load test; the vendor's peak number is at an unrealistic batch size.
Latency is fine at low load but p99 explodes at high load. Why? || Requests queue once the batch or the KV cache is full, and the wait grows steeply as utilization approaches 100% (queueing delay rises like 1/(1-utilization)). Long prompts also stall decoding for others unless prefill is chunked. I would cap concurrency, apply admission control with a fast 429 instead of an unbounded queue, use chunked prefill, and autoscale on queue depth and KV cache use rather than CPU.
Why can't you just increase the batch size until the GPU is full? || The KV cache grows with batch size times sequence length: for a 70 billion parameter model with grouped-query attention it is roughly 300 KB per token, so 100 requests of 4 thousand tokens needs about 120 GB. Memory, not compute, usually sets the limit, and a larger batch also raises per-token latency. Paged attention reduces waste, and a quantized cache or fewer layers per GPU (more tensor parallelism) buys room.
When would you choose tensor parallelism over running more replicas of a smaller setup? || Use tensor parallelism when the model does not fit in one GPU, or when you need lower latency per token, since weights are split and each GPU reads less per step; it needs NVLink-class bandwidth, so keep it within one node (2 to 8 GPUs). When the model fits on one GPU, replicas are simpler and give the best throughput per dollar, because there is no per-layer communication. I would pick the smallest parallel degree that meets memory and latency targets.
How do you roll out a new model version safely? || Shadow traffic first to compare outputs and latency, then a canary of 1 to 5% watching quality metrics, error rate and cost per request, then a ramp with an instant rollback by routing. Keep the old version warm until the new one has served a full daily traffic cycle. Loading a large model takes minutes, so capacity for both versions must be planned, and prefix caches start empty after a switch.
@@+ rag
tradeoffs: Retrieval, long context or fine-tuning. Use retrieval when the knowledge base is bigger than about 200 thousand tokens, changes weekly or faster, differs by user (permissions), or needs citations. Use long context (llm) when the whole source is under about 100 thousand tokens, is one document, and you can pay for it on every call; with prompt caching the repeated cost falls by about 90%. Fine-tune (lora) for tone, format and domain skill, not for facts. You give up, with retrieval, a pipeline to build and keep fresh and the risk that the right passage is not found; with long context, cost per call and quality on facts buried in the middle; with fine-tuning, freshness and the ability to point at a source.
Dense vectors, keyword search or both. Dense embeddings (vectorsearch) find paraphrases and cross-language matches; BM25 (bm25) finds exact names, error codes, part numbers and rare words that vectors blur. Choose hybrid search (both lists merged by reciprocal rank fusion) for most production systems, because it commonly lifts recall at 10 by 5 to 15 points over either alone; choose vectors alone for short natural-language questions over clean text, and BM25 alone when the vocabulary is exact and the corpus is small. Hybrid costs two indexes and a merge step, roughly 10 to 30 ms more.
Chunk size and reranking. Small chunks (100 to 200 tokens) match precisely but lose context; large chunks (800 to 1,500 tokens) keep context but dilute the embedding and waste prompt tokens. A common start is 300 to 500 tokens with 10 to 15% overlap, split on headings, then tune on a labelled set of questions. A cross-encoder reranker over the top 50 candidates usually adds 5 to 10 points of precision at 5 for 50 to 200 ms extra latency; skip it when the latency budget is under 300 ms or when the first-stage ranking is already good enough in your evaluation.
Approximate index or exact search. Exact (flat) search over up to about 1 million vectors of 768 dimensions takes 5 to 30 ms on one machine and has perfect recall, so use it there, because an HNSW index (ann) adds memory (often 1.5 to 2 times the vectors), build time and tuning for little gain. Above 10 million vectors, use HNSW or IVF with product quantization and accept 95 to 99% recall, which means 1 to 5% of the true best passages are never retrieved, an error you cannot see unless you measure it.
interview: Your RAG answers are wrong. How do you find out whether retrieval or generation is at fault? || I split the pipeline: for a labelled set of questions, check whether the right passage is in the retrieved top k (recall at k). If not, fix retrieval: chunking, hybrid search, query rewriting, reranking. If the passage is there but the answer is still wrong, fix generation: prompt, passage order, fewer distractors, a stronger model, or a requirement to quote the source. Measuring the two stages separately avoids tuning the wrong one.
How would you handle per-user permissions in a RAG system? || Filter at retrieval time with metadata (access control lists attached to each chunk, applied as a pre-filter in the index), never by asking the model to hide things, since anything in the prompt can leak. Pre-filtering with restrictive filters can hurt recall in graph indexes, so I test it, and I partition by tenant when the number of tenants is small. I also remove deleted or changed documents from the index promptly.
Why not just stuff 1 million tokens into the prompt? || Cost and latency per call (a million tokens of input can cost several dollars and take tens of seconds without caching), weaker recall on details in the middle, no per-user access control, and no citation of which part was used. It is a good choice for a one-off analysis of a single large document, but not for a shared service with thousands of calls a day.
How do you keep the index fresh when documents change hourly? || Use change data capture or a modified-time scan to re-chunk and re-embed only changed documents, upsert by a stable chunk ID, and delete the old chunks of a changed document. Keep the embedding model version in the metadata, because switching models means re-embedding everything (for 100 million chunks that is a large batch job), so plan that as a blue-green index swap.
Your corpus has many near-duplicate documents and the top 5 are all the same. What do you do? || Deduplicate at ingestion (hash or minhash of the text), and at query time use maximal marginal relevance or group by source document so that the context holds different information. This costs a little recall for the most relevant item and gives more varied evidence in the same number of tokens.
@@+ agents
tradeoffs: Workflow or agent. Use a fixed workflow (a chain of prompts and code with set steps and branches) when the steps are known and the task repeats thousands of times: it is cheaper by 3 to 10 times, easier to test and gives repeatable latency. Use an agent loop (reactloop) when the path depends on what the tools return (debugging code, research, support cases with unknown causes) and a wrong step can be recovered by trying again. You give up, with a workflow, flexibility for odd cases; with an agent, predictability, cost control and the ability to say in advance what it will do.
One agent or several. A single agent with good tools and a clean context is the right start; it is simpler to debug because there is one transcript. Split into sub-agents when a task is wide and its parts are independent (search ten sources, review ten files), because each sub-agent has its own clean context and they run in parallel, or when different parts need different permissions. Multi-agent setups typically use 3 to 15 times more tokens and add coordination errors, so adopt them when a measured single-agent run fails from context overflow or from time, not because the design looks neat.
How much autonomy. For actions that can be undone (reading files, drafting, running tests in a sandbox) let the agent proceed. For actions that cannot (sending email, spending money, deleting data, deploying) require approval, or use a narrow tool with limits (maximum refund of 50 dollars, only a staging environment). Approval at each step makes the agent slow and trains people to click yes without reading; no approval means one prompt-injected web page can cause real harm (adversarial). Put the guard in the tool and the permission system, not in the prompt.
Tool design and context. Few, well-named tools with clear descriptions beat 50 overlapping ones: accuracy of tool choice drops as the list grows past about 20, and each definition costs prompt tokens. Return short, structured results (the 20 relevant lines of a log, not 5 MB), because every tool output stays in the context and is paid for on every later turn unless prompt caching applies. Summarize or drop old observations after about 30 to 50 turns, at the cost of losing detail the agent may later need, so keep a notes file or memory it can re-read.
interview: An agent works in demos and fails one time in five in production. How do you make it reliable? || First I build an evaluation set of real tasks with automatic checks (tests pass, the record has the right fields) and run each 5 to 10 times to measure the success rate, since one run proves nothing. Then I read failed transcripts and group the causes: wrong tool choice, bad arguments, lost context, looping. I fix with clearer tool descriptions, validation and good error messages in tools, smaller steps, and fixed workflow parts where the path is known. Each fix is judged by the rate on the set, not by one example.
How do you stop an agent from looping or running up cost? || Set hard limits on steps, tokens, time and money per task, detect repeated identical tool calls, and make the agent state a plan and check progress against it. When a limit is reached it must stop with a clear partial result, not retry silently. I log cost per task, and alert on the 99th percentile, because a few runaway tasks can be most of the bill.
What does prompt injection mean for an agent that reads web pages and email? || Any text the agent reads can contain instructions, and the model cannot reliably separate data from commands. So I assume it will sometimes follow them. The defense is design: least-privilege tools, no single session holding both private data and an outbound channel, confirmation for risky actions, output filtering, and sandboxing. The "lethal trio" to avoid together is private data, untrusted content and the ability to send data out.
Why is a long agent session slow and costly, and what helps? || Each turn resends the whole history; without caching, cost grows with the square of the number of turns, and the context fills with old tool output. Prefix caching makes the repeated part about 10 times cheaper and faster if the prefix stays byte-identical (so do not edit earlier messages). Compaction, sub-agents for noisy subtasks and returning short tool results keep the context small.
How do you test an agent that changes behaviour when the model changes? || Keep a regression set of tasks with outcome checks, replay recorded tool responses where possible so tests are deterministic, run each case several times, and compare success rate and cost between the old and new model before switching. Pin model versions in production, and roll out with a canary.
@@+ rl
tradeoffs: Reinforcement learning or supervised learning. Use supervised learning (ml) when you have correct answers or good demonstrations, which is the case in most business problems; it is stable, cheap and needs no simulator. Use RL when the right label is unknown but you can score the outcome (a game result, a verified math answer, a passing test, a long-run business metric) and the learner's actions change the data it sees. You give up, with RL, sample efficiency (it often needs millions of episodes where supervised learning needs thousands of labels), stable training and easy debugging; with supervised learning, the ability to find strategies better than the examples.
Value-based or policy-gradient methods. Choose Q-learning or DQN (qlearning) when actions are a small discrete set (up to a few hundred), you can reuse old data from a replay buffer, and sample efficiency matters. Choose policy gradients such as PPO or GRPO (policygrad) when actions are continuous or huge (a token from a 100 thousand word vocabulary, robot joint torques), or when you need a stochastic policy; they use fresh data, so they cost more samples but are the base of RLHF and reasoning-model training. PPO needs a value model and about 2 times the memory; GRPO drops the value model by comparing a group of samples per prompt, which saves memory but needs 4 to 16 samples per prompt.
Model-free or model-based, and search. When a simulator is cheap and exact (games, code execution), model-free RL with billions of steps works, and search (mcts) on top gives large gains at inference time, at the price of 10 to 1,000 times more compute per decision. When real interaction is costly (robots, ads, patients), learn a model of the world or use offline RL from logs; errors in the learned model compound over long horizons, so keep model rollouts short (5 to 20 steps).
RL or a bandit. If the action does not change the future state (choose which of 10 banners to show), a contextual bandit (bandits) is simpler, learns in days from thousands of events and has no credit-assignment problem. If today's action changes tomorrow's state (a recommendation changes what the user likes, an inventory choice changes stock), you need RL, but start with a bandit plus a delayed-reward proxy and move only if the measured long-run gain justifies the complexity.
interview: Why is the reward function the hardest part of an RL project? || The agent optimizes exactly what you score, and finds loopholes (reward hacking): a game agent loops for points, a language model learns to please the grader. I check the reward by watching high-reward trajectories by hand, add penalties and constraints, use a held-out evaluator the policy was not trained against, and keep a KL penalty to a reference policy in RLHF so it does not drift to odd outputs. A reward that is easy to compute is often a bad proxy.
Training curves are noisy and the same code gives different results on different seeds. What do you do? || RL variance is high, so I run at least 5 seeds and report the mean and spread, not the best run. I check implementation details that matter (advantage normalization, learning rate, entropy bonus, clipping), use larger batches, and compare against a known baseline on a simple environment first. A gain smaller than the seed spread is not a gain.
When would you use offline RL or imitation learning instead of online RL? || When exploration is unsafe or costly, and you have logged data from a decent behaviour policy: start with imitation (behaviour cloning) as a baseline, which is stable, then try offline RL (conservative methods) to beat the logs. The risk is distribution shift: the learned policy takes actions the logs never covered, so value estimates are wrong exactly there. I would evaluate with off-policy estimates and then a small online test.
How is RL used in post-training of LLMs and how does it differ from classic RL? || The policy is the language model, an episode is a full response, and the reward is a learned preference model (RLHF) or a verifier (math answer, unit tests). There is usually no environment state beyond the prompt, so it behaves like a bandit with huge action sequences; a KL term keeps the model near its starting point. Verifiable rewards are less prone to hacking than learned ones, but cover only domains where answers can be checked.
How would you use RL for a recommender, and what would you worry about? || Treat a session as an episode with reward as long-run engagement or retention, and train from logs with off-policy corrections because the logging policy was different. I would worry about feedback loops (the model creates the data it learns from), a delayed and noisy reward, and harm to users when the proxy diverges from long-term value. I would start with a bandit and exploration limits, and measure with long-running holdout groups.
@@+ recsys
tradeoffs: Collaborative filtering or content-based. Choose collaborative filtering (matfact, embeddings learned from interactions) when you have at least a few interactions per item and user, in the order of 10 or more, because it finds taste patterns no metadata describes. Choose content-based models (item text, image or attribute embeddings) for new items and new users (cold start), for small catalogs and for domains where the item's properties explain the choice (jobs, news by topic). Most production systems use both: content features feed a model trained on interactions. You give up, with pure collaborative filtering, anything new; with pure content, serendipity and the signal of what people like beyond the description.
Matrix factorization or a deep two-tower model. Matrix factorization (ALS or SGD, 64 to 256 factors) trains in minutes to hours on a Spark cluster, is easy to explain, and works for 10 million users by 1 million items; choose it as the first model and as a strong baseline. Choose a two-tower deep model when you have rich features (context, device, time, text) and billions of events: the towers produce user and item vectors, items are indexed for nearest-neighbour search (ann), and the user tower runs online. You give up simplicity and cheap retraining, and gain features and a path to a ranker; the gain over a well-tuned factorization is often only a few percent until feature richness grows.
Retrieval and ranking stages. A catalog of 100 million items cannot be scored by a heavy model per request. Use two or three stages: candidate generation (several sources, each returning hundreds to a thousand items in under 20 ms with ANN), then a ranker with many features on 100 to 500 candidates (10 to 50 ms), then re-ranking for diversity, business rules and freshness. A single-stage ranker is fine only up to roughly 10 thousand items. Each extra stage adds latency and a place where recall is lost: an item the retrieval stage never returns can never be shown, however good the ranker is.
Optimize clicks or something deeper. Click-through rate is available within minutes and trains well, but it rewards clickbait; watch time, purchase, return rate or a survey-based satisfaction signal match value better and arrive slowly or sparsely. Use a multi-task model with several heads (click, dwell, purchase) combined with weights tuned by A/B tests. Explore with a bandit (bandits) for 1 to 5% of traffic to find new items, at the price of a short-term drop in the immediate metric; without exploration the system shows only what it already believes.
interview: Offline NDCG improved but the live A/B test is flat. What could be wrong? || Offline data is biased by what the old system chose to show (exposure bias), so a new model that surfaces unseen items is penalized offline or rewarded for copying the old ranking. Other causes: a metric that is not the live goal, leakage of future data into features, training-serving skew in feature values, or latency timeouts that drop results. I would use counterfactual evaluation with logged propensities and compare feature distributions online and offline.
How do you handle a new item with no history on day one? || Use content embeddings and metadata to place it near similar items, boost it in a small exploration slice (a bandit gives it a number of impressions until its estimate is reliable, for example 1,000), and use the creator's or the category's average as a prior. I limit the exploration share so that bad new items do not harm the main experience.
Why do recommenders create filter bubbles and how would you counter it? || The model learns from what it showed, so popular and familiar items get more data and more exposure, a feedback loop. I add exploration, diversity terms in re-ranking, calibrated recommendations (match the user's mix of interests), and measure long-term retention and catalog coverage, not only clicks. It costs some short-term click rate, which has to be agreed with the business.
How would you serve recommendations for 50 million users with a 100 ms budget? || Precompute what can be precomputed: item embeddings and ANN indexes updated hourly, user features in a low-latency store (a feature store), and per-user candidate lists for the heaviest users. At request time: fetch features (5 to 10 ms), retrieve candidates in parallel from a few sources (10 to 20 ms), rank in a batch on CPU or GPU (20 to 40 ms), and re-rank. Fall back to a cached or popular list if a stage times out.
When would you choose a bandit over a full recommender model? || When the choice set is small (up to hundreds), items change quickly (headlines, banners, offers), and you need to learn within hours from limited data. A bandit adapts quickly and handles exploration directly. For a large catalog with long-lived items and rich user history, a model trained on logs gives better personalization, with a bandit layered on top for exploration.
@@+ vectorsearch
tradeoffs: Exact or approximate search. Use exact (flat, brute-force) search up to about 1 million vectors of 768 dimensions: it takes 5 to 30 ms on one machine with SIMD or a GPU, has 100% recall and needs no tuning. Use an approximate index (ann) above about 5 to 10 million vectors or when you need under 10 ms at high query rates. You give up, with approximation, a few percent of the true neighbours (recall 95 to 99% at the usual settings), extra memory for the graph and a build step; with exact search, speed at scale and a per-query cost that grows linearly with the number of vectors.
HNSW, IVF or a disk index. HNSW (graph) gives the best recall and latency per query (often under 5 ms at 95 to 99% recall) but keeps all vectors and a graph (about 16 to 64 links per vector) in RAM, so 100 million vectors of 768 float32 dimensions needs around 300 GB plus the graph. IVF with product quantization (quantization, clustering) stores a vector in 32 to 64 bytes, so 1 billion vectors fit in about 64 GB, at a recall of 80 to 95% that needs a re-score with the original vectors. Disk-based indexes (DiskANN style) keep compressed vectors in RAM and full ones on NVMe, with 5 to 20 ms latency at low cost. Choose HNSW when RAM is cheap relative to the value of speed, IVF-PQ when memory is the limit, disk graphs for very large and cost-driven.
Filters. Many queries combine similarity with conditions (tenant, language, date, access rights). Pre-filtering (restrict first, then search) is exact on the filter but is slow in a graph if the filter is very selective, and can break graph connectivity; post-filtering (search, then drop) is fast but returns fewer than k results when the filter keeps under about 10% of the data. Good engines switch strategy by selectivity, and for large tenants a separate index per tenant is simplest. If most queries have a highly selective filter, consider a database or search engine with the filter first and brute-force vector scoring on the small remainder.
Where to run it. A library (FAISS, hnswlib) inside your service is the cheapest and fastest for one dataset that fits on a machine and changes rarely. A vector extension on the database you already run (pgvector, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch) is the right start up to a few million vectors, because one system holds the metadata, the transactions and the vectors; pgvector's HNSW works well to a few million rows, and after that the index build time and memory pressure grow. A dedicated vector database (Milvus, Pinecone) is justified at tens of millions of vectors, high write rates or many tenants, at the price of a new system to run and keep consistent with your source of truth.
interview: How do you measure whether your ANN index is good enough? || Build a ground truth for 1,000 to 10,000 sample queries with exact search, then measure recall at k and latency percentiles at several settings (ef_search for HNSW, nprobe for IVF), and plot recall against latency. I pick the cheapest setting that meets the recall target, and also check recall on filtered queries, which is often worse. Finally I check that retrieval recall translates into the downstream metric, because 95% recall of a poor embedding is still poor.
Your recall drops after a large batch of inserts. Why? || HNSW graph quality degrades if inserts are made with low construction effort or when many deletions leave dead links; IVF centroids were trained on old data, so new data falls into poorly balanced lists. I would rebuild or retrain on a recent sample on a schedule, raise efConstruction, and monitor recall on a fixed query set so the drop is visible.
Why is cosine similarity often used, and when do you normalize? || Embedding directions carry meaning and lengths often do not, so cosine ignores length. If you normalize vectors to unit length at write time, dot product equals cosine and is faster, and L2 distance gives the same ranking. Check what your embedding model was trained for, and use the same metric in the index.
How would you fit 1 billion vectors on a budget? || Reduce dimensions (for example 1024 to 256 with Matryoshka-trained embeddings, or PCA), use product quantization or binary quantization (32 times smaller, with a re-score of the top 100 to 1,000 by full vectors), shard across machines by ID range with scatter-gather queries, and keep full vectors on SSD. Each step lowers recall a little, so I verify with the exact-search ground truth.
When is vector search the wrong tool? || When queries are exact (IDs, codes, names), when the vocabulary is controlled and BM25 works, when you need all matches rather than top k, or when the corpus is a few thousand items that a brute-force scan handles. Often the best result is hybrid: keyword and vector lists merged, so that you cover both exact tokens and meaning.
@@+ gpucomp
tradeoffs: GPU or CPU. Choose a GPU when the work is dense, regular and parallel: matrix multiplies, convolutions, large batches, with arithmetic intensity (roofline) above about 50 to 100 floating-point operations per byte, where an H100 gives close to 1,000 TFLOPS in bf16 against about 1 to 3 TFLOPS for a server CPU. Choose a CPU for branching, pointer-chasing, small batches, low-latency single requests and anything dominated by memory capacity (a CPU server holds 1 to 4 TB of RAM; an H100 holds 80 GB). The crossover for moving data is real: sending data over PCIe at 32 to 64 GB/s costs more than the compute unless you do enough work per byte moved. You give up, with a GPU, flexibility, easy debugging and often cost when it sits idle; with a CPU, throughput on matrix work by 100 times.
Which accelerator. NVIDIA GPUs (nvgpu) with CUDA have the widest software support and are the safe choice for research and mixed workloads; choose them when your team uses PyTorch extensions and custom kernels. TPUs are strong for large, regular training and serving jobs on Google Cloud when the model is written in JAX or well-supported PyTorch/XLA, often at lower cost per FLOP at big scale. AMD Instinct (amdgpu) offers more memory per card (192 GB or more on recent parts) and a lower price, with a software stack that needs more testing. You give up portability when you write custom CUDA, and ecosystem maturity when you pick the alternatives.
Precision. Use bf16 for training by default (same range as fp32, half the memory, tensor-core speed); use fp8 on H100 and later for 1.5 to 2 times more throughput when your recipe is validated, with scaling to avoid overflow; use int8 or int4 for inference. Lower precision doubles speed and memory capacity but risks accuracy and instability, so each step needs an evaluation. fp32 remains for accumulations, optimizer state and numerically sensitive code like some scientific kernels.
Write a kernel or use a library and a compiler. Use cuBLAS, cuDNN, FlashAttention and torch.compile (mlcompilers) first: they reach 70 to 90% of peak for standard operations. Write a custom kernel (Triton or CUDA) when profiling shows that a fused sequence of small operations is memory-bound and dominates the step time, or when a new operation has no library version; Triton is faster to write and usually reaches 80 to 95% of hand-tuned CUDA. Custom CUDA gives the last 10% and costs weeks of expert work plus maintenance with each new architecture.
Buy, rent or reserve. For steady use above about 60 to 70% of the time for a year or more, owning or reserving capacity is cheaper (cloud on-demand H100 is often 2 to 4 dollars an hour, and reserved prices can be half). For experiments and bursts, rent on demand or spot, accepting preemption, and checkpoint often. A GPU cluster idle half the time costs twice as much per useful hour as the price list suggests, so utilization is the number to manage.
interview: Your GPU shows 40% utilization in nvidia-smi. How do you find the problem? || Utilization there only means a kernel was running, not that it ran well. I profile one step (PyTorch profiler, Nsight Systems) and look for gaps between kernels (data loading, Python overhead, CPU-side launches, synchronization), many tiny kernels (fuse them or use CUDA graphs), memory-bound kernels (roofline), and small batches. Typical fixes: more data loader workers, pinned memory, a larger batch, torch.compile, and overlapping transfers with compute.
Why is LLM decoding slow on a very powerful GPU? || At batch size 1 each token needs all the weights read from memory once, about 2 FLOPs per weight but 2 bytes read per weight, so arithmetic intensity is about 1 and the GPU waits on memory bandwidth (about 3 TB/s on H100), not compute. A 70 billion parameter model in fp16 is 140 GB, so each token takes around 50 ms even with perfect use. Batching many requests, quantization and speculative decoding raise the work per byte.
What does the roofline tell you before you optimize? || It plots attainable performance against arithmetic intensity: below the ridge point (around 300 FLOPs per byte for H100 bf16) you are limited by memory bandwidth, above by compute. So for a memory-bound kernel, extra FLOPs do not help; reducing bytes (fusion, lower precision, caching in shared memory) does. It tells me which kind of optimization is worth trying first and what the upper bound is.
When does adding more GPUs stop helping? || When communication or serial parts dominate (Amdahl): all-reduce time on slow interconnect, a data pipeline that cannot feed them, or batches per GPU that become too small. I measure scaling efficiency from 8 to 64 GPUs, and the cause is usually network bandwidth between nodes (400 Gb/s InfiniBand against 900 GB/s NVLink inside a node), so I keep the heaviest communication inside the node.
How would you decide between one big GPU instance and many small ones for inference? || If the model fits on one GPU, many small independent replicas give the best throughput per dollar and simple failure handling. Use a bigger multi-GPU instance only when the model needs sharding for memory or when tensor parallelism cuts latency enough to meet the target. Also consider multi-instance GPU (MIG) slicing to pack small models onto one card, when each needs under 10 GB and moderate throughput.
@@+ langruntime
tradeoffs: Ahead-of-time compiled, JIT-compiled or interpreted. Choose ahead-of-time (Rust, C++, Go) when startup time under 10 ms, a small memory footprint (a Go service often runs in 20 to 50 MB), predictable latency or a single static binary matter: command-line tools, sidecars, functions, databases. Choose a JIT runtime (JVM, V8) for long-running servers, where the JIT uses runtime profiles to inline and specialize and can match or pass AOT code after a warm-up of seconds to minutes, at the price of 200 MB or more of memory and a slow first requests. Choose an interpreter (Python, Ruby) when development speed and libraries matter more than speed: pure Python is 10 to 100 times slower than compiled code on tight loops, so keep hot loops in native libraries (NumPy, Arrow).
Garbage collection or manual and ownership-based memory. A garbage collector (gc, concgc) removes whole classes of bugs and makes code faster to write; modern collectors (ZGC, Shenandoah, Go's collector) keep pauses under 1 to 10 ms on heaps of many gigabytes, at the cost of 10 to 30% throughput and often 2 times the live heap as headroom. Choose Rust's ownership or C++ RAII when you need a hard bound on tail latency, a tight memory budget or no runtime inside a library, and accept a steeper learning curve (borrow-checker errors in the first months) and slower development. Choose the GC language when the team ships features and the p99 latency target is above about 10 ms.
Static or dynamic types. Static types (typesys) catch whole classes of errors before the code runs, make refactoring across a codebase of hundreds of thousands of lines safe, and give the compiler information to optimize. Dynamic typing gives short scripts and fast exploration. A middle path is gradual typing (TypeScript, Python type hints with mypy) that you introduce as a code base grows; the cost is that annotations can lie at boundaries, so validate input at the edges. Choose static typing for anything that will be maintained by more than a few people for more than a year.
Concurrency model. Native threads with shared memory (Java, C++) give the most control and use all cores, at the cost of data races and locks. Goroutines or virtual threads (Go, Java 21) allow hundreds of thousands of blocked tasks cheaply (a goroutine begins at about 2 KB of stack against 1 MB or more for an OS thread), and fit I/O-heavy servers. Async/await (Rust, Node, Python asyncio) runs on few threads with cooperative scheduling and is efficient for I/O, but one blocking call or one CPU-heavy task stalls everything on that thread. Python's global interpreter lock limits threads in CPU-bound code, so use processes or native extensions for CPU work.
interview: A JVM service has good average latency but a p99 spike every few minutes. What do you check? || Garbage collection pauses first: read the GC log for pause times and frequency, check allocation rate and heap sizing, and whether the old generation fills. Then JIT deoptimization or compilation storms after a deploy, safepoint pauses (long loops, biased locks), and noisy neighbours or CPU throttling in containers. Fixes range from tuning heap size or switching to G1/ZGC to cutting allocation in hot paths and warming up before taking traffic.
Why is the first request after a deploy slow, and how do you handle it in autoscaling? || The code is still interpreted, classes are loading, and caches and connection pools are cold; the JIT has not yet compiled the hot methods. I warm up with synthetic traffic or a readiness check that runs representative requests, keep new instances out of the load balancer until warm, ramp traffic gradually (slow start), or use AOT options (GraalVM native image, class data sharing, snapshots) where startup time dominates, as in functions.
When would you rewrite a hot path in Rust or C++ instead of staying in Python or Java? || Only after a profile shows a small part of the code uses most of the time and the cost is CPU, not I/O. I try first: better algorithms, vectorized libraries, a faster runtime option, or a native library call. A rewrite is justified when it replaces, say, 20 servers by 2, or removes a latency bound that cannot be tuned away; I weigh that against build complexity, hiring and the foreign function interface overhead.
How does a JIT decide what to compile, and what can make it slower? || It counts method calls and loop iterations, compiles hot ones in tiers (a quick compile first, an optimizing compile later) using type profiles collected while running, and inlines based on that. When an assumption fails (a new class is loaded, a different type shows up), it deoptimizes back to the interpreter and recompiles. Megamorphic call sites, huge methods and code that changes behaviour over time hurt it.
What would make you choose WebAssembly? || When I need to run untrusted or third-party code in a sandbox with near-native speed and fast start (microseconds to milliseconds), such as plugins, edge functions, or the same library in a browser and on a server. It gives up direct system access (needs WASI), mature threading and debugging, and garbage-collected language support is still younger, so it is a fit for compute kernels and isolation, not for whole applications.
@@+ replication
tradeoffs: Leader-follower, multi-leader or leaderless. Choose single-leader replication (PostgreSQL, MySQL, Kafka partitions) for most systems: one place decides the write order, so conflicts cannot happen, and reads can scale across followers. Choose multi-leader when each region must accept writes locally with low latency and you can resolve conflicts (calendars, collaborative documents, offline-first apps); you get 10 to 100 ms local writes instead of waiting 100 to 200 ms on a cross-ocean round trip. Choose leaderless with quorums (Cassandra, DynamoDB) when you need writes to stay available through node failures and can tolerate stale reads and conflict resolution (last-write-wins or CRDTs). You give up, with a single leader, write availability during failover (seconds to a minute) and write scale; with the others, simple reasoning about the latest value.
Synchronous or asynchronous copying. Synchronous replication to one follower in another zone (about 1 to 2 ms extra) means a committed write survives the loss of the leader, so choose it for payments, ledgers and metadata where losing a committed write is not acceptable; you give up write availability (if the follower is down, writes stop or must degrade) and add its latency to every commit. Asynchronous replication keeps commit latency at the leader's local time and survives follower outages, but a leader crash can lose the last few hundred milliseconds to seconds of writes. A common middle is semi-synchronous: wait for one of several followers, or use consensus (raft) across 3 or 5 nodes, which gives durability and availability with a majority.
How many replicas and where. Three replicas tolerate one failure and are the standard for a datacenter; five tolerate two and suit critical metadata that must survive a zone outage during maintenance. Spread replicas across zones (about 1 ms apart) for availability at low cost, and across regions (50 to 200 ms) only for disaster recovery or global reads, because quorum writes across regions then cost the round-trip time. Extra replicas multiply storage and write bandwidth, so 3 copies of 100 TB is 300 TB, and for cold data erasure coding (for example 10 data plus 4 parity fragments, 1.4 times overhead) gives similar durability at less than half the storage, with slower repair and reads.
Read consistency. Read from the leader (or a quorum, with R + W > N from quorum settings) when a user must see their own write or a decision depends on the latest value; read from followers or the nearest replica for dashboards, search results and feeds, where seconds of staleness are fine. Cheap intermediate guarantees (read your writes using a session token, monotonic reads by pinning a user to one replica) fix the confusing cases without sending every read to the leader. You give up leader capacity and latency for stronger reads, and correctness for the weaker ones.
interview: A follower is lagging by 30 seconds. What are the risks and what do you do? || Users reading from it see old data, and if the leader fails now, promoting this follower loses up to 30 seconds of writes. I find the cause: a single-threaded apply on the follower falling behind a busy leader, long queries blocking apply, a slow disk or network, or a huge transaction. I take it out of the read pool, check that failover will not choose it, and fix the cause (parallel apply, move heavy queries away, bigger instance).
How does failover go wrong with asynchronous replication? || The old leader may have accepted writes that never reached the new leader, so those writes are lost or conflict when the old node returns; two nodes may both believe they are leader (split brain) and accept writes. Safe failover uses a lease or fencing token so the old leader cannot write, a quorum-based election, and a rule to discard or reconcile the old leader's extra writes. Automation should wait long enough to avoid failing over for a short network blip.
What does quorum with N = 3, W = 2, R = 2 give you and what does it not? || Any read set overlaps any write set in at least one node, so a read sees the latest acknowledged write if that node's value is picked by version. It tolerates one node down for both reads and writes. It does not give linearizability by itself: sloppy quorums, concurrent writes, failed partial writes and clock-based last-write-wins can still return stale or lost data, so you need version vectors, read repair and care.
How do you handle conflicts in multi-region writes? || First avoid them: route each user or key to a home region so most keys have one writer. When concurrent writes are unavoidable, use last-write-wins only if losing an update is acceptable, otherwise use CRDTs (counters, sets), application-level merge, or keep the conflicting versions and ask the application or user to resolve. I decide per data type, since carts and counters can merge but balances cannot.
Why are replicas not backups? || A replica copies every change, including a mistaken DELETE or a corrupted write, within milliseconds. Backups and point-in-time recovery (snapshots plus the log) let you go back to before the mistake, and a delayed replica (for example 1 hour behind) gives a quick safety net. I keep both and test restores, as an untested backup is a guess.
@@+ partitioning
tradeoffs: Hash or range partitioning. Choose hash partitioning (hash of the key) when the access pattern is point lookups and you need an even spread of load, which is the case for user IDs, session stores and key-value workloads (DynamoDB, Cassandra, Kafka keys); a good hash makes hot spots from key patterns disappear. Choose range partitioning when queries scan contiguous keys (a time range, a prefix, an alphabetical range) as in Bigtable, HBase, Spanner and warehouses, where partition pruning means a one-day query touches 1 of 365 partitions. You give up, with hash, efficient range scans (every partition must be asked); with range, an even load, since sequential keys like timestamps send all writes to the last partition, so add a prefix such as a bucket number, or hash a leading part of the key.
Number of partitions and rebalancing. Fixed partitions created up front (for example 256 or 1,024, many more than nodes, as in Kafka topics, Elasticsearch shards and Redis Cluster's 16,384 slots) make rebalancing a matter of moving whole partitions and keep the mapping simple; choose it when you can estimate the maximum size. The cost is that too few partitions cap parallelism and size per partition, and too many add overhead: each Kafka partition adds file handles and replication work, and an Elasticsearch shard is best kept around 10 to 50 GB. Consistent hashing (conshash) with virtual nodes moves only about 1/N of the keys when a node is added, which suits caches and systems with frequent membership changes, at the price of trickier load balancing. Dynamic splitting (Bigtable, Spanner, CockroachDB) splits a range when it passes a size or load threshold, giving automatic balance with a metadata service to maintain.
Partition key choice. A good key has high cardinality (millions of values), spreads reads and writes evenly, and appears in most queries, so each request goes to one partition. User ID is typical; a country code, a boolean or a date alone is a bad choice. For skewed data (powerlaws: the top 1% of keys often carry 20 to 50% of traffic), add a salt to hot keys (key plus a random suffix 0 to 9, reading by fan-out to 10 places), cache them, or give them dedicated partitions. You give up simple point reads for the hot keys, in return for balanced load.
Local or global secondary indexes. A local index (each partition indexes only its own rows) makes writes cheap and atomic with the row, but a query by the secondary attribute must be sent to every partition and merged, so latency is set by the slowest of N shards (tail latency grows with fan-out). A global index partitioned by the indexed value answers a query from one partition, but a write must update another partition, which is asynchronous and eventually consistent or needs a distributed transaction. Choose local for analytics and low write cost; choose global for high-rate lookups by a secondary key where reading is much more frequent than writing.
interview: One partition is at 95% CPU while others are at 10%. What do you do? || First confirm it is skew and see which keys are hot (sample the key distribution or use the system's hot-key metrics). If it is one celebrity key, cache it or split it with a salt; if the key design sends sequential values to one range, add a hash prefix or bucket; if one partition is just larger, split it or rebalance. A longer-term fix is a better key or a layer that caps traffic per key. Adding nodes alone does not help if one key is the problem.
How would you choose the number of partitions for a new Kafka topic? || Take the peak throughput target and divide by a measured per-partition rate (for example 10 MB/s per partition on the producer side), and also take the number of consumers in the largest group, since parallelism is capped by the partition count. Add headroom of 2 to 3 times for growth, because increasing partitions later changes key-to-partition mapping and breaks ordering for existing keys. Avoid thousands per topic without need, as it slows leader election and recovery.
What breaks when you need a transaction or a join across partitions? || Single-partition operations are fast and simple, but cross-partition ones need two-phase commit or sagas (outbox), which add latency (extra round trips), lower availability and complexity, and joins need shuffling data or fan-out. I design keys so that data used together lives together (co-partition orders with their customer), denormalize where needed, and accept eventual consistency for the rest.
You must add shards to a database holding 10 TB with no downtime. How? || Pick the new layout (split ranges or add virtual shards), copy data in the background while capturing changes (CDC), verify counts and checksums, then cut over reads and writes per shard with a short write pause or dual writes, keeping the old data until verified. The mapping must be in a versioned routing table so clients move atomically. Throttle the copy so production traffic is not hurt, and have a rollback plan.
Why not hash(key) mod N for assigning partitions? || When N changes, almost all keys map to a different node (about (N-1)/N of them move), causing a cache stampede or a huge data move. Consistent hashing, rendezvous hashing or a fixed large number of logical partitions mapped to nodes limit movement to about 1/N of the keys. Mod N is fine only when N never changes.
