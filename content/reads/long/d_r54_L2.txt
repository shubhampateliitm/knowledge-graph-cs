@@ physsim
value: A physics engine answers one question many times per second: given where every object is and how fast it moves, where is every object a short time later, and which of them are touching? It repeats this in small steps, usually 1/60 of a second or smaller. In every step it does two jobs. First, collision detection finds which pairs of bodies overlap and where. Second, dynamics changes velocities and positions so that bodies obey Newton's laws and do not pass through each other.
The costs are what make this a technique and not just a formula. A scene with 1,000 bodies has 499,500 possible pairs, so testing every pair is far too slow. The engine first throws away most pairs with cheap bounding boxes (the broad phase) and then runs exact geometry only on the few survivors (the narrow phase). The contacts are then solved together, because a box resting on another box resting on the floor is one connected problem, not three independent ones.
For the layer above, physics engines are the base of game worlds, film effects, CAD motion checks and, now, robot learning. Robotics trains controllers inside MuJoCo, Isaac Sim and similar simulators, because a policy can be tried millions of times in simulation before it touches a real arm. Graphics and games use PhysX, Havok, Bullet and Box2D so that crates fall and ragdolls collapse in a believable way. The layers below make it work: Newtonian rigid-body dynamics (L5) gives the equations, differential equations (L4) say what "advance by one step" means, numerical analysis (L4) decides whether the answer is stable, and spatial indexes (L2) make the broad phase fast.
terms: Rigid body — an object that never bends; its state is a position, an orientation, a linear velocity and an angular velocity.
Time step (dt) — the small slice of time, such as 1/60 s, advanced in one iteration of the simulation loop.
Broad phase — a cheap first pass that lists pairs of bodies whose bounding boxes overlap, so exact tests run on few pairs.
Narrow phase — the exact geometric test for one pair; it returns a contact normal, a penetration depth and contact points.
Contact manifold — the small set of contact points (up to 4 in 3D) between two touching convex shapes.
Constraint — a rule that velocities must satisfy, for example "the two bodies must not approach each other along the normal".
Impulse — force multiplied by time (units N·s); it changes velocity instantly, by impulse divided by mass.
Tunneling — a fast, thin object passes through a wall because it was on one side in one step and on the other side in the next.
history: Simulating motion on a computer is older than graphics. Loup Verlet used a position-based integrator for molecular dynamics in 1967, and variants of it are still used. For rigid bodies with contact, the key early works came from David Baraff, whose 1989 and 1994 SIGGRAPH papers treated non-penetration as a mathematical constraint problem (a linear complementarity problem, LCP) solved each step. His Pixar course notes with Andrew Witkin, "Physically Based Modeling", taught a generation of graphics programmers.
Collision detection grew in parallel. Gilbert, Johnson and Keerthi published GJK in 1988 for the distance between convex shapes, and bounding-volume hierarchies and sweep-and-prune made large scenes practical in the 1990s.
Games forced a change of style in the 2000s. Exact LCP solvers were too slow and too fragile, so Erin Catto's Box2D (first released in the mid 2000s, with his GDC talks on "sequential impulses") used a simple iterative solver that is cheap, tolerates an unfinished answer, and reuses last frame's impulses. Bullet (Erwin Coumans) and the commercial engines Havok and PhysX took the same direction. Ageia introduced PhysX hardware in 2004; NVIDIA bought Ageia in 2008, and open-sourced the PhysX SDK in 2018 under a BSD licence.
Two more branches matter now. Position Based Dynamics (Müller and colleagues, 2007) moves points directly to satisfy constraints and became the base for cloth, soft bodies and fluids; its extension XPBD (Macklin, Müller and Chentanez, 2016) made stiffness independent of time step. And robotics wanted accuracy over looks: Emanuel Todorov's MuJoCo (2012) solved contacts as a convex optimization problem, was bought by DeepMind in 2021 and open-sourced in 2022. NVIDIA's Isaac Sim builds on PhysX and runs thousands of robots in parallel on a GPU.
how: Every engine runs the same loop. For each step of size dt: (1) apply external forces such as gravity; (2) find pairs that may touch; (3) compute exact contacts; (4) solve for velocities that respect the contacts; (5) move the bodies with the new velocities. Here is each stage and what it stands on.
Step 1: Dynamics from Newton (rigid-body dynamics, L5). A body has mass \( m \), a position \( x \) and a velocity \( v \). Newton's second law gives \( \dot v = F/m \) and \( \dot x = v \). For rotation, the body also has an orientation (a quaternion), an angular velocity \( \omega \) and an inertia tensor \( I \), and a torque \( \tau \) changes it by \( I\dot\omega = \tau - \omega \times I\omega \). The second term is why a spinning book tumbles in a way a point mass never would. This is the whole physical model; everything else is how to compute it fast and without breaking.
Step 2: Integration (differential equations and numerical analysis, L4). The equations \( \dot x = v,\ \dot v = F/m \) form an ordinary differential equation. A computer cannot solve it continuously; it advances in steps. Explicit Euler uses the old velocity for both updates and gains energy on springs. Almost every game and robotics engine instead uses semi-implicit (symplectic) Euler: first update the velocity, then use the new velocity to update the position.
```text
v = v + (F / m) * dt
x = x + v * dt
```
This order keeps energy bounded for oscillating systems, which is why bodies do not slowly explode. Stiff constraints still need small steps or implicit methods; numerical analysis tells you how large dt may be (see the example).
Step 3: Broad phase (spatial indexes, L2). Testing all pairs costs \( O(n^2) \). Engines keep each body's axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) in a spatial structure. Common choices are a uniform grid (good when bodies are similar in size), a dynamic bounding volume tree such as the one in Box2D and Bullet (good for mixed sizes and for queries), and sweep-and-prune (sort the box intervals along an axis and report overlaps; because bodies move little between steps, the sorted order is almost sorted already, so re-sorting is nearly linear). The output is a list of candidate pairs, typically a few times the number of real contacts.
Step 4: Narrow phase. For each candidate pair, exact geometry decides. For two convex shapes, GJK repeatedly builds a small simplex (a point, segment, triangle or tetrahedron) inside the Minkowski difference of the shapes and tells whether it contains the origin. If it does, the shapes overlap, and EPA (expanding polytope algorithm) grows the simplex to find the penetration depth and normal. For boxes, the separating axis test checks whether any face normal or edge cross product separates them. Concave objects are split into convex pieces or represented as triangle meshes. The result is a contact manifold: a normal \( n \), a depth \( d \), and up to 4 points. Keeping the manifold from the last step (persistent contacts) gives each point an identity so that its impulse can be reused as a starting guess.
Step 5: The solver. For a contact with normal \( n \), the relative velocity along the normal is \( v_n = n \cdot (v_B - v_A) \). The rule is: after the step, \( v_n \ge 0 \) (bodies separate or slide), the contact pushes only (impulse \( \lambda \ge 0 \)), and it pushes only when touching. Friction adds a second rule: the tangential impulse must stay inside the Coulomb cone, \( |\lambda_t| \le \mu \lambda_n \). Engines such as Box2D and Bullet use sequential impulses, which is the Projected Gauss-Seidel method: loop over contacts, compute the impulse that fixes this one contact, clamp it so \( \lambda \ge 0 \), apply it to the two bodies, and move to the next. Repeat 4 to 20 times. A stack of 10 boxes needs many iterations for the correction to travel from the floor to the top, which is why tall stacks jitter when iterations are low. Warm starting (begin from last step's impulses) is what makes few iterations enough. MuJoCo instead solves the same rules as one convex optimization problem per step, which is slower but more exact and smoother.
Step 6: Position correction and sleeping. Because bodies are moved in discrete steps, they overlap a little. Engines add a small velocity bias that pushes overlap out gradually (Baumgarte stabilization, a few percent to 20% of the depth per step), or fix positions in a separate pass. Bodies that stay still for about half a second go to sleep and cost nothing until something touches them. Islands, groups of bodies connected by contacts, are solved independently and can run in parallel.
Step 7: Continuous collision detection. A bullet moving at 300 m/s covers 5 m in a 1/60 s step. A 10 cm wall lies entirely inside that distance, so the discrete test never sees an overlap. Continuous collision detection (CCD) sweeps the shape along its path and finds the first time of impact, then stops the body there or substeps. Engines turn it on only for fast bodies, because it costs more.

example: First, why the integrator matters. Take a spring with stiffness k = 1,000 N/m holding a mass of 1 kg. Its natural angular frequency is \( \omega = \sqrt{k/m} \approx 31.6 \) rad/s. Use dt = 1/60 s, so \( \omega\, dt \approx 0.527 \). With explicit Euler, the amplitude is multiplied each step by \( \sqrt{1 + (\omega\, dt)^2} = \sqrt{1.278} \approx 1.13 \). After one second (60 steps), it is \( 1.13^{60} \approx 1{,}500 \) times larger. The spring explodes. With semi-implicit Euler, the amplitude stays bounded as long as \( \omega\, dt < 2 \), here dt below about 0.063 s, and the energy only oscillates a little. A spring ten times stiffer (k = 10,000, \( \omega \approx 100 \)) needs dt below 0.02 s, so at 60 Hz it fails too. Stiff things need smaller steps or implicit methods.
Now a contact. A 1 kg ball is 0.05 m above a floor and moves down at 4 m/s. Use dt = 1/60 s (gravity adds only 0.16 m/s per step, so ignore it here). After the integration the ball would travel 4/60 ≈ 0.067 m, so it ends 0.0167 m inside the floor. The narrow phase reports normal \( n = (0,1,0) \) and depth \( d = 0.0167 \) m. The normal relative velocity is \( v_n = -4 \) m/s. With restitution 0 (no bounce), the solver needs the impulse that makes \( v_n = 0 \): \( \lambda = m \times 4 = 4 \) N·s, which is positive and so allowed. The ball's velocity becomes 0. To remove the overlap, add a bias velocity of \( \beta\, d/dt = 0.2 \times 0.0167 \times 60 \approx 0.2 \) m/s upward, so \( \lambda \approx 4.2 \) N·s, and the ball rises out of the floor over a few steps. With restitution 0.5 the target is \( v_n = +2 \) m/s and \( \lambda = 6 \) N·s.
Finally, the broad phase. With 1,000 bodies, naive testing needs 499,500 pair tests per step, about 30 million per second at 60 Hz. A grid with cells the size of a typical body gives each body about 9 neighbour cells in 2D. If each cell holds 3 bodies on average, that is about 27 candidate tests per body, about 27,000 per step, so roughly 18 times fewer. The saving grows with n, because the naive cost grows as \( n^2 \) and the grid cost grows as \( n \).
intuition: Physics simulation is a flip-book: draw the world, move everything a tiny bit, undo any overlaps, repeat 60 times a second.
Nature is continuous, but a computer can only take steps. If the step is small, straight-line motion over one step is almost exact, and the only hard question is what happens when two bodies would pass through each other. The answer is also local and simple: if two bodies are touching, they may push on each other but may not pull and may not approach. The solver is just a loop that enforces this rule at each contact until the whole stack agrees. The trick that makes this fast is that almost nothing touches anything: the broad phase removes nearly all pairs, and sleeping removes most bodies.
mistakes: People often think a physics engine solves the exact equations of motion. In fact it integrates approximately, in fixed steps, and its contact solver stops after a few iterations. The result looks right and obeys the main laws, but energy is not exactly conserved and tall stacks can creep.
People often think a smaller time step is always the fix. In fact a smaller step helps stability and tunneling, but halving dt doubles the cost, and stacking problems are usually helped more by more solver iterations or warm starting.
People often think the render frame rate should drive the simulation. In fact a variable dt makes the simulation depend on how fast the screen is drawn: it can explode on a slow frame and it is not repeatable. Use a fixed step with an accumulator and interpolate for drawing.
People often think collision detection means checking whether shapes overlap. In fact the hard parts are the contact points, normal and depth, which are needed for a response, and doing it quickly for thousands of bodies. A yes/no overlap test is not enough to make a box rest on a table.
People often think simulation results transfer to the real world. In fact friction, contact stiffness, motor delay and sensor noise differ from the model. This sim-to-real gap is why robotics uses domain randomization (random masses and frictions during training).
friction: Contact is the weak point. The rules "no pull, no overlap, friction cone" are not smooth: a contact is either on or off. Exact formulations are hard (LCP and NCP problems can have no unique solution, and the Coulomb friction with stiction can even be inconsistent in rare cases, the Painlevé paradox). Engines therefore soften the rules, and results depend on solver settings.
Stability limits are practical. Stacks and piles need many iterations; thin objects tunnel; very different masses on the same joint (a heavy body resting on a light one) make the iterative solver converge badly; rigid joints under high speed drift. Performance scales with the number of contacts, not bodies, so a pile of 10,000 touching objects is far costlier than 10,000 separated ones.
Determinism is another cost. Running the same scene on a different CPU, compiler or thread count can change the order of floating-point additions and so the result. Lockstep multiplayer games and reproducible RL experiments must control this, usually with single-threaded fixed-order solvers or integer maths.
Deformable bodies, fluids and cloth need other methods (finite elements, PBD, particle methods) and are much more expensive than rigid bodies.
practice: Use a fixed time step and an accumulator, as in Glenn Fiedler's "Fix Your Timestep": add real elapsed time to the accumulator, run the simulation in dt steps while the accumulator is at least dt, and interpolate the drawing by the leftover fraction. Cap the number of steps per frame so a slow frame does not cause a spiral of death.
Choose shapes carefully. Boxes, spheres and capsules are far cheaper and more stable than triangle meshes; use a few convex pieces for complex objects, and keep the collision shape simpler than the visible one. Use a layer or filter system so that bodies that should never collide (for example your own bullets) are removed in the broad phase. Keep mass ratios moderate (under about 10:1 on one joint) and sizes in the range the engine is tuned for (roughly 0.1 m to 10 m).
For tuning, watch three numbers: solver iterations, step size and the contact count. Raise iterations first for jitter in stacks, lower dt for tunneling and stiff joints, and turn on CCD only for fast bodies. Let objects sleep. In robotics, check that the simulator's timestep matches the control rate (for example 500 Hz or 1 kHz physics with 50 Hz policy), randomize friction, mass and delays, and compare a few real trajectories with the simulated ones before trusting a trained policy. Learn on Box2D (small and readable), then MuJoCo.
tradeoffs: Choose sequential impulses (Box2D, Bullet, PhysX) when you need thousands of bodies at 60 Hz and the result only needs to look right; choose a convex-optimization solver such as MuJoCo's when contact accuracy and smooth gradients matter, for example in robot control and learning, and accept a slower step. Either way, you give up exactness: the first gives jitter in stacks, the second gives fewer bodies per second.
Choose a small fixed step (1 ms to 2 ms) when the model is stiff, such as a robot with motors, and when tunneling must not happen; choose 1/60 s with a few substeps when the scene is a game and the rest of the frame needs the time. Halving the step doubles the cost, so ask whether the error you see is from dt or from the solver.
Choose a uniform grid for the broad phase when objects have similar sizes and the world is bounded; choose a dynamic AABB tree when sizes vary a lot or the world is large and sparse; choose sweep-and-prune when most objects are still. The grid costs memory and fails on mixed sizes; the tree costs more per update; sweep-and-prune degrades when many objects move at once.
Choose discrete collision plus thin-object rules when objects are slow compared with their size (rule of thumb: speed times dt less than the smallest dimension); choose continuous collision detection for bullets, fast cars and thin walls. CCD costs several times more per pair, so enabling it for everything wastes the budget.
Choose a rigid-body engine when objects hardly bend; choose position-based or finite-element methods for cloth and soft bodies. A rigid body has 6 degrees of freedom; a soft body has thousands.
interview: Why does a physics engine use a fixed time step, and how do you couple it to a variable frame rate? || A variable dt changes the numerical behaviour, so stiff constraints can blow up on a slow frame and runs are not repeatable. Keep an accumulator: add the real elapsed time, run fixed steps while the accumulator holds at least dt, and interpolate rendering with the remainder. Cap steps per frame to avoid the spiral of death.
A bullet passes through a thin wall at 60 Hz. Explain why and give three fixes. || The bullet moves several metres per step, so it is outside the wall in both steps and no overlap is ever tested. Fixes: continuous collision detection with swept shapes, a smaller step or substeps, a ray cast from the old to the new position, or a thicker wall. Mention that CCD is enabled only for fast bodies because of its cost.
How does the broad phase cut collision cost from O(n²), and how do you choose a structure? || It keeps cheap AABBs in a grid, a dynamic bounding-volume tree or a sorted-interval list, and returns only overlapping boxes. A grid fits equal-sized objects, a tree fits varied sizes and ray queries, sweep-and-prune uses temporal coherence when most objects are still. The exact test then runs on a few pairs.
Why do tall box stacks jitter in iterative solvers, and what would you change? || Sequential impulses fix one contact at a time, so the correction from the floor needs many iterations to reach the top of the stack. Raise iterations, use warm starting with persistent contacts, add substeps, lower the mass ratio, use a better solver such as soft step or a direct or convex solver, and let the stack sleep.
Why is semi-implicit Euler preferred over explicit Euler, and what are its limits? || Updating velocity first and then position with the new velocity keeps the energy of an oscillator bounded, while explicit Euler multiplies the amplitude by \( \sqrt{1+(\omega\,dt)^2} \) each step. It is still only first-order accurate and stable only for \( \omega\,dt < 2 \), so stiff systems need smaller steps or implicit integration.
You train a robot policy in simulation and it fails on the real robot. How do you investigate? || Look at the sim-to-real gap in order: actuator model (delay, torque limits, backlash), contact parameters (friction, stiffness), sensor noise and latency, and mass or inertia errors. Compare real and simulated trajectories for the same commands, identify the parameters, and train with domain randomization over them.
quiz: What are the broad phase and the narrow phase? || The broad phase uses cheap bounding boxes to list pairs of bodies that might touch. The narrow phase runs exact geometry (such as GJK or SAT) on those pairs to get the contact normal, depth and points.
Why does explicit Euler make a spring simulation explode, while semi-implicit Euler does not? || Explicit Euler uses the old velocity to move the position and the old position to change velocity, which multiplies the oscillation amplitude by more than 1 each step. Semi-implicit Euler updates velocity first and uses the new value, which keeps the energy bounded when \( \omega\,dt < 2 \).
Why does the solver clamp the contact impulse at zero or above? || A contact can push two bodies apart but cannot pull them together, as there is no glue. Allowing a negative impulse would make bodies stick to each other.
A spring has k = 400 N/m and m = 1 kg. What is the largest dt for which semi-implicit Euler stays stable? || \( \omega = \sqrt{400/1} = 20 \) rad/s, and stability needs \( \omega\,dt < 2 \), so dt < 0.1 s. In practice use much less, such as 0.01 s, for accuracy.
You need a robot-learning simulator with 4,096 parallel environments, and your friend suggests a precise convex-contact solver. How do you decide? || Measure steps per second per environment and the accuracy you need. A convex solver is smoother and more accurate but costs more per step; GPU engines with simpler iterative solvers give far more samples per second. Choose accuracy for contact-rich tasks like manipulation, and throughput plus domain randomization for locomotion.

@@ neuralrender
value: Neural rendering builds a 3D scene from ordinary photographs and then draws it from any new viewpoint. You walk around a chair or a street with a phone camera, take 50 to 300 photos, and a program learns a model of the scene. After that, you can place a virtual camera anywhere, including positions where you never stood, and get a realistic image that includes reflections, soft shadows and fine detail such as hair or leaves. Classic 3D capture builds a triangle mesh and textures, and it fails on thin, shiny or fuzzy things. Neural rendering skips the mesh and learns how light appears from each direction.
There are two main designs. NeRF (Neural Radiance Fields, 2020) stores the scene inside a small neural network: you give it a 3D point and a viewing direction, and it returns a colour and a density. 3D Gaussian Splatting (2023) stores the scene as millions of small, soft, coloured 3D blobs (Gaussians), each with a position, shape, opacity and colour. NeRF is compact and smooth but slow to render, because every pixel needs hundreds of network calls. Gaussian splatting renders at 30 to 100+ frames per second at 1080p on one GPU, because the blobs are drawn with a rasterizer, the same hardware path that draws game triangles.
For the layer above, graphics, mapping and robotics now treat captured scenes as a new asset type next to meshes. Street-level maps, product views, virtual tours, film sets, simulation worlds for self-driving and robot training, and AR all use them. The layers below make it work: rasterization (L2) draws the blobs fast, gradient descent (L2) fits them to photos, automatic differentiation (L2) provides the gradients through the renderer, and homogeneous coordinates and projection (L4) tell the program exactly how a 3D point lands on each photo.
terms: Radiance field — a function that gives, for a 3D point and a viewing direction, the light that leaves that point (colour) and how much it blocks light (density).
Volume rendering — computing a pixel by sampling points along a camera ray and blending their colours, weighted by density and by how much light the earlier points block.
Transmittance (T) — the fraction of light that reaches a point along a ray without being blocked; it starts at 1 and only decreases.
Gaussian splat — a 3D blob with a centre, a 3×3 covariance (shape and orientation), an opacity and a colour; it is projected to the screen as a soft 2D ellipse.
Spherical harmonics — a small set of basis functions on a sphere used to store colour that changes with the viewing direction.
Structure from Motion (SfM) — software (for example COLMAP) that finds each photo's camera position and a sparse 3D point cloud from matching features across photos.
Differentiable renderer — a renderer whose output pixels have gradients with respect to the scene parameters, so that gradient descent can adjust the scene.
Floaters — wrong semi-transparent blobs that hang in empty space, because a few views cannot fix their depth.
history: The idea of making new views from photos is old. Image-based rendering in the 1990s (light fields, Lumigraph, 1996) interpolated between many photos with no 3D model. Structure from Motion and multi-view stereo, from the 2000s, recovered meshes and point clouds from photos, and photogrammetry tools used them for decades. Volume rendering itself comes from medical and film graphics (Kajiya and Von Herzen, 1984).
In March 2020, Mildenhall, Srinivasan, Tancik, Barron, Ramamoorthi and Ng published NeRF (ECCV 2020). The surprise was that a plain multilayer perceptron (MLP) of 8 layers, trained for one to two days on one GPU per scene, could reproduce view-dependent detail better than mesh methods. Two choices made it work: positional encoding (feeding sin and cos of the coordinates at many frequencies, so the network can express fine detail) and hierarchical sampling. Many follow-ups came quickly: Mip-NeRF (anti-aliasing, 2021), Mip-NeRF 360 (unbounded scenes, 2022), Block-NeRF (a whole San Francisco neighbourhood, 2022), and Instant-NGP (Müller et al., SIGGRAPH 2022), which replaced most of the network with a multiresolution hash table of learned features and cut training from days to seconds or minutes.
In August 2023, Kerbl, Kopanas, Leimkühler and Drettakis (Inria) published "3D Gaussian Splatting for Real-Time Radiance Field Rendering" at SIGGRAPH. It dropped the network and the ray marching. It started from the SfM points, turned each into a Gaussian, and optimized all of them with a custom CUDA tile-based rasterizer. It matched the quality of the best NeRFs, trained in tens of minutes and rendered in real time. Hundreds of follow-ups came within a year or two: anti-aliased and compressed splats, 2D Gaussian surfels for better surfaces, 4D splats for moving scenes, and phone apps and web viewers that show splats directly.
how: The pipeline has the same shape for NeRF and for splatting: (1) get camera poses; (2) define a scene representation with trainable parameters; (3) render the training views from the current scene; (4) compare with the photos; (5) back-propagate and update. Repeat tens of thousands of times. Here is each stage.
Step 1: Camera poses (homogeneous coordinates and projection, L4). Every photo needs its camera position, orientation and focal length. SfM tools such as COLMAP find these by matching features between photos. A 3D point \( X \) in homogeneous coordinates lands on the image at \( u \sim K\,[R\,|\,t]\,X \), where \( K \) holds the focal length and image centre and \( [R|t] \) is the camera pose. This one formula is the link between the 3D scene and every pixel. Errors in the poses show up as blur, so pose quality limits final quality.
Step 2a: NeRF representation and volume rendering. The network \( F(x, d) \to (c, \sigma) \) takes a point \( x \) and a viewing direction \( d \), and returns colour \( c \) and density \( \sigma \). To get a pixel, cast a ray \( r(t) = o + t\,d \) through it and take samples \( t_1 \ldots t_N \) with spacing \( \delta_i \) (the paper uses 64 coarse and 128 fine samples). Then blend:
\[ C(r) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} T_i \left(1 - e^{-\sigma_i \delta_i}\right) c_i, \qquad T_i = \exp\!\Big(-\sum_{j<i} \sigma_j \delta_j\Big) \]
The term \( 1 - e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i} \) is the opacity of segment \( i \), and \( T_i \) is the light that survives the segments before it. Dense, near segments hide far ones. This sum is differentiable, so the loss on the pixel flows back into the network weights. Positional encoding turns each coordinate \( p \) into \( (\sin 2^k\pi p, \cos 2^k\pi p) \) for \( k = 0 \ldots L-1 \); without it, an MLP learns only blurry, low-frequency functions.
Step 2b: Gaussian representation. Each Gaussian stores a centre (3 numbers), a scale (3), a rotation as a quaternion (4), an opacity (1) and view-dependent colour as spherical-harmonic coefficients (48 for degree 3, 16 per colour channel). That is 59 floats per Gaussian. Scale and rotation build the covariance \( \Sigma = R S S^T R^T \), which is always a valid positive semi-definite matrix, so gradient descent cannot break it. A typical scene needs one to six million Gaussians.
Step 3: Rendering splats (rasterization, L2). The camera transform \( W \) and the local linearisation of the perspective projection \( J \) turn each 3D Gaussian into a 2D Gaussian with covariance \( \Sigma' = J\,W\,\Sigma\,W^T J^T \) (the EWA splatting idea of Zwicker and colleagues, early 2000s). The screen is cut into 16×16 pixel tiles. Each Gaussian is assigned to the tiles it covers, and a GPU radix sort orders Gaussians by tile and depth. Each tile then walks its sorted list front to back, and for each pixel computes \( \alpha_i = o_i\,e^{-\frac12 d^T \Sigma'^{-1} d} \) and blends \( C = \sum_i c_i \alpha_i \prod_{j<i}(1 - \alpha_j) \), stopping when the remaining transmittance is below about 0.0001. This is the same alpha compositing a rasterizer uses for transparent sprites, so it runs at game speed.
Step 4: Loss and gradient descent (gradient descent, L2). The rendered image is compared with the real photo with a mix of L1 error and a structural similarity term (the paper weights them 0.8 and 0.2). Adam updates every parameter, each with its own learning rate; positions use a rate that decays over training and opacity and colour use fixed rates.
Step 5: Gradients through the renderer (automatic differentiation, L2). The loss must be differentiated with respect to millions of Gaussian parameters and to the network weights. For a NeRF this is ordinary reverse-mode automatic differentiation through the sampling sum and the MLP. For splatting the authors wrote the backward pass of the tile rasterizer by hand in CUDA, because a general framework would be too slow and use too much memory; the gradient formulas are the chain rule applied to the compositing sum above.
Step 6: Adaptive density control. Splatting is not a fixed set of blobs. Every 100 iterations, Gaussians with large position gradients are cloned (if small, to fill missing detail) or split (if large, to refine), and Gaussians that are almost transparent or too large are pruned. This lets the scene grow detail where the photos need it, from a sparse start of some tens of thousands of points.

example: First, a NeRF ray with three samples, each spaced \( \delta = 0.5 \) apart, with densities \( \sigma = 0.5, 2.0, 4.0 \). The opacities are \( \alpha_i = 1 - e^{-\sigma_i\delta} \): \( 1 - e^{-0.25} = 0.221 \), \( 1 - e^{-1.0} = 0.632 \), \( 1 - e^{-2.0} = 0.865 \). The transmittances are \( T_1 = 1 \), \( T_2 = 1 - 0.221 = 0.779 \), \( T_3 = 0.779 \times (1 - 0.632) = 0.287 \). The weights \( T_i\alpha_i \) are 0.221, 0.492 and 0.248. Say the three colours are red, green and blue. The pixel is (0.221, 0.492, 0.248): mostly green, because the middle sample is dense and not hidden. The remaining transmittance is \( 0.287 \times (1 - 0.865) = 0.039 \), so 3.9% of the pixel comes from whatever is behind (a background colour). During training, if the photo shows more blue here, the gradient raises the blue sample's colour weight, and the chain rule through \( T_i \) also lowers the density of the samples in front of it.
Now a splat pixel. A pixel lies one standard deviation from the centre of a front Gaussian with opacity 0.8 and colour red. Then \( \alpha_A = 0.8 \times e^{-0.5} = 0.485 \). Behind it is a blue Gaussian with \( \alpha_B = 0.8 \) at this pixel. The result is \( C = 0.485 \times \text{red} + (1 - 0.485) \times 0.8 \times \text{blue} = 0.485\,\text{red} + 0.412\,\text{blue} \), with 0.103 left for the next splat.
Next, the cost. A NeRF image of 800×800 pixels with 192 samples per ray needs 640,000 × 192 ≈ 123 million network calls. At roughly 1 MFLOP per call (8 layers of width 256), that is about \( 1.2 \times 10^{14} \) FLOPs per image, so on a GPU doing 10 TFLOP/s it takes more than 10 seconds. A splat scene with 3 million Gaussians needs 3,000,000 × 59 × 4 bytes ≈ 710 MB, and each frame costs a projection of 3 million Gaussians, a sort of those that fall on screen, and a blend limited to the few dozen splats that matter per pixel. That fits in about 10 ms, so the scene renders at roughly 100 frames per second.
intuition: Imagine a dust cloud of tiny coloured, translucent balloons placed in the room, tuned until every photo you took looks exactly like the view of the balloons from where you stood.
The idea is natural because rendering is a known function of the scene, and all you need is a scene that can be changed smoothly. Photos tell you what the right answer looks like from known positions. If the renderer is differentiable, the error between its image and the photo tells every parameter which way to move. Many photos from different angles force the blobs to agree in 3D, because a wrong depth looks right from one camera and wrong from the next. Gaussians are a good choice because they are smooth (so gradients exist), they project to screen as simple ellipses (so drawing them is cheap), and many of them can approximate any shape.
mistakes: People often think NeRF and Gaussian splatting produce a 3D mesh. In fact they produce a radiance field or a cloud of blobs, with no surfaces. A mesh can be extracted afterwards (marching cubes on the density, or methods such as 2D Gaussian splatting), but the result is usually noisier than the rendered images.
People often think the network or the splats "understand" the scene. In fact they only fit the photos. Outside the area covered by the cameras, or for surfaces seen from one direction only, the result is arbitrary and may show floaters, holes or smeared textures.
People often think no camera information is needed. In fact both methods need accurate camera poses. They come from SfM software run first, and if SfM fails (blank walls, mirrors, moving objects), the reconstruction fails with it.
People often think splats are just a point cloud with big points. In fact each splat is an anisotropic ellipsoid with its own opacity and view-dependent colour, and it is optimized with gradients. A raw point cloud has fixed size and colour and looks full of holes.
People often think Gaussian splatting made NeRF obsolete. In fact NeRF-type fields (and hash-grid variants) are more compact, easier to regularise and easier to put into larger learned models. Splats are best when you need fast rendering and editing.
friction: Quality depends on capture. Too few views, motion blur, exposure changes, moving people, and reflections or glass break the assumption that the scene is the same in all photos. Specular surfaces are modelled only as a view-dependent colour, so reflected objects appear to float behind the mirror. Floaters appear when depth is ambiguous.
Size and editing are hard. A typical scene of 1 to 6 million Gaussians takes hundreds of megabytes, large for the web, and research on compression (quantisation, pruning, codebooks) gives 10× to 50× smaller files with some quality loss. Splats have no surfaces, so physics, relighting and editing (moving an object, changing material) are difficult; the colour already contains the original lighting baked in. Anti-aliasing needs special care, since blobs fitted at one distance look wrong when you zoom far in or out.
Scale and motion are open problems. Training time grows with scene size; city-scale scenes are split into blocks. Moving scenes need extra structure (a deformation field or per-frame splats), and the cost grows with video length. The original 3D Gaussian Splatting code was released under a non-commercial research licence, so production use needs a check of the licence or a reimplementation, and the patents and terms of related methods must also be checked.
practice: Capture well: use a steady camera with fixed exposure and focus, overlap photos by 70% or more, move around the object in several rings at different heights, avoid people and moving things, and avoid featureless surfaces. 100 to 300 sharp photos are usually enough for an object or a room. Check the SfM result before training: the sparse points should look like the scene and the registered images should be almost all of the photos.
Choose the tool for the output. For a quick result use a hosted or mobile service (for example Polycam, Luma or Scaniverse) or the Nerfstudio toolkit, which implements many NeRF and splat variants behind one interface. For research and control, start from the Inria Gaussian Splatting code or a maintained fork such as gsplat. For web display, convert to a compressed splat format and use a WebGL or WebGPU viewer. Crop and clean floaters by deleting low-opacity or far-away Gaussians.
Track metrics on held-out photos: PSNR (higher is better, roughly 25 to 30 dB for real scenes), SSIM and LPIPS (lower is better). Judge by moving the camera too, because a still image can look right while the 3D is wrong. If you need geometry for physics or measurement, use a method designed for surfaces, or combine with depth sensors, and do not measure distances in a raw splat scene unless it has been scaled using a known reference.
tradeoffs: Choose Gaussian splatting when you need real-time viewing (30 to 200 frames per second), fast training (minutes to an hour), and a scene that you can crop or edit. Choose a NeRF or hash-grid field when file size matters, when you want a smooth continuous function to use inside a larger model (for example for robotics mapping or generative 3D), or when memory for millions of blobs is not available. You give up speed with NeRF, and compactness and regularity with splats.
Choose neural capture instead of photogrammetry meshes when the subject has hair, foliage, glass, glossy surfaces or fine detail, and the goal is to look right from new viewpoints. Choose a mesh pipeline when the output must be physically simulated, 3D printed, measured, rigged, or placed into a standard game engine; you give up photoreal view-dependent effects but get clean surfaces.
Choose more training photos and tighter capture when quality matters more than time; choose fewer photos plus learned priors (few-view methods or generative models) when capture is expensive. Priors fill gaps but may invent things that were never there, which is a problem for any use that needs the truth, such as inspection or evidence.
Choose training on a local GPU when the data is private or you capture often; choose a hosted service when you capture occasionally. Hosted services start faster but move your data to a third party.
interview: Explain how a NeRF turns a network into a pixel, and why positional encoding is needed. || For each pixel, cast a ray, take 64 to 192 samples, query the MLP for colour and density at each, and blend them with the weights \( T_i(1-e^{-\sigma_i\delta_i}) \). The scene is explicit memory, not a function to evaluate.
What does adaptive density control do and what happens without it? || It clones Gaussians in under-reconstructed small regions, splits large ones that cover too much, and prunes nearly transparent or oversized ones. Without it, the result is limited by the initial SfM points and stays blurry in detail and has holes where the sparse cloud had none.
Your reconstruction has floaters and blurry areas. How would you debug it? || Check pose quality first (SfM reprojection error, number of registered images), then capture problems (motion blur, exposure changes, moving objects, too few viewpoints in the bad area). value: Motion planning answers a question that sounds easy: how does a robot get from where it is to where it should be, without hitting anything? A robot arm with 6 joints must move a part from a bin to a fixture without touching the bin walls, the table or itself. A delivery robot must cross a room with chairs. A self-driving car must find a smooth path around parked cars that respects its turning radius. The output is not a single move but a whole path or trajectory: a list of robot states, each collision-free, joined by moves the robot can actually perform.
The difficulty is the number of ways a robot can be placed. A point moving in a flat room has 2 numbers to choose (x and y). An arm with 6 joints has 6 numbers, and a humanoid has 30 or more. If you divide each joint range into 100 steps, a 6-joint arm has \( 100^6 = 10^{12} \) positions, too many to check one by one, and the planning problem for general robots is PSPACE-hard (Reif, 1979; Canny, 1988). Motion planning is therefore a set of techniques that avoid looking at most of that space: A* with good heuristics for low dimensions, and random sampling (PRM, RRT) for high dimensions.
For the layer above, robotics software (ROS with MoveIt, warehouse robots, autonomous vehicles, drones) calls a planner many times a second. The layers below make it work: Heuristic search (L3) gives A* for grid and lattice planning, geometry and metric spaces (L4) define the space of robot configurations and the distance in it, randomized algorithms (L3) justify planning by sampling, and computational geometry (L3) provides the collision tests that every planner uses.
terms: Configuration (q) — the list of numbers that fixes the robot's pose, for example the 6 joint angles of an arm, or (x, y, heading) of a car.
Configuration space (C-space) — the set of all configurations; a robot in the real world becomes a single point moving in this space.
C-obstacle — the set of configurations in which the robot would touch an obstacle; the free space is everything else.
Degrees of freedom (DOF) — the number of independent numbers in a configuration, and so the dimension of the C-space.
Probabilistic completeness — if a path exists, the chance that the planner finds it tends to 1 as it takes more random samples.
Asymptotic optimality — the path the planner returns tends to the shortest path as the number of samples grows (for RRT* and PRM*).
Roadmap — a graph whose nodes are collision-free configurations and whose edges are collision-free short moves; planning then becomes graph search.
Nonholonomic constraint — a limit on the directions the robot can move at once, such as a car that cannot slide sideways.
history: Motion planning began as geometry. Lozano-Pérez and Wesley (1979) planned the path of a polyhedron among polyhedral obstacles, and Lozano-Pérez (1983) introduced the configuration space idea that organises the whole field: shrink the robot to a point and grow the obstacles. The same year Reif proved that the general "piano mover's problem" is PSPACE-hard, and Canny's 1988 book gave an algorithm whose cost grows exponentially only with the number of dimensions. So an exact answer was possible but impractical for real robots.
Two practical families followed. Khatib (1986) proposed artificial potential fields: the goal pulls the robot and obstacles push it away. It is fast but can get stuck in local minima. Grid and graph search with A* was the second family, used for mobile robots in the plane, and later with motion primitives (state lattices) and Hybrid A*, which the Stanford car Junior used in the 2007 DARPA Urban Challenge.
The breakthrough for high dimensions was random sampling. Kavraki, Švestka, Latombe and Overmars proposed the Probabilistic Roadmap (PRM) in 1996: sample many free configurations once, connect near ones, and answer many queries by graph search. LaValle introduced the Rapidly-exploring Random Tree (RRT) in 1998, which grows a tree from the start towards random samples and is well suited to single queries and to robots with dynamics (LaValle and Kuffner, 2001). Karaman and Frazzoli (2011) showed that plain RRT does not converge to the best path, and gave RRT* and PRM*, which do. In parallel came trajectory optimisation methods (CHOMP 2009, STOMP, TrajOpt 2013) that start with a rough path and smooth it. Software followed: OMPL (2012) collects the sampling planners, MoveIt uses them for arms, and recent GPU planners such as NVIDIA's cuRobo test thousands of trajectories in parallel.
how: A motion planner works in four layers: define the space, test points in it, search or sample it, and clean the answer. Each step uses one of the concepts below it.
Step 1: Define the configuration space (geometry and metric spaces, L4). The planner does not move the robot's shape through the world; it moves a point \( q \) through C-space. Joint angles wrap around (350° is 10° away from 0°), so an arm's C-space is a torus, not a flat box, and a free-flying body lives in 3D space times the rotation group SO(3). Distance must respect this: the distance between two configurations is usually a weighted sum of squared joint differences, with angle differences taken the short way round. For a robot that only translates, the C-obstacle of an obstacle \( O \) is the Minkowski sum
\[ \mathcal{C}O = O \oplus (-R) = \{\, o - r : o \in O,\ r \in R \,\} \]
That is, the obstacle grown by the shape of the robot. For a rotating or articulated robot the C-obstacle has no simple form, and no one computes it exactly. This is why planners use the next step as a black box instead.
Step 2: Collision checking (computational geometry, L3). The planner needs one primitive: "is this configuration free?". The robot and the world are approximated by spheres, capsules, boxes or convex meshes. Forward kinematics turns joint angles into the pose of each link, and then the engine runs bounding-volume tests and exact tests such as GJK between pairs. A check of one configuration takes microseconds to tens of microseconds, and in most planners collision checking takes the largest share of runtime. An edge from \( q_a \) to \( q_b \) is checked by testing configurations along it every small step \( \epsilon \), or with a conservative bound on how far any point of the robot moves per unit of joint motion.
Step 3a: Search planners (heuristic search, L3). In 2 to 4 dimensions (mobile robots), cut the space into a grid or a lattice of motion primitives and run A*. Each cell is a node, each allowed move an edge with a cost (its length or time), and the heuristic is a lower bound on the remaining cost, such as the straight-line distance on an 8-connected grid. A* is complete (finds a path whenever one exists on the grid) and optimal on that grid. The weakness is the curse of dimensionality: the grid size is the number of steps per axis, raised to the number of axes.
Step 3b: Sampling planners (randomized algorithms, L3). Instead of covering the space, sample it. A PRM draws N random configurations, keeps the collision-free ones, connects each to its k nearest neighbours when the straight edge is free, and searches the resulting graph with A* or Dijkstra. An RRT starts with a tree containing only the start. In each iteration it samples a random configuration \( q_{rand} \) (with a small probability, say 5%, it uses the goal instead), finds the nearest tree node \( q_{near} \), moves a fixed step \( \eta \) from \( q_{near} \) toward \( q_{rand} \) to get \( q_{new} \), and adds \( q_{new} \) if the edge is free. The trick that makes RRT work is the Voronoi bias: a node at the edge of the explored region owns a large part of space, so random samples fall near it more often, and the tree is pulled outward toward unexplored space. When the tree gets within \( \eta \) of the goal, you read the path back through the parents.
Step 3c: Optimising versions. RRT returns a path, but a jagged one, with no quality guarantee. RRT* adds two steps after \( q_{new} \) is made: choose as parent the nearby node that gives the lowest cost, and rewire nearby nodes to go through \( q_{new} \) if that is cheaper. The neighbour radius shrinks like \( (\log n / n)^{1/d} \) so the number of neighbours stays about \( \log n \). The result converges to the optimal path as the sample count grows, at a cost in time.
Step 4: Post-processing and execution. Sampling paths wander, so planners shortcut them (try to connect distant waypoints directly) and smooth them, then apply time parameterisation: assign speeds and accelerations that respect joint limits, so the path becomes a trajectory. For a car, kinodynamic planners sample controls (steering, acceleration) and simulate forward, so every edge is already feasible. A controller then tracks the trajectory, and the planner replans when the world changes. This is the usual split: a global planner finds a rough route, a local planner and controller handle details at 10 to 100 Hz.

example: First, why grids fail. A 6-joint arm with 100 steps per joint has \( 100^6 = 10^{12} \) cells. Even at 1 microsecond per collision check that is \( 10^6 \) seconds, about 12 days. A sampling planner that finds a path with 20,000 samples needs about 20,000 checks for the nodes plus some tens of checks per edge, so roughly 1 million checks, which is a second or less.
Now an RRT by hand in a 10 × 10 room. Start at (1, 1), goal at (9, 9), step size \( \eta = 0.5 \). Iteration 1: random sample (8, 2). The tree has only the start, so \( q_{near} = (1,1) \). The direction is (7, 1), with length \( \sqrt{50} = 7.07 \). Move 0.5: \( q_{new} = (1 + 0.5 \times 7/7.07,\ 1 + 0.5 \times 1/7.07) = (1.495, 1.071) \). The edge is free, so add it. Iteration 2: random sample (2, 9). The distances are 7.945 from (1.495, 1.071) and 8.06 from (1, 1), so the nearest is the new node. The direction is (0.505, 7.929), length 7.945, so \( q_{new} = (1.527, 1.570) \). After a few hundred iterations the tree spreads in a fan toward free space; with 5% goal bias, about every 20th sample is (9, 9) itself and pulls a branch toward the goal. If a wall blocks the straight route, the branch that hits it stops adding nodes, but other branches keep growing, so the tree flows around the wall.
Narrow passages show the weakness. Suppose a doorway in the wall takes up 1% of the free area, as seen by uniform samples. About 1 in 100 samples falls in it, so you need roughly 100 samples to get a single node there, and several more to connect through it. For a doorway of 0.1% you need about 1,000 samples per useful node. In 6 dimensions, the fraction of space that is a passage can fall to \( 10^{-6} \) or less, which is why bridge-test and Gaussian samplers exist that put more samples near obstacle boundaries.
Last, optimality. For RRT* in a 2D unit square with n = 1,000 nodes, the connection radius is about \( \gamma (\log n/n)^{1/2} = \gamma \times 0.083 \), so the number of neighbours inside it is a constant times \( \log n \approx 7 \). Each rewiring step re-checks only those neighbours, so the cost per iteration grows only as \( \log n \) and the path gradually straightens as n grows.
intuition: Planning a path for a robot is like threading a needle: it is easier to think of the thread as a point and to make the needle's eye a little smaller, and then to try many random straight stabs.
The idea is natural once you see the configuration space. A real robot has a shape, and shapes are awkward. Replace the robot by one point and inflate the obstacles, and the question becomes "find a curve for a point". Then you cannot afford to map all of the space, but you can ask a cheap local question: is this one point free? Random questions give an unbiased picture of the space, and growing a tree from the start only asks questions that are connected to what you already know. Because free space in robotics is usually large and open, a few thousand random answers are enough to find a route; only tight passages need clever sampling.
mistakes: People often think the planner works in the real 3D world. In fact it works in configuration space, whose dimension equals the robot's degrees of freedom. A 7-joint arm plans in 7 dimensions, even though it moves in 3D.
People often think RRT finds the shortest path. In fact it finds a feasible path, usually long and jagged, and plain RRT is not asymptotically optimal; RRT* is, and it needs many more samples. Smoothing and shortcutting give most of the practical gain.
People often think random planning means unreliable answers. In fact a sampling planner is probabilistically complete: if a path exists, the chance of finding it tends to 1 with more samples. What it cannot do is prove that no path exists, so a failure after a time limit means "not found", not "impossible".
People often think a smaller step in the planner is safer. In fact the safety of a path depends on the collision-checking resolution and on the robot model, not on the planner's step. A coarse edge check can pass through thin obstacles, and a too-fine one wastes time since checking is the dominant cost.
People often think a path is enough. In fact a path is geometry only; the robot needs a trajectory with timing that respects speed, acceleration and torque limits, and a controller that tracks it. A path that is collision-free can still be impossible to follow quickly.
friction: Collision checking is the main cost, and it is only as good as the robot and world models. A planner can be exactly right on a model that is a few centimetres wrong, and then the real robot touches something. Planners therefore add safety margins, which in cluttered places can close a path that is really there. Perception noise and moving objects make the world model stale within milliseconds.
Narrow passages, high dimensions and constraints are the hard cases. A robot that must keep a cup level, or move a heavy object, restricts the free configurations to a thin surface in C-space, where random samples almost never land; special methods project samples onto the constraint. Sampled paths are jagged and vary from run to run, which is bad for repeatability and for people who watch the robot. Optimal planners are slow, and optimisation-based planners (CHOMP, TrajOpt) are fast and smooth but can stop in a local minimum or be infeasible.
Real-time settings are demanding. A vehicle must replan in 50 to 100 ms in a changing scene with predictions of other drivers, which involves uncertainty that geometric planning ignores. Learning-based planners, including neural samplers and diffusion models for trajectories, are fast but give no guarantee of safety, and they are mostly used with a classical checker behind them.
practice: Start with a standard library. For arms use MoveIt (ROS) with OMPL planners; for general research use OMPL directly; for mobile robots use the navigation stack (a global A*-type planner with a local controller such as DWA or TEB). Try RRTConnect first for arms: it grows trees from both start and goal and is usually the fastest single-query planner. Use PRM-type planners when many queries happen in a fixed environment, since the roadmap is built once.
Treat the collision model as an engineering product. Use simple convex shapes (capsules and boxes) for the robot and a signed distance field or octree for the world; keep margins of a few centimetres; and put the correct link-to-link allowed collisions in the configuration so that neighbouring links are not tested. Measure how many collision checks per second you can do, because that number, multiplied by the number of samples you need, is your planning time.
Always add shortcutting and smoothing, then time-parameterise with joint limits. For a time budget, run the planner in anytime mode: return the first path, then keep improving it with RRT* or an optimiser in a separate thread. If planning is repeated for similar tasks, reuse earlier paths or warm-start the optimiser from the last solution. Log each failure with the start, goal and scene so that you can replay it; most problems turn out to be a start state that already collides, or limits set wrongly. For learning the topic, read LaValle's free book "Planning Algorithms" and run the OMPL demos.
tradeoffs: Choose a grid or lattice planner with A* when the robot has 2 to 4 degrees of freedom (a wheeled robot, a drone at constant height), you want the same answer on every run and a proof of optimality on the grid; choose a sampling planner when the dimension is 5 or more, as with arms. You give up repeatable, grid-optimal paths for the ability to plan at all.
Choose PRM when the environment is fixed and you need many queries (a robot cell, a factory); choose RRT or RRTConnect for a single query in a changing scene. PRM costs preprocessing time and memory and becomes stale when obstacles move; RRT starts from nothing for each query.
Choose RRT* or an optimiser when path length, smoothness or energy matters and you have time (offline planning, or an anytime thread); choose plain RRTConnect plus shortcutting when you need an answer in tens of milliseconds. RRT* needs far more samples to get a measurable gain, and optimisers can fail or stop in a local minimum.
Choose potential fields or reactive controllers for close-range obstacle avoidance at 100 Hz or more, and a global planner for the route. Fields are fast but can get stuck; global planners avoid traps but are too slow for the control loop alone.
Choose exact geometric planners only for small 2D or 3D translation problems with polygon obstacles; the cost explodes with dimension. For anything with rotation or many joints, accept probabilistic completeness.
interview: Why can't you just run A* on a robot arm's joint space? || A grid on 6 joints with 100 steps each has \( 10^{12} \) cells, so the memory and the number of collision checks are impossible, and the dimension makes heuristics weak. Sampling planners avoid building the grid and test only the configurations they sample, which suits high dimensions.
Explain the configuration space and what a C-obstacle is. || The configuration space holds all robot poses, with one axis per degree of freedom. A C-obstacle is the set of poses in which the robot overlaps an obstacle. In C-space the robot is a point, so planning is finding a curve in free space. For pure translation the C-obstacle is the Minkowski sum of the obstacle and the reflected robot shape.
What does "probabilistically complete" mean, and what does it not guarantee? || If a feasible path exists, the probability that the algorithm finds it goes to 1 as the number of samples grows. It does not bound the time, it does not prove that no path exists when the planner fails, and it does not say that the path is short; asymptotic optimality is a separate property (RRT*).
Why does RRT explore quickly, and when does it fail? || The Voronoi bias: nodes at the frontier have large Voronoi regions, so a uniform sample is likely to choose them, and the tree extends into unexplored space. It does badly in narrow passages (tiny volume, so few samples), under constraints that confine free space to a thin manifold, and with a poor distance metric for the robot's dynamics.
Your arm planner is too slow. How do you find and fix the bottleneck? || Profile first. Collision checking is usually the main cost, so use simpler collision geometry, faster broad-phase structures or signed distance fields, a coarser but safe edge check, and parallel or GPU checking. Then try RRTConnect, better sampling near obstacles, cache or reuse roadmaps, and time-limit with anytime improvement.
How would you plan for a car-like robot that cannot turn on the spot? || Plan in (x, y, heading) with kinodynamic or lattice planning: expand edges from precomputed feasible motion primitives or by forward simulation of controls, so every edge respects the turning radius. Hybrid A* keeps a continuous state in each grid cell. Then smooth the path and track it with a controller.
quiz: What is the configuration space of a planar robot arm with 3 revolute joints? || A 3-dimensional space with one angle per joint (a 3-torus, since angles wrap around). The whole arm is one point in it.
Why does RRT choose a random sample and then move only a small step toward it? || Sampling uniformly gives an unbiased drive toward unexplored regions, and a short step keeps each new edge small enough to be checked for collision cheaply and to be easy to connect to the tree.
Why is collision checking usually the bottleneck? || Every sample and every edge needs a check, and each check transforms all robot links and tests them against many obstacles. Planners need thousands to millions of checks, so the cost per check multiplies by that count.
A 7-joint arm uses a grid of 50 steps per joint. How many cells? || \( 50^7 = 7.8 \times 10^{11} \) cells, about 780 billion, too many to search or store.
When would you use PRM instead of RRT? || When the environment is static and there will be many start-goal queries, because the roadmap is built once and reused. When obstacles move often or there is just one query, RRT-type planners avoid the stale roadmap and the up-front cost.
