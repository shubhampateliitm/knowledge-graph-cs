@@ idgraph
value: An identity graph links the many identifiers that belong to the same person or household: browser cookies, mobile advertising IDs, CTV device IDs, smart-TV IDs, hashed emails and phone numbers, account IDs, and sometimes IP addresses and postal addresses. A single household might show up as twelve different IDs across two phones, a laptop, a tablet, a smart TV and a streaming stick. Without a graph, an advertiser sees twelve strangers; with one, it sees one household that watched an ad on TV and later bought on a phone.
That is why identity graphs sit underneath almost every cross-device use in advertising: targeting a household on CTV based on what it did on mobile, capping frequency across screens, measuring whether TV exposure led to a website visit or store purchase, and building audiences from first-party CRM data. Companies such as LiveRamp, Experian (which owns Tapad), TransUnion (which owns Neustar), The Trade Desk with UID2, and TV manufacturers and platforms with their own device data all maintain graphs, and the quality of a graph directly decides the quality of measurement built on it.
The lower layers supply the method. Graph algorithms (L3) turn "these two IDs were seen together" into households: a household is a connected component of the link graph. Union–find (L2) computes those components efficiently and incrementally. Probability (L4) turns weak evidence, such as two devices often using the same home IP address at night, into a calibrated confidence that two IDs share an owner, and tells you how errors compound when links are chained.
terms: Identifier (ID) — any value that recognizes a device, browser, account or person, such as a cookie ID, a mobile ad ID or a hashed email.
Deterministic link — a link based on direct evidence that two IDs share an owner, such as the same login seen on two devices.
Probabilistic link — a link inferred from indirect signals (shared IP, location, timing) with an estimated probability of being correct.
Household — the set of people and devices sharing one home, usually approximated by a residential IP address plus other signals.
Connected component — a maximal set of nodes that are all reachable from each other by links; the graph's notion of one person or household.
Over-merging — wrongly joining different people or households into one component, often through a shared IP or a bad link.
Under-merging — failing to join IDs that do belong together, so one household looks like several.
Match rate — the share of a partner's IDs that can be found in your graph.
history: In the early web, one browser cookie was a reasonable proxy for one person, and "identity" mostly meant cookie syncing between ad companies. Smartphones broke that assumption around 2010–2012: people now used several devices, and mobile apps used advertising IDs (Apple's IDFA from 2012, Google's advertising ID from 2013) instead of cookies. Companies such as Drawbridge (founded 2010, later bought by LinkedIn), Tapad (founded 2010, bought by Telenor and then Experian) and BlueCava built probabilistic cross-device graphs from IP addresses, locations and behaviour. Large platforms with logins (Google, Facebook, Amazon) built deterministic graphs from their users' sign-ins.
Offline identity had a longer history. Data companies such as Acxiom (whose identity business became LiveRamp, spun off in 2018) and credit bureaus had linked names, postal addresses and emails for direct mail for decades. Onboarding, matching a retailer's CRM file to online IDs, connected the two worlds in the 2010s.
Connected TV and privacy changes then reshaped the field. CTV devices carry their own resettable IDs (Roku's RIDA, Samsung's TIFA, Amazon's AFAI and others), and smart-TV makers can link TV viewing (through automatic content recognition) to households. Meanwhile Apple's App Tracking Transparency (April 2021) made the IDFA available only with opt-in, Safari and Firefox blocked third-party cookies, and privacy laws (GDPR, CCPA/CPRA, India's DPDP Act, California's Delete Act with its DROP deletion platform, where registered data brokers must process deletion requests from August 2026) put identity data under legal duties. The industry moved toward deterministic, consented, email-based identifiers (UID2, RampID, ID5's IDs) and toward household-level graphs anchored on residential IPs for CTV.
how: Building a graph starts with evidence. Each observation pairs two IDs with context: "device D1 and hashed email E were seen together at login on 2026-10-20", "TV T and phone P used IP address A between 21:00 and 23:00 on 14 of the last 30 days". These become candidate edges with a type, a source, timestamps and counts. Deterministic edges (login, email, a partner's matched record) are trusted directly but still checked: a shared family account or a test email used on hundreds of devices produces bad edges that must be filtered by rules such as "an email linked to more than 20 devices is suspicious".
Probabilistic edges need a model, and this is where probability adds value. Features describe how two IDs co-occur: number of shared IP addresses, nights spent on the same IP, overlap in active hours, distance between locations, whether the IP is residential, mobile carrier (CGNAT) or business. A classifier trained on pairs whose truth is known from deterministic links outputs P(same household). The output must be calibrated: an edge scored 0.9 should be right about 90% of the time, because thresholds and later decisions depend on the numbers meaning what they say. IP addresses are classified first: a home IP typically shows a handful of devices that return every night, while a coffee shop, office, hotel or carrier gateway shows hundreds of unrelated devices and must not be used to link anything.
Then graph algorithms turn edges into households and people. Treat IDs as nodes and accepted edges as links; each connected component is one identity cluster. Union–find computes components in nearly linear time: start with every ID in its own set, process each accepted edge with union(a, b), and use find(x) to get each ID's cluster representative. With path compression and union by rank, each operation costs effectively constant amortized time (the inverse Ackermann function, below 5 for any realistic size). At billions of IDs, the same idea runs distributed: Spark jobs use algorithms such as the "large-star / small-star" method of Kiveris and others (2014), which GraphFrames implements, iterating until every node points to the smallest ID in its component.
Probability also explains why components can go wrong. Transitivity multiplies errors: if each accepted probabilistic edge is correct with probability 0.95, a path of 10 such edges joins the right household with probability only 0.95¹⁰ ≈ 0.60. One wrong edge can merge two whole households, and a few wrong edges through a shared IP can create a "giant component" of thousands of devices. Real graphs therefore limit the damage: cap component size (a household rarely has more than about 15 to 20 devices), use stricter thresholds for edges that would merge two large components, keep person-level and household-level clusters separate, and give every link a decay or expiry so old evidence (a phone sold, a family member moved out) ages out.
The graph is rebuilt or updated on a schedule, often daily. Adding edges is easy with union–find: new evidence merges sets. Removing edges is not, because union–find cannot split a set. Expired links, corrected errors and deletion requests therefore usually trigger recomputation of affected components, or a full rebuild. Outputs are tables: id → cluster_id, cluster attributes (household size, devices, region), and edge provenance so every link can be explained and audited. Downstream systems join impressions, conversions and audiences through cluster_id.
Finally, graphs are evaluated. Precision asks how many linked pairs are truly the same household; recall asks how many true pairs were found. Both are measured on held-out ground truth (for example deterministic logins not used in training, or a consented panel). Match rates against partners, component size distributions and the share of IDs in giant clusters are monitored daily, because a single bad data source can corrupt the graph overnight.

  evidence (edges)                         components (households)
  TV-1  --[home IP, 25 nights]-- PHONE-1      +-----------------------+
  PHONE-1 --[login hashed email]-- LAPTOP-1   | H1: TV-1 PHONE-1      |
  TV-1  --[home IP, 18 nights]-- TABLET-1     |     LAPTOP-1 TABLET-1 |
  PHONE-2 --[home IP, 22 nights]-- TV-2       +-----------------------+
  PHONE-1 ..[cafe IP, 2 visits]... PHONE-2    | H2: TV-2 PHONE-2      |

              devices, not residential
  union(TV-1,PHONE-1) union(PHONE-1,LAPTOP-1) union(TV-1,TABLET-1)
  union(PHONE-2,TV-2)   -> find(LAPTOP-1) = find(TABLET-1) = H1
example: Consider six IDs: a smart TV (TV-1), a phone (P-1), a laptop (L-1), a tablet (T-1), a second phone (P-2) and a second TV (TV-2). The evidence is: P-1 and L-1 logged into the same streaming account with the same hashed email (deterministic). TV-1 and P-1 shared a residential IP on 25 of the last 30 nights; the model scores this 0.97. TV-1 and T-1 shared it on 18 nights, score 0.93. P-2 and TV-2 shared a different residential IP on 22 nights, score 0.95. P-1 and P-2 were both seen twice on an IP that had 400 distinct devices in a week; the IP is classified as public Wi-Fi and the pair is never scored.
With a threshold of 0.9, four edges are accepted. Union–find processes them: union(P-1, L-1) gives {P-1, L-1}; union(TV-1, P-1) gives {TV-1, P-1, L-1}; union(TV-1, T-1) adds T-1; union(P-2, TV-2) gives {P-2, TV-2}. The result is two households, H1 with four devices and H2 with two. If the café edge had been accepted, H1 and H2 would have merged into one six-device household, and a campaign measured on H1's TV would wrongly get credit for purchases made on P-2.
Now look at how confidence compounds. The tablet joins the laptop only through the path T-1, TV-1, P-1, L-1. If we treat the link probabilities as independent, the chance that all three links are right is 0.93 × 0.97 × 1.0 ≈ 0.90. That is fine for household targeting, but for person-level use (is this laptop's owner the same person as the tablet's?) it says nothing at all, because the household edges never claimed the same person.
At scale, the numbers change the engineering. A graph of 2 billion IDs and 6 billion candidate edges, with union–find running distributed in Spark using the large-star / small-star method, typically needs a handful of iterations (each a full shuffle of the edges) to converge. Tracking the size distribution is the main health check: if the largest component suddenly contains 50,000 IDs, a bad IP or a bad data source was accepted, and the run must be stopped before downstream audiences and measurement use it.
intuition: A family tree for devices: each piece of evidence says "these two belong together", and the families are whatever groups those links connect.
The connected-component view is natural because evidence arrives in pairs, while questions are asked about groups. Union–find matches that exactly: each new link either does nothing (same family already) or joins two families. Probability is needed because most evidence about homes is circumstantial, the same way we guess that two people live together if we see their cars outside the same house every night. And the main danger is equally intuitive: one mistaken link joins two families, and a shared parking lot can make a whole street look like one family.
mistakes: People often think a bigger graph with a higher match rate is always better. In fact over-merging inflates match rates while corrupting measurement and frequency capping. A graph with fewer, more accurate links is usually more valuable than one that links everything.
People often think IP address equals household. Many IPs are shared (carrier-grade NAT on mobile networks, offices, hotels, public Wi-Fi) or change over time, and IPv6 gives each device many addresses. Residential IPs are useful evidence only after classification and time-window checks.
People often think a person graph and a household graph are the same thing. A household graph says devices share a home; it cannot say which person used which device. Using household links for person-level decisions (personalized offers, sensitive categories) creates errors and privacy problems.
People often think deleting a person from an identity graph is a simple row delete. Their IDs may have contributed links that merged components, downstream audiences and aggregates may contain them, and union–find structures cannot split. Deletion needs recomputation of affected components and propagation to every derived dataset.
friction: Ground truth is scarce, so accuracy claims are hard to verify, and vendors' graphs disagree with each other substantially. Signals keep shrinking: App Tracking Transparency, third-party cookie blocking in Safari and Firefox, IP address masking in some browsers and private relays, and consent requirements all reduce evidence. CTV is a special case: TV device IDs are fairly stable and often tied to a home network, which helps household graphs, but resettable IDs and shared devices complicate them.
Regulation adds heavy obligations. Identity graphs are personal data under GDPR, CCPA/CPRA and India's DPDP Act; users have rights to access and deletion; California's Delete Act requires registered data brokers to process deletion requests submitted through the DROP platform, regularly and at scale. Propagating deletions through clusters, audiences and partner syncs is expensive. Finally, graphs drift: people move, devices are sold, families change, and links that were true last year are wrong today, so freshness rules matter as much as matching algorithms.
practice: Keep link provenance: for every edge, store its source, evidence type, first and last seen dates and score, so you can explain and remove links later. Classify IP addresses before using them (count distinct devices per IP per day, check carrier and hosting ranges) and never link on shared or business IPs.
Calibrate probabilistic models on held-out deterministic truth, set thresholds per use case (higher for measurement than for reach extension), and cap component sizes with alerts on the size distribution. Separate household and person clusters, and give links a time-to-live.
Treat deletions as a first-class pipeline: record deleted identifiers (hashed) in a suppression list checked during every rebuild, recompute affected components, and propagate to audiences and partners with an audit trail. Report graph health daily: number of IDs, clusters, share of IDs in clusters, largest component, match rates per partner and precision on a fixed test set.
quiz: What is the difference between a deterministic and a probabilistic link? || A deterministic link comes from direct evidence of a shared owner, such as the same login on two devices. A probabilistic link is inferred from indirect signals like a shared home IP, with a model's estimate of how likely it is to be right.
Why is union–find a good fit for building households, and what can it not do? || Evidence arrives as pairs, and union–find merges the sets of two IDs in nearly constant amortized time, giving connected components directly. It cannot split a set, so removing links or deleting IDs requires recomputing components.
Each accepted link is correct with probability 0.95. What is the chance that a chain of 8 links joins only the right household? || 0.95⁸ ≈ 0.66, so about a one-in-three chance that the chain contains at least one wrong link.
Your graph's largest component jumps from 18 devices to 40,000 overnight. What most likely happened, and what do you do? || A shared IP or a bad data source (for example a test email on many devices) produced edges that merged many households. Stop downstream use, find the edges joining the giant component, add the IP or source to a blocklist, and rebuild.
When would you prefer a smaller deterministic graph over a larger probabilistic one? || For measurement and sensitive or person-level uses, where wrong links distort results or privacy. A probabilistic extension can still help reach and frequency capping, where errors are cheaper.

@@ cookies
value: A cookie is a small piece of data that a website asks the browser to store and send back on later requests. It is how the stateless HTTP protocol remembers anything: that you are logged in, what is in your cart, which language you chose. Advertising found a second use: a cookie set by an ad server embedded on thousands of sites lets that ad server recognize the same browser everywhere, which made cross-site targeting, frequency capping and conversion measurement possible. Device IDs play the same role in apps and on CTV: Apple's IDFA, Google's advertising ID, and the resettable IDs of TV and streaming platforms.
These identifiers are the raw material of digital advertising. Pixels and tags carry them, exchanges pass them in bid requests, identity graphs link them, and attribution counts conversions by them. Their restriction over the past decade (Safari and Firefox blocking third-party cookies, Apple's App Tracking Transparency, consent rules under GDPR, and limits on lifetimes) is the main force reshaping ad tech toward first-party data, logged-in identifiers, contextual targeting and privacy technologies such as clean rooms.
The lower layers define how cookies work and why they are restricted. HTTP (L3) carries them in two headers, Set-Cookie and Cookie, and its statelessness is the reason they exist. Web security (L3) explains their flags (Secure, HttpOnly, SameSite): cookies are sent automatically with requests, which is exactly what attacks such as cross-site request forgery and session theft exploit.
terms: First-party cookie — a cookie set by the site shown in the address bar.
Third-party cookie — a cookie set by another domain whose content is embedded in the page, such as an ad server or analytics script.
Set-Cookie / Cookie — the HTTP response header that stores a cookie and the request header that sends it back.
SameSite — a cookie attribute (Strict, Lax, None) that controls whether a cookie is sent on cross-site requests.
HttpOnly and Secure — attributes that hide a cookie from JavaScript and send it only over HTTPS.
Cookie syncing — a redirect chain in which two ad companies learn each other's ID for the same browser and store the pair in a match table.
Advertising ID (IFA) — a resettable device identifier for ads on mobile or CTV, such as IDFA, the Android advertising ID or a TV platform's ID.
Partitioned cookie (CHIPS) — a third-party cookie stored separately per top-level site, so it cannot track across sites.
history: Lou Montulli at Netscape invented cookies in 1994 to let a shopping site remember a cart, and they shipped in Netscape Navigator that year. Third-party use appeared almost immediately: DoubleClick, founded in 1996, used its own cookie across the many sites that showed its ads to cap frequency and build profiles. The IETF standardized cookies in RFC 2109 (1997) and again in RFC 6265 (2011); privacy concerns were raised from the start, and in 2000 DoubleClick's plan to combine online cookies with offline names and addresses caused a public scandal and an FTC inquiry.
Mobile apps had no shared cookie jar, so platforms created advertising IDs: Apple's IDFA in iOS 6 (2012) and Google's advertising ID in 2013, both resettable by the user. CTV platforms followed with their own IDs, and the IAB Tech Lab published guidelines for identifiers on OTT devices so they could travel in bid requests with a type field.
Then restrictions began. Apple's Intelligent Tracking Prevention in Safari (2017) limited third-party cookies and reached full blocking in 2020; Firefox blocked known trackers by default from 2019 and partitioned all third-party cookies with Total Cookie Protection by 2022. Chrome made SameSite=Lax the default in 2020 and capped cookie lifetimes at 400 days in 2022. Google announced in 2020 that Chrome would phase out third-party cookies, delayed several times, then in 2024–2025 dropped the plan, keeping third-party cookies, and in October 2025 announced it would retire most Privacy Sandbox APIs. Apple's App Tracking Transparency (iOS 14.5, April 2021) made the IDFA available only after an opt-in prompt that most users decline. The industry responded with email-based IDs (UID2, RampID), server-side tagging and first-party data strategies.
how: The mechanism is simple. A server responds with a header such as Set-Cookie: uid=8f3a2c; Domain=ads.example; Path=/; Max-Age=31536000; Secure; HttpOnly; SameSite=None. The browser stores it in its cookie jar, keyed by domain and path. On every later request whose URL matches that domain and path, and that satisfies the attributes, the browser adds Cookie: uid=8f3a2c automatically. A cookie may hold about 4 KB, and browsers limit how many cookies each domain may keep. This automatic sending is what HTTP lacked: the server can now connect requests into a session.
First-party versus third-party depends only on context. When you visit news.example, cookies for news.example are first-party. If that page loads an image or script from ads.example, the request to ads.example carries ads.example's cookies, and in that context they are third-party. Because the same ads.example cookie is sent from every site that embeds ads.example, the ad server sees one identifier across all of them. That is the entire basis of cross-site tracking: no special technology, just automatic sending of a cookie scoped to a widely embedded domain.
The security attributes come from web security lessons. HttpOnly hides a cookie from JavaScript, so a cross-site scripting bug cannot read a session cookie. Secure sends it only over HTTPS. SameSite limits cross-site sending: Strict never sends the cookie on requests started by other sites, Lax sends it only on top-level navigations such as clicking a link, and None sends it everywhere but requires Secure. Since browsers made Lax the default in 2020, cross-site request forgery became much harder, and ad and embed providers must set SameSite=None; Secure explicitly to keep third-party behaviour where browsers still allow it.
Cookie syncing connects companies. A DSP and an SSP each have their own cookie ID for the same browser, but neither can read the other's cookie, because cookies are scoped to their domain. During a page view, the SSP's pixel redirects the browser to a DSP URL with the SSP's ID in a query parameter; the DSP's server receives its own cookie and the SSP's ID in the same request and stores the pair in a match table. Later, when the SSP sends a bid request with its user ID, the DSP looks up its own ID. Each sync costs a redirect and is lossy, so match rates between companies are often well below 100%.
Apps and CTV use device IDs instead. The app's SDK reads the platform's advertising ID through an API (on iOS only after the user allows tracking) and sends it with ad requests; OpenRTB carries it in device.ifa along with a type. On CTV, the TV or streaming device exposes a resettable ID, and server-side ad insertion systems pass it to ad servers on the viewer's behalf. Users can reset these IDs or opt out of tracking, which platforms signal with flags (for example limit-ad-tracking) that buyers must respect.
Browsers now go further. Safari limits cookies written by JavaScript to 7 days in many cases and blocks third-party cookies. Firefox partitions them by top-level site. Partitioned cookies (CHIPS) let an embedded service keep state per site, for example a chat widget, without tracking across sites. These changes keep cookies' original purpose, state within a site, while removing their tracking use.
What counts as "the same site" is itself a mechanism. Browsers compare registrable domains, computed with the Public Suffix List: shop.example.co.uk and www.example.co.uk are the same site, because co.uk is a public suffix and example.co.uk is the registrable part, while two different .co.uk companies are different sites. Trackers have tried to look first-party by putting a subdomain of the publisher (metrics.news.example) behind a DNS CNAME that points to the tracker's servers, so their cookies are set in the publisher's own domain. Safari responded by limiting cookies set through such CNAME-cloaked responses to 7 days, and some blockers resolve CNAMEs to detect the trick. Server-side tagging is the legitimate version of the same move: the publisher runs its own collection endpoint under its domain, sets a first-party cookie, and forwards selected events to vendors from its servers, which gives the publisher control over what is shared, and also makes the data flow invisible to the user's browser tools.

  browser ---GET ssp.example/sync  Cookie: sspid=K77 ------> SSP
          <--302 Location: dsp.example/match?ssp_uid=K77
  browser ---GET dsp.example/match?ssp_uid=K77 Cookie: dspid=Q12-> DSP
          DSP stores match table row: (ssp K77) <-> (dsp Q12)
example: Follow one browser through an ad journey. On Monday the user reads news.example, which embeds an SSP tag. The SSP finds no cookie, so it sets sspid=K77, valid for one year, with SameSite=None; Secure. The page also triggers a sync: the browser is redirected to dsp.example/match?ssp_uid=K77, where the DSP sets dspid=Q12 and records K77 ↔ Q12.
On Tuesday the user visits recipes.example, which uses the same SSP. The bid request carries user.id = K77. The DSP looks up K77, finds Q12, sees that Q12 visited a car maker's site last week (through the car maker's pixel), and bids higher. The ad wins and the impression pixel fires with Q12. On Friday the user buys from the car maker's site; the conversion pixel fires on ads.carmaker's tag with Q12, and the DSP credits the conversion to Tuesday's impression.
Now repeat the journey in Safari. The SSP's cookie is third-party on news.example and is blocked, so there is no K77; the sync fails, the bid request has no user ID, and the DSP can only bid on context (recipes page, time, region). The conversion on Friday cannot be linked to the impression. This is why the same campaign can show very different measured performance by browser, and why buyers push for logged-in, consented IDs.
Some simple arithmetic shows the cost of syncing. If the SSP and DSP each see the browser on most days but the sync pixel fires only on 60% of page views and succeeds 80% of the time, then a week's match rate for active users might be 85 to 95%, but for occasional users seen once, it is about 0.6 × 0.8 = 48%. Every additional company in the chain (an SSP reselling through another SSP) multiplies such losses.
intuition: A cloakroom ticket: the browser shows the same ticket every time it visits the counter that issued it, and an ad company with counters in thousands of buildings sees the same ticket everywhere.
Cookies are natural because HTTP forgets everything between requests; a small token handed back automatically is the cheapest way to remember. Their tracking use follows from one rule, "send the cookie whenever you talk to that domain", applied across sites that embed the same domain. Browser restrictions are equally natural: keep the cloakroom ticket working inside one building, but stop sharing one ticket across every building in the city.
mistakes: People often think a third-party cookie is a special kind of cookie. It is an ordinary cookie whose domain differs from the site in the address bar; the same cookie is first-party when you visit that domain directly.
People often think blocking third-party cookies ends cross-site tracking. Tracking moves to other identifiers: logins and hashed emails, server-side data sharing, IP addresses and device fingerprinting. Google even changed its advertising policy in February 2025 to allow fingerprinting-like signals such as IP addresses, which regulators such as the UK ICO criticized.
People often think device advertising IDs are permanent. Users can reset them, limit ad tracking or, on iOS, refuse tracking, in which case the IDFA returns zeros. Systems keyed on them must handle resets and opt-out signals.
People often think hashing an email makes it anonymous. A hashed email is a stable identifier that anyone with the same email can reproduce; it is pseudonymous personal data under GDPR and similar laws.
friction: The supply of identifiers keeps shrinking and fragmenting: Safari and Firefox block third-party cookies, Chrome keeps them but with user controls and shorter lifetimes, iOS users mostly decline tracking, and CTV IDs differ by platform. Measurement becomes inconsistent across browsers and devices, and walled gardens with logged-in users gain an advantage over the open web.
Consent requirements add friction everywhere: banners under GDPR and the ePrivacy rules, consent strings passed through bid requests (IAB Transparency and Consent Framework, the Global Privacy Platform), and legal disputes about whether those mechanisms are valid. Replacements have their own problems: email-based IDs need logins and reach only a part of traffic; fingerprinting is hard to detect, impossible for users to control and legally risky; server-side tracking moves data flows out of the user's view. Clean rooms and aggregated measurement are more private but less granular.
practice: For your own site, use first-party cookies with HttpOnly, Secure and an explicit SameSite value; keep session cookies short and never store sensitive data in a cookie value. For embedded services that need state, use partitioned cookies (CHIPS) rather than relying on third-party cookies.
For advertising systems, treat every identifier as personal data: record consent signals with it, honour opt-outs and resets, and set retention limits. Measure match rates and ID availability by browser, OS and device type, and report performance per segment so cookie loss is not mistaken for campaign failure.
Plan for a mixed world: logged-in and email-based IDs where users consent, household and device IDs on CTV, contextual signals everywhere, and aggregate or clean-room measurement for cross-party questions. Keep the identifier layer separate from business logic so new ID types can be added without rewriting targeting and measurement.
quiz: What makes a cookie "third-party"? || Only context: it belongs to a domain other than the site in the address bar, typically an embedded ad or analytics server. The same cookie is first-party when you visit that domain directly.
How does cookie syncing let a DSP use an SSP's user ID? || The SSP redirects the browser to the DSP with its own ID in the URL. The DSP receives that ID together with its own cookie in one request and stores the pair in a match table, which it uses to translate later bid requests.
A sync pixel fires on 50% of page views and succeeds 90% of the time. What is the match rate for users seen only once? || 0.5 × 0.9 = 45%. Users seen many times have higher match rates, because each visit is another chance to sync.
Why did browsers make SameSite=Lax the default, and what did it change for ad tech? || To block cross-site request forgery, where another site triggers requests that carry your cookies. Ad and embed providers then had to mark their cookies SameSite=None; Secure explicitly to keep cross-site behaviour where it is still allowed.
Your campaign's measured conversion rate is three times higher on Chrome than on Safari. What should you check before concluding that Safari users convert less? || Whether conversions can be linked to impressions on Safari at all: third-party cookie blocking and short cookie lifetimes break the link, so many Safari conversions are unattributed rather than missing.
