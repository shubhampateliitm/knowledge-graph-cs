@@ identity
value: Identity resolution and audience building is the system that turns raw signals about devices into groups of people an advertiser can reach and measure. It takes in identifiers from many sources (pixels on websites, app SDKs, CTV and smart-TV device IDs, automatic content recognition (ACR) viewing data, logins, advertisers' CRM files), links them into households and people, attaches behaviour and attributes, and produces audience segments such as "households that watched live cricket three times this month" or "recent visitors to a car maker's site who have not converted". Those segments are then sent to buying platforms, used for frequency capping, and used to measure whether campaigns worked.
It is the data backbone of addressable advertising, especially on CTV, where the buyer wants TV-like reach with digital-like targeting and measurement. Every company in the chain runs some version of it: TV platforms and manufacturers, DSPs, data providers such as LiveRamp, Experian and TransUnion, retail media networks, and the clean rooms between them.
Three lower concepts carry the weight. Cookies and device IDs (L2) are the raw identifiers, with all their gaps and restrictions. Identity graphs (L2) link those identifiers into households and people, using deterministic and probabilistic evidence and connected components. Privacy technology (L2), clean rooms and differential privacy, lets companies match and measure audiences with partners without handing over raw personal data. Bitmaps, key-value stores and batch and stream processing supply the machinery for computing and serving segments at scale.
terms: Identifier — a value that recognizes a browser, device, account or person: cookie ID, mobile or CTV advertising ID, hashed email, account ID.
Profile — everything known about one person or household, keyed by its cluster ID from the identity graph.
Audience segment — a named set of profiles that meet a definition, such as "sports viewers, last 30 days".
Seed audience — a known set of good examples (for example past buyers) used to find similar profiles.
Lookalike modelling — scoring all profiles by similarity to a seed and taking the top ones.
Onboarding — matching offline data (names, addresses, emails from a CRM) to online identifiers.
Activation — sending a segment to the systems that will use it, such as a DSP or ad server, in their ID space.
Recency and frequency — how long ago and how often a behaviour happened; the two most common segment conditions.
Suppression — removing profiles from a segment, for example people who opted out or already converted.
history: In the first era of online advertising (late 1990s to about 2012), identity meant a third-party cookie, and audiences were built by ad networks and data management platforms (DMPs) such as BlueKai (founded 2008, bought by Oracle in 2014) and Krux (bought by Salesforce in 2016). Tags on publishers' and advertisers' sites dropped cookies into segments, and cookie syncing moved segments between companies.
Mobile and cross-device use broke the single-cookie model in the 2010s. Cross-device graph companies (Tapad, Drawbridge), onboarding services (LiveRamp, spun out of Acxiom in 2018) and walled gardens with logged-in users (Facebook's Custom Audiences from 2012, Google's Customer Match from 2015) made identity more deterministic and people-based. CTV added household-level identity: TV device IDs, home IP addresses, and smart-TV ACR data that records what a screen actually showed.
From 2017 onward, privacy changes reshaped the stack: Safari and Firefox blocked third-party cookies, GDPR (2018) and CCPA (2020) required consent and deletion rights, Apple's App Tracking Transparency (2021) cut mobile ID availability, and India's DPDP Act (2023) and California's Delete Act with its DROP platform (data brokers must process deletions from August 2026) added obligations. DMPs faded and customer data platforms (CDPs) and clean rooms took their place, built on first-party data, hashed emails (UID2, RampID) and aggregate measurement.
how: Think of the system as a pipeline with five stages: collect, resolve, describe, segment, activate, with consent and deletion checks running through all of them.
Collect. Identifiers arrive from many places. Website pixels and tags send events with cookies; app SDKs send advertising IDs; CTV apps and server-side ad insertion send device IDs and IP addresses; smart TVs send ACR viewing records; advertisers upload CRM files. Each identifier is normalized before use: emails trimmed and lowercased, then hashed with SHA-256; phone numbers converted to a standard format (E.164) before hashing; IP addresses classified as residential, mobile or business. Each record keeps its source, timestamp and consent status. The raw identifiers and their restrictions come from the cookies and device-ID concept.
Resolve. The identity graph links identifiers into households and people (the identity-graph concept): deterministic links from logins and hashed emails, probabilistic links from shared home IPs and co-occurrence, connected components through union–find, size caps to prevent over-merging. The output is a mapping from every identifier to a cluster ID, refreshed daily or more often.
Describe. Behaviour and attributes are attached to clusters. Events are aggregated into features per household: hours of sports watched in 30 days, genres, channels, apps used, sites visited, purchases, location at region level, device types. This is usually a batch job in Spark or a lakehouse that produces a wide profile table, plus a streaming path that updates a few fast-moving features (such as "visited the pricing page in the last hour") in a key-value store.
Segment. Segment definitions are rules, models or both. Rule-based segments are filters on profile features ("sports_hours_30d ≥ 3 AND region = Maharashtra"). Model-based segments score every profile, for example a lookalike model trained to separate a seed of past buyers from random households, and keep the top N. Segment membership is materialized as sets of cluster IDs. Bitmaps (often compressed Roaring bitmaps) make this efficient: each segment is a bitmap over cluster numbers, so "sports AND not recent buyers" is one bitwise operation, and audience sizes for planning tools can be computed in milliseconds over hundreds of millions of households.
Activate. A segment is useful only once another system can use it. Activation translates cluster IDs back into the identifiers each destination understands (CTV device IDs for a TV platform, cookie IDs or UID2 tokens for a DSP, hashed emails for a walled garden), applies suppression lists, and pushes the result by file or API, often daily. When the destination is a partner that must not see your raw data, the match happens in a clean room instead, and only matched counts or approved outputs leave (the privacy-technology concept). Measurement closes the loop: exposure logs and outcomes (site visits, sales, tune-ins) are joined through the same graph to compute reach, frequency and lift.
Consent and deletion run through every stage. Each identifier carries its consent status, and segments exclude identifiers without the right permission for that use. Deletion requests (from users directly or through platforms such as California's DROP) must remove identifiers from raw data, the graph, profiles, segments and partner syncs; in practice this means a suppression list applied at every rebuild, plus an audit trail.
Serving adds a real-time path. Some decisions need audience membership in milliseconds, for example a DSP deciding whether a bid request's device belongs to a campaign's segment, or a CTV ad server choosing an ad for a household. Batch jobs therefore export membership into a low-latency key-value store keyed by device or cookie ID, with the list of segment IDs as the value. The sizes are large but manageable: 500 million identifiers with about 100 bytes of segment data each is about 50 GB, which a replicated in-memory or SSD-backed store (Aerospike, Redis or Valkey, Cassandra-like stores) can serve with lookups under a millisecond. Lookalike models usually run in batch too: a classifier (for example gradient-boosted trees over profile features) is trained with the seed audience as positives and a random sample of households as negatives, then scores every household; the planner picks a threshold that trades size against similarity, often checked by how well the top-scored households would have predicted a held-out part of the seed.

example: A beverage brand wants to reach households that watch a lot of live cricket on CTV in India's top metros, but not households that already bought its new drink.
Start with the data. The TV platform's ACR records show 40 million smart-TV households. Among them, 6.2 million watched at least 3 hours of live cricket in the last 30 days, computed from viewing events aggregated per household. Restricting to the top six metros leaves 2.1 million. Each household is a cluster in the identity graph, with on average 1.3 TV device IDs and 2.4 mobile or web identifiers attached.
Now suppression. The brand uploads hashed emails of 900,000 recent buyers. Through deterministic links (logins to the TV maker's account and partner data) the graph matches 32% of them, about 288,000, to households; 41,000 of those are in the cricket segment and are removed, leaving about 2.06 million households.
With bitmaps, this whole computation is two bitwise operations: CRICKET_30D AND METRO_TOP6, then AND NOT BUYERS. With Roaring bitmaps over 40 million households, each bitmap takes a few megabytes at most, and the result is ready in milliseconds, which is why planning tools can show audience sizes interactively.
Activation. For CTV delivery on the platform's own inventory, the 2.06 million households translate directly to device IDs. For a DSP buying on other CTV apps and mobile, the segment is translated to device IDs and UID2 tokens; suppose 70% of households have at least one identifier the DSP recognizes, so the addressable audience there is about 1.44 million households.
Measurement. After the campaign, exposed households are compared with a holdout of 10% of the segment that was deliberately not targeted. If exposed households buy at 2.6% and holdout households at 2.2%, the lift is about 18%. The quality of this number depends on every earlier stage: an over-merged graph or poor buyer matching would blur exposed and holdout groups and bias the result toward zero.
intuition: Connect the dots between devices to find the household, describe what the household does, then group households the way a media planner thinks: by what they watch and buy.
Each stage mirrors a manual process. A salesperson recognizes a returning customer (resolve), remembers what they like (describe), groups customers for a promotion (segment) and sends the right offer through the right channel (activate). The system does the same for millions of households every day. The engineering choices follow from scale: hashing and normalizing to match safely, graphs to connect, bitmaps to combine groups instantly, and clean rooms when the dots belong to someone else.
mistakes: People often think a bigger audience is a better one. Audiences grow by loosening definitions, extending lookalikes or adding probabilistic links, and each step lowers precision. For measurement and expensive CTV impressions, a smaller, accurate audience usually performs better.
People often think match rate measures data quality. Match rate only says how many identifiers were found in your graph; an over-merged graph matches more and is less accurate. Precision on known truth sets matters more.
People often think segments are static lists. Behaviour changes daily and identifiers churn; a "cricket fan" list built three months ago has stale members and misses new ones. Recency windows and regular refreshes are part of the definition.
People often think consent is checked once at collection. Consent can be withdrawn, differs by purpose (measurement versus targeting) and by region, and must be enforced again when segments are built and activated, including at partners.
friction: Signal loss is constant: cookie blocking, opt-in mobile IDs, IP masking and consent requirements shrink and fragment the raw identifiers, and each destination supports different ID types, so the same audience reaches different fractions of people on different channels. Matching across companies loses a large share at every step, and nobody sees the whole picture.
Accuracy is hard to prove. Ground truth is limited, vendor graphs disagree, and errors compound from identity to segments to measurement. Regulation adds real cost: purpose-specific consent, data minimization, deletion propagation across many systems and partners, and data-broker obligations in some jurisdictions. Sensitive categories (health, religion, children) need special handling or are off limits. Finally, the system is complex to operate: daily graph rebuilds, many large batch jobs, streaming updates and partner syncs all have to succeed for a campaign to deliver on time.
practice: Design around identifiers you can keep: first-party logins, consented hashed emails, CTV device and household IDs, and contextual signals, rather than third-party cookies. Keep the identity graph, the profile table and segment definitions as separate layers with clear contracts, so each can be rebuilt and audited.
Version segment definitions like code, record their size over time, and alert on sudden changes, which usually mean an upstream data problem. Use bitmaps for segment storage and sizing. Measure precision of identity links and of key segments on held-out truth, not only match rates.
Build consent and deletion into the data model from day one: per-identifier consent flags, suppression lists applied at every rebuild, and deletion logs that prove propagation. Prefer clean rooms for partner matching, and always keep a randomized holdout for measuring whether audiences actually change outcomes.
quiz: What are the five stages of an identity and audience system? || Collect identifiers, resolve them into households and people with an identity graph, describe each cluster with features, build segments with rules or models, and activate segments to destinations, with consent and deletion applied throughout.
Why are bitmaps a good data structure for audience segments? || Each segment is a set of cluster numbers, and bitmaps turn set operations (AND, OR, AND NOT) into fast bitwise operations with compact compressed storage. Combinations and sizes can be computed in milliseconds over hundreds of millions of households.
A seed of 500,000 hashed emails matches at 30%, and 80% of matched households are reachable at a DSP. How many households can the DSP target? || 500,000 × 0.30 = 150,000 households, and 150,000 × 0.80 = 120,000 reachable at the DSP.
Why does an over-merged identity graph bias a lift measurement toward zero? || Wrongly merged households mix exposed and unexposed people inside the same cluster, so exposed and holdout groups look more alike, and the measured difference shrinks.
When would you build a segment in a clean room instead of activating your own list to a partner? || When the partner must not receive your raw identifiers or customer list, for example matching CRM buyers with a publisher's viewers. The clean room allows matching and aggregate outputs without either side seeing the other's data.

@@ experimentation
value: An online controlled experiment, usually called an A/B test, randomly splits users (or households, devices, regions) into groups, changes one thing for some groups, and measures the difference with statistics. It is the most reliable way to answer causal questions such as "did the new ranking model increase watch time?" or "did this CTV campaign cause more store visits?". Observational data cannot answer these reliably, because the people who see a change usually differ from those who do not in many other ways.
Large tech companies run thousands of experiments a year (Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Booking, Netflix, LinkedIn and others have published about their platforms), and most ideas tested turn out to have no effect or a negative one. In advertising, the same logic underpins lift studies: a randomized holdout of the target audience does not see the ads, and the difference in outcomes between exposed and holdout groups is the campaign's causal effect.
The method rests on lower concepts. Sampling (L2) makes each group a random sample of the same population, so groups differ only by the change and by chance. Hypothesis testing (L4) decides whether an observed difference is larger than chance would produce. The central limit theorem (L4) makes averages over many users approximately normal, which is what makes standard tests and confidence intervals valid. Multi-armed bandits (L2) offer an alternative when the goal is to earn while learning rather than to measure precisely.
terms: Treatment and control — the group that gets the change and the group that does not.
Randomization unit — what is randomly assigned: a user, a device, a household, a session, a city.
Primary metric — the one outcome the decision depends on, chosen before the test.
Minimum detectable effect (MDE) — the smallest true effect the test is designed to detect reliably.
Statistical power — the probability of detecting an effect of at least the MDE if it exists; usually 80%.
p-value — the probability of seeing a difference at least this large if the change had no effect.
Sample ratio mismatch (SRM) — group sizes that differ from the planned split by more than chance; a sign of a broken experiment.
CUPED — a variance-reduction method that uses each user's pre-experiment data to remove predictable noise.
Guardrail metric — a metric that must not get worse (latency, errors, revenue) even if the primary metric improves.
history: Ronald Fisher developed randomized experiments and significance testing at the Rothamsted agricultural station in the 1920s and wrote "The Design of Experiments" (1935). Medicine adopted randomized controlled trials after the 1948 streptomycin trial. Direct marketers used split mailings for decades before the web.
On the web, experiments became cheap and fast. Amazon, Google, Microsoft and others built platforms in the 2000s; Google's 2010 paper on overlapping experiments described running many tests on the same users at once by splitting the system into layers. Ron Kohavi's team at Microsoft published widely on pitfalls and practices, later collected in "Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments" (Kohavi, Tang and Xu, 2020). Methods kept improving: CUPED variance reduction (Deng and others, 2013), always-valid sequential tests that allow continuous monitoring (Johari and others, 2017), and designs for interference in marketplaces and advertising, such as switchback tests, geo experiments (Vaver and Koehler at Google, 2011) and budget-split designs.
Advertising developed its own variants: public-service-announcement holdouts, "ghost ads" that record when a holdout user would have been shown an ad without showing it (Johnson, Lewis and Nubbemeyer, 2017), and conversion lift tools offered by large platforms.
how: Assignment comes first. A platform computes bucket = hash(experiment_salt + user_id) mod 1000 and maps bucket ranges to variants, for example 0–499 control and 500–999 treatment. Hashing makes assignment random with respect to user attributes, consistent (the same user always gets the same variant) and stateless. A different salt per experiment keeps experiments independent, which is how many tests run on the same users at once. This is the sampling concept at work: each group is a random sample, so in expectation the groups are identical except for the change.
Then data is collected and summarized per randomization unit. Metrics are computed per user (sessions, conversions, revenue) and averaged per group. The difference in means estimates the causal effect. The central limit theorem says each group's mean is approximately normal with variance σ²/n, so the difference of two means has standard error √(σ²/n₁ + σ²/n₂). A 95% confidence interval is the difference ± 1.96 standard errors; if it excludes zero, the result is significant at the 5% level. This is hypothesis testing: the null hypothesis is "no effect", and the p-value measures how surprising the data would be under it.
Sample size is planned before starting. For a two-sided test at 5% significance and 80% power, each group needs about n ≈ 16σ² / δ², where δ is the absolute effect you want to detect. The 16 comes from (1.96 + 0.84)² × 2 ≈ 15.7. Small effects are expensive: halving δ quadruples n. Variance reduction lowers σ². CUPED subtracts the part of each user's metric that is predicted by their pre-experiment value of the same metric; if the correlation between pre and post periods is ρ, variance shrinks by a factor of (1 − ρ²), so ρ = 0.7 cuts the needed sample size roughly in half.
Checks protect against broken experiments. A sample ratio mismatch test (a chi-square test on group counts) catches bugs in assignment, logging or bot filtering; when it fails, the results cannot be trusted. Guardrail metrics catch harm the primary metric misses. A/A tests, where both groups get the same experience, check that the system does not produce false positives more than 5% of the time.
The classic error is peeking. If you check results every day and stop as soon as p < 0.05, the true false-positive rate is much higher than 5%: with five equally spaced looks it is about 14%, and with continuous monitoring it approaches 100% over a long enough time. Fixes are to fix the sample size in advance, to use group-sequential designs with adjusted thresholds, or to use always-valid methods (such as mixture sequential probability ratio tests) designed for continuous monitoring.
Interference breaks the basic assumption that one unit's treatment does not affect another's outcome. In advertising, treatment and control campaigns share budgets and compete in the same auctions; in marketplaces, buyers in one group change prices for the other; in social products, friends influence each other. Designs that respect the interference randomize at a coarser level: by region (geo experiments), by time period (switchbacks), or by splitting budgets so each group has its own (budget-split). Bandits change the goal: instead of a fixed 50/50 split for precise measurement, they shift traffic toward better-performing variants during the test, which earns more but gives less precise estimates.
Two subtleties cause many wrong conclusions. The first is the difference between the randomization unit and the analysis unit. If users are randomized but the metric is click-through rate per impression, impressions from the same user are correlated, and treating them as independent understates the standard error, producing false positives. The fix is to compute ratio metrics per group as (total clicks) / (total impressions) and estimate their variance with the delta method, or to aggregate per user first. The second is multiple comparisons: a test with 20 metrics and 5 segments offers 100 chances for something to look significant at 5% by luck. Platforms therefore name one primary metric in advance, treat the rest as diagnostics, and apply corrections such as Benjamini–Hochberg when many metrics are reported.

example: A streaming app tests a new recommendation model. The primary metric is the share of users who start a paid subscription within 14 days. The baseline rate is p = 2.0%, and the team wants to detect a 5% relative improvement, from 2.00% to 2.10%, so δ = 0.001.
For a conversion metric, σ² = p(1 − p) = 0.02 × 0.98 = 0.0196. The required sample size per group is n ≈ 16 × 0.0196 / 0.001² = 313,600 users. With 100,000 eligible new users a day split evenly, each group gets 50,000 a day, so the test needs about 7 days, which also covers a full weekly cycle.
After 7 days: control has 320,000 users and 6,400 conversions (2.00%); treatment has 320,000 users and 6,784 conversions (2.12%). The difference is 0.12 percentage points. The standard error is √(2 × 0.0196 / 320,000) ≈ 0.00035, or 0.035 points. The z-statistic is 0.0012 / 0.00035 ≈ 3.4, p ≈ 0.0007, and the 95% confidence interval is 0.12 ± 0.07, about 0.05 to 0.19 points. The relative lift is 6%, with an interval of roughly 2.5% to 9.5%.
Before shipping, check the sample ratio: 320,000 versus 320,000 is exactly the planned split, so SRM passes. Check guardrails: playback errors and app start time are unchanged. If the team had peeked on day 2, when the difference happened to be 0.3 points with p = 0.03, and shipped, they would have reported a 15% lift, about two and a half times the real effect, a common way experiments overstate gains.
Finally, CUPED. Using each user's activity in the week before signup is impossible for brand-new users, but for an experiment on existing users with a pre-period correlation of 0.6, variance would fall by 1 − 0.36 = 64%, and the same test would need about 200,000 users per group instead of 313,600.
intuition: The only way to know whether X causes Y is to change X at random and watch Y: randomness makes the two groups twins in every way except the one you changed.
Experiments feel natural because they remove the arguments that plague observational data. Did sales rise because of the feature or because of the holiday? With randomization, the holiday affects both groups equally and cancels out. Statistics then answers the remaining question honestly: is the difference bigger than the noise you would get from two random groups of this size? Everything else on an experimentation platform, from hashing to SRM checks to sequential tests, exists to keep those two promises intact.
mistakes: People often think a non-significant result proves there is no effect. It only shows the effect, if any, was too small to detect with this sample. The confidence interval tells you which effects remain possible.
People often think p = 0.03 means a 97% chance that the change works. The p-value is the probability of data this extreme if there were no effect; the chance that the change works also depends on how often ideas like it succeed, which is often low.
People often think it is fine to stop a test as soon as it becomes significant. Repeated looks inflate false positives sharply unless the method is designed for them, and early stopping on a good streak overstates the effect.
People often think randomizing users is always correct. When users interact through shared budgets, auctions, inventory or social ties, the treatment leaks into control. Ad and marketplace experiments often need budget-split, geo or switchback designs.
friction: Many important effects are small, so tests need millions of users or weeks of time, and companies with less traffic cannot detect them at all. Long-term effects (novelty fading, learning, churn) differ from what a two-week test shows, and holdouts kept for months are expensive. Metrics that are easy to move (clicks) can diverge from what matters (retention, revenue), and choosing an overall evaluation criterion is hard.
Interference is common in advertising and marketplaces, and the designs that handle it (geo experiments, switchbacks) have far fewer units and wider confidence intervals. Identity problems leak in: if the same household appears as several users, it may be in both groups. Organizational problems are as large as statistical ones: pressure to ship, many simultaneous metrics tempting people to pick the one that looks good, and dashboards that invite peeking.
practice: Write the plan before starting: hypothesis, primary metric, guardrails, randomization unit, MDE, sample size and duration (at least one full weekly cycle). Randomize at the level where interference stops: household for CTV, budget or geo for ad campaigns.
Automate checks on every test: SRM, A/A calibration, guardrails, and data-quality alerts. Use CUPED or other covariate adjustment where pre-period data exists. If you must monitor continuously, use a sequential method built for it. Report effects with confidence intervals and relative lift, not only p-values, and keep a log of all experiments, including failures, so the organization learns its base rate of successful ideas.
For advertising lift, keep a randomized holdout inside the target audience, measure outcomes through the same identity resolution for both groups, and prefer ghost-ad or intent-to-treat designs over comparing exposed users with unexposed users chosen after the fact.
quiz: Why does hashing user_id with an experiment-specific salt give good assignments? || The hash is effectively random with respect to user attributes, consistent for the same user, and stateless. A different salt per experiment makes assignments independent across experiments.
Why does checking results daily and stopping when p < 0.05 inflate false positives? || Each look is another chance for random noise to cross the threshold, so the probability of at least one false crossing grows with the number of looks: about 14% with five looks instead of 5%.
Baseline conversion is 4% and you want to detect an absolute lift of 0.2 points with 80% power at 5% significance. Roughly how many users per group? || σ² = 0.04 × 0.96 = 0.0384, so n ≈ 16 × 0.0384 / 0.002² = 153,600 users per group.
An experiment's groups have 501,200 and 498,800 users with a planned 50/50 split. Should you trust the results? || Probably not without investigation. A chi-square test gives a p-value around 0.016 for this imbalance, so this is a sample ratio mismatch, which usually signals an assignment, logging or filtering bug.
When would you choose a bandit over a fixed A/B test? || When earning during the test matters more than a precise effect estimate, such as choosing among many short-lived creatives. Use a fixed test when you need an unbiased estimate of the effect for a lasting decision.

@@ rtanalytics
value: Real-time analytics answers aggregate questions over data that arrived seconds ago, with sub-second latency, for many users at once. "How many impressions did each of my campaigns deliver in the last hour, by device type?" asked by thousands of advertisers on a dashboard; "is this card's spending pattern unusual right now?" asked by a fraud service; "how many orders are delayed in each city?" asked by an operations team. A data warehouse can answer such questions, but typically with minutes of data delay, seconds of query latency and a handful of concurrent users. Real-time analytics stores, such as Apache Pinot, Apache Druid, ClickHouse and StarRocks, aim for seconds of freshness, tens of milliseconds of latency and thousands of queries per second.
This makes analytics a product feature rather than an internal report: LinkedIn's "Who viewed your profile", Uber's restaurant-manager dashboards, ad platforms' campaign reporting, and observability tools are all built this way. For the products above it (Pinot, Druid, ClickHouse), the concept explains the shared design they converged on.
That design is built from four lower concepts. Columnar storage (L2) keeps scans short and cache-friendly, reading only the columns a query needs. Inverted indexes (L2) turn equality filters into small lists or bitmaps of matching rows. Star-tree indexes (L2) pre-aggregate common group-bys so many queries read a few hundred rows instead of millions. Scatter–gather (L2) fans each query out to the servers holding relevant segments and merges their partial results.
terms: Freshness — how long after an event happens it becomes visible to queries.
Segment — an immutable chunk of a table (often one time range of one partition) stored in columnar format with its own indexes.
Real-time (consuming) segment — a segment being built in memory from a stream, queryable while it fills.
Broker — the node that receives a query, decides which servers hold the needed segments, fans the query out and merges results.
Segment pruning — skipping segments that cannot match a query, using time ranges, partition values or min/max metadata.
Pre-aggregation (rollup) — storing totals at a coarser grain (per minute, per campaign) instead of every raw event.
Upsert — inserting a row or replacing the existing row with the same primary key.
Hybrid table — a table that combines real-time segments from a stream with offline segments from batch jobs.
history: Analytics used to mean batch: nightly ETL into a warehouse, reports the next morning. As web companies grew, they wanted to show analytics to customers interactively. Metamarkets built Druid around 2011 to give advertisers interactive dashboards over programmatic ad data and open-sourced it in 2012; its 2014 SIGMOD paper described real-time nodes ingesting events into in-memory segments and historical nodes serving immutable columnar segments. LinkedIn built Pinot (around 2014, open-sourced in 2015) to serve member-facing analytics with very high concurrency; the 2018 SIGMOD paper "Pinot: Realtime OLAP for 530 Million Users" described its design, and Uber adopted it widely. Yandex built ClickHouse for its web analytics service and open-sourced it in 2016; it became popular for raw-event analytics and observability because of its very fast scans.
Kafka (2011) provided the common input: a durable, replayable stream that these stores could consume directly. Later systems such as Apache Doris and StarRocks (from about 2017–2020) added full SQL with joins, and managed services (StarTree for Pinot, Imply for Druid, ClickHouse Cloud) made operation easier. More recently, real-time stores added upserts, tiered storage on object stores to reduce cost, and better join support, narrowing the gap with lakehouse engines.
how: Ingestion starts from a stream. Each table subscribes to Kafka topics; each server consumes one or more partitions and builds a consuming segment in memory, appending rows to column buffers and updating simple indexes as rows arrive. Rows are queryable immediately, so freshness is the stream lag, usually seconds. When a segment reaches a size or time limit (for example a few million rows or one hour), the server seals it: converts columns to compressed, dictionary-encoded columnar form, builds full indexes, uploads the segment to durable deep storage (object storage), and records it in the cluster metadata. Replicas consume the same partitions so that losing a server does not lose data, and the stream's offsets make recovery a replay.
Storage is columnar so that a query reading three columns out of fifty reads only those three. Each column is dictionary-encoded (distinct values mapped to small integers), sorted columns get run-length encoding, and data is often sorted by the most common filter column within each segment. This is where the columnar-storage concept adds value: a scan of a few million values of one integer column takes milliseconds on one core, and compression keeps more data in memory.
Indexes cut work further. An inverted index maps each value of a column to the rows containing it, stored as compressed bitmaps; a filter like campaign_id = 123 AND device = 'ctv' becomes the AND of two bitmaps, so only matching rows are touched. Range indexes help numeric ranges; text and JSON indexes help search-like filters. A star-tree index goes further for known query shapes: it pre-computes aggregates for combinations of chosen dimensions (for example campaign, country, device), so "impressions by device for campaign 123 today" can be answered by reading a few pre-aggregated records instead of every matching event. That is the trade the star-tree concept describes: extra storage and build time for very fast group-bys.
Queries use scatter–gather. A broker receives SQL, uses table metadata to find which segments could match (segment pruning by time range, by partition key if the table is partitioned by campaign or customer, and by column min/max values), and sends sub-queries to the servers holding those segments. Each server runs the filter and aggregation on its segments in parallel and returns partial results (for example partial counts per group). The broker merges them, applies final ordering and limits, and returns the answer. Tail latency is the enemy: the query is as slow as its slowest server, so systems use replica selection, limits on fan-out (route each query to as few servers as possible) and timeouts.
Most deployments are hybrid. Real-time segments hold recent data from the stream; offline segments, rebuilt by batch jobs from the lakehouse, hold corrected and compacted history. The broker queries real-time segments for recent time ranges and offline segments for older ones, using a time boundary so data is not counted twice. Upserts, needed when events are updated (an order's status, a corrected impression count), are handled by keeping a map from primary key to the latest row's location and marking older versions invalid, which costs memory and requires partitioning the stream by key.
Finally, concurrency drives the design. A dashboard with 2,000 users refreshing every few seconds produces hundreds or thousands of queries per second. If each query fans out to 50 servers, the cluster handles 50,000 sub-queries per second, so partitioning data so that each query touches only one or a few servers matters more than raw scan speed.
Inside one server, a query on one segment runs as a small pipeline. The filter step evaluates each predicate with its index and combines the resulting bitmaps, producing the list of matching row numbers. The aggregation step reads only the needed columns for those rows, works on dictionary IDs instead of strings, and accumulates results in arrays indexed by group-key IDs, which is much faster than a general hash table. Modern engines process values in batches of a thousand or more with SIMD instructions, the vectorized execution idea, so a single core can filter and sum hundreds of millions of values per second. Segments are processed in parallel across cores, and each server returns one small partial result per query. This is why sealed segments are immutable: immutability lets the engine build heavy indexes once, cache freely and serve the same file from several replicas.

example: An ad platform records 3 billion impression events a day, about 35,000 per second on average and 100,000 per second at peak. Advertisers want a dashboard showing impressions, clicks and spend for their campaigns, by hour and device type, for the last 7 days, refreshed every 10 seconds. About 1,500 advertisers have the dashboard open at peak, so the system sees roughly 150 queries per second, each with a few sub-queries.
Partition the Kafka topic and the table by advertiser_id into 64 partitions. Each query for one advertiser then hits one partition: the broker prunes all other partitions and all segments outside the 7-day window. Segments are sealed every hour per partition, so 7 days gives 64 × 168 ≈ 10,750 segments, of which a single query touches about 168, all on the servers holding that one partition.
Without indexes, a large advertiser with 5% of traffic has about 150 million impressions a day, about a billion for the week. Scanning three columns of a billion rows at roughly a billion values per second per core is about 3 core-seconds, too slow for a dashboard at 150 queries per second. With an inverted index on campaign_id and a star-tree on (advertiser, campaign, hour, device), the query reads pre-aggregated rows instead: about 50 campaigns × 168 hours × 4 device types ≈ 33,600 rows, which takes a few milliseconds. End-to-end latency is then dominated by network and broker merge time, typically 20 to 80 ms.
Freshness: the consuming segment receives events within a few seconds of Kafka ingestion, so the "last hour" bar includes events from 5 to 10 seconds ago. Corrections, such as invalid traffic removed by a fraud job two hours later, arrive through the offline path: a nightly batch job rebuilds yesterday's segments from the lakehouse with filtered data and replaces the real-time ones, so totals become final the next day.
intuition: Precompute and index for the questions you know will be asked, keep the newest data in a fast lane, and send each question only to the few machines that can answer it.
Real-time analytics combines two familiar ideas: a newspaper's live ticker (fresh, approximate, fast) and its archive (corrected, complete). Columns, indexes and pre-aggregation are the same tricks a librarian uses: shelve books by the questions people ask, keep a card index, and keep running totals instead of recounting. Scatter–gather is simply asking several librarians at once and adding up their answers. The difficulty is doing all of this while new books arrive every second.
mistakes: People often think real-time analytics stores replace the data warehouse or lakehouse. They are optimized for known query shapes, recent data and high concurrency; ad hoc exploration, large joins and long history are cheaper and easier in a lakehouse. Most architectures use both.
People often think adding servers always makes queries faster. If every query fans out to every server, more servers mean more sub-queries and worse tail latency. Partitioning so that each query touches few servers usually matters more.
People often think real-time numbers are final. Late events, deduplication, fraud filtering and corrections arrive later, so real-time totals usually change. Dashboards should show which data is provisional.
People often think indexes are free. Each index costs memory, disk and ingestion time, and star-trees only help the dimensions they were built for. Indexes should follow measured query patterns, not every possible filter.
friction: Query patterns drive design, so new questions can be slow until someone adds an index or pre-aggregation. Upserts, deletes and late data are harder in append-only segment stores and cost memory. High-cardinality group-bys (by user ID, by URL) defeat pre-aggregation and can overload brokers. Joins are limited or expensive in many engines, so data is denormalized at ingestion, which complicates updates to dimension data.
Operations are demanding: balancing segments across servers, rebalancing after scaling, managing consumption lag, handling hot partitions (one huge advertiser) and keeping tail latency low under concurrency. Cost grows with retention and replicas, which is why tiered storage on object stores has become common. Finally, two copies of the truth (real-time store and lakehouse) must be reconciled, and users notice when numbers differ.
practice: Start from the workload: list the top query shapes, their filters and group-bys, the freshness and latency targets, and the peak queries per second. Choose the partition key from the most selective, most common filter (customer, advertiser, tenant), sort segments by the next most common filter, and add inverted, range or star-tree indexes only for measured needs.
Keep raw history and corrections in the lakehouse and rebuild offline segments from it; let the real-time path serve fresh, provisional numbers. Test with production-like concurrency and skew, including your largest tenant, and watch consumption lag, segment counts, p99 latency and broker fan-out. When evaluating engines (Pinot, Druid, ClickHouse, StarRocks, or search engines such as Elasticsearch used for aggregations), benchmark your own top queries at your own concurrency; published benchmarks rarely match a real workload.
quiz: How does a real-time analytics store make events queryable within seconds? || Servers consume stream partitions directly into in-memory consuming segments that are queryable as rows arrive. Later they seal them into indexed columnar segments in deep storage.
Why can a star-tree index answer some group-by queries thousands of times faster than a scan? || It stores pre-computed aggregates for combinations of chosen dimensions. A query on those dimensions reads a small number of aggregated records instead of every matching raw event.
A dashboard sends 200 queries per second and each query fans out to 40 servers. How many sub-queries per second does the cluster handle, and how could you reduce it? || 8,000 sub-queries per second. Partitioning the table by the main filter key (for example advertiser) lets the broker send each query to one or a few servers.
Why do most deployments keep both real-time segments and offline segments rebuilt from the lakehouse? || Real-time segments give freshness but may include late, duplicate or invalid events. Offline segments rebuilt in batch apply corrections and compaction, so historical numbers become accurate and storage-efficient.
When would you serve an analytics feature from a lakehouse engine instead of a real-time store? || When queries are ad hoc, need large joins or long history, run rarely, or tolerate seconds of latency and minutes of freshness. A real-time store pays off for known query shapes at high concurrency and low latency.
