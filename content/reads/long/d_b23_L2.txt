@@ layernorm
value: A deep network is a long chain of functions. The output of layer 10 is the input of layer 11, and so on for 50 or 100 layers. If each layer makes its output only 10% larger than its input, after 48 layers the values are about 1.1^48 ≈ 97 times larger. If each layer makes it 10% smaller, they shrink to about 1% of the start. Large values overflow in 16-bit arithmetic (the largest FP16 number is 65,504), small values lose precision, and the gradients that flow backward grow or shrink in the same way. Worse, the right learning rate for an early layer is then the wrong one for a late layer. Training becomes slow, needs very careful initialization, and often diverges.
Normalization layers fix this by measuring the activations and forcing them back to a standard scale at fixed points in the network. A normalization layer computes simple statistics of a group of numbers (their mean and spread), rescales the group to mean 0 and spread 1 (or only spread 1), and then applies a learned per-feature scale and shift. Batch normalization (BatchNorm, 2015) made very deep convolutional networks train much faster. Layer normalization (LayerNorm, 2016) and its cheaper relative RMSNorm (2019) are inside every transformer: a typical LLM applies one before each attention block, one before each MLP block and one before the output layer.
For the layer above, deep learning (L1), normalization is one of the two tricks, together with residual connections, that make networks with dozens or hundreds of layers trainable with ordinary learning rates. It makes training much less sensitive to initialization and allows larger learning rates. The concept below it is statistics (L4): a normalization layer is a sample mean and a sample variance, computed on the fly and used to standardize data, exactly like turning measurements into z-scores. BatchNorm even keeps running estimates of population statistics for use at inference, a small estimation problem inside every CNN.
terms: Activation — a number produced by a layer for one input, for example one feature of one token's hidden vector.
Mean and variance (μ, σ²) — the average of a group of numbers and the average squared distance from that average.
Normalization axis — the set of numbers over which μ and σ² are computed; it is the only real difference between BatchNorm, LayerNorm and GroupNorm.
BatchNorm — normalizes each feature (channel) using statistics across all examples in the mini-batch.
LayerNorm — normalizes each example (each token) using statistics across its own features.
RMSNorm — LayerNorm without the mean subtraction; it divides by the root mean square only.
Gain and bias (γ, β) — learned per-feature scale and shift applied after normalizing, so the network can choose the scale it wants.
ε (epsilon) — a small constant such as 10⁻⁵ added to the variance so the layer never divides by zero.
Pre-norm and post-norm — normalizing the input of each sub-block (pre) or the output after the residual addition (post).
Running statistics — moving averages of mean and variance kept by BatchNorm during training and used at inference.
history: The idea of standardizing data is old. LeCun and colleagues' "Efficient BackProp" (1998) advised shifting network inputs to zero mean and unit variance because gradient descent converges faster on well-scaled inputs. Glorot and Bengio (2010) and He and colleagues (2015) chose weight initializations that keep the variance of activations roughly constant from layer to layer. But initialization only controls the scale at the start; once training changes the weights, the scale drifts.
Sergey Ioffe and Christian Szegedy at Google proposed batch normalization in February 2015. They normalized every channel inside the network, not only the inputs, using the statistics of the current mini-batch. On an Inception image classifier it reached the same accuracy with 14 times fewer training steps. Their explanation was "internal covariate shift": each layer's input distribution changes as earlier layers learn, and normalization removes that change. In 2018 Santurkar and colleagues tested this and found it did not hold up: networks trained well even when noise was added after BatchNorm to shift distributions on purpose. Their alternative explanation is that normalization makes the loss surface smoother, so larger steps are safe.
BatchNorm needs a batch, and that was a problem for recurrent networks, for variable-length sequences and for small batches. Jimmy Lei Ba, Jamie Ryan Kiros and Geoffrey Hinton proposed layer normalization in July 2016: compute the statistics over the features of each single example instead. It behaves the same at training and inference and works with a batch of one. Instance normalization (2016, for style transfer) and group normalization (Wu and He, 2018, for detection with small batches) followed the same pattern with other axes.
The transformer (2017) used LayerNorm after each residual addition (post-norm), and it needed a learning-rate warm-up to train. GPT-2 (2019) moved LayerNorm to the input of each sub-block and added a final one (pre-norm). Xiong and colleagues (ICML 2020) showed why this matters: post-norm has very large gradients near the output at initialization, while pre-norm does not, so pre-norm can train without warm-up. Biao Zhang and Rico Sennrich proposed RMSNorm in 2019; it matched LayerNorm and cut the running time of the models they tested by 7% to 64%. T5 used this simplified norm, and LLaMA (2023) made pre-norm RMSNorm the default for open LLMs.
how: Take one token's hidden vector x = (x₁, …, x_d), for example with d = 4096. LayerNorm computes the sample mean μ = (1/d) ∑ x_i and the sample variance σ² = (1/d) ∑ (x_i − μ)². These are the two basic estimators from statistics, and the next step is the z-score: x̂_i = (x_i − μ) / √(σ² + ε). Now the d numbers have mean 0 and variance 1. Finally y_i = γ_i × x̂_i + β_i, where γ and β are learned vectors of length d, initialized to all ones and all zeros. The layer has 2d parameters and no weight matrix, so it is tiny: 8,192 parameters for d = 4096.
The only design choice is the axis of the statistics. A transformer activation tensor has shape (B, T, d): batch, sequence position, feature. LayerNorm reduces over d, separately for each of the B × T tokens. A convolutional activation has shape (N, C, H, W): images, channels, height, width. BatchNorm reduces over N, H and W, separately for each of the C channels, so one image's output depends on the other images in the batch. GroupNorm reduces over groups of channels and H, W within one image. The axis decides everything practical: BatchNorm needs batches large enough for good estimates (statistics from 2 images are noisy), and it behaves differently at inference, where there may be no batch. During training it therefore keeps running estimates, for example μ_run ← 0.9 × μ_run + 0.1 × μ_batch (PyTorch's default momentum is 0.1), and uses them at test time. LayerNorm needs none of this: every token is normalized by itself, so training and inference compute the same function, and sequences of any length and batches of size 1 work.
Why does this help optimization? The first reason is invariance. For any scalar a > 0 and scalar b, LN(a·x + b) = LN(x): scaling all features of a token, or shifting all of them by the same amount, does not change the output. So the next layer always receives inputs with the same scale, no matter how the earlier weights grew. The same invariance acts on the weights. If a weight matrix W feeds directly into a normalization, multiplying W by a leaves the output unchanged, and the gradient with respect to W shrinks by a factor of 1/a. Weights that grow large automatically take smaller relative steps, which is a built-in brake on runaway growth. The second reason, from Santurkar and colleagues, is smoothness: the loss changes more predictably along the gradient direction, so the optimizer can take larger steps without overshooting. Neither reason is a full theory, but both are measurable.
RMSNorm removes the centering step: y_i = γ_i × x_i / RMS(x), where RMS(x) = √((1/d) ∑ x_i² + ε), and there is usually no β. It keeps the scale invariance (scaling x by a still gives the same output) but loses the shift invariance. Zhang and Sennrich argued that re-centering is not what makes LayerNorm useful, and experiments since then agree: models train equally well, and a following linear layer can absorb any mean it needs. RMSNorm needs one reduction (the sum of squares) instead of two dependent ones (the mean, then the variance around it), and fewer element-wise operations, which matters because normalization is memory-bound: it reads d numbers, does a few operations per number, and writes d numbers. On a GPU, its cost is the cost of moving the data, so frameworks fuse it with the residual addition before it (one kernel reads x and the block output, adds them, normalizes and writes both results).
Placement matters as much as the formula, and here normalization meets residual connections. Post-norm, as in the 2017 transformer, computes x_{l+1} = LN(x_l + F(x_l)), where F is the attention or MLP sub-block. Every gradient that flows from the loss to an early layer must pass through every LayerNorm on the way, and Xiong and colleagues showed that at initialization the gradients near the output are large, so training with a normal learning rate diverges unless it starts with a warm-up. Pre-norm computes x_{l+1} = x_l + F(LN(x_l)) and adds one final norm before the output head. The residual path from the loss to the first layer is now a clean sum with no normalization on it, so gradients reach early layers directly. This is why GPT-2, LLaMA and almost every modern LLM use pre-norm. The price is that the residual stream itself is never normalized: its size grows with depth as each block adds to it, so a late block's contribution is relatively smaller. Several variants address this: DeepNorm rescales the residual branch, Gemma 2 normalizes both the input and the output of each sub-block ("sandwich" norm), and OLMo 2 normalizes sub-block outputs and also the attention queries and keys.
That last idea, QK-norm, applies the same tool inside attention. The attention logit is a dot product q·k. If the norms of q and k grow during training, logits become very large, the softmax becomes nearly one-hot and training can spike or diverge. Normalizing q and k per head (with LayerNorm or RMSNorm) caps the logit size. It was used in ViT-22B (2023) and appears in several recent LLMs.
Two implementation details matter in practice. Compute the statistics in FP32 even when activations are BF16: summing 4,096 squared BF16 values loses precision, and ε = 10⁻⁵ or 10⁻⁶ is far below BF16's resolution near 1. And the backward pass needs μ and 1/σ for each row, so implementations save these two numbers per token instead of recomputing them.

example: Take one token with d = 4 features: x = [2, 4, 6, 8]. Let γ = [1, 1, 1, 1], β = [0, 0, 0, 0], and ignore ε to keep the arithmetic clean.
LayerNorm. The mean is μ = (2 + 4 + 6 + 8) / 4 = 5. The deviations are [−3, −1, 1, 3]. Their squares are [9, 1, 1, 9], so σ² = 20 / 4 = 5 and σ = √5 ≈ 2.236. The output is x̂ = [−3, −1, 1, 3] / 2.236 = [−1.342, −0.447, 0.447, 1.342]. Check: the mean is 0 and the mean of squares is (1.8 + 0.2 + 0.2 + 1.8) / 4 = 1.
Invariance. Multiply the input by 10: [20, 40, 60, 80]. The mean is 50, the deviations are [−30, −10, 10, 30], σ = √500 ≈ 22.36, and the output is again [−1.342, −0.447, 0.447, 1.342]. Add 100 to every feature instead: [102, 104, 106, 108]. The mean becomes 105, the deviations are unchanged, and so is the output. Whatever happened to the scale upstream, the next layer sees the same numbers.
RMSNorm. The mean of squares is (4 + 16 + 36 + 64) / 4 = 30, so RMS = √30 ≈ 5.477. The output is [2, 4, 6, 8] / 5.477 = [0.365, 0.730, 1.095, 1.461]. Its RMS is 1, but its mean is 0.913, not 0: RMSNorm fixes the scale and leaves the mean alone. Multiplying the input by 10 still gives the same output; adding 100 does not (the RMS of [102, 104, 106, 108] is about 105, giving [0.971, 0.990, 1.009, 1.028]).
Learned gain. Suppose training sets γ = [2, 1, 1, 0.5]. The RMSNorm output becomes [0.730, 0.730, 1.095, 0.730]. The network has chosen to amplify feature 1 and damp feature 4. Normalization fixed the overall scale; γ lets each feature have its own.
BatchNorm, for contrast. Put a second token in the batch, z = [10, 0, 6, 4]. For feature 1, BatchNorm uses the two values 2 and 10: mean 6, standard deviation 4, so x gets (2 − 6) / 4 = −1 and z gets +1. If z were replaced by [2, 9, 9, 9], feature 1 of x would become 0 / 0 (no spread, and ε decides the result). The output for x depends on who else is in the batch. LayerNorm's output for x is the same in every batch.
Realistic sizes. A Llama-3-8B-style model has d = 4096 and 32 layers, with two RMSNorms per layer plus a final one: 65 norms with 4,096 gains each, about 266,000 parameters out of 8 billion (0.003%). But each norm reads and writes the full hidden vector of every token: in BF16 that is 8 KB in and 8 KB out per token per norm, about 1 MB of memory traffic per token in total. Cheap in parameters, not free in bandwidth, which is why it is fused with neighbouring operations.
intuition: Automatic gain control on a microphone: whatever volume comes in, the next stage receives the signal at a standard level, and a knob (γ) sets the level each channel should have.
The design is natural once you see the network as a chain of stages that must agree on units. Without normalization, every layer must be tuned to the scale produced by all earlier layers, and that scale changes every training step. With normalization, the contract between layers is fixed: "you will always receive numbers of size about 1". Each layer can then learn its own transformation without tracking what the others do to the scale. Statistics provides the cheapest possible way to enforce that contract: two numbers per group, a mean and a spread, computed in one pass.
The axis choice follows from the data. Images in a batch are similar and channels are meaningful across images, so BatchNorm's per-channel statistics make sense for CNNs. Tokens of different sentences, positions and lengths are not interchangeable, so a transformer normalizes each token by itself. RMSNorm's lesson is that the scale was the part doing the work.
mistakes: People often think BatchNorm and LayerNorm are the same operation with different names. They compute the same formula over different axes, and that changes behaviour: BatchNorm couples the examples in a batch and needs running statistics at inference, while LayerNorm treats each token alone and is identical at training and inference.
People often think normalization makes activations Gaussian. It only fixes the first two moments (mean and variance, or just the RMS) of each group. The distribution inside the group can still be very skewed, and large LLMs develop a few feature dimensions with values hundreds of times larger than the rest.
People often think the learned γ and β make normalization pointless, since they can undo it. The network can represent the same functions, but optimization is different: the scale of a feature is now controlled by one parameter γ_i instead of by the product of many weights across many layers, which gives smoother, better-conditioned gradients.
People often think normalization works by removing "internal covariate shift". That was the 2015 explanation, but experiments that deliberately add distribution shift after BatchNorm still train well. Smoother loss surfaces and scale invariance are better-supported explanations, and the question is still not fully settled.
People often think pre-norm is simply better. It trains more easily and without warm-up, which is why it won, but the unnormalized residual stream grows with depth and late layers contribute relatively less; that is why variants like sandwich norm and output norm keep appearing.
friction: The theory is incomplete. We can measure that normalization smooths training and adds scale invariance, but we cannot yet predict from first principles which placement or variant will work best for a new architecture, so choices are still made by experiment.
Large transformers develop outlier features: a few dimensions of the hidden vector carry values far larger than the rest. Dettmers and colleagues (LLM.int8(), 2022) found such outliers emerging systematically around 6.7 billion parameters. The RMS of a vector is then dominated by these few dimensions, and quantizing activations to INT8 or FP8 becomes hard, because one scale factor cannot fit both the outliers and the ordinary values. Much of today's quantization work (outlier-aware schemes, rotations of the hidden space) exists because of this.
BatchNorm has its own problems: results change with batch size, distributed training needs synchronized statistics across GPUs (extra communication) or accepts noisy per-GPU estimates, train and inference behave differently, and statistics leak information between examples in a batch, which can let contrastive models "cheat". Fine-tuning a BatchNorm model on small batches often hurts it unless the statistics are frozen.
Normalization also costs time out of proportion to its arithmetic. It is memory-bound, adds a reduction (a synchronization point inside a GPU kernel), and appears 2 to 4 times per layer. Without fusion it can take a noticeable share of step time, especially at small batch sizes during inference.
Finally, it may not be essential. NFNets (2021) reached strong image accuracy without BatchNorm using careful scaling and adaptive gradient clipping, and "Transformers without Normalization" (Zhu, Chen, He, LeCun and Liu, 2025) replaced the norm layers with an element-wise tanh(αx) with learned α (Dynamic Tanh) and matched normalized transformers on several tasks. Whether normalization is needed, or only one convenient way to control scale, is an open question.
practice: For transformers, use pre-norm RMSNorm with a final norm before the output head, ε between 10⁻⁶ and 10⁻⁵, γ initialized to 1, and statistics computed in FP32. Most training recipes exclude γ and β from weight decay. If training shows loss spikes together with growing attention logits, add QK-norm before trying lower learning rates. Use fused kernels: torch.compile, Triton or Apex kernels, and in serving engines such as vLLM the fused "add residual + RMSNorm" kernel.
For CNNs with reasonable per-GPU batches (16 or more images), BatchNorm is still a strong default. For detection and segmentation, where each GPU may hold only 1 or 2 images, use GroupNorm (32 groups is the common setting) or synchronized BatchNorm. At inference, fold BatchNorm into the preceding convolution: with W' = γW / σ and b' = γ(b − μ) / σ + β, the norm disappears and costs nothing. When fine-tuning a BatchNorm model on small batches, freeze its running statistics. And remember to switch to evaluation mode (model.eval() in PyTorch) at inference, or BatchNorm will use the statistics of whatever batch it is given.
When porting weights between implementations, check three details that cause silent errors: the ε value, whether the variance uses d or d − 1 in the denominator (normalization layers use d), and the parameterization of γ. Gemma's RMSNorm, for example, multiplies by (1 + γ), so its stored γ starts at 0, not 1.
For monitoring, log the RMS and the maximum absolute value of activations per layer during training. A slowly rising maximum in a few dimensions is the early sign of outlier features that will matter later for quantization.
quiz: In a transformer, over which numbers does LayerNorm compute its mean and variance, and why is BatchNorm a poor fit there? || Over the d features of each single token. BatchNorm would mix statistics across different sentences and positions, depend on batch size, and behave differently at inference, while LayerNorm treats each token alone and works with a batch of one.
Why does a normalization layer make the next layer insensitive to the scale of the weights before it? || Because LN(a·x) = LN(x) for any a > 0: if the earlier weights grow by a factor a, the normalized output is unchanged. The gradient with respect to those weights also shrinks by 1/a, so large weights take smaller relative steps.
Why can a pre-norm transformer train without learning-rate warm-up while a post-norm one usually cannot? || In pre-norm, x_{l+1} = x_l + F(LN(x_l)), so the residual path from the loss to early layers has no normalization on it and gradients pass directly. In post-norm, every gradient crosses every LayerNorm, and at initialization the gradients near the output are large, so a normal learning rate diverges without warm-up.
Compute RMSNorm of x = [3, 4] with γ = [1, 1] and ε ignored. || The mean of squares is (9 + 16) / 2 = 12.5, so RMS = √12.5 ≈ 3.536. The output is [3, 4] / 3.536 ≈ [0.849, 1.131], whose RMS is 1.
You train an object detector with 2 images per GPU. Which normalization do you choose and why? || GroupNorm (or synchronized BatchNorm). Per-GPU BatchNorm statistics from 2 images are too noisy and differ from inference statistics; GroupNorm computes statistics within each image, so batch size does not matter, at no communication cost.
