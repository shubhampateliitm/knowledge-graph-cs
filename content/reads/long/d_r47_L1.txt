@@ consent
value: Privacy law says that a company may process a person's data only on a legal basis, and that the person can ask the company to stop or to delete the data. For an ad-tech system this is not a legal-department matter. A bid request goes out in about 100 milliseconds, to dozens of bidders, with a device ID, a location and a page URL in it. Someone must decide, inside that window, whether each of those fields may be sent, and to whom. Later, when a person asks "delete my data", someone must find every row about that person in every table, log, model and backup, before a deadline set by law.
Privacy regulation and consent is the set of mechanisms that turn those rules into data. The main laws are the GDPR in the EU (in force since 25 May 2018), the ePrivacy rules on cookies, and a growing set of US laws starting with the CCPA in California (in force since 1 January 2020, extended by the CPRA from 2023). The main mechanisms are a **consent string** (a small encoded record of what the user agreed to) that travels with every ad request, and a **deletion pipeline** that removes a person's rows from every copy of the data. The penalties are real: the GDPR allows fines of up to 20 million euros or 4% of worldwide yearly revenue, and the French regulator fined the ad-tech company Criteo 40 million euros in 2023 for not being able to show that its users had consented.
For the layer above, this concept gives rules that other systems must obey. Prebid and OpenRTB carry the consent strings on every auction. Delta Lake needs DELETE, deletion vectors and VACUUM largely because of the right to erasure. The layers below supply the mechanics: cookies and device IDs (L2) are what consent controls, identity graphs (L2) link many IDs to one person, row lineage and change feeds (L2) show where a row was copied, deletion vectors (L2) make deletion affordable on immutable files, and clean rooms and differential privacy (L2) reduce how much personal data is processed at all.
terms: Personal data — any information that relates to an identified or identifiable person; under the GDPR this includes cookie IDs, advertising IDs and IP addresses.
Lawful basis — the legal reason for processing data; the GDPR lists six, and ad tech mostly uses consent or legitimate interest.
Consent — a freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous choice, made by a clear action; pre-ticked boxes and silence do not count.
CMP (consent management platform) — the banner and the code behind it that asks the user, stores the answer and exposes it to the page.
TC string — the TCF consent string: a compact, base64-encoded bit record of which purposes and vendors the user accepted.
GPP (Global Privacy Platform) — the IAB Tech Lab standard that carries the signals of many regions in one string made of sections.
Opt-out signal — a user choice, such as the Global Privacy Control browser header, that tells a site not to sell or share the user's data.
DSAR (data subject access request) — a request by a person to see, correct or delete their data; the company must answer within a legal deadline.
Right to erasure — the right to have personal data deleted, in GDPR Article 17; the CCPA and the California Delete Act have similar rights.
history: The EU Data Protection Directive (1995) set the ideas of purpose limitation and consent. The ePrivacy Directive (2002, amended 2009) added the rule that matters most for ad tech: a site may store or read information on a user's device, such as a cookie, only with consent, unless it is strictly necessary. For years this was ignored in practice.
The GDPR was adopted in April 2016 and applied from 25 May 2018. It raised the bar for consent, added the fines, and applied to any company that processes data of people in the EU, wherever the company sits. Ad tech reacted by building a shared standard instead of one banner per company. IAB Europe published the Transparency and Consent Framework (TCF) v1 in 2018 and v2.0 in August 2019, with defined purposes, a registry of vendors, and a string that carries the answer. TCF v2.2 followed in 2023 (it removed legitimate interest as a basis for several advertising purposes), and v2.3 became mandatory at the end of February 2026 (it adds a list of disclosed vendors to the string). A 2024 ruling of the EU Court of Justice (case C-604/22) held that the TC string can itself be personal data and that IAB Europe can be a joint controller for it.
In the US, California led. The CCPA was signed in June 2018 and applied from 1 January 2020. It gave the right to opt out of the "sale" of personal information. The CPRA, a ballot measure passed in 2020, applied from 1 January 2023 and added the right to opt out of "sharing" for cross-context advertising, plus sensitive-data limits. The IAB's first US answer was the four-character US Privacy string (such as 1YNN). As more states passed laws, one string per state became unworkable, and the IAB Tech Lab released the Global Privacy Platform (GPP) in 2022 as a container for them all. California's Delete Act (SB 362, 2023) went further: it built a state-run deletion platform, DROP, where a resident submits one request that all registered data brokers must process, starting 1 August 2026 and then at least every 45 days.
how: The mechanism has two halves: a signal that travels forward with each request, and a deletion that travels backward through stored data.
Step 1: collect the choice. A CMP shows the banner and records the user's answer per purpose (for example "store or access information on a device", "create a personalised ads profile", "measure ad performance") and per vendor. Cookies and device IDs (L2) are the objects being controlled: until the user agrees, a compliant page must not set the advertising cookie or read the advertising ID. The CMP stores the answer in a first-party cookie or local storage and exposes it through a standard JavaScript API, `__tcfapi` for TCF and `__gpp` for GPP.
Step 2: encode it. The TC string packs the answer into a bit field and writes it in base64url. The core part holds a version, two timestamps in tenths of a second, the CMP ID, the language, the vendor-list version, and then three large blocks: 24 bits for purpose consent, 24 bits for purpose legitimate-interest, and a vendor section. A vendor section is either a bit per vendor ID (bit 1 means "consent given to vendor 1"), or a list of ID ranges, whichever is shorter. The global vendor list holds over a thousand vendors, so the range form keeps the string short when a user accepts or rejects most of them. GPP puts several such sections in one string, joined by `~`, with a numeric section ID (2 for TCF EU v2, 7 for the US national section, 8 for California), and a second field, `gpp_sid`, says which sections apply to this user.
Step 3: carry it. The string must travel with every request that could use personal data. On the web, Prebid's consent modules read it from the CMP and attach it to each bidder request, and they can block a bidder from running at all when the vendor lacks consent. In OpenRTB, the bid request carries the signal in the regulation object (`regs`) and the string in the user object: in OpenRTB 2.5 these were `regs.ext.gdpr` and `user.ext.consent`, and in 2.6 they became `regs.gdpr` and `user.consent`, with `regs.gpp` and `regs.gpp_sid` for GPP. Every party that receives the string must check it before using a field. A bidder reads the TC string, looks up its own vendor ID, and checks that the purposes it needs are allowed. If not, it must not use the device ID, or the precise location, or must not bid on a personalised basis. Every hop must pass the string on unchanged, because changing it would forge the user's choice.
Step 4: apply the choice inside the data platform. Events that arrive without valid consent are either dropped at the edge or stored with the personal fields nulled and a flag. Downstream jobs read the flag. The ID graph must respect it, too: an identity graph (L2) joins device IDs, emails and cookies into one person, so a restriction on one ID may have to be applied to the whole cluster.
Step 5: delete. A deletion request arrives as an email, a device ID or, for DROP, a hashed identifier. The system first resolves it with the identity graph into the full set of IDs that belong to the person. Then it must find the rows. In a lakehouse, the lookup is a join against the list of IDs to delete; in each table the matching rows are removed with `DELETE`. On immutable columnar files, this is expensive, because changing one row means rewriting a file of hundreds of megabytes. Deletion vectors (L2) solve this: the engine writes a small bitmap that marks rows as deleted and readers skip them, so the cost is small and the data is logically gone at once. The bytes are still in the old files until a purge rewrites them and a `VACUUM` removes the old versions after the retention period (7 days by default in Delta Lake). Row lineage and change feeds (L2) tell you where the data went next: if a table feeds three others, the delete must follow the change feed into each copy, or each derived table must be rebuilt.
Step 6: prove it. Regulators and DROP ask for evidence, so the pipeline writes an audit record per request: hashed key, time received, tables processed, rows removed, completion time, and no deleted data.

example: Take a simplified consent decision. A user in Germany accepts purposes 1 (store or access information on a device), 2 (basic ads) and 7 (measure ads), and rejects purposes 3 and 4 (personalised ads), for vendors 12 and 80 only. The purpose-consent field has 24 bits; bits 1, 2 and 7 are set, so the first byte reads 11000010 (bits 1 to 8), and the rest is zero. A bid request reaches vendor 80. Its policy says "I need purposes 1, 2 and 7 to bid on a contextual basis, and purposes 3 and 4 to bid on a personalised basis". The bidder checks that its vendor bit is set (yes), purposes 1, 2 and 7 (yes), purposes 3 and 4 (no). Result: it may bid, but only with context (page, country, device type), and it must ignore the advertising ID in the request. A vendor with ID 33 receives the same request and finds its bit is 0, so it must not process anything.
Now a deletion. A table of 2,000 Delta files of about 1 GB each (2 TB) holds 30 billion events. A daily batch brings 50,000 IDs to delete, and the IDs are random with respect to file layout. Each person has on average 400 events spread over the table's history, so almost every file contains at least one match. A classic `DELETE` would rewrite nearly all 2,000 files: 2 TB read and 2 TB written, for every daily batch, and the cluster time would be hours. With deletion vectors, the job reads only the join keys, finds about 20 million matching rows and writes a small bitmap per affected file, a few kilobytes each. The same batch takes minutes. The price is deferred: weekly, a purge (`REORG TABLE ... APPLY (PURGE)`) rewrites the files that have many marked rows, and `VACUUM` deletes the old versions once the retention period has passed. So the legal clock is met by the logical delete, and the physical delete follows within a known time, which the audit table records.
```sql
DELETE FROM events e
WHERE EXISTS (
  SELECT 1 FROM drop_list d
  WHERE d.hid = sha2(e.device_id, 256)
);
```
intuition: Consent is a passport stamp that must be shown at every border; deletion is a recall notice that must find every copy of the product.
The design is natural once you see what the law asks. It asks for a decision to be made again for every use of the data, by a party that did not talk to the user. The only way to do this is to attach the user's answer to the data as it moves, in a form every receiver can read in microseconds, which is why the answer became a short standard string rather than a database lookup. The right to delete runs the other way. The law talks about a person, but the systems store rows keyed by IDs, so the engineering is a translation: person to IDs through the identity graph, and IDs to rows through lineage. A privacy system is correct when the forward signal and the backward deletion agree, so that data which was never allowed to enter a system is not there to be deleted later.
mistakes: People often think a consent string is the consent. In fact it is a claim, written by the CMP, that the user consented. A receiver cannot verify it; trust comes from audits, from the CMP being registered, and from contracts. This is why regulators looked at who runs the CMP and who must keep proof of consent, and why Criteo was fined for being unable to prove consent.
People often think that hashing an email or ID makes it anonymous. In fact a hashed identifier is still personal data under the GDPR, because anyone who has the email can compute the same hash and match it. Hashing is a good way to match a deletion list without sharing raw emails, but it does not remove the data from the law's scope.
People often think "delete" means one SQL statement on one table. In fact the person's data is in the raw log, the cleaned table, aggregates, feature stores, the ID graph, search indexes, caches, backups and sometimes trained models. A `DELETE` on a Delta table also leaves the old files readable through time travel until `VACUUM` runs. The work is the inventory of copies, not the statement.
People often think the rules are the same everywhere. In fact the GDPR is mainly opt-in (you need consent or another basis before processing), while most US state laws are mainly opt-out (you may process until the user objects, but must honour opt-out signals such as Global Privacy Control). Which model applies depends on where the person lives, not where the server is, so the pipeline needs the region on each event.
tradeoffs: Choose to enforce consent at the edge (drop or null fields when the event is collected) when the law says you must not even hold the data, as for advertising IDs without consent in the EU. Choose to store everything with a consent flag and enforce at read time when you need the freedom to change the policy later or serve several regions from one table. The first gives the strongest legal position and the smallest deletion job; the second keeps flexibility but means every query must remember the flag, and one missed filter is a violation.
Choose logical deletion with deletion vectors when you must meet a deadline of 30 to 45 days on tables of terabytes: the delete is cheap and immediate. Choose a physical rewrite (a purge, or partitioning the table by a time bucket and rewriting old partitions on a schedule) when auditors want the bytes gone, or when old files are kept in time travel. You give up cluster time and a longer job in the second case, and you give up certainty about the bytes in the first.
Choose per-person encryption keys (crypto-shredding: delete the key and the data becomes unreadable) when data lives in places that are hard to rewrite, such as backups and append-only logs. Choose plain deletion when you can reach every copy. Crypto-shredding adds a key store as a critical system and makes joins on encrypted fields hard; plain deletion has no such cost but cannot reach a tape backup.
interview: How would you design deletion for a lakehouse with 500 tables fed from one raw event log? || Keep a catalogue of tables with the personal-data columns and the key type of each. Resolve a request through the identity graph into IDs, then run a deletion job per table in dependency order, using deletion vectors and the change feed to reach derived tables. Write an audit row per table and request, run purge and VACUUM on a schedule shorter than the legal deadline, and add a test that plants a known ID and checks it is gone everywhere.
A bid request arrives with a GPP string that says the user opted out of sharing. What should your bidder do? || Parse the section named in `gpp_sid` (for California section 8, or the national section 7), read the sharing opt-out flag, and treat the request as non-personalised: no cross-context ID, no audience data, no precise location. Log the decision. If the string is missing but `regs` says the law applies, assume the stricter case.
Why can a deletion request not simply be run on the table that the user is in? || The person is known by several IDs (device, cookie, email), so you need the ID graph to find them all, and the data has been copied to derived tables, feature stores, caches and backups. The request must follow lineage to every copy, and each copy needs its own method and deadline.
What is the risk of hashing IDs with SHA-256 to match a deletion list, and how would you reduce it? || An attacker or partner who holds a list of emails can hash it and match, so the hashes are still personal data and need the same protection. Reduce the risk by limiting who can read the list, deleting it after use, using a keyed hash (HMAC) when both sides agree on the key, and logging every use.
friction: The first pain is that the law is vague and the data is exact. "Specific, informed, unambiguous" is not a bit field, and courts and regulators keep changing the reading. The TCF has been challenged in the Belgian and EU courts, and each new version (2.2, then 2.3) changes what vendors must do. Systems built on a fixed reading break when the rules move, so the purposes and the region logic must be data you can change, not code.
Consent signals also leak. One intermediary can strip a string, and some publishers pass a string even when the user was never asked. The safe rule is to treat unknown as no. Signals also age: a TC string has a creation time and a CMP must ask again, often after about a year or when the vendor list changes.
Deletion has a long tail. Backups, logs and trained models are expensive to scrub, and removing one person's influence from a trained model is an open research problem (machine unlearning); in practice companies retrain on a schedule and document the delay.
practice: Treat privacy as a data model first. Give every event a region (where the person is), a consent state per purpose, and the pseudonymous keys. Keep a catalogue of every table with its personal columns, key type, retention and deletion rule; refuse to register a table without one. Make this catalogue the input to the deletion job, so a new table is covered at birth.
For the forward path, parse the TC and GPP strings with the IAB's open-source libraries rather than your own bit code, and validate them: check the version, the creation time and that your vendor ID is present. Fail closed: a missing or invalid string means non-personalised handling. Log each decision with its reason, so you can later show how a request was treated.
For the backward path, batch requests (daily is a good start), resolve them through the ID graph, join against the tables and delete with deletion vectors. Schedule purge and VACUUM so the physical deletion is well inside your deadline, restrict time travel on personal tables, and keep an audit table of key, table, rows removed and completion time, with no raw data in it. Run a canary test: insert a synthetic person, delete them, and alert if any copy remains. Know your deadlines: one month under the GDPR (extendable by two), 45 days for the CCPA, and the 45-day DROP cycle for data brokers in California. Build the pipeline so each deadline is a configuration value.
quiz: What does a TC string contain, and who writes it? || It is a compact base64url bit record written by the CMP. It holds version and timestamps, the CMP ID, the vendor-list version, 24 bits of purpose consent, 24 bits of legitimate-interest flags and a vendor section with the vendors the user accepted.
Why do deletion vectors matter for right-to-erasure on a lakehouse? || Files are immutable, so a plain DELETE rewrites every file that holds a matching row, which for random IDs is nearly all of them. A deletion vector writes a small bitmap that hides the rows at once, and a later purge and VACUUM remove the bytes in bulk.
Why must a deletion request go through the identity graph first? || The person appears under several IDs (device IDs, cookies, emails). Deleting by the one ID in the request would leave rows under the others, so the graph resolves the request to the full set of linked IDs.
A table has 2,000 files of 1 GB and a daily batch touches every file. How much data does a classic DELETE rewrite per day, versus deletion vectors? || A classic DELETE reads and rewrites about 2 TB per day. Deletion vectors write only small bitmaps (kilobytes per file) plus the key join, and the rewrite is deferred to a periodic purge.
When would you choose crypto-shredding over deleting rows? || When copies are hard to reach, such as backups, archives and append-only logs: encrypt each person's data with their own key and delete the key. The cost is a critical key store and difficult joins on encrypted fields.

@@ dataquality
value: A data pipeline can fail without any error. A job finishes with exit code 0, the table is updated on time, and the dashboard loads, but a source changed a field from dollars to cents, or a join key started to arrive null, or one region stopped sending events. Nobody sees it until a person in finance notices a number that looks wrong, often weeks later. By then, reports have been sent, models have been trained on the bad data, and the fix means recomputing months of tables. Software engineers catch this kind of failure with tests. Data quality is the same idea applied to data, which is harder because the input changes every day and nobody wrote down what "correct" means.
The practice has two parts. **Data quality checks** (also called expectations) are rules that you write down and run with each pipeline step: this column is never null, this ID is unique, this amount is between 0 and 10,000, today's table has at least 80% of yesterday's rows. A failing rule can stop the pipeline, drop or quarantine the bad rows, or raise an alert. **Data observability** adds monitoring for what you did not think to write down: it watches freshness, volume, schema and value distributions, learns what is normal, and alerts when a table drifts. Together they move the discovery of a problem from the end of the chain (the consumer) to the point where it enters.
For the layer above, dbt tests run after each model, and Lakeflow Spark Declarative Pipelines on Databricks (earlier named Delta Live Tables) attach expectations to every table. The layers below supply the method. Statistics and estimation (L4) give the baseline: a mean, a spread and an interval for each metric. Hypothesis testing (L4) says when a change is too large to be chance. Sampling (L2) makes checks affordable on billions of rows. Schema evolution (L2) defines which structural changes are safe, and anomaly detection (L2) is the engine behind the volume and distribution monitors.
terms: Expectation — a rule about data that should hold, such as "order_id is unique"; also called a check, test or assertion.
Data contract — an agreement between the team that produces a dataset and the teams that read it, covering schema, meaning, freshness and quality limits.
Freshness — how old the newest data in a table is compared with when it should have arrived.
Completeness — the share of expected rows or non-null values that are present.
Distribution drift — a change in the shape of the values in a column, such as a mean that moves or a new category that appears.
Quarantine — a side table where rows that fail a check are stored for inspection, so the main table stays clean.
Data lineage — the map of which tables and columns feed which others; it shows what a bad table has contaminated.
Data downtime — the period when data is missing, late or wrong, in the same sense as service downtime.
history: Quality control in manufacturing is older than computers. Walter Shewhart introduced control charts at Bell Labs in the 1920s: plot a measurement over time, draw lines at the mean plus and minus three standard deviations, and investigate any point outside them. Most volume monitors today are a direct copy of this idea.
In databases, constraints (NOT NULL, UNIQUE, CHECK, foreign keys) were the first quality tool, enforced at write time. Data warehouses in the 1990s moved data in nightly batches from many sources, and ETL tools added validation steps and "reject" tables. The big-data era broke this model: files in a data lake have no constraints, schemas change without notice, and nobody enforces anything on write.
Several tools appeared around 2018. Amazon described Deequ, a library on Spark that defines "unit tests for data" and computes metrics in one pass (Schelter and others, VLDB 2018). Great Expectations, an open-source Python library, introduced human-readable expectations. Google's TFX team published a data-validation system for machine learning (Breck and others, 2019) that learns a schema and flags drift between training and serving data. dbt, from 2016, put simple tests (not null, unique, accepted values, relationships) beside every model. In 2019 the company Monte Carlo and its founder Barr Moses popularised the term data observability, borrowed from software observability, with five pillars: freshness, volume, schema, distribution and lineage. Databricks added expectations to Delta Live Tables, now part of Lakeflow Spark Declarative Pipelines, and the idea of a data contract, popular since about 2021, pushed the checks to the producer.
how: A data quality system has three jobs: define what is expected, run the checks at the right points, and decide what happens on failure.
Step 1: define expectations. They come in layers. Schema checks say which columns exist and their types; they rely on the schema-evolution rules (L2) that say adding a nullable column is safe while renaming or retyping one is breaking. Row-level checks look at each row alone: not null, in a range, matches a pattern, in an allowed set. Table-level checks look at a whole batch: unique keys, row count, share of nulls, sum of amounts. Cross-table checks test relationships: every `campaign_id` in impressions exists in the campaigns table. Freshness checks compare the newest timestamp with the clock. Some rules are written by hand from business knowledge. Others are learned from history: a tool profiles a column and proposes a range or a null-rate limit.
Step 2: pick the place. The cheapest place to check is at the entrance of the pipeline, where the data is still close to its producer and the owner is known. The next is between the layers of a lakehouse, for example raw to cleaned to curated, where each step trusts only what the previous step has checked. Checks at the end, on the final report, find the problem but give no clue about where it started. A good rule is to check each table once, as it is written, and to make the check part of the same job, so it cannot be skipped.
Step 3: choose the action per rule. A critical rule, such as a unique primary key, should fail the job so bad data never reaches readers. A rule about a few rows, such as a malformed email, should drop or quarantine those rows and let the rest through. A soft rule, such as a slow drift in a metric, should only warn. In Lakeflow, an expectation can retain the row and count the failure, drop the row, or fail the update. Delta tables also support `NOT NULL` and `CHECK` constraints that reject the whole write.
Step 4: make checks statistical where rules cannot be exact. A null rate of 0.4% today against 0.3% yesterday is not a problem, but against 5% is. This is a hypothesis test (L4): the null hypothesis is that today's data comes from the same process as the history. Statistics gives the baseline and the spread. In practice, monitors use robust estimates, the median and the median absolute deviation, because one bad day in the history should not widen the limits. Seasonality matters, so the baseline for a Monday is built from earlier Mondays. Sampling (L2) keeps the cost low: a profile of 1% of the rows is enough to estimate a null rate or a mean with a small error, though not to check uniqueness or rare values.
Step 5: detect the unknown. An anomaly detector (L2) watches many metrics per table (row count, null rate per column, distinct count, mean, quantiles, time since last update) and flags points outside the learned band. It catches problems nobody wrote a rule for, at the price of more false alarms.
Step 6: route and learn. An alert goes to the owner of the table, which lineage and ownership metadata provide, with the failing check, the size of the problem, and the downstream tables at risk. Failed rows land in a quarantine table with the reason. Each incident ends with a new rule, so the same failure cannot happen silently twice.
Step 7: store the metrics. Every run writes its check results and profile metrics (row count, null rate per column, distinct count, quantiles, newest timestamp) to a small metrics table, keyed by table, column and run time. This table is the history that the statistical monitors read, the evidence for audits, and the source of a quality dashboard. Because the metrics are computed in the same pass as the load, in one scan with many aggregates, the extra cost is small, usually a few percent of the job. When a rule fails, lineage answers the next question, which tables below this one are now suspect, so owners can pause those jobs or mark their data as unreliable instead of finding out one by one. After the fix, the affected partitions are recomputed and the checks run again on the new output before readers are told it is safe.

example: Monitor the daily row count of an impressions table. The last 8 Mondays gave (in millions of rows) 102, 98, 105, 101, 99, 103, 100 and 97. Sorted: 97, 98, 99, 100, 101, 102, 103, 105; the median is (100 + 101) / 2 = 100.5. The absolute distance of each value from 100.5 is 1.5, 2.5, 4.5, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 0.5 and 3.5; sorted: 0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 1.5, 2.5, 2.5, 3.5, 4.5; the median distance (the MAD) is (1.5 + 2.5) / 2 = 2.0. The MAD scaled by 1.4826 estimates the standard deviation of a normal variable, so sigma is about 2.97. Today is Monday with 81 million rows: z = (81 − 100.5) / 2.97 ≈ −6.6, far outside a limit of 3 or 4, so the alert fires. A day with 95 million gives z ≈ −1.9 and passes. A mean and standard deviation of the same eight values would give 100.6 and 2.7, nearly the same, but if one history day had been an outage with 40 million, the plain standard deviation would jump above 20 and hide today's failure, while the MAD hardly moves.
Now count the cost of false alarms. A table of 2,000 monitored metrics, each tested daily at a limit of 3 sigma (a two-sided tail of 0.27% for normal data), produces about 2,000 × 0.0027 ≈ 5 false alerts every day. People will stop reading them within a week. At a limit of 4 sigma the tail is 0.0063%, about 0.13 false alerts a day, one per week, though small real problems are missed. This is the multiple-testing problem from hypothesis testing, and it is why serious monitors tune the limit by the number of checks and group related alerts.
The same table can have row-level rules in a declarative pipeline:
```sql
CREATE OR REFRESH STREAMING TABLE silver_imps (
  CONSTRAINT id_present EXPECT (imp_id IS NOT NULL)
    ON VIOLATION FAIL UPDATE,
  CONSTRAINT cost_range EXPECT (cost BETWEEN 0 AND 100)
    ON VIOLATION DROP ROW,
  CONSTRAINT ts_recent   EXPECT (event_ts > current_date() - 7)
)
AS SELECT * FROM STREAM(bronze_imps);
```
A missing ID stops the update, because every later join would be wrong. A cost outside the range drops that row only. A stale timestamp is kept but counted in the pipeline's quality metrics, which a dashboard shows.
intuition: A factory puts inspectors at the gates between stations and a gauge on every line, because a bad part found at the end costs far more than the same part caught at the first station.
Data flows in one direction through many hands, so an error at the start is copied into every table that follows. The cost of a defect therefore grows with distance from where it was made, and the best check sits as close to the source as possible. The two kinds of check fit two kinds of knowledge. Rules encode what you know: an ID must be unique. Statistical monitors encode what the history shows: row counts on a Monday are near 100 million. Between them they cover the failures you expected and many you did not, and both are cheap compared with a wrong number in a board report.
mistakes: People often think data quality means zero bad rows. In fact real sources always have some bad rows, and the goal is a known, bounded and visible error rate. A rule that fails the whole job for one malformed row turns a small problem into an outage; quarantine and a threshold ("fail if more than 1% of rows are bad") fit better.
People often think more checks mean better quality. In fact each check has a false-alarm rate, and 2,000 checks multiply it. A team that gets many alerts a day ignores all of them. A few high-value rules on key columns, tied to a named owner, protect more than thousands of generic ones.
People often think a pipeline that succeeds has produced correct data. In fact a job reports only that code ran. A silent change, such as a source dropping a partition, gives a green pipeline and a wrong table. This is why freshness and volume checks exist apart from job status.
People often think a fixed threshold is enough for volume. In fact traffic has daily and weekly cycles and grows over time. A fixed "at least 90 million rows" fails on every Sunday and passes a real 20% drop on a busy Friday. The baseline must follow the pattern of the metric.
tradeoffs: Choose blocking checks (fail the job) for keys, join columns and fields that money depends on, where a wrong row is worse than a late table. Choose non-blocking checks (drop, quarantine or warn) for descriptive fields and for sources you do not control, where a late table is worse than a few bad rows. You give up freshness in the first case and some correctness in the second.
Choose hand-written rules when you know the business meaning and the failure would be expensive; choose learned monitors when you have hundreds of tables and cannot write rules for each. Rules have almost no false alarms but cover only what you thought of; monitors cover more and cost alert fatigue, and they need weeks of history before they are useful.
Choose to check on a sample when the table has billions of rows and the check is a rate or a mean: 1% of the rows gives a precise estimate at 1% of the cost. Choose a full scan for uniqueness, referential integrity and rare values, which a sample cannot see. For uniqueness on large data, an approximate distinct count gives a cheap signal.
Choose checks at the producer, with a data contract, when you control both sides and can make the producer own quality. Choose checks at the consumer when the source is a third party. Producer checks stop the problem for everyone; consumer checks protect only one team, and each team repeats the work.
interview: Your daily revenue table is wrong for three days before anyone notices. What do you change? || Find the cause and the first table where the error appeared using lineage, then add a rule there, such as a freshness or sum check against the source. Add a volume and distribution monitor with a same-weekday baseline and route alerts to the table owner. Backfill the three days, tag the affected downstream tables, and add the incident as a permanent test.
How do you set the alert threshold for a row-count monitor without drowning the team? || Use a seasonal baseline (same weekday or hour) with robust statistics (median and MAD) so one past outage does not widen the band. Choose the limit from the number of monitored metrics, since 2,000 checks at 3 sigma give about 5 false alarms a day. Group related alerts and send only the top-level one, and review the false-positive rate every month.
A source team adds a column and renames another. How should the pipeline react? || Adding a nullable column is a compatible change and should pass with a notice. A rename or type change is breaking: the schema check should fail the ingest, or route the new data to quarantine, and notify the producer. A data contract with versioned schemas makes the rule explicit.
Why quarantine bad rows instead of dropping them? || Dropped rows are gone, so you cannot see what was wrong, tell the producer, or recover the data after the rule is fixed. A quarantine table keeps the row with the failed rule and the time, so you can count, inspect and replay it.
How would you check that two large tables agree after a migration? || Compare row counts, then sums and distinct counts per key column and per partition, then compare row-level hashes by key for the partitions that differ. A full diff by key is exact but costly, so start with aggregates to narrow down where to look.
friction: Quality rules need owners, and ownership is the hard part. A check that fails at night needs someone who understands the data and can act, but the producer is often another team with other priorities. Without a contract and a named owner, the alert goes to the consumer, who can only wait. Many programmes fail here, not on the technology.
Detection is also limited by what you measure. A monitor on row counts will not see a column filled with plausible but wrong values, and a distribution monitor will miss a slow drift of 0.1% per day that adds up to 30% in a year. Checks on meaning ("revenue matches the finance system") need a second source of truth, which is costly to build.
There is a cost in compute and delay. Full-table checks on large tables add minutes to every run, profiling many columns adds scans, and blocking checks add failure paths that must be retried and backfilled. Observability tools that read data to compute metrics may also copy sensitive data, so they need the same access controls as the pipeline.
Last, the cost of too many alerts. Anomaly detection has a high false-positive rate when tables are small, new or seasonal, and alert fatigue is the most common reason that teams switch such systems off.
practice: Start with the tables that matter most: those behind revenue, billing and board metrics. For each, write 5 to 10 rules: primary key unique and not null, key foreign keys present, amounts in range, freshness within the expected hour, and a row count against the same weekday. Put them in the pipeline, not in a separate tool that runs later, and decide for each whether it fails, quarantines or warns.
Use what your stack gives you. In dbt, add `unique`, `not_null`, `accepted_values` and `relationships` tests to each model, and run them after the model builds, so a failure stops the models that depend on it. In Lakeflow Spark Declarative Pipelines, use expectations with DROP ROW or FAIL UPDATE, and read the quality metrics the pipeline stores in its event log. On Delta tables use `NOT NULL` and `CHECK` constraints for rules that must never be broken. Add a monitor for freshness and volume on every ingested table, even where you have no other rules.
Make ownership part of the metadata: each table has an owning team and an on-call channel. Write data contracts for the datasets that other teams depend on, including the schema, the freshness promise and the allowed rate of bad rows. Review alerts monthly, remove noisy checks, and add a rule after every incident. Track a few simple numbers: the share of tables with an owner, the time from a bad load to its detection, and the false-alarm rate.
quiz: Why use the median and MAD instead of the mean and standard deviation for a volume monitor? || One outage or spike in the history pulls the mean and widens the standard deviation, which hides the next failure. The median and MAD barely change, so the band stays tight.
Why should a row-count baseline use the same weekday? || Traffic has a weekly cycle, so a Sunday count compared with weekday history always looks low. A same-weekday baseline removes the cycle, so only real changes are flagged.
Which Lakeflow expectation action would you use for a null primary key, and which for an out-of-range cost? || FAIL UPDATE for the null key, because all later joins would be wrong. DROP ROW for the bad cost, which removes only that row and lets the rest through.
You monitor 2,000 metrics daily at 3 sigma. How many false alerts do you expect per day? || About 2,000 × 0.0027 ≈ 5. At 4 sigma it is about 2,000 × 0.000063 ≈ 0.13 per day.
When is a check better placed at the producer than at the consumer? || When you control the producer and many teams read the data: one contract and one check protect everyone, and the owner can fix the cause. Consumer checks are right for third-party sources and protect only one team.

@@ finops
value: In a data centre you buy servers once, and the cost is fixed after you have bought them. In the cloud, every engineer can create a cost with one API call: a cluster with 200 nodes, a terabyte of storage, a data transfer between regions. The bill arrives monthly, in one line per service, and it does not say which team or which product caused it. For a company with a large data platform the bill can reach millions of dollars a month, and a single forgotten cluster or a badly written query can add tens of thousands before anyone notices.
FinOps (cloud financial operations) is the practice of making that spending visible, owned and efficient. It has three jobs. First, **attribution**: every resource carries an owner tag, so cost can be shown to each team (showback) or charged to its budget (chargeback). Second, **optimisation**: reduce the price paid per unit of work (spot capacity, committed-use discounts, cheaper storage tiers) and reduce the units used (right-sizing, shutting down idle compute, deleting data nobody reads). Third, **governance**: budgets, alerts, and policies that stop expensive mistakes before they happen. The aim is not the lowest bill but the best cost per unit of value, such as cost per thousand ad impressions or per query.
For the layers above, Databricks bills in DBUs and exposes billing system tables, cluster policies and tags for chargeback. The cloud providers sell the spot, reserved and committed-use prices that FinOps uses. Snowflake bills credits and suspends idle warehouses. The layers below give the levers. Autoscaling (L2) makes the amount of compute follow the load. Separating storage from compute (L2) lets compute be switched off when idle while data stays cheap in object storage. Tiered storage (L2) moves cold data to cheaper classes.
terms: Showback and chargeback — showback reports each team's cost to the team; chargeback actually moves the cost into the team's budget.
Tag (label) — a key and value, such as `team=segments`, attached to a cloud resource so that its cost can be grouped.
On-demand, spot, reserved — on-demand is full price with no commitment; spot is spare capacity at a large discount that the provider can take back; reserved or committed-use is a discount for promising to use a certain amount for one to three years.
Savings Plan / committed-use discount — a commitment to spend a fixed amount per hour for one or three years in return for a lower rate.
Right-sizing — choosing the instance size or cluster size that matches the real load instead of the original guess.
Unit cost — cost divided by a business measure, such as dollars per 1,000 impressions; it shows efficiency even when the total grows.
Egress — data transferred out of a cloud region or to the internet, billed per gigabyte.
FOCUS — the FinOps Open Cost and Usage Specification, a common column format for billing data from different providers.
history: Cloud billing started with AWS in 2006: EC2 at 10 cents an hour and S3 at 15 cents a gigabyte-month, billed to a credit card. Early users were excited by the lack of purchase orders, and soon surprised by bills. Cost control was done by hand, in spreadsheets, by a few engineers.
The pricing models grew in steps. AWS added Reserved Instances in 2009 (a commitment for a discount), Spot Instances in 2009 (an auction for spare capacity, later simplified into a stable price with a two-minute warning), and Savings Plans in 2019 (a commitment to spend per hour, flexible across instance types). Google offered sustained-use discounts automatically and later committed-use discounts, and Azure added reservations. Storage got classes: Glacier (2012), S3 Standard-IA (2015) and Intelligent-Tiering, which moves objects between classes by itself (2018). Data warehouses and Databricks introduced per-second billing, and serverless products that bill by use.
The practice got its name and shape around 2019. J.R. Storment and Mike Fuller wrote "Cloud FinOps" (O'Reilly, 2019; second edition 2023), describing a cycle of Inform, Optimize and Operate and a cross-functional team of engineering, finance and product. The FinOps Foundation, now a project of the Linux Foundation, formed in 2019 to set the framework and certifications. A recurring problem was that every provider reported cost in a different format, so in 2022 the Foundation started FOCUS, an open specification for billing columns. Version 1.0 appeared in June 2024, and the large cloud providers have added exports in it; version 1.3 was released in December 2025. The scope has grown from cloud bills to SaaS, data centres and, more recently, the cost of AI services.
how: FinOps is a loop: see the cost, assign it, reduce it, and keep it from growing back.
Step 1: get complete, detailed billing data. Each provider produces a detailed export, one row per resource, per hour or per day: AWS Cost and Usage Report, Google billing export to BigQuery, Azure cost exports. Load it into a table, ideally in the FOCUS column layout so that several clouds look the same. For Databricks there is a second source, the billing system table `system.billing.usage`, which has one row per usage record with the SKU, the quantity in DBUs and the custom tags. Note that on classic compute, Databricks charges DBUs and the cloud charges the virtual machines, so the full cost of a cluster is in two places and joins on tags.
Step 2: attribute. Cost is grouped by tags: team, product, environment, cost centre. Tags must be required at creation, by cluster policies in Databricks, by tag policies and service control rules in AWS, or by the infrastructure-as-code module that creates the resource. Resources that cannot be tagged, and shared platforms such as a Kafka cluster or a shared warehouse, need allocation rules: split by measured usage (bytes, queries, CPU-hours) or in proportion to direct cost. The share of untagged cost is the first health number; teams that reach 90% or more can make real decisions from the data.
Step 3: set the unit. Total spend rises when the business grows, which is good news. A better measure divides cost by a business output: dollars per million bid requests, per processed terabyte, per active campaign. Unit cost shows whether engineering is getting more efficient, and it ties cost to the product conversation.
Step 4: reduce the price per unit. This uses the lower concepts directly. Separation of storage and compute (L2) means that data lives in object storage and compute can be rented only while a job runs, so idle clusters can be stopped. Autoscaling (L2) lets a cluster grow for a heavy stage and shrink after it, so you pay for the average, not the peak. Spot capacity gives discounts of often 60% to 90% on virtual machines for work that can survive losing a machine, which suits Spark with its task retries. Commitments (Savings Plans, reservations, committed-use) cut 20% to 40% or more on the steady baseline. Tiered storage (L2) moves objects not read for 30 or 90 days to cheaper classes, with a rule that runs by itself.
Three details decide whether these levers work. Spot capacity is taken back with a warning of about two minutes, so the job must be able to lose a node: Spark re-runs the lost tasks, and shuffle data held on the lost node is recomputed, which is why a few on-demand nodes and a retry limit are kept. Autoscaling reacts to load with a delay of minutes and has a minimum and a maximum; a high minimum is a hidden fixed cost, and a low maximum caps the speed. A storage lifecycle rule works on object age or last access: for example, move to an infrequent-access class after 30 days and to an archive class after 180 days, and expire old versions after 90. The rule must match real read patterns, or the retrieval fees will erase the saving.
Step 5: reduce the units. Right-size clusters from measured CPU and memory use; shut off dev and test environments overnight; delete unused snapshots, volumes and old table versions; compact small files so queries scan less; drop columns and partitions nobody reads. Often the biggest saving is a query that scans ten times more data than it needs.
Step 6: keep it. Set budgets with alerts per team, anomaly alerts on daily spend, and a regular review (weekly for large spenders) where owners see their unit cost and open optimisation tasks. Policies make the cheap path the default: maximum cluster size, required auto-termination, approved instance types.

example: A nightly Spark job runs 3 hours on a cluster of 32 workers. Take on-demand VM price as $0.62 an hour, spot price as $0.25 an hour, and the Databricks charge as 1 DBU per node-hour at $0.20 per DBU (these are round numbers for the sum, not a price list). On-demand cost per night: VMs 32 × 3 × 0.62 = $59.5, DBUs 32 × 3 × 0.20 = $19.2, total $78.7, so about $2,360 a month. Move 28 workers to spot and keep 4 on-demand, with 10% extra runtime for lost tasks. VMs: 28 × 3.3 × 0.25 = $23.1, plus 4 × 3.3 × 0.62 = $8.2, so $31.3. DBUs: 32 × 3.3 × 0.20 = $21.1. Total $52.4, or $1,570 a month, a saving of 33%. Notice that spot discounts only the VM part; the DBU part stays and even grows with the longer run. If the cluster is also idle for half of its time because the job is skewed, the next saving is autoscaling or fixing the skew, not a bigger discount.
Now a commitment. Say compute use is 60 units in the quietest hours and 100 at the peak, and on-demand costs $1 per unit-hour. A one-year commitment gives a rate of $0.70 per unit-hour, but you pay for the committed amount every hour, used or not. If you commit to 60 units, they are always used, and you save 0.30 × 60 = $18 per hour, 30%. If you commit to 80 units and the extra 20 are used only 50% of the hours, those 20 units cost you 0.70 × 20 = $14 each hour but would have cost $10 on average on demand, a loss of $4 per hour. The break-even is a use of 70% of the hours, because 0.70 / 1.00 = 0.70. So commit to what you use nearly always; leave the variable part on demand or spot.
Finally, find waste with a query on the usage table:
```sql
SELECT custom_tags['team'] AS team,
       sku_name,
       sum(usage_quantity) AS dbus
FROM system.billing.usage
WHERE usage_date >= date_sub(current_date(), 30)
GROUP BY 1, 2
ORDER BY dbus DESC
LIMIT 20;
```
Rows where `team` is null are the untagged spend. They are the first thing to fix.
intuition: A shared office electricity meter: when nobody sees who uses what, every light stays on; when each team sees its own line, the waste is turned off by the people who understand it.
Cloud turned a purchase decision into a stream of small decisions made by engineers. Finance cannot review them one by one, and engineers do not see prices. FinOps puts the price and the owner in the same place as the decision. The levers are natural because they follow how a cloud is built: you pay for what you reserve, not for what you use, so reserve less by following the load; you pay by the hour, so stop what is idle; storage costs by the gigabyte-month, so keep the cold part on cheaper media. Each lever trades flexibility or speed for a lower price, and the work is to find the trades that cost you nothing you need.
mistakes: People often think FinOps means cutting the bill. In fact cutting is easy and often harmful: a smaller cluster that delays a revenue report costs more than it saves. The goal is the best value per dollar, so measure unit cost and quality together.
People often think spot instances are cheap but unreliable and so only for tests. In fact spot suits any work that can survive losing a node, and Spark batch jobs retry lost tasks, so they use spot well. What does not fit is a single-node stateful service, or the driver and shuffle-heavy stages with no fallback. Mix a few on-demand nodes with spot workers.
People often think a commitment is always a saving. In fact a commitment is a fixed cost that you pay even when usage falls. If a migration or an optimisation lowers your use below the commitment, the commitment turns into waste. Commit to the stable baseline, after you have optimised, not before.
People often think storage is cheap so it needs no attention. In fact object storage is cheap per gigabyte, but the volume only grows, every old table version and snapshot counts, and the request and egress charges are separate. Moving a petabyte between regions at roughly 2 cents per gigabyte costs about $20,000, roughly one month of storage for the same data.
People often think the cloud bill is enough to know who spends. In fact the bill shows services, not owners. Without tags and allocation rules, the largest line is "shared platform", and nobody feels responsible for it.
tradeoffs: Choose spot capacity when the work is retryable and a delay of minutes is acceptable, as in batch ETL, backfills and model training with checkpoints; choose on-demand for services with a latency target, drivers, and single points of failure. Spot gives 60% to 90% off the VM price, but the provider can take the node back with about two minutes of warning, so you give up predictability of finish time.
Choose a one-year or three-year commitment for a baseline you have used above about 70% of the hours for a year and expect to keep; choose on-demand for anything that may be migrated, shrunk or retired within the term. The 3-year term saves more per unit but locks the platform decision, so you give up the freedom to change instance families, regions or vendors.
Choose chargeback when teams control their own spend and the finance process can accept allocated costs; choose showback when allocation rules are still rough and you want teams to learn their cost without a fight over every dollar. Chargeback creates strong incentives and also arguments about shared costs and tag errors; showback is gentler and often is ignored.
Choose aggressive right-sizing for batch and development environments, where a slower job is acceptable. Keep headroom of 20% to 30% for latency-sensitive services, because the saved money is less than the cost of an outage. Choose a colder storage class only when the data is read less than about once a quarter: infrequent-access classes charge retrieval fees and a minimum storage time of 30 days (archive classes 90 to 180 days), so moving data that is still read often costs more than leaving it.
interview: Your Databricks spend grew 40% in a quarter while traffic grew 10%. How do you investigate? || Break the cost down by tag, SKU and workspace from the billing system table to see what grew, and compute unit cost per workload. Look for new always-on clusters, jobs that run on all-purpose compute instead of job compute, growth in scanned data from a changed query or a missing partition filter, and an untagged increase. Fix the biggest item first and set budget alerts for the team that owns it.
When is a Savings Plan a bad purchase? || When use falls below the commitment, since you pay the committed hourly amount anyway. The break-even is the discount ratio: with a 30% discount, the committed capacity must be used more than 70% of the hours. It is a bad purchase before a migration, before an optimisation that cuts use, or for a variable part of the load.
How would you allocate the cost of a shared Kafka cluster to ten teams? || Measure each team's share by bytes produced, bytes consumed and partitions held, weight them (storage and network dominate), and split the cluster's cost by those shares. Publish the method, because teams accept an imperfect rule they can verify more readily than a perfect rule they cannot.
Why can moving data between regions or out of the cloud cost more than storing it? || Transfer is billed per gigabyte, at roughly 2 cents between regions and about 9 cents to the internet in common price lists, while storage is around 2 cents per gigabyte per month. A single full copy therefore costs about as much as one to four months of storage, and a service that reads across regions pays on every read.
What would you put in a cluster policy to stop cost surprises? || Required owner and cost-centre tags, a maximum node count, mandatory auto-termination after a short idle time, an allowed list of instance types, spot with fallback by default for jobs, and a maximum runtime for interactive clusters.
friction: The data is hard. Bills have millions of rows, shared costs are not attributable by tags, and some charges (support, taxes, discounts, credits) are applied at the account level and must be spread out. Tagging is never complete: resources created by managed services or by old scripts come without tags, and teams change names, so the tags drift. Allocation of shared platforms is political as much as technical.
Savings have a cost in engineering time and risk. Moving a job to spot adds retries and longer tails; right-sizing a cluster may expose a job that needs the memory only once a month; deleting old data needs an owner who confirms it is not needed. Commitments reduce flexibility: a three-year plan can block a move to a new instance family or another provider. Prices and discount programmes change, so a model built on one price list ages.
There is a human limit as well. Engineers are rewarded for features and reliability, not for savings, so cost work is the first to drop when a deadline comes. The practice works when cost is part of the review of designs and of the on-call dashboard, and when leaders ask for unit cost, not for a single percentage target. Finally, cost reduction can reach a floor: after the easy 20% to 30% is gone, further savings need architecture changes such as different file layouts, caching or removing a duplicated pipeline.
practice: Start with visibility. Turn on the detailed billing export, load it into your lakehouse, and build a table with team, product, environment and unit cost. Make owner tags mandatory at creation (cluster policies, infrastructure-as-code modules, tag policies), and track the untagged share each week until it is under 10%. Add a budget and an anomaly alert per team before you start optimising, so that regressions are noticed.
Then take the big, safe savings in order. Stop idle: auto-termination on every interactive cluster, schedules that shut down dev and test overnight. Right-size from measured utilisation. Use job compute and spot with on-demand fallback for batch, and make sure jobs are idempotent. Add lifecycle rules for object storage, delete stale snapshots and old table versions, and compact small files. Review the top ten most expensive queries or jobs each month, because a single fix often saves more than a discount programme.
Buy commitments last, on the baseline you still have after optimising, and cover about 60% to 80% of it, leaving room to shrink. Review them every quarter for utilisation. Keep the loop alive: a monthly review with engineering, finance and product, owners present, showing unit cost, the top changes and the savings backlog. In Databricks, use system tables for usage and list prices, compute policies for guardrails, and serverless only where its higher unit price is repaid by lower idle time and less operations work.
quiz: What is the difference between showback and chargeback? || Showback shows each team its cost without moving money; chargeback moves the cost into the team's budget. Chargeback gives stronger incentives and more disputes.
Why does moving a Databricks cluster to spot not cut the whole bill? || Spot discounts only the virtual machine price. The DBU charge from Databricks stays the same, and a longer run from lost tasks increases it.
Why is the break-even of a 30% commitment discount 70% usage? || You pay the committed rate (70% of on-demand) every hour. It beats on-demand only if the capacity is used at least 70% of the hours; below that, on-demand for those hours would have cost less.

What is the monthly waste, and the fix? || Idle 15 hours × 40 × $0.80 = $480 per day, about $14,400 per 30 days. Fix with auto-termination or by using job clusters that start for the run, or autoscaling to a small minimum.
When should you choose an infrequent-access storage class? || When the data is read less than about once a quarter and will stay at least 30 days. Retrieval fees and minimum storage durations make it more expensive for data that is read often or deleted soon.

@@ sre
value: Every service team faces the same conflict. Developers are rewarded for shipping features, and each release is a risk to the service. Operations people are rewarded for stability, and the safest system is one that nobody changes. In the traditional model these two groups sit in different teams, hand code over a wall, and argue about each release. Operations work also grows with the service: if each new server needs one more hour of manual work a week, then a service ten times larger needs ten times the staff.
Site reliability engineering (SRE) is Google's answer, started in 2003 by Ben Treynor Sloss, who described it as what happens when you ask a software engineer to design an operations team. Three ideas carry it. First, reliability gets an explicit numeric target, the SLO, and the gap between the target and perfect is an **error budget** that developers may spend on risk. Second, repetitive manual operations work, called **toil**, is capped (Google's rule is at most 50% of an SRE's time) and the rest of the time goes to engineering that removes toil. Third, every serious incident ends in a **blameless postmortem** that looks for faults in systems and processes, not in people, and produces tracked actions.
For the layer above, Prometheus alerting rules implement SRE's burn-rate alerts, and incident tools such as PagerDuty and incident.io implement its on-call and incident workflow. The layers below supply the content. SLIs, SLOs and error budgets (L1) are the targets. Observability (L1) is the evidence, since you cannot run what you cannot see. Load shedding and admission control (L2) keep an overloaded service alive. Progressive delivery (L2) limits the damage of a bad release. Backups and disaster recovery (L2) turn the worst case into a practised procedure.
terms: Toil — manual, repetitive, automatable work that is tied to running a service and grows with its size; it has no lasting value after it is done.
Error budget — the allowed amount of unreliability, equal to 100% minus the SLO; a 99.9% SLO leaves 0.1%, or 43.2 minutes per 30 days.
Burn rate — how fast the budget is being used compared with the rate that would use it exactly at the end of the window; a burn rate of 1 uses it all in the window.
On-call — the duty to answer pages about a service outside normal hours, usually in a weekly rotation.
Incident commander — the one person who coordinates a major incident, assigns roles and makes decisions, while others do the technical work.
Postmortem — a written review of an incident: impact, timeline, causes, what went well and badly, and action items with owners.
MTTR — mean time to recovery; the average time from the start of an incident to normal service; mitigation first, root cause later.
Production readiness review — a checklist review before a service is handed to SRE or launched, covering monitoring, capacity, rollback and failure modes.
history: Google was small in 2003, and Ben Treynor Sloss, a software engineer, was asked to run its production systems. He staffed the team with people who could write code and used the same discipline for operations. The reason was practical: Google's services were growing faster than any team could hire sysadmins, so operations had to scale by software, not by people. The rules that came out of this, such as the 50% cap on operations work, were a way to stop the team drifting into the old model.
The practice stayed inside Google for over a decade. In 2016 O'Reilly published "Site Reliability Engineering: How Google Runs Production Systems" (Beyer, Jones, Petoff and Murphy), free to read online. It was followed by "The Site Reliability Workbook" (2018), which added concrete recipes such as multi-window burn-rate alerting, and "Building Secure and Reliable Systems" (2020). Other companies adopted the name and parts of the practice: Amazon, Meta, LinkedIn, Netflix, Dropbox and many smaller companies created SRE teams, often adapting them to their own size. The same period saw the growth of the DevOps movement (from about 2009), which shares the goal of joining development and operations. A common saying is that "class SRE implements interface DevOps".
Tools followed practice. PagerDuty (2009) made on-call paging a product, Prometheus (2012, from SoundCloud, modelled on Google's Borgmon) and Grafana made service metrics standard, and incident-management products such as Opsgenie, Statuspage and incident.io (2021) automated the coordination around incidents. Postmortem culture spread from Google and also from older safety fields like aviation and medicine, and from John Allspaw's 2012 essay on blameless postmortems at Etsy.
how: SRE is a set of habits joined by one rule: reliability is measured, and the measurement drives decisions.
Step 1: set the target. For each user-facing service the team and the product owner pick a few SLIs (the measurement, such as the share of requests answered in under 300 ms without error) and an SLO for each (the target, for example 99.9% over 30 days). The SLO is not 100%, because the last 0.1% costs more than users value and a perfect service cannot change. The remainder is the error budget, and the team writes an error-budget policy in advance: while budget remains, releases go ahead; when it is spent, releases are frozen except for fixes to reliability, until the service recovers. This turns the argument between speed and stability into a shared number.
Step 2: see the service. Observability (L1) provides metrics, logs and traces, and dashboards for the golden signals: latency, traffic, errors and saturation. Alerts are written on the SLO, not on causes like CPU. The Workbook's method is multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting: page when the budget is being used 14.4 times too fast over 1 hour (that is 2% of a 30-day budget in an hour), page at 6 times over 6 hours (5%), and open a ticket at 1 time over 3 days (10%). Each alert pairs a long window with a short one, such as 5 minutes, so it also stops quickly once the problem is fixed. Alerts that need no action are removed.
Step 3: limit the damage of change. Google's experience is that roughly 70% of outages follow a change to a live system. So releases use progressive delivery (L2): canary a small share of traffic, compare its SLIs with the baseline, widen in stages, and roll back automatically on a bad signal. Feature flags separate deploying code from turning it on. Load shedding and admission control (L2) are designed in: when load passes capacity, the service rejects low-priority work early, with retry budgets and backoff, instead of failing for everyone in a cascade.
Step 4: respond to incidents. On-call engineers carry the pager in a rotation, and the load is capped. Google's rule of thumb is no more than two incidents per 12-hour shift, so each one gets time for follow-up. When an incident is large, the team uses a role structure borrowed from emergency services: an incident commander, an operations lead who changes the system, and a communications lead who updates stakeholders. The first goal is mitigation, which means stopping the user impact by rolling back, failing over or shedding load, not finding the root cause. Runbooks list known mitigations, and training exercises such as "Wheel of Misfortune" role-plays practise them.
Step 5: learn. After a significant incident the team writes a postmortem within days: impact in users and minutes, the timeline, the trigger and the underlying causes, what went well, what went badly, where luck helped, and action items with owners and deadlines. It is blameless because people hide information when they fear punishment, and the usual cause is a system that allowed a reasonable person to make a mistake. Actions are tracked, and the SRE team reviews the pattern across postmortems.
Step 6: remove toil and plan capacity. The team measures how much time goes to tickets and manual work and automates the largest items. It forecasts demand, plans capacity ahead of launches and practises recovery: restoring a backup (L2) to prove that it works, and running disaster-recovery drills. Google's yearly DiRT exercise turns off systems on purpose to test the plans. Backups that have never been restored are only hopes.

example: A search API has an SLO of 99.9% good requests over 30 days and serves 1,000 requests per second on average. Over 30 days that is 1,000 × 2,592,000 = 2.592 billion requests. The error budget is 0.1%, about 2.6 million failed requests, or 43.2 minutes of full outage.
A bad deploy raises the error rate to 10% for 15 minutes. The failed requests are 0.10 × 1,000 × 900 = 90,000, which is 3.5% of the monthly budget. The burn rate is the observed error rate divided by the allowed rate: 10% / 0.1% = 100. At that rate the whole budget would be gone in 720 hours / 100 = 7.2 hours.
Now see when the alerts fire. The 1-hour page threshold is a burn rate of 14.4. With errors at 100 times the allowed rate, the one-hour average reaches 14.4 after 14.4 / 100 × 60 minutes = 8.6 minutes, and the 5-minute window agrees, so the page goes out at about 9 minutes. With a slower fault of 2% errors (burn rate 20), the one-hour average passes 14.4 after about 43 minutes. A fault at 0.4% errors (burn rate 4) stays under both page thresholds (14.4 and 6) and raises only the ticket alert, which is right: the budget would last 7.5 days, and a person can fix it in working hours.
Now the toil sum. On-call receives 40 manual tickets a week, each 15 minutes: 10 hours. If 30 of them have the same cause, a script that takes 3 days to write (24 hours) and removes 7.5 hours of work a week pays for itself in about 3 weeks, and the time is released for engineering. Measuring the work is what makes this argument possible.
intuition: An error budget is a household budget for risk: the family can spend it on something useful, but only if somebody counts what is left.
The design is natural because it gives each side a reason to cooperate. Developers want to ship, and the budget tells them how much risk they can take; if the service is very reliable, they can ship faster. SRE wants a stable service, and the budget gives a neutral number rather than an opinion, so a freeze is a rule they agreed in advance, not a veto. Automation follows from a plain count: if the work grows with the service and the team does not, the only way out is software. The blameless postmortem follows from the same view: people are the part that learns, so the review should make them talk.
mistakes: People often think SRE is a new name for the operations team. In fact an SRE team is mostly software engineers who are asked to treat operations as a software problem. The 50% cap on toil is a rule that protects engineering time, and a team that is above the cap pushes work back to the developers.
People often think the goal is 100% reliability or the highest number of nines. In fact each extra nine costs about ten times more and users cannot see it beyond the reliability of their phone, network and browser. The right SLO is the lowest one that keeps users satisfied, and the leftover budget is for change.
People often think blameless means nobody is accountable. In fact the review is blameless about individuals but strict about actions: every cause must lead to a tracked fix with an owner. Accountability moves from "who made the error" to "who will make this error impossible".
People often think an alert on high CPU is good monitoring. In fact users do not care about CPU, and a busy server can serve perfectly. Alerts on symptoms the user sees (errors, latency against the SLO) are actionable, while cause-based alerts create pages with no action.
tradeoffs: Choose a dedicated SRE team when the service is large, business-critical and has enough complexity to keep several engineers busy, usually from tens of services or a few hundred engineers. Choose "you build it, you run it" with a platform team that provides tools when you have fewer than about ten services or a small company. The dedicated team gives deeper reliability skill and a second opinion on launches; the other gives faster feedback to developers, and costs less. You give up depth in the second case and developer ownership in the first.
Choose a tight SLO (99.99%) when each minute of downtime costs a lot, such as payments or bidding with revenue per second, and you can afford redundancy, careful releases and on-call staff. Choose a looser SLO (99.9% or 99.5%) for internal or batch systems. A tight SLO leaves 4.3 minutes a month, which makes every deploy a risk and means automation must act, since people cannot respond in time.
Choose burn-rate alerts on the SLO when you have a clear user-facing SLI. Choose threshold alerts on causes (disk 90% full, queue length) as a second layer for slow faults that will become an outage later. The first reduces noise; the second gives a hint earlier and is more prone to false pages.
Choose a follow-the-sun on-call (teams in several time zones, each on duty in daytime) when you can afford three sites and the handover process; choose a single-site rotation with a strict cap on night pages when the team is small. The first protects sleep and costs coordination; the second is cheaper and wears people down.
interview: What is an error budget, and what do you do when it is exhausted? || It is 100% minus the SLO over a window, the amount of unreliability the service may have. When it is spent, the pre-agreed policy applies: stop feature releases, work on reliability (fix the causes of the errors, add tests or capacity), and resume when the service is back within the SLO. Exceptions are security fixes and reliability fixes.
Explain multi-window, multi-burn-rate alerting. Why not just alert when error rate exceeds 0.1%? || A single threshold either pages on short blips that use little budget or misses slow burns. With a burn rate you compare the error rate with the allowed rate, and use several pairs: fast burn over a short window pages at once (14.4x over 1 hour), slow burn over a long window creates a ticket (1x over 3 days). A short second window makes the alert reset quickly after the fix.
A new release makes error rates go up 2% at 3 a.m. What should the on-call engineer do first? || Mitigate: roll back or stop the rollout, since roughly 70% of outages follow changes, and confirm on the SLI that the errors fall. Declare an incident if the impact is large, assign an incident commander, and update stakeholders. Root cause analysis happens after users are safe.
How do you decide a task is toil, and what do you do about it? || It is manual, repetitive, automatable, has no lasting value and grows with service size. Measure the hours per week, rank the tasks by hours and by how easy they are to automate, and automate the best ratio first. If toil stays above 50%, hand the work back to the developers or stop taking new services.
Your team is paged 10 times a night. What do you change? || Look at every page: remove or downgrade those that need no action, tie the rest to SLO symptoms, fix repeat causes and automate the mitigation. Add a rotation limit and escalation, and treat the page count as a budget with an owner. A page that a script could fix should be fixed by the script.
friction: SRE depends on culture that is easy to describe and hard to keep. Error budget policy works only if leaders respect a freeze when a launch date is near; the first time a freeze is overridden for a deadline, the number loses its meaning. The 50% toil cap is hard to enforce when the team is understaffed, and the work drifts back to firefighting. Blameless postmortems degrade into blame by another name when managers read them for names.
On-call has a human cost: interrupted sleep, stress and burnout. Rotations with too few people, or services with noisy alerts, cause attrition, and the engineers with the most knowledge leave. The load is not fairly measured by page count alone, because one severe night is worse than many small interruptions.
Measuring reliability is harder than it looks. A good SLI must reflect the user's experience, and choosing it needs product knowledge. SLOs set from dependencies compound: a service that depends on three services with 99.9% each cannot promise more than about 99.7%. Error budgets also say little about severity: ten minutes of full outage at peak hurts more than ten minutes at night.
Finally, small companies cannot copy Google. Google's practice assumes hundreds of services, a common platform and enough engineers to rotate. A company with ten engineers needs the ideas (SLOs, postmortems, toil tracking) without the organisation, and it must resist the title without the practice, where a renamed operations team still answers tickets all day.
practice: Begin with one critical service. Pick two or three SLIs that match user experience, measure them for a month, then set an SLO a little below the measured level and write down the error-budget policy with the product owner, including who can approve an exception. Build a dashboard for the golden signals and add burn-rate alerts on the SLO. Remove each alert that did not lead to action in the last month.
Make releases safe: canary and automatic rollback, feature flags for risky changes, a rule that every change can be rolled back, and a record of deploys on the dashboards so you can see which change preceded a fault. Test overload: know your limit, add load shedding with priorities and retry budgets, and rehearse a failover. Restore a backup on a schedule and record the time it took.
Set the people practices. Keep a weekly rotation of at least five or six people so that nobody is on call more than about one week in six. Write a runbook for each alert and review it after use. Hold a postmortem for each incident that spent more than a set share of the budget, within a week, with owners and dates for the actions, and publish it internally. Track toil time each quarter. A simple set of numbers is enough: SLO attainment, budget burn, pages per shift, time to mitigate, the share of postmortem actions finished on time.

value: One disk holds a few terabytes and fails every few years. A data set of a petabyte does not fit on one machine, and a job that reads it cannot wait for one disk to deliver it at 200 MB per second: at that speed one disk needs about 58 days to read a petabyte. A distributed file system solves both problems. It spreads the data across hundreds or thousands of machines so that many disks read in parallel, and it keeps several copies, or coded pieces, so that a machine can die without losing data. To a program it still looks like a file system: paths, directories, open, read, write.
The design has one idea that almost all systems share. The work is split into two parts with different needs. **Metadata** (the directory tree, which blocks make up each file, where each block lives) is small, changes often and must be exact, so one server, or a small group, holds it. **Data** (the bytes) is huge and read in bulk, so clients fetch it directly from many data servers and the metadata server never touches the bytes. This is how Google's GFS (2003), Hadoop's HDFS and the Lustre and Ceph systems work, in different forms.
For the layers above, HDFS is an open-source copy of the GFS design, and Hadoop MapReduce reads its input splits from a distributed file system and writes results back, running each task on a machine that holds the block (data locality). The layers below supply the parts. File systems (L3) give the familiar interface: names, directories, offsets, permissions. Replication (L2) copies each block to several servers. RPC (L3) is how clients talk to metadata and data servers. Leader election and leases (L2) keep a single active metadata master and fail it over safely. RAID (L2) is the ancestor of the redundancy scheme: parity across disks in one box became erasure coding across machines.
terms: Metadata server (NameNode, master, MDS) — the server that stores the namespace and the map from files to blocks; it does not serve file data.
Data server (DataNode, chunkserver, OSD) — a machine with disks that stores blocks and serves read and write requests directly to clients.
Block (chunk) — a large fixed-size piece of a file, typically 64 to 256 MB, which is the unit of placement and replication.
Replication factor — the number of full copies kept of each block; 3 is the usual default.
Erasure coding — storing a block as k data pieces plus m parity pieces, so any k of the k + m pieces rebuild it; Reed-Solomon (6,3) is common.
Rack awareness — placing copies on different racks, because a rack switch or power feed is a shared failure point.
Lease — a time-limited right granted by the server, such as the right of one replica to order the writes to a chunk, or of a client to cache a file.
Fencing — making sure an old master that is no longer elected cannot make changes, so two masters cannot both act.
Data locality — running the computation on the machine (or rack) that stores the data, to avoid moving it over the network.
history: Sun Microsystems built NFS (Network File System) in 1984 so that workstations could mount a remote directory as if it were local. NFS version 2 (1989) used a stateless server: each request carried everything needed, so a server could crash and restart without clients noticing, and repeated requests were safe. It was built on ONC RPC and XDR, and it scaled to a department but not to a data centre: one server, with every byte passing through it. At Carnegie Mellon, the Andrew File System (AFS, 1980s) added whole-file caching on clients and "callbacks", where the server promises to tell a client if a cached file changes. That scaled better for many readers.
High-performance computing needed bandwidth. Lustre (first released about 2003) separated metadata servers from object storage servers and striped each file over many of them. Ceph (Sage Weil, 2006) removed the lookup table for data placement: the CRUSH algorithm computes where an object lives from its name and a map of the cluster, so clients and servers agree without asking a central server.
The change that mattered most for data engineering was Google's File System paper (Ghemawat, Gobioff and Leung, SOSP 2003). Google ran on thousands of cheap machines whose failures were routine, and its workloads were huge files, appended and read in long streams. GFS dropped POSIX rules that did not fit: files are append-mostly, chunks are 64 MB, there is a single master, and consistency is relaxed. Doug Cutting and Mike Cafarella copied the design into HDFS for Nutch and Hadoop (2006), and Yahoo ran it at scale. Google later replaced GFS with Colossus, which stores its metadata in a Bigtable-like system and so removes the single-master limit. Hadoop 3.0 (December 2017) added erasure coding to HDFS. In the 2010s cloud object stores (S3 and others) took over much of the role for analytics, and since December 2020 S3 gives strong read-after-write consistency.
how: Take HDFS as the main example, since it is the best known open copy of the GFS design.
Step 1: the namespace. The NameNode holds the whole directory tree and the map file to list of blocks in memory, and writes every change to an edit log on disk (and to a shared journal, see step 6). Periodically it merges the log into a checkpoint image. A file is a list of block IDs; a block is 128 MB by default. Keeping the metadata in memory makes lookups fast, and costs about 150 bytes for each file, directory and block object, so a NameNode with 100 million files and 100 million blocks needs about 30 GB of heap. This is the real scaling limit of the single-master design.
Step 2: the write path. A client asks the NameNode to create a file. The NameNode checks permissions and chooses three DataNodes for the first block, using rack awareness: the first replica on the writer's node (or a random one), the second on a different rack, the third on another node of the second rack. This survives the loss of one whole rack and keeps most traffic inside racks. The client opens a pipeline: it sends the data in packets of about 64 KB to the first DataNode, which forwards them to the second, which forwards them to the third. Acknowledgements flow back along the chain. When a block is full, the client asks for the next one. RPC (L3) carries the control messages; the bulk data uses a streaming protocol.
Step 3: the read path. The client asks the NameNode for the block locations of a file range. The NameNode returns, for each block, the DataNodes that hold it, sorted by network distance to the client. The client then reads directly from the nearest one and verifies checksums. The NameNode is not in the data path, so total read bandwidth grows with the number of DataNodes: 1,000 nodes with 10 disks at 150 MB/s give on the order of 1.5 TB/s in aggregate.
Step 4: failure and repair. Every DataNode sends a heartbeat every 3 seconds and a full list of its blocks now and then. A node that is silent for about 10.5 minutes is declared dead, and the NameNode finds the blocks that have fewer replicas than requested and orders surviving nodes to copy them. This is failure detection (L2) with a long timeout on purpose, to avoid a storm of copying after a short network glitch. A corrupt block, found by a checksum mismatch, is also replaced from another replica. The speed of repair matters, since a second failure before repair can lose a block: with random placement, many nodes take part in each repair, so a 10 TB node is rebuilt in minutes to hours.
Step 5: erasure coding. Three-way replication stores 3 bytes for 1 byte of data, which is expensive for cold data. An erasure code such as Reed-Solomon (6,3) splits a block group into 6 data cells and computes 3 parity cells; any 6 of the 9 rebuild the data. The overhead is 1.5x instead of 3x, and it survives any 3 losses instead of 2. This is the RAID idea (L2) moved from disks in a box to machines in a cluster. The cost is that rebuilding one lost cell reads 6 others, six times the network traffic of copying a replica, and small files cannot be coded efficiently.
Step 6: keeping the master alive. A single NameNode would be a single point of failure. In HDFS high availability, an active and a standby NameNode share an edit log through a quorum of JournalNodes (a majority must accept every write), so the standby is always nearly current. A failover controller using ZooKeeper elects the active one with a lease-like lock (leader election, L2), and fencing stops the old active from writing if it comes back, since two active masters would corrupt the namespace.

example: Store a 1 GB file in HDFS with 128 MB blocks and replication 3. The file has 8 blocks and so 24 replicas. The NameNode keeps 1 file object and 8 block objects, about 9 × 150 B = 1.4 KB. Compare 10 million small files of 1 MB each (10 TB in total). Each is one file and one block, so 20 million objects, about 3 GB of NameNode memory, and a job that opens each file does 10 million NameNode calls. The same 10 TB in 128 MB blocks is about 80,000 blocks, tens of megabytes of metadata. This is the small-files problem, and the reason lakehouse engines compact files to 128 MB to 1 GB.
Now the cost of durability for 1 PB of data. With 3x replication you buy 3 PB of raw disk. With RS(6,3) you buy 1.5 PB. At 20 TB per disk the saving is 75 disks, plus the servers, power and space around them. The price is reconstruction. If a node with 10 TB dies, replication repairs by copying 10 TB from other nodes. Erasure coding reads 6 × 10 = 60 TB across the network to rebuild the same 10 TB. With 1,000 nodes sharing the work, each reads about 60 GB, which takes under a minute of link time at 10 Gbit/s. In a small cluster, or with a busy network, the same repair can saturate the links and slow every job.
Next, the failure arithmetic. A cluster has 1,000 nodes, each with an annual failure probability of 5%, so about 50 node failures a year, roughly one a week. A node fails in a given hour with probability 0.05 / 8,760 ≈ 5.7 × 10⁻⁶. Suppose one replica of a block is lost. The block is lost for good only if both other replicas also fail before the repair ends. If repair takes 1 hour, that chance is about (5.7 × 10⁻⁶)² ≈ 3 × 10⁻¹¹ for each affected block. If repair takes 24 hours, each probability is 24 times larger and the product 576 times larger, about 2 × 10⁻⁸. With millions of blocks per failed node, that is no longer negligible, which is why repair speed and rack-aware placement matter as much as the number of copies.
intuition: A library with one catalogue desk and many shelf rooms: the desk knows which room holds each book, but you walk to the shelf yourself, and every important book has copies in different buildings.
The split into metadata and data is natural because the two have opposite needs. The directory tree is small, so it can sit in one machine's memory and be exact, which gives simple semantics and fast lookups. The bytes are huge, so they must never pass through that one machine. If clients carry the data traffic themselves, bandwidth grows with the number of data servers. Large blocks make the metadata small and the reads sequential, which is what spinning disks like. Copies in different racks match how hardware fails: disks, machines and racks, in that order of frequency. The remaining difficulty, a single master that can fail, is handled by the tools of the layers below: a log replicated by a quorum, an election, and fencing.
mistakes: People often think a distributed file system behaves like a local disk. In fact most relax something: GFS and HDFS allow only appends and no random writes in the middle of a file; NFS may show stale cached data for seconds; many systems have no atomic rename across directories. Software built on POSIX assumptions can break in subtle ways.
People often think replication alone means no data loss. In fact replicas protect against hardware failure, not against deleting the file, a bug that writes bad data (all copies are wrong), or an operator mistake. HDFS trash, snapshots and backups to another cluster handle those, and they are separate from replication.
People often think more replicas always mean faster reads. In fact extra copies help hot files spread load, but each copy costs disk and write bandwidth, as every write goes through all replicas. For cold data the better choice is fewer bytes, with erasure coding.
People often think object stores such as S3 are simply a distributed file system. In fact they have a flat key space with no real directories, whole-object writes, and (on S3) no atomic rename, so a "rename" is a copy and a delete. Engines that rely on atomic rename for commits had to be changed, for example with new commit protocols and table formats such as Delta Lake.
People often think the NameNode is a throughput bottleneck for reads. In fact clients read data directly from DataNodes. The NameNode limits the number of files (memory) and the rate of metadata operations, not the data bandwidth.
tradeoffs: Choose three-way replication for hot data, for small clusters, and for data written and rewritten often: reads are simple and fast, repair is a copy, and any replica can serve. Choose erasure coding (such as RS(6,3)) for cold or write-once data in a cluster of at least ten or so nodes, where halving storage from 3x to 1.5x matters more than the 6x repair traffic and the loss of locality. You give up speed of repair and cheap small writes.
Choose HDFS on local disks when jobs are scan-heavy, you want data locality and you control the hardware; choose an object store (S3, GCS, ADLS) with a separate compute tier when you want elastic compute, no cluster to operate and lower cost per terabyte, and can accept higher latency per request (tens of milliseconds) and weaker rename semantics. You give up locality and file-system semantics in the second case, and elasticity in the first.
Choose a large block size (128 to 256 MB) for big sequential scans, since it cuts metadata and seek overhead. Choose smaller blocks or an object store with range reads for many small or random reads. A large block also reduces parallelism for small files, since a 100 MB file in one 128 MB block runs as one task.
Choose a single active master with a hot standby when your file count is under about 500 million and operations are simple. Choose a federated or distributed-metadata design (HDFS federation, Colossus, CephFS with several MDS) when metadata outgrows one machine's memory, and accept the complexity of partitioned namespaces and cross-partition operations.
interview: Why does HDFS use 128 MB blocks, and what breaks if you use 4 KB blocks? || A hard disk seeks in about 10 ms and then streams at 100 to 200 MB/s, so a block must be big enough that the seek is a small share of the read; at 128 MB the seek is under 2%. With 4 KB blocks, the NameNode would hold 32,768 times more block objects, run out of memory, and every read would be dominated by seeks and RPCs.
A DataNode holding 10 TB dies. Describe what the system does, and what can go wrong. || After about 10.5 minutes without heartbeats the NameNode marks it dead and queues all its blocks for re-replication from the other replicas. Risks: a second failure on the same blocks before repair finishes, repair traffic saturating the network and slowing jobs (so repair is throttled), and the timeout being too short during a network partition, which would trigger needless copying.
How do you prevent two NameNodes from both acting as active? || Use a lock with a lease in a coordination service (ZooKeeper) for election, and fence the old active: cut it from the shared journal, kill its process or shared storage access. The JournalNodes accept writes only from the current epoch's writer, so a stale master cannot commit changes even if it still believes it leads.
Why might you choose erasure coding for archives and replication for hot tables? || Erasure coding gives 1.5x instead of 3x storage with the same or better fault tolerance, but each read of a lost cell needs many nodes, writes need parity computation, and small files code poorly. Hot data benefits from simple local reads and fast repair, and its volume is small, so the extra copies are cheap.
Your cluster has 400 million small files and the NameNode is out of memory. What are the options? || Reduce the object count: compact files into larger ones (Parquet or Delta compaction, SequenceFiles, HAR archives), change ingestion to write bigger batches, and delete old data. Longer term: federation to split the namespace, move data to an object store, or use a metadata layer such as a table format that tracks files outside the NameNode.
friction: The single metadata server is the main limit. Memory bounds the file count, a long garbage-collection pause on a big heap can look like a failure, and every metadata operation passes through one lock. Federation and Colossus-style designs raise the limit and bring distributed transactions for operations such as rename across partitions. Operating the system is also work: balancing disks, decommissioning nodes, upgrading with no downtime and watching for slow disks that damage the tail latency of jobs.
Consistency semantics are weaker than they seem. Appends from many writers can duplicate records (GFS record append is at-least-once), readers may see data that is later lost if a writer fails, and NFS-style caches can show old data. Applications must tolerate this or use higher layers, such as table formats with transaction logs.
Cost and flexibility have shifted. Local-disk clusters tie storage to compute: to store more you must buy CPU, and to compute more you may buy disk. This is why many teams moved analytics to object storage with separate compute, giving up data locality for elasticity. Network speeds of 25 to 100 Gbit/s made the loss of locality tolerable.
Erasure coding has its own problems: repair cost, small files, and loss of the ability to read a block from several replicas. Lastly, security and multi-tenancy are weak spots: file permissions, Kerberos tickets and quotas are retrofitted, and one tenant creating millions of tiny files can slow all others.
practice: Treat the file count as a first-class resource. Watch NameNode heap, objects and RPC queue time, set quotas per directory, and compact small files into blocks of 128 MB or more. Choose table layouts (partitions, file sizes of 128 MB to 1 GB) so each task reads a few big files instead of many small ones.
Place data deliberately. Use rack awareness with a correct topology script, keep replication 3 for hot data and use erasure coding policies for cold directories, and keep enough free space (a common level is 20% free) so repair has room to work. Throttle re-replication so repair does not starve jobs, and monitor under-replicated and corrupt block counts as alerts. Turn on HA with a quorum of three JournalNodes and test failover regularly, including fencing.
If your platform is on object storage, remember the differences: list operations are slow and eventually costly at scale, rename is not atomic, and each request has a latency and a price. Use a table format or a committer designed for object stores. Choose the system for the workload: HDFS or Ceph for on-premises high-throughput scans, object stores for elastic analytics, Lustre or a parallel file system for HPC and training jobs that need very high bandwidth, and NFS-like systems for shared home directories.
quiz: What are the two roles in a GFS/HDFS design, and why are they separate? || A metadata server holds the namespace and the block map in memory; data servers hold the blocks. They are separate because metadata is small and needs exactness, while data is huge and must flow directly between clients and many servers so bandwidth scales.
Why does HDFS place the second and third replicas on a different rack from the first? || A rack switch or power failure takes down every node in the rack, so replicas on other racks survive it. Putting the third replica on the same rack as the second saves cross-rack bandwidth on the write.
Compare the storage and repair cost of 3x replication with RS(6,3) for 1 PB of data. || Replication stores 3 PB, and repairing one lost block copies one block. RS(6,3) stores 1.5 PB, and repairing one lost cell reads six others, so repair traffic is six times larger.
A NameNode uses about 150 bytes per object. How much heap for 200 million objects? || 200 million × 150 B = 30 GB.
When would you choose an object store over HDFS for a Spark platform? || When you want elastic, separate compute and storage, lower cost per terabyte and no cluster to operate, and you can accept higher request latency, no data locality and non-atomic rename.

@@ wsc
value: A search query, a video recommendation or a bid decision is not served by one machine. It is served by hundreds of programs on thousands of machines that work together on the same request, inside tens of milliseconds. The data center that holds them is then no longer a room of separate servers. It behaves like one very large computer, and the people who build it design it as one: the network is its memory bus, the cluster scheduler is its operating system, and the program that runs on it is one distributed system, not a set of separate programs. Luiz André Barroso and Urs Hölzle of Google called this a warehouse-scale computer (WSC) in their 2009 book "The Datacenter as a Computer".
The difference from a normal data center is the owner. In a hosting data center, many customers run unrelated programs on rented machines, and each machine is made as reliable as possible. A WSC belongs to one organisation, runs a few large services on all of its machines, and chooses to buy cheap, standard hardware that fails often, because it can make the software tolerate failure. At this scale a failure is not an event but a constant condition: with 10,000 servers and a failure every three years per server, about nine machines fail every day. Cost is then dominated by buying the servers, powering and cooling them, and building the network that joins them, and a gain of a few percent saves millions of dollars.
For the layers above, cloud computing (L1) rents out pieces of a WSC, and AWS, GCP and Azure regions are made of such buildings. The layers below supply the physics and the techniques. Data-centre networks (L3) join the servers with a Clos topology so that any two servers have high bandwidth between them. Power and energy in chips (L3) explain why electricity and heat are the largest running cost and the main limit on how many machines fit in a building. Tail latency and hedged requests (L2) handle the effect that, with many servers in each request, the slowest one decides the answer time. Failure detection and heartbeats (L2) let software notice dead machines quickly and route around them.
terms: Warehouse-scale computer (WSC) — a very large cluster of servers in one building or campus, owned and operated as a single machine for a few large services.
Cluster scheduler — the software that places jobs on machines and restarts them after failures; Google's Borg and Kubernetes are examples.
PUE (power usage effectiveness) — total facility power divided by the power used by IT equipment; a PUE of 1.1 means 10% overhead for cooling and power conversion.
Clos / fat-tree network — a network built from many identical small switches in layers, so the capacity between any two servers is high and there are many equal paths.
Oversubscription — the ratio between the bandwidth servers could send and the bandwidth the network can carry between racks; 1:1 means none.
Rack — a frame holding about 20 to 40 servers with a shared top-of-rack switch and power feed; the basic unit of failure and of network layout.
Tail latency — the slow end of the latency distribution, such as the 99th percentile; fan-out makes it dominate user-visible time.
Energy proportionality — the property that a server's power falls in proportion to its load; real servers draw a large part of peak power even when idle.
Fault domain — a group of machines that fail together, such as a rack, a power distribution unit or a cooling zone.
history: Large web companies met the problem first. Google began in 1998 on cheap PCs in racks, chosen because their performance per dollar was far better than that of big servers, and it wrote software to survive their failures. The Google File System (2003), MapReduce (2004) and Bigtable (2006) were the first layers of what became a WSC operating system. In 2007 Barroso and Hölzle published "The Case for Energy-Proportional Computing", showing that servers spent most of their time at 10% to 50% load yet drew more than half of their peak power when idle.
The 2009 book, written as a Morgan and Claypool lecture, gave the field its name and a cost model; a second edition appeared in 2013 and a third, with Parthasarathy Ranganathan, in 2018. Jeff Dean's 2009 talks listed what happens in the first year of a new cluster: about 1,000 individual machine failures, thousands of disk failures, about 20 rack failures that remove 40 to 80 machines at once, and a power-distribution failure that removes hundreds of machines for hours. Dean and Barroso's "The Tail at Scale" (CACM, 2013) showed how fan-out turns rare slow responses into the common case, and described hedged and tied requests.
The network changed too. Al-Fares, Loukissas and Vahdat (SIGCOMM 2008) showed how to build a fat-tree from commodity switches, and Google's Jupiter fabric (SIGCOMM 2015) reached 1.3 petabits per second of bisection bandwidth; later work reports the fabric growing tenfold to about 13 petabits per second. Efficiency became a major goal: Google reports a fleet-wide PUE around 1.1, against industry survey averages of roughly 1.5 to 1.6. Since the mid-2010s, machine-learning accelerators (GPUs and TPUs) are deployed in the same kind of buildings, and AI training clusters now push power density and the network to new limits.
how: A warehouse-scale computer is built in layers, from the building to the application, and each layer is designed for failure and for cost.
Step 1: the building and its power. A WSC is a large hall or campus with a power feed of tens of megawatts. Power passes through transformers, backup batteries and generators to power distribution units, and then to rows of racks. Heat is removed by air or liquid cooling, with outside air, evaporative cooling or chilled water. The efficiency is measured by PUE, the total power divided by the IT power. Power and energy in chips (L3) set the limit: a CPU or accelerator turns nearly all electrical power into heat, so every watt used by a chip needs cooling capacity, and the chips' power per rack limits how many fit in the building.
Step 2: the servers. A node is a standard server, with 1 or 2 CPU sockets, DRAM and flash, bought in volume with a custom board design, and increasingly with accelerators such as GPUs or TPUs. Designers avoid expensive features, such as redundant power supplies in each box or hardware RAID, because the software already replicates data. The key figure is performance per total cost of ownership, which includes the purchase price spread over three to five years, power and cooling over the same period, and staff.
Step 3: the network. Servers in a rack connect to a top-of-rack switch. Switches then connect in layers: leaf (rack) switches, spine switches and, for a campus, super-spines. This is a Clos network (L3). With k-port switches a three-layer fat-tree connects k³/4 servers at full bisection bandwidth, and traffic between any two servers has many equal-cost paths, spread by hashing flows. Full bandwidth is expensive, so some designs accept a 2:1 or 4:1 oversubscription between racks, and schedulers place chatty jobs in the same rack. The network is the WSC's memory bus: the time to reach another server is a few tens of microseconds in the best case, a hundred times a local memory access.
Step 4: the software layer. A cluster scheduler (Borg at Google, Kubernetes elsewhere) treats the machines as a pool: users submit jobs with resource requests, and the scheduler places tasks, packs several jobs per machine to raise utilisation, restarts tasks that die and moves them off failing machines. Storage systems (distributed file systems such as GFS and Colossus, and databases such as Bigtable and Spanner) replicate data across racks. Common libraries provide RPC, load balancing, retries and tracing. Together they play the role an operating system plays on one machine.
Step 5: failure as the normal case. Failure detection (L2) uses heartbeats and health checks, with timeouts set to seconds, and when a node is declared dead, the scheduler restarts its tasks elsewhere and the storage layer re-replicates its data. Fault domains are known to the software: replicas are placed on different racks and, for important data, different power zones or buildings. Changes are rolled out gradually, because a bad software push is the most common cause of a large outage.
Step 6: the tail. A request that fans out to 100 leaf servers waits for the slowest. If each server is slow (over 1 second) in 1% of cases, then the chance that at least one of 100 is slow is 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63%. So the system uses hedged requests (send a second copy after a short delay and take the first reply), tied requests that cancel the other copy, small timeouts and backup tasks, together with the removal of the causes of variance, such as background jobs, garbage-collection pauses and queuing on shared resources.

example: Count the failures first. A WSC has 10,000 servers, each with a mean time between failures of 3 years (about 1,095 days). Failures per day are 10,000 / 1,095 ≈ 9. A distributed file system that stores each block three times across racks must repair the data of nine machines every day. If each holds 10 TB, that is 90 TB of copying a day, about 1 GB/s of background traffic. Software that needed a human for each failure would need a person on shift all the time; so everything from detection to restart is automatic.
Now the tail. A search request fans out to 100 shards. Each shard answers within 10 ms in 99% of cases and takes 1 second in 1% of cases because of a disk hiccup or a competing job. The probability that a request is fast is 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 37%, so 63% of requests take at least a second. A hedged request helps. The front end sends the query to a replica of each shard, and if no answer arrives after 10 ms, it sends a second copy to another replica. The chance that both copies of one shard are slow is 1% × 1% = 0.01%, so over 100 shards the chance of a slow request falls to about 1%, at the cost of sending a second copy for about 1% of the shard requests. Google reported a similar effect on a Bigtable benchmark of 1,000 key reads: the 99.9th percentile fell from 1,800 ms to 74 ms for about 2% more requests.
Last, the power bill. A building has 30 MW of IT load. At PUE 1.1 the total is 33 MW. At PUE 1.6 it would be 48 MW. The difference is 15 MW, which over a year is 15 × 8,760 = 131,400 MWh. At about $60 per MWh that is roughly $7.9 million a year for each such building, and a gain of just 0.05 in PUE saves 1.5 MW at 30 MW of IT load, which is 1.5 × 8,760 × $60 ≈ $0.8 million a year. Even this rough sum shows why designers spend effort on cooling.
intuition: A ship with a thousand engines: the captain cannot keep every engine perfect, so the ship is built to keep sailing when some of them stop.
The design is natural once you count. Perfect machines are expensive and still fail, and a program that needs all of 10,000 machines healthy at once would never run. Cheap machines plus software that tolerates failure give more computing per dollar, and the saved money buys more machines. But a computer of that size has its own physics: the network between machines is slow compared with memory, power and heat set the building's size, and fan-out makes rare slowness common. The architecture of a WSC is the sum of answers to these three limits: replicate everything across fault domains, design the network and placement for bandwidth, and use hedging and spare capacity against the tail.
mistakes: People often think a warehouse-scale computer is just a very big data centre. In fact the difference is in the design goal: a hosting data centre sells machine time to many customers and promises each machine's reliability; a WSC runs one organisation's software on all machines and gets reliability from software, with hardware chosen for cost per work done.
People often think cheaper, less reliable hardware means a less reliable service. In fact the service's reliability comes from replication and fast recovery across many machines. A machine that fails twice as often costs little extra in a system built to lose machines every day, while a premium machine costs more on every unit.
People often think the biggest cost is the servers' electricity bill. In fact in most cost models the purchase of the servers (spread over their life) is still the largest part, with power, cooling and the building next, and the network a smaller but critical share. As servers get cheaper per unit of work and power per rack rises, the power-related share grows, and for AI clusters it is a first-order limit.
People often think a 99.9% machine gives a 99.9% service. In fact fan-out multiplies the probabilities: a request needing 100 servers that are each fast 99% of the time is fast only 37% of the time. Tail effects, not average speed, set the user's experience.
tradeoffs: Choose a fully non-blocking network (1:1) when workloads move large volumes between arbitrary servers, as in distributed training, shuffles and search; choose oversubscription of 2:1 to 4:1 when most traffic stays in the rack and the saved switches and fibre pay for themselves. You give up predictable bandwidth for placement-sensitive jobs, and you must teach the scheduler about racks.
Choose commodity servers and software fault tolerance when you run many copies of large, replicable services; choose expensive, highly reliable hardware for the few components that cannot be replicated easily, such as a single-master metadata server or a database primary. The first has a lower unit cost and demands more software engineering, and the second costs more and makes the failure rarer but not impossible.
Choose to run hot (high utilisation, 60% or more, by packing many job types on a machine) when the cost of idle capacity is high and the jobs tolerate interference; choose spare capacity (30% to 50% idle) for latency-critical services where the tail matters. High utilisation saves money and increases tail latency and the chance of a sudden overload.
Choose air cooling for rack power up to about 20 to 30 kW, and liquid cooling for denser racks, such as those with GPUs at 40 kW or more. Liquid cooling raises the cost of the plumbing and the complexity of maintenance, and removes more heat with less energy.
Choose your own WSC only when you need more than a few tens of thousands of machines and can staff it; below that, rent from a cloud, which pools the capital cost and gives you the benefits of a WSC for an hourly price.
interview: Why do WSCs use commodity hardware instead of reliable servers? || Reliability bought in hardware costs more per unit and still does not remove failures, while software replication and automatic restart do. If a server costs half as much and fails twice as often, a fleet that already loses machines daily loses little, and the saved money buys more capacity. The design needs fast failure detection and data spread across racks.
A request fans out to 200 backends, each with a p99 of 50 ms. What is the p50 of the request, roughly, and what do you do about it? || The request waits for the slowest of 200, so the chance of at least one exceeding p99 is 1 − 0.99²⁰⁰ ≈ 87%. Even the median request sees roughly a p99.7 backend. Reduce variance (isolate noisy work, avoid queues), use hedged requests after the p95 delay, and cut fan-out with caching or hierarchical aggregation.
What does PUE measure, and what is its limit as a design goal? || Total facility power divided by IT power, so it measures overhead. A PUE of 1.1 cannot go below 1.0, so the remaining gains come from using the IT power better: higher utilisation, energy-proportional servers and removing idle machines. PUE also ignores water use and the carbon of the electricity.
How would you design the fault domains for a service that must survive a rack and a power-zone failure? || Spread replicas of each shard across at least three racks in different power zones, so any single domain holds at most one replica, or fewer than a quorum. The scheduler needs this domain information, spread constraints and a limit on how many tasks of a job can be taken down at once during maintenance.
Why is the network called the computer's bus? What breaks when it is oversubscribed? || Tasks on different machines exchange data through the network at tens of microseconds, so its bandwidth and latency set the speed of shuffles, replication and RPC. If oversubscribed, cross-rack traffic queues at the spine, and tail latency and job time rise whenever many racks send at once, such as in a big shuffle or after a failure triggers re-replication.
friction: The largest limit is power. Chips and racks are getting denser, a campus may need hundreds of megawatts, and grid connections, transformers and cooling can take years to build. AI accelerators raise rack power from 10 to 20 kW to 100 kW and more, which forces liquid cooling and new building designs. Sites are chosen by where the electricity and water are available.
Utilisation is the second problem. Studies of large clusters show average CPU use of 20% to 40%, because services are sized for peak and different jobs interfere with each other. Packing more work onto each machine raises utilisation and the risk of interference and tail latency. Energy proportionality is still incomplete: an idle server draws a significant share of peak power, so low utilisation wastes power as well as capital.
Failures are correlated. Cheap, independent failures are handled well, but shared causes are not: a bad software release, a configuration push to every region, a power distribution failure, or a network device bug can remove thousands of machines at once and expose that replicas were less independent than assumed. Large outages usually come from changes and from hidden dependencies, such as a service that every other service needs to start.
Finally, the scale itself is a barrier. Only a handful of organisations can build a WSC, and others depend on them through the cloud, with the cost of egress, lock-in and shared risk. Hardware is also changing quickly: accelerators, disaggregated memory and new interconnects change the balance of the design, so the "right" shape of the machine is a moving target.
practice: As a user of a WSC (your own or a cloud), design for its properties. Assume any machine, rack or zone can disappear, spread replicas across fault domains, make jobs restartable and idempotent, and test by killing tasks. Use the network knowingly: keep chatty traffic inside a zone, count cross-zone and cross-region bytes (they cost money and latency), and place data and compute together.
Control the tail. Measure p99 and p99.9, not only averages, trace slow requests to find the cause (a noisy neighbour, a garbage-collection pause, a hot partition), use timeouts with retries and a retry budget, and add hedged requests where the extra load is a small percentage. Keep headroom on latency-critical services rather than running them at full load.
If you plan capacity or cost, think in total cost of ownership: price per unit of work including power over the life of the hardware, not the purchase price. Choose regions partly for power cost and carbon intensity when the workload can move. If you read about the field, start with the Barroso, Hölzle and Ranganathan book, "The Tail at Scale", and the Borg and Jupiter papers.

@@ embedded
value: Most processors are not in servers or phones. They are in car engines, washing machines, insulin pumps, drones, routers, keyboards and the sensors in a factory. Billions of microcontrollers ship every year, and each one runs a single program that controls one physical thing. These programs have very small resources: a Cortex-M4 chip may have 1 MB of flash for code, 192 KB of RAM and a clock of 168 MHz, and a small one has 32 KB of flash and 4 KB of RAM. There is often no operating system, no virtual memory and no file system.
The special demand is **time**. A program that controls a motor, a brake or a spark plug must react to an input within a fixed limit, every time. A delay is not slow service but a failure: an engine misfires, a drone tips over. This is a real-time system, and the key property is predictability, the guarantee of the worst case, not the average speed. Embedded engineering is about how to write software that reacts to hardware events in bounded time, uses memory that is fixed at build time, runs for years without being restarted and sips power from a battery.
For the layers above, Arm Cortex-M chips dominate embedded systems, and RISC-V's first large market is microcontrollers and embedded cores. The layers below give the methods. Real-time scheduling (L3) decides which task runs next so that deadlines are met, and gives tests to prove it. Interrupts (L3) let hardware events such as a timer tick or an incoming byte stop the running code at once. Hardware clocks and timers (L3) create precise periodic events and measure time. The instruction set (L3) is what the code runs on directly, often compiled with no operating system between the program and the chip.
terms: Microcontroller (MCU) — one chip with a processor, flash memory, RAM and peripherals such as timers, ADCs and serial ports, made to control a device.
Firmware — the program stored in the MCU's flash memory that runs when the chip is powered.
Hard and soft real-time — in a hard real-time system a missed deadline is a failure; in a soft one it reduces quality, as when a video frame is late.
Interrupt service routine (ISR) — the function the hardware calls when an event occurs; it should be short.
RTOS (real-time operating system) — a small kernel that runs tasks by priority with bounded delays, such as FreeRTOS or Zephyr.
Worst-case execution time (WCET) — the longest time a piece of code can take; deadline analysis needs it, not the average.
Watchdog timer — a hardware timer that resets the chip if the software does not "kick" it in time, which recovers from a hang.
Memory-mapped I/O — control registers of peripherals placed at fixed addresses, so that reading and writing them controls hardware.
Jitter — the variation in the time at which a periodic action really happens.
history: The first microprocessor, the Intel 4004 (1971), was designed for a calculator, so embedded use is as old as the microprocessor. The Apollo Guidance Computer (1966) is an early famous embedded real-time system: on the Apollo 11 landing in 1969 it ran out of capacity because of extra radar data and shed its low-priority tasks, which is priority scheduling in practice. Single-chip microcontrollers followed in the 1970s (TI's TMS1000, Intel's 8048 and the 8051 of 1980), and the 8051 family is still produced.
The theory arrived in 1973, when Liu and Layland proved results for periodic tasks: rate-monotonic scheduling (a fixed priority, with the shortest period highest) is optimal among fixed-priority schemes, and earliest-deadline-first (EDF) can use the CPU fully. In the 1980s and 1990s, commercial real-time kernels (VxWorks, QNX) went into telecom, aerospace and cars. The Mars Pathfinder lander in 1997 reset itself on Mars because of priority inversion, and was fixed from Earth by enabling priority inheritance. It made the field remember that scheduling theory has to match implementation.
Arm introduced the Cortex-M family in 2004 (Cortex-M3) with a built-in interrupt controller (the NVIC) and a uniform programming model, so one toolchain ran on chips from many vendors. FreeRTOS, started by Richard Barry in 2003, became the most used open-source RTOS, and Amazon took over its stewardship in 2017. Zephyr, a Linux Foundation project from 2016, offers a larger and more modern RTOS. Safety standards such as IEC 61508 (1998), the MISRA C coding rules, ISO 26262 for cars and DO-178C for aircraft shaped the process. RISC-V open cores and Rust for embedded are the recent changes.
how: An embedded program is a loop that reacts to the hardware within a time budget.
Step 1: boot and memory. At reset the processor reads a table at the start of flash: the first word is the initial stack pointer and the second the address of the reset handler. The startup code copies initialised data from flash to RAM, clears the uninitialised data, sets up the clock and calls `main`. The linker places code in flash, data in RAM and peripherals' registers at fixed addresses chosen by the chip. There is no virtual memory: addresses are physical, which is what makes timing predictable, since there are no page faults and no translation misses. There is usually no heap, or a very careful one: memory is allocated statically at build time, because a heap can fragment and fail after months of running.
Step 2: talk to hardware. Peripherals are memory-mapped: writing a value to an address starts a timer, reads a pin or sends a byte. Code declares such registers `volatile`, so the compiler does not remove or reorder the accesses. The instruction set (L3) matters here: a small, regular set such as Thumb-2 gives dense code that fits in flash, and the programmer or compiler can count cycles. DMA controllers move data between memory and peripherals with no CPU involvement.
Step 3: react with interrupts. A peripheral raises an interrupt line when something happens: a timer expires, an ADC conversion ends, a byte arrives. The CPU finishes the current instruction, saves a few registers on the stack in hardware, and jumps to the handler address found in the vector table. On a Cortex-M3 or M4 this takes 12 cycles, about 70 ns at 168 MHz, and the hardware can chain from one pending interrupt to the next in 6 cycles. Priorities in the interrupt controller let an urgent interrupt preempt a less urgent handler. A handler must be short and must not block: it reads the data, clears the flag, and sets a flag or queue entry for a task to process later. This split keeps the time with interrupts disabled small and bounded.
Step 4: keep time. Hardware timers are driven by a crystal and count cycles. A periodic timer interrupt, the system tick (often 1 kHz), runs the scheduler and the software timers. Other timers generate PWM signals to drive motors, capture the time of an external edge to measure speed, and generate precise delays. A real-time clock keeps calendar time at very low power.
Step 5: schedule. With a few simple tasks, a "super-loop" plus interrupts is enough. With more, an RTOS provides tasks with fixed priorities. At every tick or event, the scheduler runs the highest-priority ready task, and the cost of a context switch is a few microseconds or less. Designers use the theory of real-time scheduling (L3): give shorter-period tasks higher priority (rate-monotonic), compute each task's worst-case response time from its execution time and the interference of higher-priority tasks, and check that it is below its deadline. They also bound blocking from shared resources, using priority inheritance or ceiling protocols so that a low-priority task holding a lock cannot be starved by a medium-priority one.
Step 6: stay alive and save power. A watchdog resets a hung chip. Brown-out detectors reset when the voltage dips. For battery devices, the CPU sleeps most of the time: a timer or an external event wakes it, it does a short burst of work, and it sleeps again. Average current is dominated by how long the chip is awake. Updates over the air need a bootloader, signed images and a fallback slot, so that a failed update does not brick the device.

example: Three periodic tasks run under rate-monotonic priorities. Task A has execution time C = 1 ms and period T = 5 ms (deadline equals period), B has C = 2 ms and T = 10 ms, and C has C = 6 ms and T = 30 ms. The CPU use is 1/5 + 2/10 + 6/30 = 0.2 + 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.6. The Liu and Layland bound for 3 tasks is 3 × (2^(1/3) − 1) ≈ 0.78, so 0.6 is safely below it, and the set is schedulable.
Check the lowest-priority task C by response time. Its response time R is its own execution time plus the work of the higher-priority tasks that arrive during R: R = 6 + ceil(R / 5) × 1 + ceil(R / 10) × 2. Start with R = 6: ceil(6 / 5) = 2 and ceil(6 / 10) = 1, so R = 6 + 2 + 2 = 10. Try R = 10: ceil(10 / 5) = 2 and ceil(10 / 10) = 1, so R = 10 again. It converges at 10 ms, well below the 30 ms deadline.
The bound is sufficient, not necessary. Take two tasks, A with C = 2 and T = 4, and B with C = 3 and T = 8. The use is 0.5 + 0.375 = 0.875, above the two-task bound of 0.83. But the response time of B: R = 3 + ceil(R / 4) × 2. Start at 3: ceil(0.75) = 1 gives 5; at 5: ceil(1.25) = 2 gives 7; at 7: ceil(1.75) = 2 gives 7. B finishes at 7 ms, inside its 8 ms deadline. So the exact test accepts a set that the simple bound rejects.
Now a battery budget. A sensor node uses 5 mA for 20 ms every second to measure and send, and sleeps at 2 µA. The average current is 5 mA × 0.02 + 0.002 mA × 0.98 ≈ 0.100 + 0.002 = 0.102 mA. A coin cell of 220 mAh then lasts about 220 / 0.102 ≈ 2,150 hours, roughly 90 days. If a bug keeps the chip awake and drawing 5 mA all the time, the same cell lasts 44 hours. Most of a battery life is decided by how short the awake time is.
A timing budget as code: a 1 kHz control loop on a 168 MHz core has 168,000 cycles per period.
```c
void TIM2_IRQHandler(void) {          /* fires every 1 ms */
    TIM2->SR = 0;                     /* clear the interrupt flag */
    int16_t err = target - read_adc();
    pwm_set(pid_step(err));           /* about 2,000 cycles of work */
}
```
The handler uses about 2,000 of 168,000 cycles, 1.2% of the CPU, so the other 98.8% is left for tasks and sleep.
intuition: A fire station: the crew does routine work while waiting, but when the bell rings they must be on the truck in a fixed time, and the plan is checked against the slowest crew, not the average.
Embedded software is natural once the world is treated as a clock that does not wait. A physical process moves at its own speed, so the computer has to answer within a time that the physics dictates. That is why the design chooses predictability over speed: no virtual memory, no heap that fragments, a small kernel with bounded delays, and interrupts that put the hardware first. The theory of scheduling turns "it seems fast enough" into a calculation, and the hardware timers and interrupt controller give the means to meet the plan. A system that is 10% slower but always meets its deadline is better than one that is faster on average.
mistakes: People often think real-time means fast. In fact real-time means predictable: a bounded response time, guaranteed in the worst case. A slow 8-bit chip can be hard real-time if it meets its deadlines, and a fast desktop processor cannot be, since caches, page faults and the scheduler give no worst-case bound.
People often think an RTOS makes a system real-time. In fact the RTOS only provides tools, such as priorities and bounded kernel calls. The system meets its deadlines only if the tasks' execution times, the priorities and the resource sharing are analysed. Pathfinder ran a real-time OS and still failed because of priority inversion.
People often think interrupt handlers can do the real work. In fact a long handler delays all other interrupts of equal or lower priority and breaks the worst-case analysis. Handlers should capture the data and signal a task. They also must not call blocking functions or non-reentrant library code such as `printf` or `malloc`.
People often think "it worked in testing" shows that it is correct. In fact timing bugs depend on rare interleavings of interrupts and tasks, and the test runs may never reach the worst case. Races on shared variables, such as a 32-bit value updated by an ISR and read in two steps, appear once in millions of cycles. Static analysis, WCET measurement under stress and review are needed.
tradeoffs: Choose a bare-metal super-loop with interrupts when the device has a few functions, 16 KB of RAM or less, and you want the smallest code and the simplest timing. Choose an RTOS (FreeRTOS, Zephyr) when you have many concurrent activities, network or file stacks, or several developers. The RTOS costs about 5 to 10 KB of flash, some RAM for stacks (every task needs its own, often 512 bytes to 2 KB) and context-switch time, and gives clearer structure and priorities.
Choose fixed-priority rate-monotonic scheduling for most systems: it is simple, analysable and supported by every RTOS. Choose EDF when you need CPU use above the rate-monotonic bound, up to 100%, and accept that it is less supported and that overload hurts several tasks at once instead of the lowest-priority one.
Choose polling when events are frequent and the loop is fast, so the overhead of an interrupt is not worth it. Choose interrupts when events are rare or urgent, or when the CPU should sleep between them. Polling is simpler to analyse and wastes power; interrupts save power and add concurrency bugs.
Choose static memory allocation for safety-critical systems, where a failed allocation after a year of running is not acceptable; choose a heap only in non-critical parts, with a pool allocator. You give up flexibility, because every buffer has a fixed maximum size.
Choose a language by risk: C with MISRA rules is the common choice and has the best tool and vendor support; C++ gives better abstractions without a runtime cost if used carefully; Rust gives memory safety with a smaller ecosystem of drivers and certification tools.
interview: What is priority inversion, and how was it fixed on Mars Pathfinder? || A high-priority task waits for a mutex held by a low-priority task, and a medium-priority task preempts the low one, so the high task is delayed by unrelated work. On Pathfinder it caused a watchdog reset. Priority inheritance raises the lock holder to the waiter's priority, and the fix was to enable this option in the OS from Earth.
How would you show that a set of periodic tasks meets its deadlines? || Assign rate-monotonic priorities, measure or bound each task's WCET, and compute the worst-case response time of each from its own time, the interference of higher-priority tasks and blocking by shared resources. Check each is below its deadline. The utilisation bound of n(2^(1/n) − 1) is a quick sufficient test; response-time analysis is exact for fixed priorities.
Why must an ISR be short, and what would you do if the work takes 2 ms? || A long ISR blocks lower-priority interrupts and adds jitter to everything else. The ISR should read the data, clear the interrupt and post to a queue or semaphore, and a task at a suitable priority does the 2 ms of work.
A device must run 5 years on a 1,000 mAh battery. How do you set the budget? || 5 years is about 43,800 hours, so the average current must be below 1,000 / 43,800 ≈ 0.023 mA, or 23 µA. With a sleep current of 2 µA and an active current of 10 mA, the active duty cycle can be about 0.2%, so the awake time must be tiny, and radio use dominates.
The firmware hangs once a month in the field. How do you find it? || Add a watchdog and record the reset cause and the last task or program counter before it, add stack-usage checks, and log timing and queue depths to a ring buffer in non-volatile memory. Suspect stack overflow, a race between an ISR and a task, a deadlock on a lock, and an unhandled peripheral error. Reproduce with stress tests and fault injection.
friction: Resources are tight and debugging is hard. There may be no screen, no console and no operating-system tools; a debugger stops the processor and so changes timing, which hides the very races you look for. Tools such as hardware trace and logic analysers help, and cost money. Hardware bugs, silicon errata and noisy sensors mix with software bugs, and a problem may depend on temperature or the battery voltage.
Timing analysis is more difficult on modern chips. Caches, branch predictors, pipelines and bus contention make the worst-case time of code far above its typical time, and Cortex-M7 and higher chips and multicore parts weaken the predictability that simple MCUs have. Many teams use scratchpad memory, disable caches on critical paths, or use measurement-based margins instead of a proof.
Software cannot easily be fixed after shipping. A bug in a car or a medical pump needs a field update with security checks and regulatory approval, and a device may be unreachable. This is why safety standards require traceability from requirements to tests, coding rules and independent review, which can make the process slower than the code. Security is another burden: small devices with network access, weak update paths and long life are attractive targets.
Finally, the growth of features stretches the old methods. Devices now run networking, cryptography, machine-learning models and graphical interfaces on chips designed for control loops, which pushes teams towards larger processors with embedded Linux and loses the hard real-time guarantees, or to split designs, with a real-time MCU beside an application processor.
practice: Start from the timing requirements. List each activity with its period, deadline and worst-case execution time, and compute the CPU use and response times before you write code. Reserve at least 30% to 40% of the CPU as margin. Keep interrupt handlers to a few dozen lines, and move work to tasks.
Write defensively. Allocate memory statically, size each stack with measurement (fill with a pattern and check the high-water mark), and use a watchdog that is fed only when all the important tasks have made progress. Mark shared variables `volatile` and protect multi-word data with a critical section or use atomic operations and queues. Avoid blocking calls inside handlers. Follow a coding standard such as MISRA C, and run a static analyser and compiler warnings as errors.
Test on the real hardware, in the real timing. Measure the interrupt latency and the task execution times with a GPIO pin and a logic analyser or the chip's cycle counter. Stress the system with the worst combination of events. Plan for power, using sleep modes and clock gating, and measure current with a proper instrument, since the average depends on rare wake-ups. Design the update path first: a bootloader, signed images, two slots and a rollback. To learn, use a Cortex-M development board with FreeRTOS or Zephyr and build a small control loop of your own.
quiz: What does real-time mean, and how does it differ from fast? || A real-time system guarantees a response within a fixed deadline in the worst case. Fast systems have good average speed; a real-time system needs a bounded worst case, which caches, paging and unbounded queues can destroy.

When would you choose an RTOS over a bare-metal loop? || When there are many concurrent activities with different rates and priorities, or a network or file stack and several developers. Accept the flash and RAM cost for clear structure and analysable priorities; use bare-metal for small devices with few functions.

@@ robotics
value: A robot is a computer that must act on the physical world and live with the result. Software that classifies images can be wrong and try again; a car that misjudges a pedestrian's speed, or an arm that applies the wrong torque, cannot undo it. The world is also uncertain: sensors are noisy and slow, wheels slip, objects move, and the robot's model of itself is never exact. So a robot cannot just compute an answer. It has to run a loop, many times a second, that senses, estimates what is true, decides what to do and acts, and corrects its estimate from the effect of the last action.
Robotics and autonomous systems is the engineering of that loop. A typical machine, whether a warehouse robot, a drone, a self-driving car or a humanoid, has four main stages. **Sense**: cameras, lidar, radar, inertial sensors (IMUs) and wheel or joint encoders produce raw data at different rates. **Estimate**: a state estimator fuses them into one belief about where the robot is and what is around it. **Plan**: a planner chooses a goal and a collision-free motion to reach it. **Act**: feedback controllers turn the planned motion into motor commands, a thousand times a second, and correct errors as they appear.
For the layer above, embodied AI agents act in the physical world through exactly this perception, planning and control stack, with a learned model often replacing one or more stages. The layers below supply each piece. State estimation (L2) gives the Kalman and particle filters that turn noisy readings into a position. PID and feedback control (L2) drive the motors. Motion planning (L2) finds paths that avoid obstacles. Rigid-body dynamics (L5) is the physics of arms and vehicles that the controllers must respect. Computer vision (L1) turns pixels into objects and depth. Automated planning (L2) sequences the tasks. Physics simulation (L2) lets teams test and train in MuJoCo or Isaac Sim before touching hardware.
terms: State — the quantities that describe the robot and its world at an instant: pose (position and orientation), velocity, and often the positions of nearby objects.
Pose — position plus orientation of a body; in 2D it is (x, y, heading), in 3D it has six numbers.
Odometry — estimating motion by adding up wheel, joint or visual movement; it drifts over time.
SLAM — simultaneous localisation and mapping: building a map of an unknown place while estimating the robot's position in it.
Localisation — estimating the robot's pose in a known map, with a Kalman or particle filter.
Configuration space — the space of all poses (or joint angles) of the robot; planning searches this space for a collision-free path.
Trajectory — a path with timing: positions and velocities at each moment, which a controller can follow.
Actuator — a device that moves something: an electric motor, a hydraulic cylinder, a brake.
Sim-to-real gap — the difference between how a simulated robot behaves and how the real one does, which can make a policy trained in simulation fail on hardware.
ROS 2 — the Robot Operating System 2, a set of libraries and a message-passing middleware, based on DDS, used to connect robot components.
history: The first industrial robot, the Unimate of George Devol and Joseph Engelberger, was installed in a General Motors die-casting plant in New Jersey in 1961. It repeated a stored sequence of joint positions and had no sensing at all. The first robot that reasoned was Shakey, built at SRI from 1966 to 1972. It used a camera and a range finder, a planner (STRIPS) to choose actions, and the A* search algorithm (1968) to find paths, and it showed both the promise and the slowness of the "sense, plan, act" approach: it was very slow to decide each move.
Rodney Brooks argued in the mid-1980s for reactive control without a central world model (the subsumption architecture), and the field settled on layers: fast reactive control at the bottom, deliberate planning above. Kalman's filter (1960), first used in the Apollo navigation computer, became the core of estimation. Probabilistic robotics grew in the 1990s: Monte Carlo localisation with particle filters (Dellaert, Fox, Burgard and Thrun, 1999), SLAM, and sampling-based planning with probabilistic roadmaps (Kavraki and others, 1996) and RRT (LaValle, 1998). Sebastian Thrun, Wolfram Burgard and Dieter Fox collected these in the textbook "Probabilistic Robotics" (2005).
Software and applications followed. The DARPA Grand Challenge of 2005 was won by Stanford's Stanley after the first attempts in 2004 had failed to finish, and the 2007 Urban Challenge showed driving in traffic. Google began its self-driving project in 2009, which became Waymo; Waymo and others now run paid driverless services in several cities. ROS began at Willow Garage in 2007 and gave robotics a shared set of tools. ROS 2, built on the DDS data-distribution standard to provide real-time and multi-robot support, released its first version in December 2017; the release of May 2026 is Lyrical Luth. Since the 2020s, learned policies trained from demonstrations or in simulation (diffusion policies, vision-language-action models) have started to replace hand-built parts of the stack.
how: The core is a pipeline of four stages running at different rates, with feedback at every level.
Step 1: sense. A mobile robot carries several sensors with different strengths. An IMU gives acceleration and rotation rate at 200 to 1,000 Hz, accurate in the short term and drifting over seconds. Wheel or joint encoders give motion at 100 Hz and more, but wheels slip. Cameras give rich images at 30 to 60 Hz. Lidar measures distance by laser at 10 to 20 revolutions per second with hundreds of thousands of points per second. GPS gives position at 1 to 10 Hz with an error of metres, or centimetres with RTK correction. No sensor is complete, so the robot combines them. Computer vision (L1) turns images into objects, lanes and depth with neural networks, and visual or lidar SLAM matches scans against a map.
Step 2: estimate. The state estimator (L2) keeps a belief about the state as a probability. A Kalman filter represents it as a mean and a covariance and alternates two steps. Predict: use the motion model to move the estimate forward, which makes it more uncertain.
\[ \hat{x}^- = F\hat{x} + Bu, \qquad P^- = FPF^\top + Q \]
Update: when a measurement \( z \) arrives, compare it with the prediction and correct the estimate by a weight \( K \), the Kalman gain, which is large when the measurement is more certain than the prediction.
\[ K = P^-H^\top (HP^-H^\top + R)^{-1}, \quad \hat{x} = \hat{x}^- + K(z - H\hat{x}^-) \]
Nonlinear problems use the extended Kalman filter, and problems with several possible positions (a robot lost in a symmetric corridor) use a particle filter, which keeps thousands of sampled hypotheses and weights them by how well they match the sensors.
Step 3: plan. Planning has levels. A task planner (automated planning, L2, or a behaviour tree) decides the sequence: go to shelf 12, pick the box, go to the dock. A route planner searches a map graph with A* for a global path. A local planner or motion planner (L2) searches the configuration space for a collision-free trajectory that respects the robot's limits. For a robot arm with six joints, this space has six dimensions and is too large to search on a grid, so sampling methods (RRT, PRM) build a tree or graph of random collision-free poses, and optimisation methods refine the path. For a car, the planner predicts other road users and picks a trajectory that is safe, comfortable and legal, re-planning 10 times a second.
Step 4: act. A controller turns the desired trajectory into motor commands. The workhorse is the PID controller (L2): command = Kp × error + Ki × integral of error + Kd × rate of error, run at 1 kHz on a microcontroller close to the motor. For arms and legs, the controller also uses the dynamics of rigid bodies (L5), written as \( \tau = M(q)\ddot{q} + C(q,\dot{q})\dot{q} + g(q) \): the torque needed depends on the mass matrix, the velocity-dependent forces and gravity. Computed-torque and model-predictive control use this model to predict and cancel the forces, which a plain PID cannot do well for fast, heavy or multi-joint motion.
Step 5: connect and supervise. The stages run as separate processes, often on separate computers. ROS 2 supplies publish-subscribe topics, services and actions over DDS, with quality-of-service settings for latency and reliability, plus tools for recording and replaying data. A safety layer is independent of the planner: it watches speed, distance and force limits and stops the motors if a limit is crossed, a watchdog notices a stalled process, and an emergency stop cuts power through hardware.
Step 6: test in simulation. Physics simulators (L2) model the dynamics and the contacts, and render sensor data. Teams run thousands of scenarios and train learned policies there, using randomised friction, lighting and mass (domain randomisation) to narrow the sim-to-real gap, and then validate on hardware and in the field.

example: One-dimensional fusion. A robot on a rail estimates its position. The current estimate is x = 10.0 m with variance P = 4 m² (standard deviation 2 m). A GPS-like sensor reports z = 12.0 m with variance R = 9 m² (standard deviation 3 m). The Kalman gain is K = P / (P + R) = 4 / 13 ≈ 0.31. The new estimate is 10.0 + 0.31 × (12.0 − 10.0) = 10.62 m. The new variance is (1 − K) × P = 0.69 × 4 ≈ 2.77 m², so the fused estimate (standard deviation 1.66 m) is better than either source. The filter trusted the more certain source more. If the robot then moves with velocity noise Q = 1 m² for one step, the variance grows to 3.77 before the next measurement.
Now a latency budget for a car. At 20 m/s (72 km/h), perception, planning and control together take 100 ms from the moment of sensing. In 100 ms the car travels 2 m, so any object seen is already 2 m behind in reality, and a planner must predict forward. The stopping distance adds a reaction time and a braking distance: with 0.5 s of reaction, the car moves 10 m; braking at 6 m/s² takes v² / (2a) = 400 / 12 ≈ 33 m. The total is about 43 m, and every extra 100 ms of latency adds 2 m. This is why perception and control run on dedicated hardware with bounded latencies, and why the loop must be fast.
The loop rates make a hierarchy. A motor controller at 1 kHz has 1 ms for each cycle. A state estimator at 100 Hz, a motion planner at 10 Hz and a task planner at 1 Hz or slower, each wrapped around the faster one. If the 10 Hz planner misses one cycle, the 1 kHz controller keeps following the last trajectory for another 100 ms, which is safe only if the trajectory is valid for that time. A good design lets each layer fail softly into the layer below it.
A small control sketch shows the lowest level:
```python
def pid_step(err, state, kp=2.0, ki=0.5, kd=0.05, dt=0.001):
    state.i += err * dt                 # integral of error
    d = (err - state.prev) / dt         # rate of change of error
    state.prev = err
    return kp * err + ki * state.i + kd * d
```
intuition: Walking in the dark with a hand on the wall: you guess where you are, touch the wall to correct the guess, plan a few steps, and take them while your feet keep adjusting the balance.
The loop structure follows from three facts. Sensors are noisy, so an estimate is a belief that must be updated by evidence, which is what Bayes' rule and the Kalman filter do. Actions have effects that are not exactly as commanded, so feedback is needed at the lowest level, and the quickest correction is the simplest. Decisions have different time scales: a motor needs an answer in a millisecond, a path in a tenth of a second, a task plan in a few seconds, so the system is layered by speed, each layer working with a simpler, faster model than the one above. The physics at the bottom, the rigid-body laws, set what any command can achieve.
mistakes: People often think a robot is a program that follows a plan. In fact the plan is out of date as soon as the robot moves; a robot keeps comparing its estimate with the plan, and re-plans. The feedback loop, not the plan, is what makes it work in an uncertain world.
People often think sensor fusion means averaging sensors. In fact fusion weights each source by its uncertainty, and in a Kalman filter the weights change with time. A good sensor is trusted more, a drifting one less, and an outlier is rejected by comparing it with the prediction.
People often think better perception is all that autonomy needs. In fact perception errors are only one source of failure; the rest are prediction of other agents, planning in rare situations, latency, and the control of a vehicle on a slippery road. Real systems fail in the long tail of unusual cases.
People often think a policy that works in simulation will work on the real robot. In fact simulators leave out friction details, sensor noise, flexibility and delays, so a controller tuned in simulation can oscillate or fall on hardware. Domain randomisation, system identification and testing on hardware reduce the gap but never remove it.
People often think the fastest controller is the best one. In fact a controller with too much gain amplifies noise and delay and goes unstable; a PID loop needs enough margin against the sensor and actuator lags. The aim is a stable system with the right bandwidth, not the highest gain.
tradeoffs: Choose a modular stack (separate perception, prediction, planning and control) when you need to test, certify and debug each part, and when safety rules ask for explainable behaviour. Choose an end-to-end learned policy when the task is hard to write by hand, such as dexterous manipulation, and you have much data or good simulation. Modular gives traceability and costs hand-built interfaces that lose information; end-to-end uses all the information and is hard to verify.
Choose a Kalman filter when the model is nearly linear, the noise is roughly Gaussian and you need a few microseconds per update on a microcontroller. Choose a particle filter when the belief has several peaks or the model is strongly nonlinear, accepting thousands of samples per update and a higher compute cost.
Choose sampling-based planners (RRT, PRM) for arms and high-dimensional spaces, where they find a path fast but not the best one; choose grid or graph search (A*) for 2D navigation where optimal paths are cheap; choose trajectory optimisation or model-predictive control when smoothness and dynamics matter, accepting local minima and the need for a good initial guess.
Choose lidar when you need accurate depth day and night and can pay for it; choose cameras alone when cost and resolution matter and you have strong vision models, accepting weaker depth in fog and glare. Most safety-critical systems fuse several sensor types for redundancy.
Choose to train in simulation when real trials are slow, costly or dangerous, and the sim can be made close to reality; choose to learn on hardware, or from demonstrations, when contact and materials are hard to simulate. You give up scale in the second case, and fidelity in the first.
interview: Why use a Kalman filter instead of simply trusting the newest measurement? || Each measurement is noisy, and between measurements the robot moves. The filter combines the motion prediction with the measurement and weights them by their variances, so the result has lower variance than either. It also gives a covariance that says how uncertain the position is, which planners and safety checks can use.
Your robot's position drifts over several minutes indoors. What do you check? || Odometry and IMU errors accumulate, so check that a correcting sensor is used (lidar scan matching against a map, visual features, fiducial markers), and that the filter's noise parameters match reality. Look at time synchronisation between sensors, wheel slip, and whether the map matches the environment. Add loop closure if building a map.
How do you split the control loop rates, and what happens if the planner is late? || Run the low-level controller at a high fixed rate (about 1 kHz) on a real-time-capable processor, the state estimator at 50 to 200 Hz, the motion planner at 5 to 20 Hz, and the task planner slowly. If the planner is late, the controller keeps following the last valid trajectory for a bounded time, and a watchdog commands a safe stop if no new trajectory arrives within the limit.
Why does an arm controlled by independent PID loops perform badly at high speed? || The joints are coupled: the torque on one depends on the motion of the others (the inertia matrix, Coriolis and gravity terms), which are not constant. Independent PIDs treat these as disturbances and respond with lag. Computed-torque or model-predictive control uses the dynamics model to cancel them.
How would you make a policy trained in simulation work on a real robot? || Randomise physical parameters, noise and delays in training so the policy is robust, identify real parameters (mass, friction, motor response) to tune the sim, add safety limits and a fallback controller, and test progressively on hardware, starting with low speeds. Collect real data to fine-tune.
friction: The real world has a long tail. A system can run well for millions of kilometres or lifts and still fail on a rare case: a person in an unusual costume, glare on a wet road, a pallet left in the wrong place. Each rare case needs data, tests and often a change to the model. Proving safety is hard, since statistical evidence of billions of miles is needed to show a very low rate of failure, and learned components resist formal analysis.
Hardware is a limit of its own. Batteries bound running time, motors overheat, sensors fail or get dirty, and calibration drifts. Mechanical parts wear, and machines in the field need maintenance. Cost matters: lidars, actuators and compute are expensive, and a robot that is not cheaper than the labour it replaces will not be used outside special cases.
Integration is hard. Dozens of components with different rates and clocks must be synchronised; a time-stamp error of 50 ms makes sensor fusion wrong in a way that is hard to see. ROS 2 and DDS settings (QoS, discovery) cause many problems, and real-time behaviour in a general-purpose OS needs care. Uncertainty about how humans will behave near the robot adds social and legal difficulty.
Finally, learning-based methods change the engineering. They need large and diverse data sets, show unpredictable failures, and are hard to certify against standards such as ISO 26262 and ISO 21448 for road vehicles or ISO 10218 for industrial robots. Most deployed systems use learning for perception and prediction and keep classical, checkable code for planning limits and safety.
practice: Build the loop first, then improve each stage. Start in simulation with a ROS 2 stack (Gazebo, MuJoCo or Isaac Sim) and a simple robot: estimate pose from odometry and an IMU with an extended Kalman filter, plan with A* on a map, and drive with a PID controller. Log all sensor data with timestamps so you can replay failures. Measure end-to-end latency from sensor timestamp to motor command and keep a budget for each stage.
Put the safety layer in early and make it independent: speed and force limits, a watchdog on each critical process, and a hardware emergency stop. Tune controllers from step responses, increase gains slowly, and check the gain and phase margins. Calibrate sensors (camera intrinsics, lidar-to-camera transform, IMU bias) and synchronise clocks, since most fusion errors come from time and geometry.
Test in layers: unit tests for the maths, simulated scenarios with randomised conditions, hardware-in-the-loop, and then slow field trials with a safety operator. Keep a data engine: every failure in the field becomes a test scenario and training data. For learning-based policies, keep a classical fallback and monitor out-of-distribution input. For study, read "Probabilistic Robotics" for estimation, "Planning Algorithms" by LaValle for motion planning, and the Modern Robotics course for kinematics and control.

@@ speech
value: Speech is the fastest way people communicate, at about 150 words a minute, and the hardest to handle for a computer. The signal is a pressure wave sampled 16,000 times a second, with no spaces between words, with every speaker and microphone sounding different, and with the same word pronounced differently each time. Speech recognition (ASR, automatic speech recognition) turns this wave into text. Speech synthesis (TTS, text to speech) does the reverse: it turns text into a wave that sounds like a person. Together they make voice assistants, captions, call-centre analytics, dictation, voice search on a television and accessibility tools.
The modern method has a common shape. First, turn the audio into a **spectrogram**, a picture of which frequencies are present at each moment, about 100 frames per second. Then a neural network maps the frames to text. Two designs dominate: CTC, which gives a label (or a "blank") for each frame, and encoder-decoder models, which read the whole spectrogram and then write the text token by token, as Whisper does. Synthesis runs the other way: a network predicts a spectrogram from text, and a **vocoder** turns the spectrogram back into a waveform.
For the layer above, voice assistants and multimodal models take speech as input and produce it as output; a spoken question to an assistant passes through ASR, a language model and TTS. The layers below supply the parts. The fast Fourier transform (L3) computes the spectrogram. Phonetics and speech acoustics (L5) explain why the spectrogram looks the way it does: the vocal tract shapes sound into vowels and consonants with known frequency patterns. Hidden Markov models (L2) were the classic recogniser and still explain the idea of decoding. Recurrent networks (L2) and then self-attention (L2) are the networks that replaced them.
terms: Sampling rate — the number of audio measurements per second; 16 kHz is common for speech, 8 kHz for telephones.
Spectrogram — a table of signal energy by frequency (rows) and time (columns), computed with short-time Fourier transforms.
Mel scale — a frequency scale that follows human hearing, with fine steps at low frequencies and coarse steps at high ones; 80 mel bins describe speech well.
Phoneme — the smallest sound unit that distinguishes words, such as /k/ in "cat"; English has about 40.
CTC (connectionist temporal classification) — a loss that lets a network output one label or a blank per frame, and sums over all alignments that give the correct text.
WER (word error rate) — substitutions plus deletions plus insertions, divided by the number of words in the reference.
Language model — a model of which word sequences are likely; it helps choose between words that sound alike.
Vocoder — a model that turns a predicted spectrogram into a waveform.
Streaming — recognising speech while the person is still speaking, with a latency of a few hundred milliseconds.
history: In 1952, Bell Labs built "Audrey", which recognised spoken digits from one speaker by matching the energy in a few frequency bands. IBM showed "Shoebox" in 1962 with 16 words. In the 1970s, Carnegie Mellon's Harpy understood about 1,000 words with a network of allowed sentences. The decisive step was statistical. Frederick Jelinek's group at IBM and James and Janet Baker at CMU (and then Dragon Systems) treated recognition as a probability problem solved with hidden Markov models, and Lawrence Rabiner's 1989 tutorial taught a generation. Dragon NaturallySpeaking (1997) brought continuous dictation to PCs.
For about 30 years the recipe was fixed: Gaussian mixture models for the sound, HMMs for the order of sounds, a pronunciation dictionary and an n-gram language model, all joined in a search. In 2012 Hinton and colleagues at several labs showed that deep neural networks could replace the Gaussian mixtures and cut errors by 20% or more. Alex Graves introduced CTC in 2006 and the RNN transducer in 2012, which allowed the network to learn alignments itself. Baidu's Deep Speech (2014) showed an end-to-end recurrent network trained on thousands of hours of data. Listen, Attend and Spell (2015) used attention, and the Transformer and Conformer (2020) models followed. Microsoft reported human-level error on the Switchboard telephone test in 2016 and 2017.
Self-supervised pre-training (wav2vec 2.0, 2020) learned from audio without text. In September 2022 OpenAI released Whisper, a Transformer encoder-decoder trained on 680,000 hours of weakly labelled web audio in many languages, and the weights were open. Because the training data was so varied, it was robust to accents and noise without fine-tuning. On the synthesis side, WaveNet (DeepMind, 2016) first gave natural-sounding speech, Tacotron 2 (2017) predicted spectrograms from text, HiFi-GAN (2020) made vocoders fast, and VALL-E (2023) and later models generate speech with language-model methods and clone a voice from a few seconds of audio.
how: The recogniser has two halves, the front end that makes features and the model that makes text.
Step 1: capture and frame. The microphone signal is sampled at 16 kHz, which captures frequencies up to 8 kHz, where most speech information lies. The signal is cut into overlapping windows of 25 ms (400 samples) every 10 ms (160 samples). This gives 100 frames per second. Speech changes slowly enough that each window is nearly steady, like one still picture of the vocal tract.
Step 2: the spectrogram. Each window is multiplied by a smooth taper and passed through the fast Fourier transform (L3), which gives the energy at 201 frequencies for a 400-sample window. The energies are grouped by a bank of overlapping triangular filters on the mel scale, \( m = 2595 \log_{10}(1 + f/700) \), into 80 numbers, and the logarithm is taken, since loudness is perceived logarithmically. The result is a log-mel spectrogram: 80 numbers per frame, 100 frames a second. The phonetics (L5) matters here. A vowel shows as a few bands of energy called formants, produced by the resonances of the vocal tract, and the first two formants identify most vowels. Consonants show as bursts, hisses (high-frequency noise for /s/) or gaps. The spectrogram makes these patterns visible to the network.
Step 3a: the classical recogniser. By Bayes' rule, the best word sequence for audio \( X \) is
\[ \hat{W} = \arg\max_W P(X \mid W)\, P(W) \]
where \( P(X \mid W) \) is the acoustic model and \( P(W) \) the language model. The acoustic model is an HMM (L2): each phoneme is a small chain of hidden states, each state emits frames with a probability given by a Gaussian mixture or a neural network, and a pronunciation dictionary links phonemes to words. The Viterbi algorithm (L2) searches the combined graph of acoustic states, words and language-model probabilities for the most likely path. It is accurate but needs many separately built parts.
Step 3b: CTC. A neural network (recurrent, convolutional or Conformer) outputs for each frame a probability over the characters or word pieces plus a blank symbol. A frame sequence is turned into text by merging repeated labels and removing blanks. The CTC loss adds up the probability of all frame sequences that collapse to the correct text, so the network learns the alignment without labels for each frame. It is fast and good for streaming, and it assumes that each output is independent given the audio, so an external language model improves it.
Step 3c: encoder-decoder with attention. The encoder, a Transformer built on self-attention (L2), reads the entire spectrogram and produces a sequence of vectors. The decoder generates text one token at a time, attending over the encoder output and over the tokens already written, which gives it a built-in language model. Whisper works this way. Its input is exactly 30 seconds, so 3,000 frames; a convolution with stride 2 reduces this to 1,500 positions for the encoder. Special tokens at the start of the decoder choose the task (transcribe or translate), the language and whether to give timestamps, so one model handles many jobs. Longer audio is cut into 30-second windows and the text of one window is used as a prompt for the next.
Step 4: synthesis. A text front end normalises numbers and abbreviations ("Dr." to "doctor") and converts text to phonemes. An acoustic model (Tacotron-style attention, or a non-autoregressive model such as FastSpeech) predicts a mel spectrogram, with the duration and pitch of each sound. A vocoder (WaveNet, HiFi-GAN) generates the waveform at 16 to 24 kHz or more; since a spectrogram has no phase, the vocoder must invent a consistent one. Newer systems predict discrete audio codes with a language model and decode them, which makes it easy to clone a voice from a short sample.
Step 5: the pipeline around it. A voice-activity detector finds where speech starts and ends. Streaming systems use chunked or transducer models and emit partial words with a revision. Punctuation, capitalisation, speaker labels (diarisation) and custom vocabulary are added after or inside the model. Quality is measured by WER on test sets that match the real audio.

example: Start with the front end for a 3-second clip at 16 kHz. It has 48,000 samples. With a 25 ms window (400 samples) and a 10 ms hop (160 samples), the number of frames is about (48,000 − 400) / 160 + 1 = 298. Each frame becomes 80 mel values, so the network receives a table of 298 × 80 = 23,840 numbers instead of 48,000 raw samples. For Whisper, a 30-second clip has 3,000 frames and after the stride-2 convolution 1,500 encoder positions, each about 20 ms of audio.
Now CTC decoding. Suppose the network is asked to write "hi" from 6 frames and the best label at each frame is: _ h h _ i i, where "_" is the blank. Merge repeats: _ h _ i. Remove blanks: "hi". Several frame paths give the same text: "h h h i i i", "_ h _ _ i _" and so on. The CTC loss adds the probabilities of all of them. For a repeated letter such as "hello", the two l's must be separated by a blank in the frames (l _ l), or they would be merged into one. This is why the blank is needed.
Next, WER. The reference is "turn the volume up" (4 words). The system writes "turn a volume up now". Alignment: "the" is replaced by "a" (1 substitution), and "now" is added (1 insertion), with no deletions. WER = (1 + 0 + 1) / 4 = 50%. Note that WER can exceed 100% if there are many insertions. A WER of 5% on a 1,000-word call means about 50 word errors, which is acceptable for search over transcripts but not for legal records.
Last, a cost estimate for batch transcription. A call centre has 10,000 hours of audio a month. A Whisper-large-class model on one modern GPU may run at 20 to 50 times real time with batching (this depends strongly on hardware and implementation). At 30 times, 10,000 hours take 333 GPU-hours, so a few GPUs finish in a day. The same audio through a streaming API is billed per minute and has the advantage of low latency instead.
intuition: Reading a spectrogram is like reading sheet music: the notes are not the words, but a trained reader can tell the song from the pattern, and a language model supplies what was most likely meant.
The design follows how speech is made. The vocal tract is a source (the vocal cords) that is shaped by a filter (the mouth and tongue), so the information is in the frequency shape that changes every few tens of milliseconds, and a time-frequency picture is the natural input. What remains is the matching between a long audio sequence and a short text sequence, with unknown alignment. HMMs, CTC and attention are three answers to that alignment problem: a hidden state path, a sum over all frame paths, and a learned soft pointer. The language model matters because sound alone is ambiguous ("recognise speech" and "wreck a nice beach" sound nearly the same), and context tells the system which is more likely.
mistakes: People often think speech recognition works on the raw words one at a time. In fact there is no pause between spoken words, and sounds blend across the boundary. The system must find the word boundaries and the words together, using a language model for context.
People often think a model with a low WER on a benchmark will work on any audio. In fact accent, noise, microphone, topic and vocabulary change the error a lot. Names, product terms and code-switching (mixing two languages) are common failures. Test on audio from your own use.
People often think end-to-end models need no language knowledge. In fact an end-to-end model has learned a language model inside its decoder from the training text, and CTC models still gain from an external language model or from a list of expected words. For rare names, adding a phrase list or fine-tuning is needed.
People often think a fluent transcript is a correct transcript. In fact encoder-decoder models can hallucinate: on silence, music or noise, Whisper-style models sometimes produce plausible sentences that were never said, or repeat a phrase. Voice-activity detection, confidence thresholds and checks on the output length reduce this.
People often think synthesis only plays back recorded pieces. In fact modern TTS generates new audio from a learned model, which is why it can say any text and clone a voice. This brings consent and misuse concerns.
tradeoffs: Choose CTC or a transducer model (RNN-T, Conformer-transducer) when you need streaming with a latency of a few hundred milliseconds, on-device recognition, or predictable cost. Choose an encoder-decoder such as Whisper when you want the best accuracy on messy audio and can wait for the end of a segment; it is easier to use for many languages and tasks. You give up a few points of accuracy in the first case, and real-time behaviour and sometimes control (hallucination) in the second.
Choose a cloud ASR API when the volume is small or variable, you want many languages and no operations; choose a self-hosted open model when audio is sensitive, volume is high (tens of thousands of hours a month) or you need to fine-tune on your own vocabulary. You trade a per-minute price against GPU cost and engineering time.
Choose a larger model (1.5 billion parameters) for offline transcription where accuracy matters, and a small or distilled model (40 to 250 million) for devices and real time. WER differences of 1 to 3 points between sizes on clean audio can grow to 10 points on noisy audio.
Choose 16 kHz audio for recognition; telephone audio at 8 kHz limits accuracy and needs models trained on it. For synthesis, choose a neural vocoder at 24 kHz or higher for quality, and a lighter model on device.
Choose cascade (ASR then a language model then TTS) when you need control, logging and exact text; choose a direct speech-to-speech model when you need fast, natural turn-taking and can accept less control and inspection.
interview: Explain CTC and why it needs a blank symbol. || The network outputs a distribution over labels plus a blank at each frame. The text is obtained by merging repeated labels and removing blanks. The loss sums the probability over all frame sequences that collapse to the target. The blank lets the model say "no new label here" and separates genuine repeated letters (l _ l in "hello") from one long sound.
Why does Whisper use 30-second windows, and how do you transcribe a 2-hour file? || The encoder was trained on fixed 30-second inputs (3,000 frames), so the audio is padded or cut to that size. For long files, split into 30-second windows, ideally at silences from a voice-activity detector, decode each, and use the previous text as context. Handle overlaps and timestamps, and watch for repeats and hallucination at the boundaries and in silence.
Your ASR gives a 12% WER on call audio but 4% on the benchmark. What do you do? || Check the mismatch: 8 kHz telephone codec, noise, crosstalk, accents, domain words. Collect and label a few hours of real audio, measure WER by segment type, and try a phrase list, a domain language model or fine-tuning. Add noise-augmented data and check whether most errors are names or numbers, since those might be fixed by post-processing.
How would you build a voice assistant with a 1-second response time? || Stream the audio to a transducer or chunked ASR with endpointing, so the text is ready when the user stops; run the language model with streaming output; start the TTS on the first sentence while the rest is generated; and keep each stage within a budget (for example 300 ms, 400 ms and 200 ms). Allow barge-in, where the user interrupts the voice.
What is WER, and why can it mislead? || It is (substitutions + deletions + insertions) divided by the reference word count. It treats all words equally, so dropping "not" counts the same as a wrong article, it ignores meaning, punctuation and names, and it depends on text normalisation (numbers, case). Use task metrics too, such as entity accuracy or intent accuracy.
friction: Accuracy drops outside the training conditions. Noise, reverberation, several speakers, children, accented or low-resource languages and domain jargon all raise errors, and for many of the world's languages there is little data. Far-field microphones and overlapping speech remain hard, and speaker diarisation has its own errors.
Large end-to-end models are expensive and sometimes unreliable. Hallucination, repeated phrases and missing sentences make them risky for legal or medical records without checks. Streaming with a high-accuracy model is difficult because the model wants future context. Latency, battery and memory limit on-device use.
Synthesis has trade-offs of its own: expressive, controllable speech needs large models and data; prosody (rhythm and emphasis) can sound flat or wrong for the meaning; and a cloned voice raises consent, fraud and deepfake problems. Detecting synthetic speech is an arms race.
Evaluation is also hard. WER is simple but does not measure what users care about, and human judgement of TTS (mean opinion score) is expensive and noisy. Privacy is another issue: audio contains the identity, health and location of the speaker, so recording, retention and consent rules apply, including for voice features on televisions and phones.
practice: Start with an open model such as Whisper (the large-v3 or turbo variants) or a cloud API on a sample of your own audio, and measure WER on 5 to 10 hours of labelled data. Look at the errors by type before choosing a fix. For names and domain terms, try a phrase list or prompt; for a consistent mismatch (telephone audio, an accent), fine-tune with a few hundred hours.
Build the pipeline around the model. Resample to 16 kHz mono, normalise level, use a voice-activity detector to drop silence and to cut long files, and run in batches on a GPU. Use a faster implementation (CTranslate2-based faster-whisper, or a vendor runtime), and quantise to 8 bits when speed matters. Store the timestamps and confidence, and keep the audio for a retention period you can justify.
For interactive voice, use a streaming model with endpointing, budget the latency of each stage, and support barge-in. For synthesis, choose a voice with a licence that allows your use, normalise text for numbers and dates, and add a mark that disclosures it as synthetic where required. For study, read the Whisper paper, Rabiner's HMM tutorial, the CTC article on Distill by Awni Hannun, and the Tacotron 2 and HiFi-GAN papers.

@@ mobilearch
value: A mobile app runs on a device that the developer does not control. The network goes away in a lift and returns in a car park; a call, a battery warning or the user swiping to another app can stop the app at any moment; the operating system kills background apps without notice to save power. The code on the device cannot be rolled back at will: an update goes through a store review and takes days to reach the users, and some users stay on an old version for years. And the user's patience is short: a screen must appear in a fraction of a second and respond within one frame, about 16 ms at 60 Hz.
Mobile app architecture is the set of patterns that make an app feel fast and reliable under these conditions. The central pattern is **offline-first**: the app keeps a local database as the single source of truth for every screen. The screens read from it and the user's actions write to it, so the interface never waits for the network. A separate sync layer moves changes in both directions in the background, with retries and rules for conflicts. The server's API is designed for this: it pages with cursors and returns changes since a position. Push notifications (APNs on iOS, FCM on Android) wake the app when something new exists.
For the layer above, SQLite is the usual on-device store, used directly or through libraries such as Room on Android and Core Data or SwiftData on iOS. The layers below supply the methods. CRDTs (L2) show how offline edits from several devices merge without losing data. Cache eviction (L2) keeps local storage bounded. Retries, backoff and jitter (L2) make sync safe on bad networks. API design (L2) gives pagination, versioning and idempotency. WebSocket, SSE and long polling (L3) deliver live updates while the app is open.
terms: Offline-first — a design where the app works fully from local data and treats the network as an optional, slow background channel.
Source of truth — the one place that screens read from; here the on-device database, not the server response.
Outbox — a local table of user changes not yet confirmed by the server, sent in order with retries.
Sync cursor (token) — an opaque value from the server that marks the position up to which the client has received changes, so the next call returns only newer ones.
Cursor pagination — fetching a list in pages using a pointer to the last item seen, rather than a page number, so inserts do not shift or repeat items.
Idempotency key — a unique ID sent with a write so that the server applies it once even if the request is repeated.
Push notification — a short message delivered through the platform's service (APNs or FCM) that can wake the app or show an alert while the app is not running.
Silent (data) push — a push with no visible alert that asks the app to refresh in the background; the system may delay or drop it.
Conflict resolution — the rule for combining two edits to the same data made on different devices, such as last-writer-wins or a field-by-field merge.
history: The first smartphones (the iPhone in 2007, Android in 2008) had slow, unreliable data networks, and early apps were thin clients: each screen fetched a page from a server, and a failed request showed a spinner or an error. As apps grew, developers saw that this feels slow and breaks offline. Apple launched the push service APNs with iOS 3 in 2009, so that apps could be notified when they were not running; Google followed with C2DM (2010), then GCM (2012), renamed Firebase Cloud Messaging (FCM) in 2016.
Operating systems then began to restrict background work to save battery. Android introduced Doze mode in Android 6 (2015) and background execution limits in Android 8 (2017). iOS limited background refresh and later introduced the BackgroundTasks framework (iOS 13). The tools changed: WorkManager (2018) for deferred, guaranteed background work on Android, and background modes on iOS. These limits made it important to do sync in short, resumable bursts.
Meanwhile, patterns spread. Google's Android architecture guidance (2017 onward) recommended a repository over a local Room database, with the UI observing it. Apple added SwiftData in 2023. Apps such as email, notes and messaging showed offline-first at scale, and the 2019 essay "Local-first software" (Kleppmann and others at Ink and Switch) argued for apps where the local copy is primary, using CRDTs for merging. Sync services appeared (Firebase, Realm Sync, CouchDB and PouchDB, and later libraries built on CRDTs), although some, such as Realm's device sync, have been withdrawn by their vendors, a reminder that a sync stack is a long-term dependency.
how: Seen from the inside, an offline-first app is a loop between the screen, the local database and the server.
Step 1: the screen reads only from the database. The UI layer subscribes to queries on the local store (Room with Kotlin Flow, Core Data or SwiftData with observation). When the data changes, the query emits new results and the screen redraws. A screen shows data at once, even with no network, and always shows the same state that the sync layer has written. On a slow device this also keeps the main thread free: database calls run on a background thread, and the main thread has 16 ms per frame at 60 Hz (8 ms at 120 Hz) to draw.
Step 2: the user's action writes locally first. A tap on "send" or "like" inserts or updates a row at once, with a client-generated ID (a UUID) and a status such as `pending`, and adds an entry to the outbox. The screen updates immediately (an optimistic update). Nothing waits for the server, and if the app is killed, the pending change is still on disk.
Step 3: the sync worker pushes. A background worker drains the outbox in order. Each request carries an idempotency key, which is the change's ID, so that a retry after a lost response does not create a duplicate. If the request fails, the worker retries with exponential backoff and jitter (L2): for example wait 1 s, 2 s, 4 s and so on up to a cap, each multiplied by a random number, so that thousands of devices that lost the network at the same time do not return at the same instant. Permanent errors (a validation failure) are marked and shown to the user, not retried. The worker is scheduled by the platform (WorkManager, BGTaskScheduler) with constraints such as "network available" and "battery not low".
Step 4: the sync worker pulls. The client sends the cursor it holds; the server returns the changes after that cursor, a page at a time, and a new cursor. Pagination uses an opaque cursor that encodes the sort key and ID of the last item, and the server's query is a keyset query, not an offset, so a new item inserted at the top does not shift the pages (API design, L2). The client applies each page in one database transaction, together with the new cursor, so a crash in the middle never leaves a half-applied page.
Step 5: conflicts. Two devices may edit the same record offline. The simplest rule is last-writer-wins with a server timestamp or version number; it is easy and loses one edit. A better rule is a field-level merge, where each field has its own version, so edits to different fields both survive. For collaborative text and lists, CRDTs (L2) give a merge that is the same on every device whatever the order of arrival. The server must be the arbiter of order for ordinary data, and the client must accept the server's version after the merge.
Step 6: stay current. While the app is open, a WebSocket or an SSE stream (L3) delivers changes within a second. When the app is not running, a push notification wakes it. The best practice is to treat the push as a hint, not as data: it carries only "something changed" (with a small payload, since both APNs and FCM limit it to about 4 KB), and the app then pulls from its cursor. A push can be dropped or delayed, so the app also syncs when it is opened and on a slow schedule.
Step 7: bound local storage. Caches of images and responses are limited by size and evicted with LRU (L2): for example, an image cache capped at 250 MB. The database is pruned by age or by relevance (keep 90 days of messages locally, fetch older ones on demand), and large downloads are in a cache directory that the system may clear. Migrations of the local schema run at app update, and must be tested for old versions.

example: A chat user goes into a tunnel and sends three messages. Each message is inserted into the local `messages` table with a new UUID and status `pending`, and into the outbox; the three bubbles appear on the screen at once with a clock icon. The worker tries to send the first, fails after a timeout of 10 s, and schedules a retry after about 1 s with random jitter between 0 and 1 s. It fails again and waits up to 2 s, then up to 4 s, with a cap of, say, 5 minutes. When the network returns, the first request is sent with its UUID as the idempotency key. The response is lost in a weak signal, so the worker sends the same request again; the server sees the same key and returns the stored result without inserting a second message. The server's answer carries a server ID and a timestamp, and the client replaces the pending status with `sent`. The order of the messages follows the server sequence, so the order is the same on every device.
Now the pagination problem. A feed has 10,000 items, newest first, 20 per page. With offset paging, page 2 is `OFFSET 20`. After the client loads page 1, 5 new items arrive; page 2 at `OFFSET 20` now starts 5 items earlier, so the client sees 5 items twice. With a cursor, the server returns the last item's sort key and ID, and the next query continues after it:
```sql
SELECT id, created_at, body
FROM feed
WHERE (created_at, id) < (:last_created_at, :last_id)
ORDER BY created_at DESC, id DESC
LIMIT 20;
```
The new items do not change the result, and with an index on (created_at, id) the query reads 20 index entries however deep the page is, while an offset of 9,980 would read and discard nearly 10,000 rows.
Finally, the thundering herd. A server outage ends and 1 million clients are waiting to retry. If all retry every 1 second without jitter, the server receives about 1 million requests in the same second and falls again. With full jitter, each client waits a random time between 0 and a window that doubles up to 30 s; the load spreads to roughly 1,000,000 / 30 ≈ 33,000 requests per second at the cap, a level that a recovering service can usually absorb, and it decreases as clients succeed.
intuition: Writing in a notebook and later copying the notes to the shared file: you never wait for the office to be open, and a clerk reconciles the two when you meet.
The design is natural because the device is the only part that is always present, while the network is sometimes there. Putting the database on the device makes the interface fast and independent, and the network becomes a replication problem, which distributed systems already know how to solve: log of changes, positions in the log, idempotent retries, and rules for conflicts. Treating the push as a hint reflects that the delivery services are best-effort. The mobile case adds the bounded battery and the loss of control over when code runs, which leads to short, resumable, batched sync jobs.
mistakes: People often think a mobile app is a thin client that shows server data. In fact a good app has a real data layer: a database, a sync engine and a cache policy. A thin client works until the first tunnel, and then it shows a blank screen.
People often think push notifications carry the data. In fact push is unreliable, limited to about 4 KB, can be delayed, and on iOS silent pushes are throttled by the system. Use the push to say "sync now", and let the app fetch the data with its cursor. Also sync on app start.
People often think retry is simple: try again on failure. In fact a retry without a limit, backoff and jitter can overload a recovering server, a retry of a non-idempotent write creates duplicates, and a retry on a permanent error loops forever. A good retry has a cap, a deadline, an idempotency key, and a distinction between temporary and permanent errors.
People often think the server version can be changed whenever needed. In fact old app versions live for months or years, so every API change must be backward compatible, and the server must handle clients several versions old. Breaking changes need a new version and a minimum supported version with a forced-upgrade screen.
People often think page numbers are a fine way to paginate. In fact offsets break when the data changes, and they cost the server more for deep pages. Keyset (cursor) pagination is stable and constant in cost.
tradeoffs: Choose offline-first with a local database when users need the app in poor connectivity, the data is personal (messages, notes, tasks) and a stale view is acceptable for seconds. Choose online-first with a small cache when the data must be always exact and shared (a trading price, seat availability, payments) and an offline write could be wrong. You give up simplicity and storage in the first case, and resilience in the second.
Choose last-writer-wins when conflicts are rare and the loss of an occasional edit is acceptable (a profile setting). Choose field-level merge or a CRDT when users edit the same record from several devices or collaborate on text, accepting more metadata per record and more complex code and tests.
Choose push (APNs, FCM) for events that matter when the app is closed, and accept the platform's limits and delays; choose a WebSocket or SSE while the app is in the foreground for sub-second updates, accepting a battery cost for an open connection. Choose periodic background sync (every 15 minutes or more, as the OS allows) only as a fallback, since it is neither prompt nor cheap.
Choose a full re-sync (download everything) for small data sets under a few megabytes, for its simplicity; choose delta sync with cursors for anything larger, accepting the need for a change log on the server and a path to rebuild when the cursor is too old.
Choose a native stack (Swift, Kotlin) when you need the best performance, platform APIs and background behaviour; choose a cross-platform framework (React Native, Flutter) when one team must cover both platforms, and accept an extra layer for native features.
interview: Design the sync for a notes app that works offline on two devices. || Keep notes in a local SQLite database with a version per note and an outbox. The client pushes changes with idempotency keys and pulls changes since a cursor. Resolve conflicts by field-level merge or, for text, a CRDT; keep the server as the order authority. Add retries with backoff and jitter, apply each pulled page and cursor in one transaction, and a full re-sync path when the cursor expires.
Why use an idempotency key, and where does the server store it? || A response can be lost after the server has processed the request, so the client must retry, and without a key the write would run twice. The server stores the key with the result for a retention window (hours to days) in the same transaction as the write, and returns the stored result for a repeated key.
How do you handle an API change that removes a field when 30% of users run an older app? || Never remove it at once. Keep the field and version the API, measure the share of old versions in telemetry, announce a minimum version, show an upgrade prompt, and remove the field only when the old version share is below an agreed limit. Use feature flags to turn off the new behaviour quickly.
A silent push should refresh data but often does not arrive on iOS. What do you do? || Do not depend on it: treat it as a hint and also sync on app open, on the app-refresh background task and on a visible alert push for important events. Keep the payload small, set the right priority and `content-available` flags, and monitor the delivery rate. Do the work in a short, resumable job.
How would you keep the local database from growing without limit? || Set a retention rule by age and by importance, delete rows and their media on a schedule, evict image caches by LRU with a size cap, and fetch older data on demand. Store large files outside the database, run VACUUM sparingly, and test migrations on large databases.
friction: Platform limits shape everything. The OS decides when background work runs, and the rules change with each release; a design that worked on one Android version may be delayed on another, and manufacturers add their own battery managers. iOS gives no guarantee about silent pushes or the time of background refresh. Testing this behaviour is hard because it depends on the battery, the usage pattern and the vendor.
The sync engine is the hardest component. Conflict rules, partial failures, schema migrations on the device, the case where the cursor is older than the server's change log, and data corruption all need design and tests. Bugs lose users' data, which is the worst failure for an app. Many teams underestimate it and later move to a library or service, with its own lock-in.
Device variety and slow releases add cost. Thousands of devices with different screens, memory and OS versions need testing, old versions stay in use, and a bad release cannot be recalled but only superseded, after store review and a staged rollout. This is why remote configuration and feature flags are common: they let the team turn off a broken feature without a release.
Security and privacy are harder on a device the user owns. The local database holds personal data that must be encrypted at rest (the platform keystore holds the key), tokens must be refreshed and stored safely, and a lost phone must not leak data. Consent and deletion rules apply on the device as well as the server. Finally, battery, data cost and memory are visible to the user: heavy sync or an always-open socket is noticed in the battery screen and in reviews.
practice: Make the local database the data source for every screen, and test the app in airplane mode as a normal case. Give every created object a client-generated ID, keep an outbox, and send changes with idempotency keys. Design the server API for sync: list endpoints with keyset cursors, a "changes since cursor" endpoint, a stable sort, a server timestamp or sequence per record, and a way to say "your cursor is too old, resync".
Make retries safe: exponential backoff with full jitter and a cap, a distinction between retryable (timeouts, 5xx, 429 with Retry-After) and permanent errors, and a maximum lifetime for a pending change. Apply each pulled page and its cursor in one transaction. Use the platform schedulers for background work (WorkManager, BGTaskScheduler) and keep each job short and resumable.
Plan for the long life of clients. Version the API, log the app version in every request, define a minimum supported version, and use remote config and feature flags with staged rollouts to limit damage. Bound storage with LRU caches and retention rules, encrypt the local database, and watch cold-start time, crash-free rate, sync success rate, battery use and the share of requests that are retries. Keep push payloads small and use them only as hints.
quiz: What is the source of truth for the screens in an offline-first app, and why? || The local database. Screens never wait for the network, user actions write there first, and sync moves changes to and from the server in the background.
Why is cursor pagination more stable than offset pagination? || A cursor continues from the last item's sort key and ID, so new inserts do not shift the page boundary; an offset counts rows from the top, so inserted rows cause repeats or gaps and deep pages cost more.
Why should a push notification be treated as a hint? || Delivery is best-effort: pushes can be delayed, dropped or throttled, and the payload is limited to about 4 KB. The app should use it to trigger a sync from its cursor and also sync on open.
1 million clients retry after an outage with full jitter over a 30-second window. What is the average request rate? || About 1,000,000 / 30 ≈ 33,000 requests per second, instead of 1 million in one second without jitter.
When would you pick last-writer-wins and when a CRDT? || Last-writer-wins when conflicts are rare and losing an occasional edit is acceptable, as with a settings value. A CRDT or field-level merge when several devices edit the same document or list and no edit should be lost.
