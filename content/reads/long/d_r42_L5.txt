@@ mooreslaw
value: Moore's law says that the number of transistors you can put on one chip at the lowest cost doubles about every two years. Dennard scaling says that when you shrink a transistor, its voltage and capacitance shrink with it, so a chip full of smaller, faster transistors uses the same power per square millimetre. Together they gave computing about four decades of free improvement. From 1971 to 2024, the transistor count of a flagship chip went from 2,300 (Intel 4004) to about 208 billion (Nvidia B200), a factor of nearly 90 million. That is 26 doublings in 53 years, which is one doubling every two years almost exactly.
For software engineers the important point is what these two laws did to expectations. For roughly 30 years, a program got faster by itself every 18 to 24 months, because the next processor had a higher clock speed and a better design, and no one changed any code. This is why single-threaded code, garbage-collected runtimes, layers of abstraction and ever bigger frameworks were affordable. The hardware paid for the waste.
Dennard scaling stopped working around 2005 to 2006, and clock speeds stopped near 3 to 5 GHz. Moore's law did not stop, but it changed: transistors are still getting denser, while cost per transistor, SRAM (cache memory) density and energy efficiency improve more slowly. The result is the world you work in now: multicore CPUs, GPUs, custom accelerators such as TPUs, chiplet packages, and a much stronger reason to write efficient code. This read explains the physics that made the trend possible and then ended part of it. It stands on three lower concepts: the transistor (the unit being shrunk), photolithography (the way it is printed smaller each time) and thermodynamics (the reason heat became the limit).
terms: Moore's law — the observation that the number of components on an integrated circuit, at the lowest cost per component, doubles about every two years; it is an economic trend, not a law of physics.
Dennard scaling — the 1974 rule that if all dimensions and the voltage of a MOSFET shrink by the same factor, the transistor gets faster and the power per unit area stays constant.
Power density — power consumed per unit of chip area, in watts per square centimetre; heat must be removed at this rate.
Dynamic power — the energy spent switching transistors, about P = α × C × V² × f, where α is the fraction of gates switching, C capacitance, V supply voltage and f clock frequency.
Leakage current — current that flows through a transistor even when it is "off"; it grows exponentially as the threshold voltage is lowered, and it burns power all the time.
Threshold voltage (Vth) — the gate voltage at which a transistor turns on; the supply voltage must stay well above it for the transistor to switch fast.
Dark silicon — the part of a chip that must stay powered off (or run slowly) at any moment because the chip cannot cool the whole die at full speed.
Process node — the generation name of a manufacturing process (7 nm, 3 nm, 2 nm); since the 1990s it is a marketing label and no longer matches a real length on the chip.
history: Gordon Moore, then at Fairchild Semiconductor, published "Cramming more components onto integrated circuits" in the magazine Electronics on 19 April 1965. He plotted the cheapest number of components per chip for the previous few years, saw that it doubled every year, and extrapolated to about 65,000 components by 1975. The paper is mostly about cost: more components per chip lowered the cost per component, until chips got so complex that yield (the share of working chips) dropped. The minimum of that cost curve was the point he tracked. In 1975, at the IEDM conference, he looked back and changed the rate to a doubling every two years, because the easy gains from larger chips and clever circuit layout were ending.
Robert Dennard and his IBM colleagues (Gaensslen, Yu, Rideout, Bassous and LeBlanc) published "Design of ion-implanted MOSFETs with very small physical dimensions" in the IEEE Journal of Solid-State Circuits in 1974. They showed on paper that a MOSFET shrunk by a factor k in all dimensions and in voltage still works well, with delay lower by k and power per transistor lower by k². The rule gave the industry a recipe, and from the late 1970s to the early 2000s each new process generation followed it, roughly a 0.7 times shrink in length every two to three years.
The recipe broke in the 2000s. Intel's Pentium 4 pushed the clock to 3.8 GHz in 2004 with a power rating of about 115 watts, and in 2004 Intel cancelled its planned successor, "Tejas", because of power. Mark Bohr's 2007 article "A 30 year retrospective on Dennard's MOSFET scaling paper" is the standard statement that voltage scaling had stopped because of leakage. Herb Sutter's essay "The Free Lunch Is Over" (2005) told software developers what followed. In 2011, Esmaeilzadeh and co-authors named the next problem "dark silicon". Intel's own cadence slowed too: its 10 nm process arrived years late (shipping in volume around 2019), and in 2015 to 2016 the company said the gap between generations was stretching toward 2.5 years. Hennessy and Patterson's 2019 paper "A New Golden Age for Computer Architecture" argued that domain-specific hardware is the way forward.
how: Start with the transistor, the lowest concept in the stack. A MOSFET is a switch controlled by a voltage on its gate. Charging the gate and the wires attached to it stores energy of about ½ C V² each time the transistor switches, and the chip switches billions of times per second. So the dynamic power of a chip is roughly P = α C V² f, summed over all transistors. This one formula explains most of the story. Three things can lower P: less capacitance C, lower voltage V, or lower frequency f. Voltage is the strongest, because it appears squared.
Dennard's insight was about what shrinking does to each term. Suppose you scale every length by 1/k (say k = 1.4, which is a 0.7 times shrink). The transistor's area falls by 1/k², so about twice as many fit in the same area. Its capacitance falls by 1/k, because capacitance is proportional to area divided by insulator thickness, and both change. If you also lower the voltage by 1/k, the electric field inside the transistor stays the same, so it keeps working correctly, and its current falls by 1/k. Delay is about C V / I, which falls by 1/k, so the clock can rise by k. Power per transistor is C V² f, which is (1/k)(1/k²)(k) = 1/k². Since there are k² more transistors per area, the power density does not change. You get twice the transistors, each about 40% faster, in the same area and at the same power. That is why chips of the 1990s sped up from tens of MHz to about 1 GHz in one decade without melting.
Photolithography is what delivered the shrink. A chip is made by projecting a pattern of light through a mask onto a photosensitive layer on the silicon wafer, then etching or depositing material, and repeating this for dozens of layers. The smallest feature you can print is limited by the wavelength of the light and the optics, roughly CD = k₁ × λ / NA. The industry moved from 248 nm to 193 nm lasers, then used immersion in water, multiple patterning (several masks per layer) and finally 13.5 nm extreme ultraviolet (EUV) light, in volume production from about 2019. Each lithography step is the real supply of Moore's law: without it, the transistor cannot be made smaller and the doubling stops. It also makes the economics work: all transistors on a wafer are printed at once, so smaller ones on the same wafer cost little extra. This is why cost per transistor fell.
Thermodynamics explains why Dennard scaling ended. The recipe requires lowering the voltage in step with the size. But a transistor never turns completely off. Below the threshold voltage, current still flows, and it grows by about ten times for every 60 to 100 millivolts that you lower the threshold. This slope is set by thermal physics: electrons at room temperature have a spread of energies of about kT ≈ 26 meV, and an ideal MOSFET cannot switch more sharply than about 60 mV per decade of current at 300 K. To keep the transistor fast, the supply voltage must stay well above the threshold, so when threshold voltage cannot be lowered (it would raise leakage a hundred times), the supply voltage cannot be lowered either. Around the 90 nm and 65 nm generations (2003 to 2006) supply voltage stopped near 1 volt, from 5 V in the 1980s. Gate oxide was only a few atoms thick, and electrons tunnelled through it, adding another leakage path. Leakage can be 30% or more of total power in a hot chip.
After voltage stops scaling, the algebra changes. Power per transistor falls only by 1/k (capacitance) when the frequency is held fixed, while the number of transistors per area still grows by k². Power density therefore grows by k each generation, about 1.4 times. A chip cannot remove that heat: air-cooled packages handle roughly 100 to 150 watts per processor, and stopping the clock at about 4 GHz was the practical answer. The transistors kept coming, so designers spent them on parallelism (multiple cores on one die), bigger caches, vector units and specialised blocks that do one job with much less energy than a general core. A core running at half speed uses far less than half the power, because voltage can drop with frequency, so many slow cores beat one fast core for parallel work. Pollack's rule explains the other side: single-core performance grows only with the square root of its area.
Today the process has additional physics. Planar transistors were replaced by FinFETs (Intel, 2011) to regain control of the channel, and by gate-all-around "nanosheet" transistors in the 2020s (Samsung started in 2022, TSMC with its N2 node in 2025). Density gains are uneven: logic still shrinks, but SRAM, the memory used for caches, shrank very little at TSMC's N3E (the cell stayed about the same size as at N5, around 0.021 square micrometres), and analog and input-output circuits barely shrink at all. This is one reason for chiplets: a package can combine a logic die on the newest node with cache and I/O dies on older, cheaper nodes.

example: Check the trend with a pen. The Intel 4004 (1971) had 2,300 transistors. Nvidia's B200 GPU (2024) has about 208 billion. The ratio is 208 × 10⁹ / 2,300 ≈ 9.0 × 10⁷. Now log₂(9.0 × 10⁷) ≈ 26.4 doublings. The time span is 2024 − 1971 = 53 years. So 53 / 26.4 ≈ 2.0 years per doubling. The pure count has followed Moore's 1975 prediction well, although the B200 is a large package of two big dies, so a single die is a bit smaller.
Now Dennard's arithmetic for one generation, with k = √2 ≈ 1.41, so lengths shrink to 0.7. Area per transistor is 0.7 × 0.7 = 0.49, so 1 / 0.49 ≈ 2.04 times as many transistors fit in a square millimetre. Capacitance is 0.7. Voltage is 0.7, so V² = 0.49. Frequency rises by 1 / 0.7 ≈ 1.43. Power per transistor = 0.7 × 0.49 × 1.43 ≈ 0.49. With 2.04 times as many transistors, the power density is 0.49 × 2.04 ≈ 1.0. The chip is the same temperature but does 2.04 × 1.43 ≈ 2.9 times as many switching operations per second.
Now the same step after Dennard, with voltage stuck at 1 V and the clock held fixed at 4 GHz to keep power under control. Power per transistor = 0.7 × 1 × 1 = 0.7. With 2.04 times as many transistors, power density is 0.7 × 2.04 ≈ 1.43 times higher. Suppose the chip is allowed 100 W. After one generation, if you use all the new transistors at full activity, you need 143 W. To stay at 100 W, only 1 / 1.43 = 70% of the transistors may be active. After five generations the active share is 1 / 1.43⁵ ≈ 17%. The numbers are simplified, but the shape is real: transistors are cheap, power is not.
intuition: A city that can keep adding houses on the same land, but whose power station and cooling pipes stopped getting bigger: you can still build, but you can no longer switch every house on at once.
Moore's law is a statement about how many switches you can print for a given cost; Dennard scaling is a statement about how much heat each switch makes. For thirty years both moved together, so a bigger count of switches was also a faster and equally cool chip. This was a special coincidence: voltage could fall at the same rate as size because the threshold voltage could fall too. Thermodynamics then put a floor under the threshold voltage. At room temperature thermal noise lets some electrons cross even a closed gate, so an "off" transistor leaks, and the leak grows exponentially as you lower the threshold. Once the floor was reached, heat per switch stopped falling at the rate needed. The same lithography still printed more transistors, but the power plant could not feed them. The natural response was to spend extra transistors on many slower units, memory, and circuits that do one task efficiently.
mistakes: People often think Moore's law is a law of physics, or that it says computers double in speed every two years. In fact it is an observation about the economics of manufacturing: the number of components at the lowest cost per component. It says nothing directly about speed. Speed doubling came mostly from Dennard scaling, and that ended around 2005.
People often think Moore's law is dead because clock speeds stopped. In fact clock speed and transistor count are separate trends. Transistor density still increases with every new node, though more slowly, and the doubling period for the whole industry is now closer to three years than two. What has clearly weakened is the cost trend: cost per transistor has stopped falling at the leading edge, and at some nodes it has risen.
People often think that the node name (3 nm, 2 nm) is the size of a transistor. In fact the names stopped matching any physical length around the late 1990s. A "3 nm" process has gate lengths and metal pitches of tens of nanometres. Names are marketing, and numbers from different companies are not comparable. Compare real measures such as transistors per square millimetre, SRAM cell area or measured performance per watt.
People often think multicore chips were chosen because they are better. In fact they were the best response to a power limit; a faster single core would have been preferred if it could be cooled. Multicore moved the burden to software, and many programs cannot use more than a few cores because of the serial part in Amdahl's law.
friction: The first limit is money. Rock's law says the cost of a leading fab grows by a factor of two every few years, and a single modern fab costs tens of billions of dollars. EUV machines cost well over 150 million dollars each, and the newer high-NA EUV tools cost roughly double that. Only three companies (TSMC, Samsung, Intel) still try to make logic at the leading edge, and only TSMC does it at very large volume. Because design and wafer costs keep rising, only high-volume products such as GPUs and phone processors can afford the newest nodes.
The second limit is heat. Even with many cores, a modern server CPU is rated at 300 to 500 watts and an AI accelerator at 700 to 1,000 watts or more. Energy efficiency (computations per joule) used to double about every 1.6 years, a trend named after Jonathan Koomey, and after 2000 it slowed to something nearer 2.5 to 3 years. Moving data costs more energy than computing on it: reading a value from off-chip memory costs on the order of a hundred times the energy of a floating-point operation, which is why caches, HBM memory and on-chip data reuse dominate accelerator design.
The third limit is atoms. Silicon atoms are about 0.5 nanometres apart. Today's critical dimensions are tens of nanometres, so a transistor is a few hundred atoms across in places, and variation between devices, tunnelling and interconnect delay all grow. Thin wires have high resistance, so wires are now a main source of delay and energy.
practice: Treat hardware as improving slowly, unevenly and at a price. Do not plan a system on the assumption that next year's CPU will make a slow single-threaded design fast. Plan for parallelism and efficiency: partition data, keep hot loops cache-friendly, and measure cost per request and cost per query instead of raw speed.
When you size infrastructure, use performance per dollar and performance per watt, not clock speed. Newer cloud instance families (for example Arm-based Graviton or Axion-class CPUs) are often advertised as giving up to 20 to 40 percent better price-performance than the previous generation, which is a post-Dennard gain from design and efficiency, not from clock speed. Test your own workload.
Match the workload to the hardware that spends its transistors well. Dense matrix and vector work belongs on GPUs or TPUs. Branchy, latency-sensitive logic belongs on a few fast cores. Scan-heavy analytics, as in Spark or Databricks, is limited by memory bandwidth and I/O, so columnar formats, compression and fewer bytes moved give more than more cores. Where the same job can be done in half the CPU time, the saving now translates directly into cloud cost.
To study this topic, read chapter 1 of Hennessy and Patterson's "Computer Architecture: A Quantitative Approach" (technology trends and the power wall), Sutter's essay and Bohr's 2007 retrospective. For current status, follow the IEEE IRDS roadmap reports and papers from ISSCC and IEDM.
tradeoffs: Moore's law as a density trend versus as a cost trend. Choose the density version (transistors per square millimetre) when you are comparing process technologies or predicting how large a design can be; it is still growing at roughly 1.3 to 1.8 times per full node generation, depending on how much of the chip is SRAM and analog. Choose the cost version (cost per transistor, or per unit of performance) when you are planning budgets and product plans, because wafer prices have risen faster than density at the newest nodes. Either way, one curve hides that logic, SRAM and I/O scale at different rates.
Dennard model versus the post-Dennard model. The Dennard model (voltage and capacitance scale together, power density constant) describes chips well from about 1975 to 2004 and is the right tool for reading old performance history. The post-Dennard model (voltage roughly fixed, power density grows about 1.4 times per generation if all transistors are used) describes anything built since about 2006. Use the second for any forecast. It also breaks down: it ignores lower activity factors, leakage-cutting FinFET and gate-all-around devices, and slow voltage scaling (from 1.0 V to about 0.7 V), so it is a pessimistic bound on heat, not a prediction.
More cores versus faster cores versus specialised hardware. Choose more general cores when the work is parallel and the serial fraction is below about 5%; Amdahl's law then still gives roughly 10 to 15 times on 32 to 64 cores. Choose a few fast cores when the work is latency-bound or has long serial chains, because Pollack's rule means doubling a core's area gives only about 1.4 times the speed, but it is the only way to speed one thread. Choose specialised accelerators (GPU, TPU, video or crypto blocks, network cards) when the workload is stable and large: they can be 10 to 100 times more efficient per joule, but you pay with narrow use and harder programming.
Waiting for hardware versus optimising software. Today a new CPU generation typically adds 10 to 30 percent of single-thread speed per year, not 40 to 50 percent as in the 1990s. Choose optimisation when your code runs at high volume, because a 2x saving in CPU time is available now and grows with scale. Choose to buy larger instances when engineer time costs more than the compute.
Extrapolating the exponential versus expecting an S-curve. A straight line on a log chart is a good short-term guide for density: use it for 3 to 5 years, while the roadmap (EUV, high-NA EUV, gate-all-around, backside power, 3D stacking) is known. Beyond that, expect an S-curve, because atomic size, wire resistance and cost put limits on each path. Stacking chips in 3D and packaging many dies can continue the trend, but they raise cost, so do not assume the old rate of improvement per dollar.

It stopped (about 2005) because the threshold voltage could not drop without exponentially higher leakage current, so supply voltage stopped falling near 1 V.

Your batch pipeline is 90% parallel. Your manager suggests buying a 64-core machine instead of the 16-core one. What do you tell them? || Amdahl's law: speed-up on 16 cores is 1 / (0.10 + 0.90/16) ≈ 6.4, and on 64 cores 1 / (0.10 + 0.90/64) ≈ 8.8. Four times the cores gives only 38% more speed, and the limit is 10 times. Reduce the serial 10% first.
