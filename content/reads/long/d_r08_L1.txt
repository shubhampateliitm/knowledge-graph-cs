value: Computer vision is the field of making machines extract meaning from images and video. The tasks range from simple to hard. Classification says what is in a picture. Detection says what is where, with a box around each object. Segmentation labels every pixel. Other tasks read text (OCR), estimate depth and motion, track objects over time, recognize faces, match a frame against a reference library, or generate new images. The input is a grid of numbers. A 224 × 224 colour image is 150,528 numbers between 0 and 255, and nothing in those numbers says "cat". The whole field is about turning that grid into something useful.
It matters because so much of the world is captured by cameras. Medical scans, factory lines, cars, phones, satellites and every connected TV produce images all day. In your own domain, a smart TV can sample what is on the screen and match it against a catalogue to know which programme or ad was shown. That is a vision problem too, although it is usually solved with compact fingerprints rather than neural networks. Photo search, content moderation, document extraction and checking that an ad creative matches its metadata are all vision tasks in everyday production systems.
For the layer above, this concept gives you working building blocks. You get pretrained backbones (ResNet, ViT) that turn any image into a vector of features, detectors and segmenters that locate things, and aligned image–text models (CLIP) that let you search images with words. Higher layers such as PyTorch and TensorFlow ship these models as ready-made libraries. The layers below make it work. Convolution (L2) builds in the fact that a pattern means the same thing wherever it appears. Self-attention (L2) lets distant parts of an image influence each other. Contrastive learning (L2) gives the models labels for free from image–text pairs. Adversarial examples (L2) are the reminder that these models see differently from people and can fail in ways people would not.
terms: Pixel — one cell of an image grid; a colour pixel has three numbers (red, green, blue), usually 0 to 255.
Backbone — the part of a vision network that turns an image into feature maps or a feature vector; heads for specific tasks sit on top.
Feature map — a grid of numbers produced by one layer, where each position says how strongly a learned pattern appears at that place.
Receptive field — the region of the input image that can influence one value in a feature map.
Patch (token) — in a vision transformer, a small square of the image (for example 16 × 16 pixels) treated like one word.
Bounding box — a rectangle (x, y, width, height) that marks where an object is.
IoU (intersection over union) — the overlap area of two regions divided by their combined area; used to decide whether a predicted box matches the true box.
Transfer learning — starting from a network trained on a large dataset and fine-tuning it on a smaller one.
Data augmentation — creating extra training images by flipping, cropping, colour-shifting or blurring existing ones.
history: Computer vision began with optimism. In summer 1966, Seymour Papert at MIT wrote a memo for a "Summer Vision Project" that planned to link a camera to a computer and have it identify objects in a scene, as a task for a few students. It took decades. Early systems worked with edges and simple shapes. David Marr's book "Vision" (1982) gave a framework from edges to surfaces to 3D objects. In 1959–1962, Hubel and Wiesel had shown that neurons in cat visual cortex respond to oriented edges in small regions. Fukushima's Neocognitron (1980) turned that into a layered artificial model, and Yann LeCun's LeNet (1989 onward) trained such a convolutional network with backpropagation to read handwritten digits for the postal service and banks.
From the late 1990s, engineers designed features by hand. SIFT (David Lowe, 1999) described local patches so that they matched across scale and rotation. Viola and Jones (2001) detected faces in real time with cascades of simple filters. HOG (Dalal and Triggs, 2005) described shapes by gradient directions. A linear classifier or SVM sat on top. Progress was steady but slow, and every new task needed new feature design.
Two things changed that. Fei-Fei Li's group built ImageNet (2009), eventually millions of labelled images, and the ILSVRC challenge used 1.2 million training images over 1,000 classes. Graphics processors (GPUs) became programmable. In 2012, AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton) cut the top-5 error from about 26% to about 15%, trained on two GPUs. After this, deeper networks arrived quickly: VGG and GoogLeNet (2014) and ResNet (2015), whose skip connections allowed 152 layers and passed human-level top-5 accuracy as measured on ImageNet. Detection (R-CNN 2014, Faster R-CNN 2015, YOLO 2015, Mask R-CNN 2017) and segmentation (U-Net 2015) followed. Vision Transformers (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020) showed that plain attention over image patches matches CNNs when pretrained on enough data. CLIP (2021) trained on 400 million image–text pairs, and Segment Anything (2023) released a promptable segmenter trained on over a billion masks. Today, large multimodal language models also read images directly.
how: A modern vision system has a backbone, a head and a training recipe. The backbone converts pixels into features. The head turns features into the answer you need. The recipe decides what data and loss teach the network.
The CNN backbone is built on convolution. A convolution layer slides a small filter, for example 3 × 3 pixels across all input channels, over the image and computes a weighted sum at every position. Two properties make this efficient. The same filter weights are reused at every position (weight sharing), so a "vertical edge" detector does not have to be learned separately for each location. And each output looks only at a small neighbourhood (locality). Compare a fully connected layer from a 224 × 224 × 3 image to an output of the same width and 64 channels: it would need about 4.8 × 10¹¹ weights. A 3 × 3 convolution from 64 to 64 channels needs 3 × 3 × 64 × 64 = 36,864. Stacking layers grows the receptive field: early layers see edges and colours, middle layers see textures and parts, late layers see whole objects. Pooling or strided convolution halves the resolution while doubling the channels, so the network gradually trades "where" for "what". ResNet adds a skip connection, output = F(x) + x, so each block only has to learn a correction and gradients flow straight back through the sum. That is what made 50 to 152 layers trainable.
The Vision Transformer takes a different route, built on self-attention. It cuts the image into patches (16 × 16 pixels gives 14 × 14 = 196 patches for a 224-pixel image), flattens each patch, maps it through a linear layer to a vector (the embedding), adds a position embedding, and feeds the sequence of 196 tokens, plus a special class token, into a standard transformer. In every layer, each patch attends to all others, so a patch at the left can influence one at the right already in layer one. A CNN needs many layers to build such long-range links. The price is that attention compares every pair of tokens, so cost grows with the square of the token count: 196 tokens mean about 38,000 pairs per head and layer, but a 1,024 × 1,024 image with the same patch size has 4,096 tokens and about 16.8 million pairs. ViTs have fewer built-in assumptions than CNNs, so they need more data or strong self-supervised pretraining, but then they scale better. Hybrid designs (Swin, ConvNeXt) bring locality back.
Heads define the task. For classification, a linear layer on the pooled feature gives class scores trained with cross-entropy. For detection, Faster R-CNN proposes regions and then classifies and refines them. YOLO-style detectors predict boxes and classes for a grid in a single pass, which is fast enough for video. DETR (2020) uses a transformer decoder that outputs a set of objects directly. For segmentation, U-Net and Mask R-CNN produce a label per pixel using an encoder that downsamples and a decoder that upsamples with skip links to keep fine detail. Detection quality is judged by IoU: a predicted box counts as correct if IoU with the true box is above a threshold such as 0.5, and the mean average precision (mAP) summarises precision across thresholds and classes.
Training uses labels, or avoids them. Supervised training needs a label per image or per box, which is expensive. Contrastive learning, the base of CLIP, avoids this: an image encoder and a text encoder are trained so that the embedding of a photo is close to the embedding of its caption and far from other captions in the batch. The result can classify new categories by comparing an image with text such as "a photo of a forklift" (zero-shot). Self-supervised methods (DINO, masked autoencoders that hide 75% of patches and reconstruct them) learn features from unlabeled images. Data augmentation (random crops, flips, colour jitter) teaches invariances. Transfer learning then reuses a pretrained backbone, so a few thousand labelled images can be enough for a new task.
Finally, the adversarial-examples concept sets a limit on what the features mean. Tiny, carefully chosen pixel changes, invisible to a person, can flip a classifier's answer, because the network relies on features that correlate with labels but that are not what humans use. This is why vision systems used in security or safety settings need testing beyond average accuracy.

example: Suppose you want to detect whether a retail ad creative contains a product logo, using only 2,000 labelled images. Training a deep network from scratch on 2,000 images would overfit badly. So you do the following.
Step 1. Take a ResNet-50 pretrained on ImageNet. It has about 25.6 million parameters and needs roughly 4 billion multiply-adds for one 224 × 224 image. Remove its final 1,000-class layer.
Step 2. Add a new linear layer with 2 outputs (logo, no logo). Freeze the backbone and train only this layer for a few epochs. With 2,048 features per image, the new layer has 2,048 × 2 + 2 = 4,098 parameters, so 2,000 images are plenty. Say validation accuracy reaches 91%.
Step 3. Unfreeze the last block of the backbone and continue training with a learning rate about 10 to 100 times smaller than for the new layer, so that the pretrained features change gently. Use flips, crops and colour jitter as augmentation. Accuracy reaches 95%.
Step 4. Now look at errors by slice, not just the average. Suppose the 5% of errors are concentrated in one case: logos printed small, under 40 pixels wide, in the corner. A 224-pixel resize shrinks such a logo to a few pixels. The fix is not a bigger model. You tile the image into overlapping 448 × 448 crops, or you switch to a detector that works at higher resolution, and add 300 labelled examples of small logos. Accuracy on that slice moves from 60% to 85%.
Step 5. Check cost. Detection at 448 pixels costs about 4× the compute of 224 (area grows by 4), roughly 16 billion multiply-adds per crop. At 10 creatives per second with 4 crops each, that is about 640 billion multiply-adds per second, which one modern GPU handles with large margin. Now the same method on 30 frames per second of video would be 30× more, so you sample one frame per second and check only when the scene changes.
The example shows the usual pattern: pretrained backbone, small head, careful per-slice evaluation, and a resolution and cost decision driven by the object size.
intuition: A vision network is a staircase of "what looks like what", from edges to textures to parts to objects, learned from examples instead of written by hand.
The design is natural because images have structure. Nearby pixels belong together, and the same object can appear anywhere, at any size. Convolution builds exactly these two facts into the weights, so the network does not need to rediscover them for every position. Attention relaxes the locality assumption and lets data decide which parts relate. Both work because there is a lot of regularity in the visual world to learn, and a pretrained model has already learned most of it. You are mostly teaching it what is special about your task.
mistakes: People often think a high ImageNet accuracy means the model understands the scene. In fact, networks often rely on texture, background and context. Geirhos and colleagues (2019) showed that ImageNet-trained CNNs lean on texture more than on shape, and a cow on a beach can be misclassified because cows were mostly seen on grass. Accuracy on the benchmark does not guarantee the same accuracy on your cameras.
People often think vision transformers have replaced CNNs. In fact, both are used. CNNs and hybrids are still common on devices with small compute budgets and in real-time detection, because convolution is cheap and works well with less data. ViTs dominate where there is a lot of pretraining data and compute, and in the vision encoders of multimodal models.
People often think more data always fixes bias. In fact, what matters is whether the data covers the cases that you serve. The Gender Shades study (Buolamwini and Gebru, 2018) found commercial face-analysis error rates of up to about 35% for darker-skinned women against under 1% for lighter-skinned men, because training and test sets were unbalanced. Averages hid this. You must measure per group.
People often think a detector's confidence score is a probability that it is right. In fact, deep networks are often over-confident and the scores need calibration on your own data. Choosing a threshold should be done from a precision–recall curve on a validation set, not at 0.5 by habit.
People often think pixel-perfect input is needed for a good result. In fact, JPEG compression, resizing method and colour space conversions change predictions measurably. Use in production the same preprocessing as in training.
friction: Labels are the main cost. Segmentation masks can take minutes per image, and rare cases (the defect that occurs once in 10,000 parts) are hard to collect. Self-supervised and foundation models reduce this, but you still need a labelled test set, because you cannot trust a model you cannot measure.
Distribution shift is the main failure. A model trained in daylight may fail at night, with a different camera, a new compression level or a new TV panel. Recht and colleagues (2019) rebuilt the CIFAR-10 and ImageNet test sets following the original process, and models lost accuracy on the new samples. Robustness benchmarks such as ImageNet-C (corruptions) show the same sensitivity. Adversarial perturbations are an extreme form: small, targeted changes, and even physical stickers on a stop sign, can force a wrong output.
Compute and latency are real constraints. Video multiplies cost by the frame rate; 30 frames per second is 108,000 frames per hour per stream. Attention cost grows quadratically with patch count, so high resolution is expensive. On-device models must fit in memory and power budgets, which pushes to quantization and smaller backbones.
Finally there are social and legal problems: face recognition accuracy differences across groups, privacy and consent for camera data, copyright of training images, and the gap between automatic metrics and what a user would call correct. Evaluation of generated or described images is still partly human work.
practice: Start with a pretrained backbone and a small head, and measure before you scale. Pick the backbone by deployment: a ResNet or EfficientNet-class model or a small ViT for edge and high throughput, a larger ViT or a foundation model when accuracy matters and you have GPUs. Fine-tune with a lower learning rate for the backbone than for the head, and use augmentation that matches the real world (blur, compression, lighting), not random effects that never occur.
Collect data from the real capture conditions, label edge cases deliberately, and keep a frozen test set that never touches training. Report metrics per slice: camera, location, time of day, object size. For detection, tune the confidence and IoU thresholds on a validation set, and track both false positives and misses, because their costs differ.
Keep preprocessing identical between training and serving, and test it with the same image files in both. Control resolution and frame sampling to control cost: sample frames, crop to regions of interest, and batch requests. Export to ONNX or TensorRT, or use quantization to INT8, when latency matters, and check the accuracy after export.
Use zero-shot models such as CLIP for fast prototypes and for searching your own image library, but verify against labelled examples before trusting them. After launch, monitor input statistics and prediction distributions to detect drift, keep a sample of low-confidence cases for review, and plan periodic retraining. For anything safety related, add tests with corrupted and adversarial inputs, and keep a human fallback.
tradeoffs: Choose a CNN or hybrid backbone when data is limited (tens of thousands of images), when you deploy on phones, edge boxes or CPUs, or when you need high frame rates at high resolution. Convolution cost grows linearly with pixels, and its locality is a helpful prior. Choose a Vision Transformer when you can start from a strong pretrained checkpoint, when you have large data or compute, or when you need to feed a language model, since most multimodal models use ViT-style encoders. You give up cheap high-resolution processing and some data efficiency, and gain scaling and flexibility.
Choose a pretrained foundation model (CLIP, DINOv2, SAM) with a light head when you have under a few thousand labels or many changing categories, and accept that it may be larger and slower than needed. Choose a dedicated small model trained on your data when volume is high and the task is fixed, for example a detector for 3 object types on every frame of many streams. The small model can be 10 to 100 times cheaper per image, at the price of labelling and maintenance work.
Choose a one-stage detector (YOLO family) when speed matters, for example real-time video, and a two-stage or transformer detector when small objects and best accuracy matter more than a few milliseconds. Choose a segmentation model rather than boxes only when the exact shape drives the decision (area measurement, medical contours, background removal), because masks cost much more to label.
Choose a multimodal language model that reads images when the task is open-ended (describe, extract fields from varied documents, answer questions), and a specialised vision model when the task is narrow, high-volume and must be cheap and predictable. A multimodal call may cost cents and take seconds; a specialised model costs a fraction of a cent and takes milliseconds. The LLM route needs far less labelled data and handles surprises better, but is harder to test exhaustively.
interview: Why does a convolution layer use far fewer parameters than a fully connected layer, and what assumption does that make? || It shares one small filter across all positions (weight sharing) and connects each output only to a local neighbourhood (locality). A 3 × 3 convolution from 64 to 64 channels has about 37,000 weights, whereas a fully connected layer over a 224 × 224 image would need hundreds of billions. The assumption is that patterns are local and mean the same everywhere (translation equivariance), which fits images but not all data.
A ViT works well on your large dataset but badly on a small one where a ResNet does fine. Why? || A ViT has little built-in structure; it has to learn locality and translation behaviour from data, which needs a lot of examples or strong pretraining. A CNN has these as built-in priors, so it is more data efficient. Remedies are a pretrained checkpoint, heavy augmentation, distillation from a CNN, or a hybrid.
Your detector has 94% mAP in testing, and complaints arrive from one region. How do you investigate? || Suspect distribution shift. Slice errors by camera, lighting, resolution, compression and object size, and compare the input statistics of that region with the training data. Check preprocessing parity between training and serving. Then add labelled examples from that region, retrain or fine-tune, and add the slice to the permanent evaluation set.
How would you cut the cost of running vision on 10,000 video streams? || Reduce the number of frames (sample, detect motion or scene change first), reduce resolution or crop to regions of interest, use a smaller or quantized model with batching on GPUs, and cascade: a cheap model filters, an expensive model checks only the uncertain cases. Measure accuracy at each step since each shortcut has its own failure cases.
Why is it risky to trust CLIP zero-shot classification in production without a test set? || Zero-shot accuracy depends on how well the text prompt matches how the concept is described in web data, and it is uneven across categories, languages and fine-grained classes. It can also be fooled by text written in the image. A small labelled test set tells you the real accuracy and the best prompt wording, and shows whether a linear probe or fine-tuning is worth the effort.
quiz: What are the two properties of convolution that reduce parameters? || Weight sharing (the same filter is used at every position) and locality (each output depends only on a small neighbourhood of the input).
Why does a ViT with 16 × 16 patches on a 224 × 224 image process 196 tokens, and how does the attention cost change if the image side doubles? || 224 / 16 = 14, and 14 × 14 = 196 patches. Doubling the side gives 4× the patches (784), and attention compares all pairs, so its cost grows about 16×, while the rest of the network grows 4×.
How does a ResNet skip connection help to train 100+ layers? || Each block computes F(x) + x, so it only learns a correction to its input. The gradient can flow straight through the addition to earlier layers, which avoids vanishing gradients and degradation in deeper networks.
A predicted box covers 60 square units, the true box covers 80, and their overlap is 48. What is the IoU, and is it a match at threshold 0.5? || The union is 60 + 80 − 48 = 92, so IoU = 48 / 92 ≈ 0.52. It is just above 0.5, so it counts as a match.
You have 3,000 labelled images and a fixed three-class task. Should you train a ViT from scratch? || No. Start from a pretrained backbone and fine-tune, because 3,000 images are far too few for a ViT to learn visual structure from scratch. A pretrained CNN or ViT with a new head will be more accurate and cheaper.

value: Natural language processing (NLP) is the field of getting computers to read, write and reason about human language. The tasks are everyday ones: classify a support ticket, find names and dates in a contract, translate a page, answer a question from a document, summarize a meeting, search by meaning, or hold a conversation. Language is hard for a computer because it is ambiguous ("I saw her duck"), depends on context ("bank" in a river story and a finance story), has no fixed rules, and is different in every language and community.
For about sixty years, the field's main problem was how to turn text, which is a string of symbols, into numbers that a model can compute with, and how to make those numbers capture meaning. The answer that won is: split text into pieces called tokens, map each token to a learned vector, and let a deep network, today a transformer, update those vectors using the whole context. The same network, trained to predict the next token on a very large amount of text, can then be adapted to almost every task. That is why tasks that once needed separate systems (spelling, tagging, parsing, translation, question answering) are now mostly handled by one family of models.
The layers below provide the pieces. Tokenization with byte-pair encoding (L2) gives a fixed vocabulary that can still represent any word, even a new one. Embeddings (L2) place tokens in a space where distance means similarity. Self-attention (L2) lets each token's representation depend on every other token in the sentence, which resolves the ambiguity. The layer above, such as PyTorch, TensorFlow and the Hugging Face Transformers library, packages these models so that an engineer can load a pretrained model in a few lines, and large language model products sit on top. For an engineer in ad tech, NLP shows up in contextual targeting, brand-safety checks of page and video text, search over catalogs and creative text, and extraction of entities and topics from content metadata.
terms: Token — a piece of text that the model treats as one unit; it can be a word, part of a word, or a single character or byte.
Tokenizer — the program that splits text into tokens and maps each to an integer ID from a fixed vocabulary.
Embedding — a learned vector (for example 768 numbers) for a token or a sentence, placed so that similar meanings are close.
Language model — a model that gives the probability of the next token given the tokens before it.
Contextual representation — a vector for a token that depends on the surrounding words, so "bank" gets different vectors in different sentences.
Pretraining — training on a very large amount of unlabeled text with a self-supervised objective such as predicting the next or a masked token.
Fine-tuning — continuing training on a smaller labelled dataset for one task.
Prompting — adapting a pretrained model by describing the task and giving examples in its input, without changing its weights.
Perplexity — a measure of how surprised a language model is by text; lower is better, and it is the exponential of the average negative log probability per token.
history: Machine translation was the first big NLP goal. The 1954 Georgetown–IBM demonstration translated 60 Russian sentences and led to over-optimistic promises. Joseph Weizenbaum's ELIZA (1966) imitated a therapist with pattern rules. Until the 1980s, systems were hand-written grammars and rules, which broke on every sentence the authors had not imagined.
From the late 1980s, statistics replaced rules. IBM researchers built statistical translation models (Brown et al., 1990 and 1993) learned from parallel text. N-gram language models, hidden Markov models for tagging, and conditional random fields (Lafferty et al., 2001) became standard. The Penn Treebank (1993) gave shared labelled data and BLEU (2002) gave a shared metric for translation. These systems needed careful feature engineering for each task.
Neural methods arrived in steps. Bengio et al. (2003) proposed a neural language model with learned word vectors. Mikolov et al.'s word2vec (2013) made training word vectors cheap and showed that directions in the space carry meaning (\( \text{king} - \text{man} + \text{woman} \approx \text{queen} \)). Sequence-to-sequence networks with LSTMs (Sutskever et al. and Cho et al., 2014) did translation end to end, and Bahdanau et al. (2014) added attention so that the decoder could look back at the source words. The Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) removed recurrence and used attention alone, which made training parallel and scalable.
Then pretraining took over. ELMo (2018) gave contextual vectors, GPT (2018) pretrained a decoder, and BERT (Devlin et al., October 2018) pretrained a bidirectional encoder by masking tokens and set records on many benchmarks at once. GPT-2 (2019) and GPT-3 (2020, 175 billion parameters) showed that larger decoders could do new tasks from a few examples in the prompt. Instruction tuning and reinforcement learning from human feedback (InstructGPT, 2022) made models follow requests, and ChatGPT (November 2022) brought them to the public. Reasoning models, retrieval-augmented generation and tool use are the current extensions.
how: Every modern NLP system follows the same path from text to prediction: tokenize, embed, contextualize, and decode or classify.
Step one is tokenization, built on byte-pair encoding (BPE). A pure word vocabulary is too large and cannot handle new words, and a pure character vocabulary makes sequences very long. BPE starts with single characters (or bytes) and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair in a training corpus into a new symbol. After tens of thousands of merges, common words are one token and rare words split into pieces, for example "unbelievably" may become "un", "believ", "ably". Because bytes are the base, any string can be represented. Typical vocabularies are 32,000 to 256,000 tokens. English averages roughly 1.3 tokens per word. Other languages and scripts can need several times more tokens for the same meaning, because the corpus had less text in them, and this directly affects cost, speed and how much fits in the context window.
Step two is the embedding lookup. The embedding concept says that each token ID indexes a row in a matrix of size vocabulary × d (d is 768 in BERT-base and several thousand in large models). Rows are learned during training, so tokens used in similar contexts end up with similar vectors. A position signal is added, since the next step otherwise ignores order. Static embeddings such as word2vec give one vector per word. Modern models only use the lookup as a starting point.
Step three is contextualization with self-attention. In each transformer layer, every token creates a query, a key and a value vector. It compares its query with the keys of all tokens, turns the scores into weights with a softmax, and takes a weighted sum of the values. A token such as "bank" thus pulls in information from "river" or "loan", and its vector changes. After a dozen or a hundred such layers, the vector for each token holds a representation of its meaning in this sentence, including grammar and, to some extent, world knowledge. Attention is also what lets the pronoun "it" in a sentence find the noun it refers to, even 50 words away.
Step four is the head. Three architectures cover most uses. An encoder-only model (BERT style) reads the whole text in both directions and is used for classification, tagging, and embeddings for search; a linear layer on top of a token or the whole-sentence vector gives the label. A decoder-only model (GPT style) reads left to right and predicts a probability distribution over the next token; text is generated by sampling or choosing a token, appending it, and repeating. An encoder-decoder model (T5, original Transformer) encodes the input and decodes the output with cross-attention, and suits translation and summarization. Today, decoder-only models dominate because one next-token objective scales to huge data, and prompting covers all tasks.
Training has two stages. Pretraining minimises cross-entropy on predicting the next token (or masked tokens) over trillions of tokens; this is where the model learns grammar, facts and patterns. Then post-training teaches behaviour: supervised fine-tuning on instruction examples and preference optimisation. A task can be solved by prompting (describe it, give examples), by fine-tuning the weights on labelled data (for cost or quality on a narrow task), or by adding retrieval (find relevant documents with embedding search and put them in the prompt) so the answer is grounded in your data.
Evaluation is part of the mechanism. Classification uses accuracy, precision, recall and F1. Translation and summarization use overlap metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) that correlate only loosely with quality, so human review and LLM-based judges with careful rubrics are used. Language models are measured by perplexity on held-out text, which tells you about modelling, not about usefulness.
Generation has its own details. The model outputs a score for each vocabulary token, a softmax turns the scores into probabilities, and a decoding rule picks one. Greedy decoding always takes the most likely token and is repetitive. Sampling with a temperature (dividing the scores by T before the softmax) makes the choice more random when T is above 1 and more deterministic when T is below 1. Top-p sampling keeps only the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to p, for example 0.9, which cuts off the unlikely tail. To avoid recomputing attention over the whole prefix at every step, servers keep the keys and values of past tokens in a KV cache, so each new token costs one pass over the new position only. This is why the first token of an answer is slow (the whole prompt is processed) and later tokens arrive at a steady rate.
Training signal matters too. The next-token loss is cross-entropy: the model is penalised by the negative log of the probability it gave to the true next token. Over a sentence, these penalties add up to the log of the probability of the whole text, which is why perplexity (the exponential of the average penalty) measures how well the model predicts language. A perplexity of 10 means the model is, on average, as unsure as if it were choosing among 10 equally likely tokens.

example: Here is a worked task. A video platform wants to tag each of 1 million page descriptions per day with one of 20 topic categories, to use in contextual targeting. Each description has about 300 tokens.
Option A, prompt a large language model. Each request contains an instruction and some examples (say 500 tokens) plus the 300-token text, so 800 input tokens. Per day that is 800 million input tokens. If the price is $0.25 per million input tokens (an assumed price for illustration; real prices vary a lot between models and providers), the cost is 800 × 0.25 = $200 per day, about $6,000 per month. You can start the same afternoon, and it handles new categories by editing the prompt. Accuracy might be 88% on your labelled sample.
Option B, fine-tune a small encoder such as a BERT-base model (110 million parameters). You label 5,000 examples, which takes a few days. A forward pass costs about 2 × parameters × tokens = 2 × 110 million × 300 = 66 billion floating-point operations per description. For 1 million descriptions this is 6.6 × 10¹⁶ operations. A GPU that sustains 10¹⁴ operations per second in practice does this in about 660 seconds, which is well under an hour of GPU time per day, costing a few dollars. Accuracy on the same sample might be 91%, since the model is trained on exactly your categories.
Option C, combine both. Use the large model to label 50,000 more examples cheaply (about 50,000 × 800 = 40 million tokens, which is $10), have people check a sample of those labels, then train the small model on the larger set. You now have the quality of the big model's knowledge at the running cost of the small one.
The decision rests on numbers you can compute: tokens per item, price per token, labels available, and accuracy on a held-out sample. Check also the tokenization cost for non-English pages: if 30% of descriptions are in Hindi and need three times as many tokens, Option A's cost grows by about 60% (0.7 × 1 + 0.3 × 3 = 1.6).
intuition: Language is a pattern of usage, so a model that has read enough text to predict the next word has had to learn a good deal about grammar, facts and intent.
The idea is natural once you see prediction as a teacher. To guess the next word well in "The capital of France is ___", you need a fact. To guess it in "She dropped the glass, and it ___", you need physics and grammar. No one has to label these skills, since the text itself supplies the answer for free, at the scale of the internet. Tokens and embeddings make text computable, attention lets context decide meaning, and the rest is scale: more data, more parameters, more compute.
mistakes: People often think NLP models understand language as people do. In fact they model statistical patterns of text. They often act as if they understand, and they can still fail on simple counting, negation or facts they have not seen. Treat fluent output as a draft to verify, not as evidence of knowledge.
People often think a token is a word. In fact a token is a subword piece, and the split differs per model. "ChatGPT" may be 3 tokens, a long German compound word may be 6, and a Hindi or Thai sentence can use several times more tokens than its English translation. Prices, limits and speed are counted in tokens, so this matters for cost and for fairness across languages.
People often think bigger models are always needed. In fact, for a narrow, high-volume task, a fine-tuned model with 100 million parameters can match a model 1,000 times larger and costs a tiny fraction to run. Size buys breadth and ease of use, not necessarily accuracy on one task.
People often think a high BLEU or ROUGE score means a good translation or summary. In fact, these scores count word overlap with reference texts. A summary can have high overlap and still contain a wrong fact, or be correct and different in wording. Use them as rough signals and add human or carefully validated model-based review.
People often think retrieval and fine-tuning are alternatives for adding knowledge. In fact, they solve different problems. Retrieval supplies facts that change or that are private, at query time, with sources. Fine-tuning changes style, format and task skill, and is a poor way to store facts that must stay current.
friction: Ambiguity and context remain hard. Sarcasm, implied meaning, cultural references, long documents with distant dependencies, and questions that need multi-step reasoning still produce errors. Models also state wrong facts fluently (hallucination), because training rewards plausible text, not truth. Grounding with retrieval and citations reduces this but does not remove it.
Language coverage is uneven. Of the thousands of languages in the world, only a small number have large amounts of text. Low-resource languages, dialects, code-mixed text (Hindi and English in the same sentence) and non-standard spelling get lower accuracy and higher token costs. Tokenizers trained mostly on English text split other scripts into many small pieces.
Evaluation is difficult. Open-ended outputs have many valid answers, benchmarks leak into training data, and a number from a public benchmark often does not predict performance on your documents. Bias is learned from the text: models reproduce stereotypes and can treat names, dialects or groups differently. There are also privacy and copyright questions about training data, and security problems such as prompt injection, where text inside a document tells the model to ignore its instructions.
Operational limits include context length (the model sees only a limited window of tokens, and long inputs cost more and are used less reliably in the middle), latency (generation is one token at a time), and drift (a model version change can alter outputs that downstream code relied on).
practice: Start every NLP project with an evaluation set of real examples, 200 to 1,000 items, including edge cases: other languages, slang, very short and very long inputs, and the cases where a wrong answer is costly. Measure before you pick a method.
Try a strong general model with a clear prompt and a few examples first, because it is the fastest way to a baseline. Ask for structured output (JSON with a fixed schema) and validate it in code, rejecting and retrying malformed answers. Then decide on cost: compute tokens per item times items per day times price, and compare with the cost of a small fine-tuned model, which usually wins for stable, high-volume, narrow tasks. Use the big model to generate or check training labels, but have people verify a sample.
For search and similarity, use embedding models and a vector index, and combine them with keyword search (BM25), since exact terms like product codes are better matched by keywords. For answers about your own data, retrieve passages and put them in the prompt, ask the model to cite them, and test with questions whose answers are not in the data.
Plan for tokenization effects: measure token counts for each language you serve, truncate or chunk long texts on sentence boundaries, and keep prompts short. Pin model versions, log inputs and outputs, and rerun your evaluation set whenever you change the model or the prompt. Guard against prompt injection by treating all retrieved or user text as untrusted data, never as instructions, and limit what actions the model can trigger.
tradeoffs: Choose prompting a large general model when you have fewer than a few hundred labelled examples, the task changes often, or volume is low (under roughly tens of thousands of items per day). You pay more per item and accept some variance in outputs, but you ship in days. Choose fine-tuning a small model (encoder or small decoder) when the task is fixed, volume is high, you have at least a few thousand clean labels, and latency must be in milliseconds. You give up flexibility and must maintain training data and a retraining process.
Choose an encoder-only model (BERT-family) for classification, tagging and embedding search, where you need the whole text read at once, low cost and fast, predictable inference. Choose a decoder-only LLM when the output is free text, the task is described in words, or you want one model for many tasks. An encoder of 100 to 400 million parameters can serve thousands of requests per second per GPU; an LLM with billions of parameters serves a small fraction of that.
Choose retrieval-augmented generation when knowledge changes weekly, is private, or must be cited. Choose fine-tuning when you need a stable format, tone or domain skill. Choose long context (put whole documents in the prompt) when documents are few and moderate in size; the cost grows with every call, so for large corpora retrieval is cheaper. Retrieval adds a search system to maintain and can miss the right passage; long context pays tokens again on every request and loses accuracy on details buried in the middle.
Choose a multilingual model when your users write in several languages, and test each language separately. A single multilingual model is simpler to run, but may be weaker in a specific language than a model trained for it, and you pay more tokens for languages that tokenize poorly.
interview: Why do language models use subword tokens instead of words or characters? || Word vocabularies are huge and cannot represent new words (out-of-vocabulary). Character sequences are very long, which makes attention expensive and learning harder. Subwords (BPE) keep the vocabulary at 30,000 to 250,000 tokens, handle any string through bytes as a fallback, and keep common words as single tokens. The cost is uneven token counts across languages.
Explain what self-attention gives NLP that an LSTM did not. || In an LSTM, information passes through one hidden state step by step, so distant words are hard to connect and training cannot run in parallel over the sequence. Attention connects every pair of tokens directly in one layer and computes all positions in parallel, which makes long-range dependencies easier and training scalable. The cost is quadratic compute in sequence length.
You must classify 50 million short texts per day. How do you decide between an LLM API and a fine-tuned model? || Compute cost per item for both: tokens times price for the API, GPU time for the fine-tuned model. Compare accuracy on a held-out labelled set, and consider latency and category stability. At this volume a fine-tuned small model almost always wins on cost, possibly trained on labels produced by the LLM and checked by people. Keep the LLM for rare, hard cases through a cascade.
Your RAG system gives fluent but wrong answers. How do you find the cause? || Separate retrieval from generation. Check whether the right passage was retrieved (recall at k on questions with known sources). If not, fix chunking, the embedding model, hybrid search or the query rewriting. If it was retrieved but ignored or misread, change the prompt, reduce irrelevant context, ask for quotes, and measure faithfulness. Add questions with no answer in the data to test refusal.
Why can a model score well on a public benchmark and fail on your data? || The benchmark may have leaked into training data, may differ in domain, language and length, and measures average behaviour while you care about specific slices and costly errors. Only an evaluation set built from your real inputs measures what matters to you.
quiz: What does BPE do, and why is it used? || It builds a vocabulary by repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent symbol pair, starting from characters or bytes. This gives a fixed-size vocabulary where common words are single tokens and rare or new words split into pieces, so nothing is out of vocabulary.
Why does attention help a model resolve the word "bank" in different sentences? || The query of "bank" is compared with the keys of every other token, and the resulting weights mix in the values of words such as "river" or "loan". The output vector therefore depends on the context, so the same word gets different representations.
When would you choose an encoder-only model over a decoder-only LLM? || For classification, tagging or embedding search at high volume with low latency and low cost, where the whole input is read at once and the output is a label or a vector rather than free text.
A BERT-base model has 110 million parameters and a text has 300 tokens. About how many operations does one forward pass need? || About 2 × 110 million × 300 = 66 billion floating-point operations, using the rule of two operations per parameter per token.
A team wants the model to know this week's product catalogue. Fine-tune or retrieve? || Retrieve. The catalogue changes weekly, and retrieval gives current facts with sources at query time. Fine-tuning is slow to update and stores facts poorly; use it only for format or style.

@@ diffusion
value: A diffusion model generates data, such as an image, audio, video or a protein structure, by starting from pure random noise and removing the noise a little at a time until a clean sample appears. The model is never asked to draw a picture in one shot, which is a very hard problem. It is asked a much easier one many times: given this slightly noisy picture, what does the noise look like? A neural network trained for that simple question, applied 20 to 1,000 times in a loop, turns static into a photograph.
This approach solved the main problems of earlier image generators. GANs produced sharp images but trained unstably and often covered only part of the data (mode collapse). Variational autoencoders trained stably but made blurry images. Autoregressive pixel models were slow and hard to scale. Diffusion training is a plain regression problem with a stable loss, it covers the whole data distribution, and quality improves with more compute and data. It also accepts guidance: a text prompt, a depth map or a reference image can steer every step. Since 2021 this has made diffusion the standard for image generation (DALL-E 2, Imagen, Stable Diffusion, Midjourney) and video generation, and it is used in science, for example in protein structure prediction.
The layers below supply each part. The denoising objective (L2) is the training task. Gradient descent (L2) trains the network on that regression loss. Self-attention (L2) lets the network relate distant image regions and, through cross-attention, the words of the prompt. Variational autoencoders (L2) compress images into a small latent space where diffusion becomes much cheaper. Normalizing flows (L2) and the idea of continuous transformation between noise and data gave the newer flow-matching form. The layer above, Stable Diffusion (L0) and similar products, packages these parts into tools that people use.
terms: Forward process — the fixed procedure that adds a little Gaussian noise to a clean sample at each of T steps until only noise remains.
Reverse process — the learned procedure that removes noise step by step, going from pure noise back to a sample.
Noise schedule — the list of numbers \( (\beta_1, \dots, \beta_T) \) that sets how much noise is added at each step.
Denoising network — the neural network (a U-Net or a transformer) that takes a noisy sample and the step number and predicts the noise.
ε-prediction — training the network to output the noise ε that was added, rather than the clean sample.
Classifier-free guidance — a way to make samples follow the prompt more closely by extrapolating from the unconditional prediction towards the conditional one.
Latent diffusion — running diffusion in the compressed latent space of an autoencoder instead of in pixel space.
Sampler (solver) — the numerical method that turns the network's predictions into the next, less noisy sample; DDIM and DPM-Solver are examples.
Flow matching — a training method that learns a velocity field moving noise to data along near-straight paths.
history: The idea came from physics. Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan and Ganguli (2015) proposed "Deep Unsupervised Learning using Nonequilibrium Thermodynamics": destroy structure in data with a slow diffusion process and learn to reverse it. It worked on small images and got little attention. Meanwhile Yang Song and Stefano Ermon (2019) developed score-based models, which learn the gradient of the log data density (the "score") at several noise levels and sample by following it with Langevin dynamics.
Jonathan Ho, Ajay Jain and Pieter Abbeel's "Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models" (2020) joined the two lines. They showed that a simple loss, mean-squared error on the predicted noise, gives high-quality images, with T = 1,000 steps and a U-Net network. Song, Meng and Ermon's DDIM (2020) made sampling deterministic and cut the steps to about 20 to 50. Song and colleagues (2021) unified these methods as stochastic differential equations. Dhariwal and Nichol (2021) titled their paper "Diffusion Models Beat GANs on Image Synthesis". Ho and Salimans (2022) introduced classifier-free guidance, which is what makes text prompts work well.
Cost was the next barrier, since pixel-space diffusion at high resolution is slow. Rombach and colleagues (2022) ran diffusion in an autoencoder's latent space, and Stability AI with partners released Stable Diffusion in August 2022 as open weights, which started a large ecosystem. DALL-E 2 and Imagen appeared in spring 2022. Peebles and Xie's Diffusion Transformers (DiT, 2022) replaced the U-Net with a transformer, and flow matching and rectified flow (Lipman et al., Liu et al., 2022) gave straighter paths and fewer steps; Stable Diffusion 3 (2024) combined both. Video models such as Sora (announced February 2024) apply the same ideas to space–time patches. Consistency models (Song et al., 2023) and distillation reduce sampling to 1 to 4 steps.
how: Training. Take a clean sample \( x_0 \), for example an image. Pick a random step t between 1 and T. Draw random Gaussian noise \( \varepsilon \). Build the noisy version in one step with a closed formula:
\[ x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon \]
Here \( \bar\alpha_t \) is a number between 1 and 0 that is read from the noise schedule: near 1 for small t (almost clean) and near 0 for large t (almost pure noise). Feed \( x_t \) and \( t \) to the network and ask it to output \( \hat\varepsilon \), its guess of the noise. The loss is the mean-squared error between \( \varepsilon \) and \( \hat\varepsilon \). This is the denoising objective. It is ordinary regression, trained with gradient descent (Adam), and a single random t per example is enough, so training is stable and parallel. No adversary, no special balancing.
Why is predicting noise equivalent to generating? If the network knows the noise in \( x_t \), then it can estimate the clean sample: \( \hat x_0 = \big(x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\hat\varepsilon\big) / \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \). More precisely, the noise prediction points towards the data. The score-based view says the same thing: \( \hat\varepsilon \) is proportional to the negative score, the direction in which the sample becomes more likely under the data distribution at that noise level. So repeated denoising follows the gradient of the data density from noise toward real-looking samples.
Sampling. Start from \( x_T \) drawn from pure noise \( \mathcal{N}(0, I) \). For t from T down to 1, run the network to get \( \hat\varepsilon \) and compute the next sample. The original DDPM rule is \( x_{t-1} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \left( x_t - \dfrac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \hat\varepsilon \right) + \sigma_t z \), with \( z \) fresh noise and \( \alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t \). A small random z is added in each step in DDPM, but DDIM and other solvers drop it and use larger steps along the same path, so 20 to 50 steps are enough. Treating generation as solving an ordinary differential equation is what allows good solvers (DPM-Solver, Heun) to reduce steps further.
Conditioning and guidance. For text-to-image, a text encoder (CLIP or a language model) turns the prompt into a sequence of vectors. The network receives them through cross-attention layers: image features are the queries, and the text vectors are keys and values, so every region can look up the words that matter to it. Self-attention layers inside the network relate far-apart regions of the image. During training, the prompt is dropped in about 10% of examples, so the same network learns both conditional and unconditional prediction. At sampling time, classifier-free guidance combines them: \( \tilde\varepsilon = \varepsilon_{\text{uncond}} + w\,(\varepsilon_{\text{cond}} - \varepsilon_{\text{uncond}}) \). A guidance scale w of 1 means no extra push; values of 5 to 8 are common in Stable Diffusion. A higher w follows the prompt more strongly but reduces diversity and can produce over-saturated images. It costs two network passes per step.
Latent diffusion. A variational autoencoder is trained first. Its encoder compresses a 512 × 512 × 3 image (786,432 numbers) into a 64 × 64 × 4 latent (16,384 numbers), a factor of 48, and its decoder reconstructs the image. Diffusion then runs entirely on the small latent, and only the final latent is decoded. Because attention cost grows with the square of the number of positions, working on 64 × 64 instead of 512 × 512 positions is dramatically cheaper. The price is that the image quality is bounded by what the autoencoder can reconstruct, for example in fine text and small faces.
Architecture and newer training forms. The network used to be a U-Net: a convolutional encoder that downsamples, a decoder that upsamples, skip connections, and attention blocks at low resolution. Newer models use a transformer on patches of the latent (DiT), which scales cleanly with parameters and compute. In flow matching, the training target changes: the sample is a straight-line mix \( x_t = (1 - t)\, x_0 + t\, \varepsilon \), and the network predicts the velocity \( \varepsilon - x_0 \). Straight paths mean that fewer solver steps are needed. Finally, distillation trains a student that copies a many-step teacher in 1 to 4 steps, for real-time use.

example: Work through one tiny case with a single pixel, so you can follow every number. Say the clean value is \( x_0 = 1.0 \). At step t, the schedule gives \( \bar\alpha_t = 0.64 \), so \( \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} = 0.8 \) and \( \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} = 0.6 \).
Training step. Draw noise ε = 0.5. The noisy pixel is x_t = 0.8 × 1.0 + 0.6 × 0.5 = 0.8 + 0.3 = 1.1. The network sees 1.1 and the step number and outputs ε̂ = 0.4. The loss is (0.5 − 0.4)² = 0.01. Gradient descent changes the weights to reduce this, averaged over millions of pixels, images and random values of t.
What the prediction means. From ε̂ we can estimate the clean value: x̂₀ = (1.1 − 0.6 × 0.4) / 0.8 = (1.1 − 0.24) / 0.8 = 1.075. It is close to the true 1.0. If the network had predicted ε̂ = 0.5 exactly, the estimate would be (1.1 − 0.3) / 0.8 = 1.0. So a small error in the noise estimate gives a small error in the clean estimate. At large t, where \( \bar\alpha_t \) is near 0, the factor \( 1/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \) is huge and small noise errors are amplified, which is why the early, very noisy steps decide the coarse layout of the picture and the late steps only add detail.
Guidance. At some step the network gives ε_uncond = 0.30 without the prompt and ε_cond = 0.40 with it. With a guidance scale w = 7.5: ε̃ = 0.30 + 7.5 × (0.40 − 0.30) = 0.30 + 0.75 = 1.05. The denoising step now moves much further in the direction that the prompt prefers than the plain conditional prediction would.
Cost of one image. Stable Diffusion with 30 sampler steps and guidance runs 30 × 2 = 60 network passes, plus one VAE decode. In pixel space the image has 512 × 512 = 262,144 positions, against 64 × 64 = 4,096 latent positions, which is 64 times more. Attention compares all pairs of positions, so its cost would be about 64 × 64 = 4,096 times higher. This is why latent diffusion made open image generation on consumer GPUs possible. If a distilled model needs 4 steps without guidance, the same image costs 4 passes, 15 times fewer than 60.
intuition: Sculpting from static: it is easy to ruin a picture with noise, so learn to undo one small bit of ruin, and then repeat it.
Making a picture from nothing is hard because there are countless ways to be wrong. Removing a little noise from an almost-formed picture is easy because the answer is nearly visible. Diffusion turns one very hard problem into a thousand easy ones, each trained with a simple, stable loss. The forward process is free (we just add noise), so we get unlimited training pairs. The reverse process gets the structure in order: first the layout when there is a lot of noise, then shapes, then texture. That coarse-to-fine order is natural for images.
mistakes: People often think a diffusion model stores and recalls training images. In fact it learns a function that predicts noise, and the images are not stored as such. It can still memorise rare, repeated images, and researchers have extracted near-copies of some training pictures (Carlini et al., 2023), so the risk is real but limited to duplicated data. Deduplication reduces it.
People often think the model starts from a blurry sketch and sharpens it. In fact it starts from independent random noise with no information about the answer. The prompt, the random seed and the sampler together determine the result. The same seed and settings reproduce the same image, which is useful for debugging.
People often think more sampling steps always give better images. In fact, quality saturates quickly with a good solver: 20 to 30 steps are often enough, and 1,000 steps (the original DDPM) are no longer needed. After that, extra steps add cost and not detail. Too high a guidance scale, in contrast, hurts quality.
People often think diffusion is only for images. In fact the same loss works on any continuous data: audio (speech, music), video, 3D shapes, robot actions (diffusion policies), molecules and protein structures. Applying it to discrete data such as text needs adaptations, and diffusion language models are still an active research area, not a replacement for autoregressive models yet.
People often think "higher guidance scale means a better prompt match, therefore better". In fact guidance trades diversity and realism for prompt adherence. The best value depends on the model, and flow-based models often need lower values than older ones.
friction: Sampling is slow compared with a GAN, which needs one pass. Even with 20 steps and guidance (40 passes), a large model takes seconds on a GPU. Distillation and consistency models reduce the steps, but with some loss of diversity or detail, and they need extra training. Video is much harder, since it multiplies the number of values by the frame count and needs consistency over time.
Control and correctness remain weak points. Models struggle with exact counts, spatial relations ("a red cube left of a blue sphere"), rendering of text, and hands and fine anatomy, though each generation of models improves. ControlNet, LoRA, and image prompts add control, but each is an extra component to manage. There is also no built-in guarantee about content, so safety filters and classifiers are separate systems.
Evaluation is difficult. FID (Fréchet Inception Distance) compares feature statistics of generated and real images and is easy to compute, but it correlates imperfectly with human taste. Human preference tests are costly. Training needs large datasets, often scraped from the web, which raises copyright, consent and privacy disputes that courts and regulators are still resolving. Provenance tools (watermarking, C2PA metadata) help but can be removed. Finally, training a base model from scratch costs from hundreds of thousands to millions of dollars of GPU time, so most teams adapt existing models.
practice: Do not train from scratch. Start with an open pretrained model, or an API, and adapt it. LoRA fine-tuning (a small set of extra weights, a few megabytes) with 20 to 200 images can teach a style or a subject. ControlNet or similar adapters add pose, edges or depth conditioning. Image-to-image and inpainting reuse the same network by starting from a partly noised input image.
Control cost with four settings: resolution (cost grows with the number of latent positions), number of steps (start at 20 to 30 with a modern solver such as DPM-Solver++), guidance scale (start around 5 to 7 for Stable Diffusion-style models, lower for flow models), and distilled "turbo" or "lightning" variants when you need under a second per image. Batch requests to fill the GPU and use half precision (fp16 or bf16).
Fix the random seed for reproducibility, log prompts, seeds and settings with each output, and generate several candidates and select by a score, a human, or a classifier. Evaluate with a fixed set of prompts that cover your use case, side by side across model versions, with human judgement; automatic scores such as FID or CLIP score are only a rough guide.
Treat legal and safety questions as part of the design: check the licence of the model and of any training data you add, keep records of what produced each asset, add content filters on prompts and outputs, and attach provenance metadata where it is required. For production services, plan for the GPU memory (a model with 1 to 12 billion parameters needs 2 to 24 GB in fp16 for weights alone) and for queueing, because requests take seconds and not milliseconds.
tradeoffs: Choose diffusion (or flow-matching) models when sample quality, diversity and controllability matter more than speed: image, video and audio generation, and any task where you can accept 1 to 30 seconds of GPU time per sample. Choose a GAN-style one-step generator when you need real-time output on limited hardware (for example super-resolution at video frame rates), and you can accept less stable training and narrower coverage of the data. Choose an autoregressive transformer over image tokens when the model should share a single architecture and token interface with a language model, and accept the loss from quantising images into tokens.
Choose latent diffusion when resolution is high and compute is limited; it is cheaper by a factor of tens. Choose pixel-space diffusion for cases where the autoencoder's loss of detail is unacceptable (fine text, medical images, exact textures), and pay with a higher cost or cascades of models at increasing resolution.
Choose a U-Net backbone when you want to start from a mature ecosystem of checkpoints, adapters and tooling, and when training budgets are small. Choose a diffusion transformer when you plan to scale to billions of parameters, to video, or to joint text–image models; it scales predictably but needs more data and compute to match a U-Net at small scale.
Choose many sampling steps with guidance for the best fidelity in offline work (marketing images, creative review), and a distilled model with 1 to 4 steps for interactive tools and for generating millions of images. You pay for distillation with some loss of diversity, reduced responsiveness to guidance and extra training work.
Choose fine-tuning with LoRA for a style or subject (small data, cheap, reversible, many adapters on one base model); choose full fine-tuning only when the domain is far from the base data and you have thousands of examples and the budget. Prompting alone is the cheapest and should be the first attempt.
interview: Why does diffusion train more stably than a GAN? || The loss is a plain regression: predict the noise that was added, with a known target in every step. There is no adversary with a moving objective, so no equilibrium to balance and no mode collapse from a discriminator. Each training example is a random pair of data point and noise level, so the gradients are well behaved.
Explain classifier-free guidance and its cost. || The network is trained with the prompt randomly dropped, so it can predict both conditionally and unconditionally. At sampling time the two predictions are combined as uncond + w × (cond − uncond), pushing the sample further towards the prompt. The cost is two network passes per step (often batched), and a high w reduces diversity and can cause saturated, artefact-prone images.
Why does latent diffusion make generation so much cheaper, and what does it risk? || A VAE compresses the image about 48 times (512 × 512 × 3 to 64 × 64 × 4) and the network works only on the latent, and attention cost depends on the square of positions. The risk is that the VAE becomes the ceiling for detail: it can blur small text, faces and fine texture, and errors in the decoder cannot be fixed by the diffusion model.
A product needs an image in under 300 ms. Measure the quality drop of each option on a prompt set, since fewer steps reduce diversity and detail.
What is the connection between score matching and noise prediction? || The score is the gradient of the log density of noisy data. For Gaussian noise, the optimal noise prediction equals the negative score scaled by the noise level. So training a network to predict the noise is the same as learning the score, and sampling by denoising is following the score uphill toward high density data, with randomness added by the sampler.
How would you check whether a model leaks training images? || Generate many samples for prompts that match likely duplicated training items, and search for near-duplicates using an image-embedding or perceptual-hash similarity to the training set. Check the training data for duplicates, since repeated images are the ones memorised. Mitigate by deduplication, filtering, and lower guidance, and document the residual risk.
quiz: What does the network predict in DDPM training, and what is the loss? || It predicts the noise ε that was added to a clean sample to make the noisy input x_t; the loss is the mean-squared error between ε and the prediction ε̂.
Why does sampling need many network evaluations, and what reduces the number? || Each step can only remove a small amount of noise, because the network's prediction is accurate only locally. Better solvers (DDIM, DPM-Solver), flow matching with straighter paths, and distillation or consistency models cut steps from about 1,000 to 20 or even 1 to 4.
A latent is 64 × 64 × 4 for a 512 × 512 × 3 image. By what factor is the data reduced? || The image has 786,432 values and the latent has 16,384, so the reduction is 48 times.

value: A reasoning model is a language model that thinks before it answers. Before the final reply, it writes a long internal chain of thought, often thousands of tokens, in which it breaks the problem into steps, tries an approach, checks the result, notices a mistake and backs up. A standard chat model starts answering at once and commits to each word as it goes. A reasoning model uses extra computation at the moment of the question, called test-time or inference-time compute, to buy accuracy. On hard maths, programming and planning problems, this has moved results from "often wrong" to "often right".
Two ideas came together. First, chain of thought (L2) showed that a model reasons better when it writes intermediate steps. Second, reinforcement learning (L2, here the same machinery as RLHF) can train a model to produce good chains by rewarding only final answers that can be checked automatically: the maths answer matches, the code passes its tests. The model then discovers useful habits on its own, such as verifying and trying again. Scaling laws (L2) add the third part. Accuracy improves not only with more training compute but also with more thinking tokens at inference, so a company can trade cost and latency for quality per question.
For the layer above, this matters because all current frontier models (GPT, Claude, Gemini, DeepSeek and others) offer a reasoning mode, often with a setting for how much to think. For an engineer it changes system design: some requests deserve 10 seconds and 10,000 tokens, most do not, so you route between fast and slow models and set a thinking budget. It also changes economics, because you pay for the thinking tokens that you never see in full.
terms: Chain of thought (CoT) — the intermediate text a model writes while working towards an answer.
Test-time (inference-time) compute — the amount of computation spent per question when the model is used, mostly the number of tokens it generates.
RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards) — training with a reward computed by a program that checks the answer, such as an exact-match checker or unit tests.
GRPO (group relative policy optimization) — an RL method that samples a group of answers per prompt and scores each answer relative to the group average, with no separate value network.
Verifier — a program or model that decides whether an answer or a reasoning step is correct.
Majority voting (self-consistency) — sample several answers and return the one that appears most often.
Best-of-N — sample N answers and return the one that a verifier or reward model scores highest.
Process reward model (PRM) — a model that scores each reasoning step, in contrast to an outcome reward model that scores only the final answer.
Thinking budget — a cap on the number of reasoning tokens the model may use before it must answer.
history: The path started with prompting. Nye et al. (2021) trained models to write "scratchpads", and Wei et al. (2022) showed that adding worked steps to a prompt greatly improved arithmetic and logic benchmarks for large models. Kojima et al. (2022) found that even "Let's think step by step" helps. Wang et al. (2022) added self-consistency: sample many chains and vote. In parallel, Cobbe et al. (2021, GSM8K) trained verifiers that rank candidate solutions, and Lightman et al. (2023, "Let's Verify Step by Step") showed that rewarding each step beats rewarding only the outcome. STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) had a model learn from its own correct chains.
OpenAI released o1-preview in September 2024 and said that accuracy rose smoothly with both more RL training and more thinking time. Snell et al. (2024) analysed this in "Scaling LLM Test-Time Compute Optimally can be More Effective than Scaling Model Parameters". Alibaba's QwQ (November 2024) and Google's Gemini 2.0 Flash Thinking (December 2024) followed. The decisive public step was DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025), which published the recipe and open weights. Its R1-Zero variant applied RL with simple rule-based rewards directly to a base model, with no human-written reasoning examples, and the chains grew longer and began to include self-checking. DeepSeek used GRPO, introduced in their DeepSeekMath work (2024). Anthropic's Claude 3.7 Sonnet (February 2025) brought a hybrid mode with a user-set thinking budget, and by mid 2025 every frontier lab shipped such models. In July 2025, systems from OpenAI and Google DeepMind reached gold-medal level at the International Mathematical Olympiad. Researchers now focus on efficiency (shorter chains for the same accuracy), reliability of long agentic tasks, and extending verifiable rewards beyond maths and code.
how: A reasoning model is built in two phases on top of an ordinary pretrained language model, and used with a third idea at inference.
Phase one is a base model with a good prior. A large pretrained model (or one already instruction-tuned) can already produce reasonable step-by-step text when prompted. This matters, because RL can only reinforce behaviour that the model sometimes shows. Many recipes begin with a small "cold start" set of long, high-quality reasoning examples so that the first RL samples are readable and in a consistent format.
Phase two is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. This uses the machinery of RLHF (L2): a policy (the language model), sampled outputs, a reward, and a policy-gradient update with a penalty for drifting too far from a reference model. The difference lies in the reward. RLHF uses a learned reward model that predicts human preference, which can be gamed. RLVR uses a program: for maths, compare the final answer with the known one; for code, run the tests; for format, check that thinking is inside the required tags. Such a reward is cheap, objective and hard to flatter. In GRPO, for each prompt the model samples a group of G answers (for example 16). Each answer gets a reward r_i. The advantage of answer i is (r_i − mean of the group) / (standard deviation of the group). Answers better than their siblings are made more likely, and worse ones less likely, token by token. No separate value network is needed, which saves memory. Over thousands of steps, the model finds that longer chains with checking, alternatives and backtracking raise the average reward, and these behaviours grow without being written by hand. In the DeepSeek-R1-Zero report, accuracy on the AIME 2024 maths contest rose from about 16% to about 71% in this way, with average response length growing as training continued.
Later stages in typical pipelines polish the model. The RL model generates many solutions, correct ones are filtered (rejection sampling) and used for supervised fine-tuning together with general data, then RL runs again with a preference reward added for helpfulness and safety on ordinary prompts. The result can be distilled: a smaller model is fine-tuned on the large model's reasoning traces, and this transfers much of the skill cheaply (the R1 report distilled into models of 1.5 to 70 billion parameters).
Phase three is test-time scaling. Even for a trained model, spending more compute per question helps in two directions. Sequentially, the model can think longer: the budget sets how many tokens it may use, and a model can be nudged to continue ("Wait, let me check") instead of stopping, as in the s1 work of Muennighoff et al. (2025). In parallel, you can sample N independent solutions and combine them. Majority voting needs no verifier, only a final answer to compare. Best-of-N needs a verifier or reward model to pick. Process reward models allow search: expand the promising partial chains and drop the bad ones, like a tree search. Gains from more compute are roughly logarithmic: each doubling adds a smaller improvement, and the curve flattens when the model lacks the needed knowledge or the verifier is weak.
Why does this work at all? A transformer does a fixed amount of computation per generated token. A hard problem may need more serial computation than one forward pass offers. Writing tokens gives the model extra "working memory" and extra serial steps: each emitted token is fed back as input, so the chain acts as a scratchpad. Training with outcome rewards teaches it how to use that scratchpad productively, which is what the scaling-laws concept predicts: a second axis (inference compute) along which quality grows.
Serving also changes. Long outputs mean long decode times (decoding is one token at a time) and large KV caches. Providers bill the thinking tokens as output tokens, and some return only a summary of the chain. Reasoning effort settings (low, medium, high) or explicit token budgets control this.

example: Take GRPO on one maths prompt with a group of G = 4 sampled answers. The verifier marks them correct (1) or wrong (0): rewards = [1, 0, 0, 1]. The mean is 0.5 and the standard deviation is 0.5. The advantages are (1 − 0.5)/0.5 = +1 for the two correct chains and −1 for the two wrong ones. The update raises the probability of every token in the correct chains and lowers it in the wrong ones. If all four had been correct, every advantage would be 0 and the prompt would teach nothing; the same if all four were wrong. So useful training prompts are those of medium difficulty, where the model is right sometimes. This is why recipes filter or schedule prompts by pass rate.
Now test-time voting. Suppose a model solves a problem type correctly with probability p = 0.6 per independent sample. Take 5 samples and majority vote. If the correct answer gets at least 3 votes, it surely wins. The probability of that is C(5,3)·0.6³·0.4² + C(5,4)·0.6⁴·0.4 + 0.6⁵ = 0.3456 + 0.2592 + 0.0778 = 0.683. So accuracy goes from 60% to at least 68%, because wrong answers usually disagree with each other and do not accumulate votes. With 15 samples, the same calculation gives about 0.79, and with 50 samples about 0.90; returns only shrink later, as accuracy nears 100%. The calculation also assumes errors are independent, and in practice they are correlated (a systematic misconception is repeated in every sample), so real gains are smaller.
Now cost. A routine question answered by a standard model uses about 500 output tokens. A reasoning model may think for 8,000 tokens before a 500-token answer. At an assumed output price of $10 per million tokens (prices vary widely by model), the first costs $0.005 and the second 8,500 × $10 / 1,000,000 = $0.085, which is 17 times more. At a decode speed of 60 tokens per second, the delay is about 8 seconds for the standard model and 142 seconds for the reasoning one. For 100,000 requests per day, the difference is $500 against $8,500. If a router sends only the 10% of hard requests to the reasoning model, the cost is 90,000 × $0.005 + 10,000 × $0.085 = $450 + $850 = $1,300 per day, with the reasoning model used where it matters.
intuition: Give the model scratch paper and time, and train it, with a strict teacher who only checks the final answer, to use both well.
A person cannot multiply two six-digit numbers in one glance but can do it on paper. A language model has the same limit: a fixed amount of thinking per word. Letting it write its work out gives it the paper. Reinforcement learning with checkable answers is the teacher that does not care how the work looks, only whether the result is right, so the model keeps whatever habits lead to right answers: trying alternatives, checking, going back. That is why the behaviour appears without anyone writing out examples of it.
mistakes: People often think the visible chain of thought is the real reason behind the answer. In fact, studies show that written reasoning can leave out the factors that actually influenced the model. Turpin et al. (2023) found that biases in the prompt changed answers without being mentioned, and Anthropic's 2025 study "Reasoning models don't always say what they think" found hints used by the model but mentioned in its chain only some of the time. Treat the chain as a useful trace, not as a guaranteed explanation or an audit log.
People often think thinking longer is always better. In fact, accuracy flattens, and models can overthink simple questions, talk themselves out of a right answer, or loop. For easy tasks, a short answer from a standard model is cheaper, faster and as accurate. Set a budget and measure.
People often think reasoning models are a new kind of architecture. In fact they are ordinary transformers. The difference is the training (RL on verifiable outcomes) and the use of many more output tokens. This is also why a smaller model can be distilled on reasoning traces from a larger one.
People often think RL teaches the model new knowledge. In fact the evidence suggests RL mostly sharpens and reorders what the base model can already do: it raises the chance of sampling a correct solution that existed in the base model's distribution. Some studies find that for very large sample counts the base model matches the RL model on coverage. RL makes the right path easy to find at one try, which is valuable, but it is not a replacement for pretraining knowledge.
People often think these models are good everywhere. In fact the gains are largest where an answer can be checked automatically (maths, code, logic puzzles) and smaller in open-ended writing, taste and judgement, where no cheap verifier exists.
friction: Cost and latency are the first limits. Thinking tokens are billed and take seconds to minutes to generate, which is unacceptable for interactive paths, and long chains make serving more expensive (large KV caches, fewer concurrent users per GPU). Streaming a summary of the thought helps the user experience but not the cost.
Verification is the second limit. RLVR works where a reliable checker exists. For research, strategy, medicine or writing there is none, and learned reward models can be exploited ("reward hacking"): the model finds outputs that please the grader without being right, such as special-casing the tests in code. Rewarding only the final answer can also reward lucky guesses and flawed reasoning that arrives at the right result.
Faithfulness and safety are open problems. The chain may not reflect the true computation, and a model can learn to hide intent if the chain is trained to look good, which is why some researchers argue against putting heavy optimisation pressure on the chain itself so that it stays monitorable. Models also display odd behaviours: very long loops, repeated checking of correct answers, and inconsistent effort across similar questions. Evaluation is expensive, since a benchmark run needs millions of tokens, and results depend on the budget and the sampling method, so published numbers are hard to compare. Finally, training these models needs a large RL infrastructure: sampling many long chains per prompt, verifying them in sandboxes, and updating the model, which is a major engineering cost.
practice: Use a reasoning model for multi-step problems: maths, debugging, planning, data analysis with several constraints, code that must satisfy tests, and extraction tasks where careful cross-checking matters. Use a standard model for lookup, rewriting, classification and chat. Put a router in front: a cheap classifier, a rule, or the fast model itself can decide when to escalate, and you can fall back to the reasoning model when a verifier or confidence check fails.
Set the thinking budget or effort level explicitly and measure accuracy, cost and latency at low, medium and high on your own evaluation set. Do not rely on public benchmarks, since gains often do not transfer. Cap the output tokens to stop runaway chains, and set client time-outs that respect the long latency. Stream the output where the product allows it.
Keep prompts simple: state the goal, constraints and the output format, and avoid "think step by step" instructions that duplicate what the model already does. Give the model tools (code execution, search) when the answer can be computed or looked up, because verification by execution is more reliable than by thought. Where you can, build a verifier of your own (tests, schema checks, a calculator, a second model with a rubric) and use best-of-N or retry on failure.
Do not store or show raw chains of thought as authoritative explanations, and do not feed them back as instructions. Log token usage per request type, since cost surprises come from long thinking. For training your own, start from an open reasoning model and distil or fine-tune with RLVR only if you have a task with a cheap and trustworthy verifier.
tradeoffs: Choose a reasoning model when errors are expensive, the task needs several dependent steps, and a few seconds to a few minutes of latency is acceptable. Examples are code changes verified by tests, financial or data analysis, and complex extraction. Choose a standard model for latency under about two seconds, for simple tasks, or when the volume is so high that a 10 to 20 times higher cost per request is not affordable. You give up speed and cost for accuracy on the first, and accuracy on hard cases for speed on the second.
Choose parallel sampling with voting or a verifier when you have a reliable way to compare answers, answers are short and comparable (numbers, code that passes tests), and latency can be hidden by running samples at the same time. It multiplies cost by N but not latency. Choose a longer single chain when answers are long or open-ended, so voting does not apply, or when the problem needs deep serial steps. It multiplies latency instead.
Choose a large reasoning model when the task is novel and varied. Choose a distilled small reasoning model when the task family is narrow and volume is high: a 7 to 32 billion parameter distilled model can serve at a small fraction of the cost and often keeps most of the accuracy on maths and code, but it generalises less well to unusual problems.
Choose outcome rewards (RLVR) when the final answer is checkable: they are simple and hard to game. Choose process rewards when you need to find the first wrong step, to prune search, or when correct final answers can come from bad reasoning. You pay with expensive step-level labels or a learned model that can itself be wrong.
Choose to show the reasoning to users only when it helps them check the work and you do not depend on it being faithful; hide or summarise it when it may be long, confusing or leak instructions. Either way, do not use it as the audit trail.
interview: How does training with verifiable rewards differ from RLHF, and why does it produce long reasoning? || RLHF optimises a learned reward model of human preference, which can be exploited and is subjective. RLVR uses a program that checks correctness, so the reward is objective and hard to game. Since only the final answer is rewarded, the policy finds whatever behaviours raise the chance of being right, and longer chains with checking and backtracking do that, so length and reflection grow during training.
Walk through GRPO and say why it drops the value network. || For each prompt it samples a group of answers, gets a reward for each, and normalises within the group: advantage = (reward − group mean) / group standard deviation. The group average replaces the baseline that a learned value function would give, which saves the memory and training of a second large network. A clipped policy-gradient update and a KL penalty to a reference model keep updates stable. Prompts where all answers get the same reward give zero signal.
Majority voting over 5 samples helps a model that is 60% accurate. When would it not help? || When errors are correlated: if the model has a systematic misconception, most samples repeat the same wrong answer and voting amplifies it. It also fails when answers cannot be compared (long free text) or the base accuracy is below the rate at which wrong answers agree. A separate verifier, tools, or a different model would be needed.
A product manager wants to use the strongest reasoning model for every request. What do you advise? || Measure the cost and latency effect: thinking tokens may make requests 10 to 20 times more expensive and many seconds slower, with little gain on easy requests. Use a router or an effort setting, send only hard or high-value requests to the reasoning model, add a verifier-based fallback, and run an A/B test on your own tasks with both quality and cost as metrics.
Why can the chain of thought not be trusted as an explanation, and what follows for safety monitoring? || It is generated text trained to lead to correct answers, not a recording of the computation. Studies show influences that go unmentioned. For monitoring it can still reveal intent in many cases, so it is worth reading, but it should not be the only control, and heavy optimisation to make it look clean can teach the model to hide things. Combine it with behavioural tests, tool-use limits and output checks.
Why does a transformer benefit from generating more tokens on hard problems? || A transformer performs a fixed amount of computation per token, so the depth of serial reasoning in one pass is limited. Each generated token is fed back as input, which gives the model extra serial steps and a place to store intermediate results. Training teaches it to use these steps productively.
quiz: What is the reward signal in RLVR for a coding task? || A program runs the model's code against unit tests; the reward is 1 if the tests pass (and possibly a format reward), otherwise 0. No human label or learned reward model is needed.
Why does GRPO give zero learning signal on a prompt where all sampled answers are correct? || The advantage is the reward minus the group mean, divided by the standard deviation. When all rewards are equal the advantage is zero for every answer, so the gradient has nothing to prefer.
Why does majority voting often improve accuracy beyond a single sample? || Correct answers tend to agree, while wrong answers are spread over many different values, so the correct one collects the most votes. With p = 0.6 and 5 samples the chance of at least 3 correct votes is about 68%.

@@ multimodal
value: A multimodal model works with more than one kind of data in the same system: text, images, audio, video, and sometimes sensor data or documents laid out as pages. You can show it a photo of a broken part and ask what is wrong, give it a chart and ask for the trend, speak to it and hear a spoken answer, or ask it to describe what happens in a video. Before such models, each modality had its own specialised system, and combining them meant chaining separate models (speech to text, then a language model, then text to speech), with information lost at every hand-off, such as tone of voice or the layout of a table.
The core idea is to translate every modality into the same kind of object that a transformer can read: a sequence of vectors. Text is already tokens. An image is cut into patches and each patch becomes a vector. Audio becomes frames or discrete codes. Once everything is a sequence of embeddings, one attention-based network can relate a word to a region of an image or a sound to a sentence. What the model learns in one modality helps the others, and you get a single interface, a prompt, that accepts any mix of inputs.
The layers below supply the pieces. Contrastive learning (L2, as in CLIP) learned to place images and text in one shared embedding space from hundreds of millions of captioned pictures. Embeddings (L2) are the common currency. Self-attention (L2) mixes tokens from all modalities in one context. Generative adversarial training (L2) lives on inside the image tokenizers and decoders, where an adversarial loss makes reconstructions sharp. The layer above, the GPT, Claude and Gemini products, offers these abilities as ordinary features: upload a screenshot, a scanned invoice, an audio clip.
terms: Modality — one kind of data or sense, such as text, image, audio or video.
Vision encoder — a network (usually a Vision Transformer) that turns an image into a sequence of feature vectors, one per patch.
Projector (adapter) — a small network, often a linear layer or two-layer MLP, that maps vision-encoder outputs into the language model's embedding space.
Contrastive pretraining — training two encoders so matching pairs (an image and its caption) get similar embeddings and mismatched pairs get dissimilar ones.
Early fusion — feeding all modalities as tokens into one model from the start of training, so it learns them together.
Late fusion — combining separately trained modality models, for example by passing an image encoder's output to a frozen language model.
Visual token — one vector (or discrete code) that stands for a patch of an image inside the model's context.
Image tokenizer — a model (VQ-VAE or VQGAN style) that compresses an image into a grid of discrete codes and can decode codes back into an image.
Audio codec — a neural compressor (SoundStream, EnCodec) that turns waveforms into discrete tokens and back.
history: Early work joined vision and language with captioning models in 2014 and 2015 ("Show and Tell" and others), which fed a CNN's features into an LSTM that wrote a caption. Visual question answering datasets (VQA, 2015) added questions. These models were small and task-specific.
The break came in 2021 with CLIP (Radford et al., OpenAI): an image encoder and a text encoder trained on 400 million image–text pairs from the web, with a contrastive loss, so that a picture and its caption land close together. It classified images into categories it had never been trained on, just by comparing the image with text prompts. Google's ALIGN (2021) did the same with 1.8 billion noisier pairs. In 2022, DeepMind's Flamingo connected a frozen language model to a vision encoder through new cross-attention layers and handled few-shot examples that mixed images and text. BLIP-2 (2023) connected a frozen image encoder and a frozen language model with a small "Q-Former". LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023) showed the cheapest recipe: a CLIP encoder, a linear projection, and an open language model, tuned on instruction data that GPT-4 had written about images.
Meanwhile, products moved quickly. GPT-4 with vision arrived in September 2023, Google's Gemini (December 2023) was described as multimodal from the start of training, and Claude 3 (March 2024) accepted images. GPT-4o (May 2024) handled text, audio and images in one network, with audio response times that OpenAI reported averaging 320 milliseconds, against several seconds for the older chain of speech recognition, language model and speech synthesis. Meta's Chameleon (2024) put images and text into one token stream with a single transformer. Generation is advancing in parallel: images from diffusion or autoregressive decoders, speech from audio codecs, and video from diffusion transformers. Whisper (2022) made robust speech recognition an off-the-shelf component. Today the research questions are efficient video understanding, long documents, and models that read and write all modalities natively.
how: There are three common designs, and the product models mix them.
Design one is the dual encoder with a contrastive loss (CLIP). An image encoder and a text encoder each output a vector, normalised to length 1. For a batch of N pairs, compute the \( N \times N \) matrix of cosine similarities. The correct pairs are on the diagonal. Scale the similarities by a temperature, apply a softmax across each row (pick the right caption for each image) and across each column (pick the right image for each caption), and minimise the cross-entropy. This pulls true pairs together and pushes the \( N^2 - N \) other combinations apart. Large batches (CLIP used 32,768) provide many negatives. The embeddings form a shared space, used for zero-shot classification, image search with text queries, and as the vision encoder inside larger models. SigLIP replaces the softmax with an independent sigmoid per pair, which scales better with batch size. This design does not generate text; it only matches.
Design two is the encoder-projector-language-model recipe (Flamingo, BLIP-2, LLaVA). A pretrained vision encoder (often a CLIP or SigLIP ViT) turns an image into a grid of patch vectors, for example 24 × 24 = 576 vectors for a 336-pixel image with 14-pixel patches. A projector maps each vector to the language model's embedding dimension. The projected vectors are placed in the language model's input sequence like word embeddings, in the position where the image appears. The language model then treats them as visual tokens and attends to them with ordinary self-attention (LLaVA) or reads them through added cross-attention layers (Flamingo). Training usually has stages. First, align: train only the projector on image–caption pairs, with the encoder and language model frozen, so image vectors land where the language model can read them. Then instruction tune: train the projector and the language model on question–answer data about images, charts, documents and screenshots. A final stage uses preference data to cut hallucinations. This design reuses strong existing parts and is cheap, but the vision encoder was not trained with the language model, so fine details (small text, counting, spatial relations) can be lost at the interface.
Design three is native or early fusion (Gemini, GPT-4o, Chameleon). All modalities are converted into tokens and one transformer is trained from the start on interleaved data: web pages with text and images, video with audio, and so on. Images enter either as continuous patch embeddings or as discrete codes from an image tokenizer. The tokenizer is a VQ-VAE or VQGAN: an encoder compresses the image to a grid of vectors, each is snapped to the nearest entry of a learned codebook, and a decoder reconstructs the image from the codes. VQGAN adds a patch-based discriminator to the training, which is where adversarial training helps, because it pushes reconstructions to be sharp and not blurry. With discrete image tokens, the same next-token loss can train the model to generate images too. Audio works similarly: a codec compresses the waveform into discrete tokens (using residual vector quantization, several codebooks per frame), so the model can listen and speak in one network without converting to text in between and so keep tone, pauses and emotion.
Generation is often handed to a specialised decoder. A multimodal model can write a detailed prompt or a set of latent tokens, and a diffusion model renders the image; or the transformer emits image tokens that a decoder turns into pixels. The input side (understanding) is mostly solved with the encoder-projector design; the output side (generating images and speech) is where native designs are strongest.
Token budgets matter. Each image is turned into hundreds to thousands of tokens that share the context window with text. The cost scales with the patch count, and so with resolution. Anthropic's documentation, for example, describes about one token per 28 × 28 pixel patch, so a 1000 × 1000 image is about 1,300 tokens, and images larger than the model's limit are resized down first. Video multiplies this by the number of frames, which is why video models sample frames sparsely, merge neighbouring tokens, or compress over time.

example: First, the contrastive loss on a tiny batch of 3 image–caption pairs. Suppose the cosine similarities are: image 1 against captions 1, 2, 3 = 0.9, 0.1, 0.2; image 2 = 0.2, 0.8, 0.1; image 3 = 0.1, 0.3, 0.7. The diagonal is correct. Use a temperature of 0.5, so each similarity is multiplied by 2 before the softmax. Row 1 gives logits 1.8, 0.2, 0.4, with exponentials 6.05, 1.22, 1.49, sum 8.76, so the probability of the right caption is 6.05 / 8.76 = 0.69 and the loss is −ln 0.69 ≈ 0.37. Row 2 gives logits 0.4, 1.6, 0.2, probability 4.95 / 7.67 = 0.65, loss 0.44. Row 3 gives logits 0.2, 0.6, 1.4, probability 4.06 / 7.10 = 0.57, loss 0.56. The mean is about 0.46. Training raises the diagonal and lowers the off-diagonal entries, and a lower temperature makes the softmax sharper. (CLIP learns its temperature; the final value is much smaller than 0.5.)
Second, a token budget. You want a model to read a 20-page scanned report and answer questions. Assume each page is rendered as an image and costs about 1,500 tokens. That is 30,000 tokens for the report, before your question and the answer. If you ask 10 questions, and each call includes the whole report, you send 300,000 input tokens. At an assumed $3 per million input tokens (illustrative only), this is $0.90. If instead you run OCR once, the pages become perhaps 500 tokens each, so the report is 10,000 tokens, and 10 questions cost $0.30. The OCR route is three times cheaper, but OCR loses tables, charts and layout, so tables with numbers are better read by the image route or a hybrid: OCR text for plain pages, images for pages with charts.
Third, video. A 10-minute clip sampled at 1 frame per second is 600 frames. At 256 tokens per frame that is 153,600 tokens, which already fills most long-context windows. Sampling every 5 seconds gives 120 frames and 30,720 tokens. The fix is to choose frames by scene change, so that a long static shot costs one frame, and to add the audio transcript as text, which is cheap and carries most of the information in speech-heavy video.
intuition: Translate every sense into the same internal language, then let one reader attend to all of it.
Meaning does not belong to one medium. The word "dog", a photograph of a dog, and a bark are different signals about the same thing. If each is mapped to nearby points in a shared space, the model can use knowledge of one to understand another. Contrastive training finds that mapping from pairs that people already made (captions, subtitles). Attention then lets the language model use the visual tokens as freely as words. The design is natural because a transformer does not care what a token stands for, only that it is a vector with a position.
mistakes: People often think a multimodal model sees an image the way a person does. In fact it receives a fixed number of patch vectors. Details smaller than a patch, exact counts, precise spatial relations and small text can be missed or invented, and the model can describe things confidently that are not there (visual hallucination). Always verify extracted numbers.
People often think image input is free, like a few words of text. In fact an image takes hundreds to thousands of tokens, in the same context window and billing as text, and video takes tens or hundreds of thousands. Resolution, number of images and frame rate are cost settings.
People often think CLIP-style models understand sentences. In fact a CLIP text encoder is good at matching a set of objects and attributes but weak at word order and relations: "a horse eating grass" and "grass eating a horse" can look similar to it, and counting and negation are poor. Benchmarks like Winoground exposed this. Large multimodal language models do better but are not perfect.
People often think multimodal means a model is better at text too. In fact adding modalities can cost text quality if the data mixture or capacity is not balanced, and training costs rise. Gains are on tasks that need the other modality, and for text-only tasks a model without vision may be as good.
People often think OCR is obsolete. In fact, for clean printed documents at large volume, dedicated OCR plus a text model is cheaper, faster, and easy to audit. Vision-language models are better for layout, charts, handwriting and mixed content, and the two are often combined.
friction: Compute and data are heavy. Pretraining needs billions of image–text pairs and much more memory, and each image adds hundreds of tokens to every example. Video and long audio are the hardest, since the number of tokens grows with duration. Quality data with aligned modalities is scarce, and web captions are noisy and often just alt text.
Grounding and reliability are weak points. Models hallucinate objects, misread small text and charts, miscount, and fail at spatial reasoning ("what is left of the cup"). Benchmarks give an incomplete picture because they leak into training data and are mostly in English with natural photos, whereas real inputs are scans, screenshots and dashboards. Evaluating generated images, speech and video needs human judgement.
There are also safety and privacy issues: images can contain faces, location clues, personal documents or hidden text that instructs the model (prompt injection through an image). Biases in captions are inherited. Face identification is often blocked by policy. Alignment between modalities can break under unusual inputs, and adversarial images can be built to mislead the vision encoder. Architecturally, the interface between a frozen encoder and a language model loses information, and native models are expensive to train and hard to modify afterwards, so most teams depend on providers.
practice: Test on your real inputs. Build a set of 100 to 300 of your own documents, screenshots, photos or audio clips with known answers, grouped by type, and score each type separately. Quality varies enormously between a clean photo, a scanned form, a dense table and a small-font chart.
Control cost and quality with the input. Resize images to what the model needs (more pixels do not help beyond the model's limit, and they cost tokens), crop to the region of interest, and split dense pages into tiles when text is tiny. Send text next to the image when you have it: OCR output, alt text, metadata, a table of contents. The model can then use the text for exact characters and the image for layout. Ask for structured output and validate it, and for numbers, check them against totals or run a second extraction and compare.
For video, sample frames by scene change, send the transcript as text, and ask narrow questions about narrow time ranges. For search over your own image library, use a CLIP or SigLIP embedding model with a vector index: embed the images once, embed each text query at search time, and rank by cosine similarity. Combine it with metadata filters.
For voice products, decide between a native speech-to-speech model (low latency, keeps tone, less control over each step) and a pipeline of speech recognition, text model and speech synthesis (more control, easy logging, higher latency). Treat any text found inside an image as untrusted data, never as instructions. Keep humans in the loop for high-stakes extraction (invoices, medical forms) and monitor accuracy by document type after launch.
tradeoffs: Choose the encoder-projector-LLM design (or an API model built that way) for most document, chart and screenshot understanding. It is quick to build on open parts, a projector can be aligned with a few hundred thousand pairs, and quality is high. You accept some loss of fine detail at the interface. Choose a natively multimodal model when you need real-time speech, interleaved image and text generation, or tight cross-modal reasoning, and accept that you depend on a provider and have little control over internals.
Choose a CLIP or SigLIP dual encoder, not a generative model, for search, deduplication, clustering and filtering of large image sets. It encodes each image once into a vector (a few hundred bytes), so searching a billion items is a nearest-neighbour lookup, whereas a generative model must be run for every query–image pair. You give up detailed reasoning about the image.
Choose images over OCR text when layout, tables, charts, handwriting or figures carry meaning. Choose OCR when pages are plain and volume is large, since text is three to ten times cheaper per page and exact. A hybrid router that sends only complex pages to the image path often gives the best cost.
Choose a higher resolution or tiling when the details are small (receipts, dense tables, small labels) and pay in tokens, which scale roughly with the number of pixels; choose lower resolution for scene-level questions, where a thumbnail is enough. Choose a speech pipeline over native speech-to-speech when you need transcripts, tool calls and auditing at each step, and accept 1 to 3 seconds of extra latency.
interview: How does CLIP's contrastive loss work, and why does batch size matter? || For N image–text pairs the model computes an \( N \times N \) similarity matrix, and a symmetric cross-entropy asks each image to pick its own caption and each caption its own image. The \( N^2 - N \) off-diagonal entries are negatives. Larger batches give more and harder negatives, which gives better embeddings, but need more memory and communication. SigLIP uses an independent sigmoid per pair to scale more easily.
In the LLaVA recipe, what does the projector do and why train it first? || It maps vision-encoder vectors into the language model's embedding space so the language model can read them as tokens. Training it first with both big parts frozen is cheap and aligns the spaces without damaging the pretrained language model. Afterwards the language model (and sometimes the encoder) is tuned on instruction data.
Why is native speech-to-speech lower in latency than the ASR, LLM and TTS chain, and what do you lose? || The chain runs three models in sequence, and each must finish (or at least start) before the next, and the text bottleneck throws away tone, emphasis and timing. A native model starts producing audio tokens directly, with latency of a few hundred milliseconds. You lose the easy transcript, the ability to swap components, and fine control and logging at each step.
A multimodal model reads your invoices well in testing but misreads digits in production. What do you check? || Resolution and resizing (digits may be below patch size after downscaling), compression artefacts, scan quality and skew, differing layouts, and prompt differences. Fixes: higher resolution or tiling, OCR text as an extra input, constrained structured output, validation by totals and checksums, and a human review path for low-confidence fields.
How would you build image search over 500 million product photos? || Embed every image once with a CLIP or SigLIP encoder, store vectors (for example 512 floats, quantised) in an approximate nearest-neighbour index sharded across machines, embed the query text at request time, retrieve the top k by cosine similarity, and rerank with metadata or a heavier model. Re-embed when you change the model, because embeddings from different models are not compatible.
What goes wrong when you feed long video to a multimodal LLM, and how do you manage it? || Token count scales with frames times tokens per frame, so cost and context limits are hit quickly, and details in the middle are used less reliably. Sample by scene change, lower the per-frame resolution, add the transcript as text, split the video into segments with summaries, and ask narrow questions.
quiz: What do the diagonal and off-diagonal entries of the CLIP similarity matrix represent? || The diagonal holds the similarities of true image–caption pairs, which the loss pushes up. The off-diagonal entries are mismatched pairs (negatives), which the loss pushes down.
Why is a projector needed between a vision encoder and a language model? || The two networks live in different embedding spaces. The projector maps each patch vector to the language model's input dimension and region of space, so the language model can use it like a word embedding.
A 336 × 336 image is split into 14 × 14 patches. How many visual tokens result? || 336 / 14 = 24 patches per side, and 24 × 24 = 576 tokens.
A 10-minute video is sampled at 1 frame per second with 256 tokens per frame. How many tokens, and what is one way to reduce them? || 600 × 256 = 153,600 tokens. Sample by scene change or every few seconds, lower the tokens per frame, and use the transcript for speech.
When would you choose OCR plus a text model over sending page images to a multimodal model? || For clean, plain-text documents at high volume, where text is cheaper per page, exact, and easy to audit. Use images when layout, tables, charts or handwriting matter, or route only the complex pages to the image path.

value: Event sourcing stores what happened, not what is true now. A normal database keeps the current state of an order: status "shipped", total 120. When the order changes, the old value is overwritten and the history is gone. With event sourcing, the database holds an append-only list of facts: OrderPlaced, ItemAdded, PaymentCaptured, OrderShipped. The current state is not stored as the truth; it is computed by replaying these events from the start. Nothing is ever updated or deleted. A correction is a new event.
CQRS (command query responsibility segregation) is a companion idea, often used together. It separates the model that handles writes (commands that decide what happens and produce events) from the models that serve reads (tables, search indexes and caches built from the events and shaped for each query). The write side is small and strict about rules. The read sides can be many, denormalised, and rebuilt at any time.
Why do this? Because some systems need the history as a first-class asset: finance, orders, inventory, audit, compliance, collaborative editing. You can answer "what did we believe on March 3rd?", find the bug by replaying real traffic, add a new report next year that reads the old events, and feed the same facts to many downstream systems. The layers below make it work. The log (L2) is the storage structure: an append-only, ordered, durable sequence. Idempotence (L2) makes replay safe, because projections will see events more than once after crashes and retries. Above it, Kafka (L0) is a common transport for events, though it is not by itself an event store with per-entity guarantees.
terms: Event — an immutable record of something that happened, named in the past tense (PaymentCaptured), with a timestamp and data.
Aggregate — a cluster of related data treated as one unit for decisions and consistency, for example one order; its events form one stream.
Event stream — the ordered list of events of one aggregate, each with a version number 1, 2, 3 ....
Command — a request to do something (CapturePayment); it may be rejected, unlike an event, which has already happened.
Projection (read model) — a view built by processing events in order, such as a table of balances or a search index.
Snapshot — a saved copy of an aggregate's state at some version, used to avoid replaying from the beginning.
Optimistic concurrency — appending an event only if the stream is still at the version the writer expected; otherwise the append fails and the writer retries.
Upcasting — converting an old stored event to the current schema when it is read, so stored events never change.
Checkpoint — the position of the last event a projection has processed, stored so it can resume.
history: The roots are old. Accountants have used append-only ledgers for centuries, and databases have long used write-ahead logs, in which the log is the truth and tables are a cached view. Martin Fowler described "Event Sourcing" as a pattern in 2005. Eric Evans's Domain-Driven Design (2003) supplied the idea of aggregates as consistency boundaries. Bertrand Meyer's command–query separation (1980s) said a method should either change state or return data. Greg Young combined these ideas around 2010 and named the pattern CQRS, and he was a driving force behind Event Store, a database built for event streams.
Around 2013 to 2015, the data community reached the same idea from another side. Jay Kreps's essay "The Log" (2013) argued that a log is the central abstraction of data systems. Martin Kleppmann's "Turning the database inside-out" (2015) described applications that treat the log as the source of truth and databases as materialised views, and his book Designing Data-Intensive Applications (2017) spread the vocabulary. Kafka made durable logs cheap and widely available, and change data capture tools (Debezium) turned existing databases into event sources. Frameworks such as Axon (Java), Akka Persistence (Scala), Marten (PostgreSQL for .NET) and EventStoreDB (now renamed KurrentDB) support it directly. The pattern has also met sober experience: many teams found that its costs (schema evolution, rebuilds, eventual consistency) are real, and today it is recommended for the parts of a system where history matters, not for all of it.
how: The write path has four steps. A command arrives, for example "withdraw 30 from account A". The handler loads the aggregate by reading its event stream (or the latest snapshot plus the events after it) and folds the events into the current state with a pure function: state = fold(apply, initial, events). The handler then decides by checking business rules against that state, for example "balance must stay at or above 0". If the command is valid, it produces one or more new events (MoneyWithdrawn 30). Finally it appends them to the stream with an expected version. If two commands race, the second append sees that the stream version is no longer what it loaded and fails, so the handler reloads and retries or reports a conflict. This is optimistic concurrency, and it gives each aggregate a strict order of events without locks. The decision logic is a pure function of state and command, which makes it easy to test: given these events, when this command, then these events.
The log (L2) is what makes this storage model work. Appending is a sequential write, the cheapest operation a disk offers, and the event store needs only two reads: all events of a stream, and all events after a global position. Because the log is ordered and durable, replay is deterministic: the same events always produce the same state. Many implementations use an ordinary database underneath, for example a table (stream_id, version, type, data, metadata, global_position) with a unique index on (stream_id, version). That unique index is what enforces the optimistic concurrency check.
The read path is separate. Projectors subscribe to the global event stream, read events in order, and update read models: a SQL table of current balances, an Elasticsearch index for search, a Redis cache, a data warehouse table. Each projector stores a checkpoint of its position. Delivery is at-least-once: if a projector crashes after updating a read model and before saving its checkpoint, it will see the last events again. Idempotence (L2) is therefore a requirement: the handler must produce the same result when an event is applied twice. Two common techniques are to write the read-model change and the checkpoint in a single database transaction, or to make the update itself idempotent (set balance = 70 at version 3, instead of subtract 30), or to ignore events whose version is not greater than the stored one.
This design gives a very useful property: any read model can be thrown away and rebuilt by replaying the log from the start. A new report, a new search index or a bug fix in a projection requires no data migration; you deploy the new projector, let it catch up, and then switch queries to it. Replays take time: at 20,000 events per second a 200-million-event history needs about 3 hours, so large systems rebuild in parallel and keep new and old projections side by side during the switch.
Snapshots handle long streams. After every N events (for example 100), the system stores the aggregate's state and its version. Loading then reads the snapshot and only events after it. Snapshots are a cache, never the truth, and can be dropped and rebuilt.
Schema evolution is the hardest part, because events are stored forever. You cannot alter old events. Instead you add fields with defaults, version event types (OrderPlaced.v2), and use upcasters that translate old versions into the current shape while reading. Schema evolution rules (L2 concept) such as additive changes and explicit versions apply to events as they do to Kafka messages. Events should capture business facts and intent (CustomerMovedAddress), not table diffs (AddressColumnChanged), since facts stay meaningful when the implementation changes.
Finally, consistency. After a command succeeds, the read models lag by milliseconds to seconds. A user who submits a form and is redirected to a list may not see their change. Systems handle this by returning the new state or version from the command, by letting the UI wait until the read model passes that version, or by showing the result optimistically. Publishing events to other systems needs care: writing to the event store and publishing to Kafka are two operations that can fail separately, so use a subscription from the store, or an outbox table written in the same transaction and relayed to the broker.

example: Take a bank account A with this stream: v1 AccountOpened, v2 MoneyDeposited(100), v3 MoneyWithdrawn(30). Replaying gives balance = 0 + 100 − 30 = 70.
Command one: "withdraw 50". The handler loads three events, folds them to balance 70, checks the rule 70 − 50 ≥ 0, and appends MoneyWithdrawn(50) at expected version 3. The stream now has v4, and the balance is 20.
Race. Two clients send "withdraw 50" at the same moment. Both load three events and both compute balance 70. Both decide the withdrawal is valid. Both try to append at expected version 3. The unique index on (stream, version) allows only one to create v4. The second append fails. It reloads four events, folds to balance 20, checks 20 − 50 < 0, and rejects the command with "insufficient funds". No lock was held, and no overdraft happened.
Projection and duplicates. A projector keeps a table balance(account, amount, last_version). It receives MoneyWithdrawn(50) at version 4. It runs, in one transaction: update balance set amount = 20, last_version = 4 where account = A and last_version < 4, and store the checkpoint. If the process crashes before commit, nothing is saved and the event is reprocessed. If it crashes after commit but before an acknowledgement, the event is delivered again. The condition last_version < 4 makes the second run do nothing. That is idempotence in practice.
Snapshots. Suppose the account has 10,000 events and applying an event takes 10 microseconds. A full replay costs 100 ms for each command, which is too slow at 1,000 commands per second on one account. With a snapshot every 100 events, a load reads the snapshot and at most 99 events, about 1 ms. The price is a stored snapshot every 100 events (1% extra rows).
Rebuild. A new report needs "total withdrawn per month". You write a new projector and replay 50 million events at 25,000 events per second: 50,000,000 / 25,000 = 2,000 seconds, a bit over half an hour. You switch the dashboard to it when it has caught up. No migration of the old data was needed because the facts were already there.
intuition: A bank ledger: you never erase an entry, you add a correcting one, and the balance is something you compute from the lines.
The design is natural because state is a summary and the events are the truth. If you keep the truth, you can compute any summary now or later, but if you keep only one summary you can never recover what it left out. Append-only writes are the cheapest and safest kind: no update in place, no lost history, and conflicts are detected by a simple version number. The cost is moved to the readers, who must build the views they need, and CQRS accepts this by making those views separate and disposable.
mistakes: People often think event sourcing means using Kafka. In fact event sourcing is a way of storing the state of an aggregate. Kafka is a distributed log that can carry events, but it has no cheap "read all events of key X" operation and no per-key optimistic concurrency check, so it is a poor primary store for aggregates. Many teams keep the event store in a database and publish events to Kafka for others.
People often think event sourcing and event-driven architecture are the same. In fact event-driven architecture means services communicate by sending events to each other. You can do that with ordinary databases. Event sourcing means one service stores its own state as events. They combine well but are independent.
People often think CQRS requires event sourcing, or the reverse. In fact CQRS only needs separate write and read models; they can be fed from a normal database through change data capture. Event sourcing can be used with a single model, for example by loading the aggregate and querying events directly for small systems. Use each when it earns its cost.
People often think that "immutable" means personal data cannot be deleted. In fact laws such as GDPR and the California Delete Act require deletion. Practical approaches are to keep personal data out of the events (store a reference to it in a separate, deletable store), or to encrypt each person's data with their own key and delete the key (crypto-shredding), or to rewrite the stream in a controlled migration. Plan this before the first event is written.
People often think replaying is cheap and always works. In fact replay depends on code that must still understand every old event. If the meaning of an event changed, or a handler depended on a changing outside system (a price list, exchange rates), replays give different answers. Events should carry the facts they used (the price at that time), and projections should avoid calling outside systems.
friction: The largest cost is schema evolution. Events live for years, and every producer and projection must understand all versions. Upcasters pile up, and renaming a concept means a migration strategy, not a refactor. The second cost is eventual consistency between the write side and read models, which affects user interfaces and tests, and needs work such as version tokens or wait-for-projection logic.
Operations are heavier: you run an event store, projectors with checkpoints, monitoring of projection lag, replay tooling, and dead-letter handling for events that a projection cannot process. A bug in a projector can poison a stream, and fixing it can need a rebuild that takes hours. Querying is limited: you cannot ask the event store "which accounts have balance below 10", only the read model. Cross-aggregate rules (a transfer between two accounts) need sagas or process managers, because a single append touches one stream, and these add failure modes with compensating events.
Storage grows forever. A busy system generating 10 million events per day with 500 bytes each adds 5 GB per day, close to 1.8 TB per year before compression, so you need archiving or tiered storage, and snapshots do not remove old events. Privacy deletion is awkward (see above). Finally, there is a cultural cost: developers must think in events and behaviours, not in tables, and many teams find the learning curve and boilerplate large. For simple CRUD screens with no value in history, it is the wrong tool.
practice: Use event sourcing for the part of the domain where the history is the business: payments, ledgers, orders, inventory, bookings, audit trails, collaborative editing, workflows with long lives. Keep the rest of the system as simple CRUD. Do not event-source everything.
Design events as business facts in the past tense, named in the language of the domain (PaymentCaptured, not PaymentUpdated). Make them immutable and small, include the data needed to understand them later (identifiers, amounts, currency, the price used), and add a version field and metadata (command id, correlation id, actor, time). Keep personal data out of events or encrypt it with per-subject keys.
Choose boundaries carefully. An aggregate should be a small consistency unit (one order, one account), with at most a few thousand events in its life, to keep loading cheap. Use snapshots only when a measured load time needs them. Enforce optimistic concurrency with a unique (stream, version) constraint.
Build projections as idempotent, replayable and disposable. Store the checkpoint with the read model in one transaction. Monitor lag per projector, with alerts on seconds behind the head. Practise a full rebuild on a copy regularly, and time it. Test with given–when–then scenarios on events.
Publish events outward with the outbox pattern or a store subscription, and give consumers a schema registry and compatibility rules. For a data platform, land the event stream in the lakehouse (for example via Kafka into Delta tables), where it gives a complete, replayable history for analytics. Begin with a store you already run (PostgreSQL) before adopting a specialised database, and move later if throughput requires.
tradeoffs: Choose event sourcing when the history has business value (audit, finance, dispute handling, time travel), when many different read models will be derived from the same facts, or when behaviour is complex and best described as a sequence of decisions. You accept a permanent event schema, eventual consistency and extra operations. Choose a normal state-based model with an audit table or change data capture when you only need a history for compliance. It captures what changed, not why, but costs far less.
Choose CQRS with separate read models when read and write loads or shapes differ strongly (writes need strict rules, reads need fast denormalised views, or search), or when different teams own different views. Choose a single model when one database serves both and read traffic is moderate, because every added read model is another thing to keep in sync and to monitor.
Choose snapshots when streams are long (thousands of events) and load latency matters; choose short-lived aggregates (one event stream per day per account, or per order) to avoid snapshots entirely. Snapshots add code and a cache to invalidate; short streams change the domain model instead.
Choose a purpose-built event store (KurrentDB, Axon Server) when you need per-stream guarantees, subscriptions and tooling out of the box and volumes are high. Choose PostgreSQL with a streams table when your team already runs it, volumes are under a few thousand events per second, and transactional writes of event plus outbox are valuable. Choose Kafka as the distribution layer, not as the aggregate store, unless you only need a stream of facts and not per-entity decisions.
Choose synchronous projections (updated in the same transaction as the append) when users must see their writes at once and volumes are small; choose asynchronous projections when read models are many, slow or in other systems. Synchronous ones are simple and consistent but couple the write latency to every read model. Asynchronous ones scale and isolate failures but give stale reads.
interview: How do you guarantee that two concurrent commands cannot both overdraw an account in an event-sourced system? || Each append states the expected stream version. A unique constraint on (stream id, version) lets only one writer create the next version. The loser gets a conflict, reloads the new events, re-evaluates the rule and either retries or is rejected. No locks are needed because the aggregate is the consistency boundary, and the order of events within one stream is total.
Why must projections be idempotent, and how do you achieve it? || Delivery from the log is at-least-once: a crash between applying an event and saving the checkpoint causes redelivery. Make the effect repeatable by writing the read-model change and the checkpoint in one transaction, using absolute updates keyed by version (apply only if event version is greater than the stored one), or storing processed event ids.
Your event schema must change for events written three years ago. What are your options? || Never rewrite stored events in the normal path. Add fields as optional with defaults, introduce a new event version and upcast old ones when reading, or run a controlled migration to a new stream while the old one is kept for audit. Use a schema registry or tests that replay old samples to prove compatibility.
How do you delete a user's personal data from an event store? || Prevent the need by keeping personal data out of events (store only an id and keep the data in a separate store that supports deletion). If the data is already in events, encrypt it per person and destroy the key (crypto-shredding), so that events remain but their personal fields become unreadable. Rewriting history is a last resort because it breaks replay guarantees.
A projection is 2 hours behind and growing. How do you diagnose it? || Compare event arrival rate with processing rate; look for a slow dependency (a database, external call), a poison event that retries forever, hot aggregates, single-threaded processing, or a missing index on the read-model update. Fixes include batching writes, partitioning the projector by stream hash, removing external calls, and rebuilding with a faster path. Alert on lag so this is caught in minutes.
When would you refuse to use event sourcing? || When the domain is simple CRUD with no value in history, when the team lacks the operational maturity for projections and replays, when strong cross-entity transactional queries dominate, or when privacy rules make immutable personal data impractical. A relational table with an audit log is then cheaper and sufficient.
quiz: How is the current state of an event-sourced aggregate obtained? || By reading its events in order and folding them, applying each to the state, optionally starting from a snapshot and replaying only the events after it.
What does optimistic concurrency prevent in an event store? || Two writers both deciding on the same old state and both appending. The second append, which carries the old expected version, fails, so the writer reloads and re-checks the rules.
Why is a snapshot not the source of truth? || It is a cache derived from the events at some version. It can be deleted and rebuilt by replay, while the events cannot be reconstructed from it.
An aggregate has 20,000 events, applying one takes 5 microseconds, and snapshots are taken every 200 events. What is the worst-case load time with and without snapshots? || Without: 20,000 × 5 µs = 100 ms. With: at most 199 events after the snapshot, about 1 ms, plus reading the snapshot.
When would you prefer change data capture on a normal database over event sourcing? || When you only need a stream of changes for downstream systems or for audit, and do not need to rebuild state from events or to record the intent behind each change. It adds far less complexity.

value: Data mesh is a way of organising people and data, not a product. In the usual setup, one central data team collects data from every part of the company, cleans it, models it and serves it to everyone. This works at first. Then the queue grows: every new report waits weeks for the central team, which does not understand the sales, ad-serving or billing details well enough, while the teams that do understand them have no responsibility for how their data is used. When a producing team renames a column, dashboards and models break far away, and nobody notices until a number is wrong.
Data mesh, proposed by Zhamak Dehghani in 2019, reverses the ownership. Each domain team (the team that runs the pixel service, or billing, or the segment pipeline) owns its analytical data and publishes it as a data product: a dataset or stream with a named owner, documentation, quality checks, a service level and a stable interface. A data contract is the written, versioned and automatically checked promise of that interface: schema, meaning of fields, freshness, quality, and how changes will be announced. A central platform team does not own the data; it provides the shared tools (storage, catalog, access control, pipelines, quality checks) so that domain teams can publish easily. A small federated governance group sets the rules that must hold everywhere, such as identity keys, privacy classes and naming.
Why it matters: it applies to data the same lesson that software engineering learned about services and APIs. The layers below help. Schema evolution (L2) is what a contract enforces: which changes are compatible (adding an optional column) and which are breaking (renaming or retyping). The data catalog (L1) is how data products are found, understood and governed. Above them, platforms such as Databricks with Unity Catalog and Delta Sharing are often used to build a mesh.
terms: Domain — a business area with its own language and team, such as billing, ad serving or identity.
Data product — a dataset, table, stream or API that a domain publishes for others, with an owner, documentation, quality guarantees and a stable interface.
Data contract — an explicit agreement between a producer and its consumers about schema, meaning, quality, freshness and change rules, checked by machines.
SLO / SLA — a measurable target for the data, such as "99% of events appear in the table within 15 minutes", and the commitment around it.
Self-serve data platform — shared infrastructure and tooling that lets domain teams build and publish data products without asking a central team.
Federated computational governance — rules decided jointly by domains and a central group, and enforced by code in the platform, not by manual review.
Data lineage — the recorded path of data from sources through transformations to its consumers.
Breaking change — a change that can make existing consumers fail or give wrong results, such as removing, renaming or retyping a column.
Producer / consumer — the team that publishes a data product and the teams that read it.
history: The older pattern was a central data warehouse (Inmon and Kimball, 1990s), where an expert team modelled all enterprise data. In the 2010s, data lakes made storage cheap by accepting any raw data, and the lakehouse (Delta, Iceberg, Hudi; Databricks popularised the name around 2020) added tables and transactions to files. Technology got better, but the organisational shape stayed: sources push data to a central team, which builds pipelines for everyone else.
Zhamak Dehghani, then at Thoughtworks, wrote "How to Move Beyond a Monolithic Data Lake to a Distributed Data Mesh" in 2019 and "Data Mesh Principles and Logical Architecture" in 2020, both on Martin Fowler's site, and her book Data Mesh (O'Reilly) followed in 2022. She named four principles: domain-oriented ownership, data as a product, a self-serve platform, and federated computational governance. The link to software is direct. Conway's law says systems mirror the communication structure of the organisation, and microservices and domain-driven design had already pushed software ownership towards domains.
The contract part grew from practice. Andrew Jones at GoCardless described data contracts around 2021 to 2022 after seeing database changes break analytics, and Chad Sanderson and others spread the idea of "shifting left" so that data quality is the producer's duty. Open specifications appeared, for example the Open Data Contract Standard (ODCS) and the Data Contract Specification, and tools began to support contracts directly: schema registries for streams, dbt model contracts (since version 1.5, 2023), and quality tools such as Great Expectations and Soda. Data mesh also attracted criticism: it is a large organisational change, many companies adopted only parts, and for small organisations it is more structure than needed. Today the most widely used parts are domain ownership, contracts for key datasets, and a catalog.
how: The mesh has four moving parts, and each addresses a specific failure of the central model.
First, domain ownership. The team that creates the data is responsible for publishing it in a form others can use. This means that the team has people with data skills (or an embedded data engineer), and a backlog item for its data products as for its service features. The reasoning is the same as "you build it, you run it": the producer knows what a field means and notices when it changes.
Second, data as a product. A data product has a defined interface (a Delta or Iceberg table, a Kafka topic, a view, an API), documentation in plain language, a named owner and an on-call route, quality checks, a freshness target, and a lifecycle (versions, deprecation dates). Dehghani's list of qualities is that it is discoverable, addressable (a stable name), understandable, trustworthy, natively accessible, interoperable, valuable by itself, and secure. In practice, a product is published to the catalog with its metadata and shows up in search with lineage and usage.
Third, the data contract. The contract makes the interface checkable. It lists the schema (columns, types, nullability, keys), the semantics (what "impression" means, time zone, units such as micros of currency), quality rules (null rate, uniqueness, value ranges, referential checks), service levels (freshness, completeness, availability), the privacy classification, and the change policy (how much notice before a breaking change, how long old versions live). This builds on schema evolution (L2): the contract states which changes are compatible. Adding an optional column is allowed at any time. Removing or retyping a column is a breaking change and requires a new major version, published in parallel with the old one until consumers have moved. A contract is enforced in three places. In CI, a producer's pull request is compared against the registered contract, and a breaking change fails the build. At write time, the pipeline validates the data (for a stream, through a schema registry; for tables, through constraints and expectations) and quarantines bad records. At run time, monitors check freshness and quality and alert the owner before consumers see it.
Fourth, the self-serve platform and federated governance. If every domain had to build storage, scheduling, access control, a catalog and quality tooling on its own, the result would be duplicated work and inconsistent practices. The platform team provides paved paths: templates to create a data product, a catalog (the data catalog and governance concept), automatic lineage, identity and access policies, cost reporting by domain, and ready-made quality checks. Governance is "computational": policies such as "all columns holding personal data must be tagged and masked for non-privileged readers" are implemented in the platform (tags, column masks, row filters, in Unity Catalog or similar), so that they apply automatically, and a small group of domain representatives decides the global standards: common identifiers (for example how a customer or device key is defined), time and currency conventions, interoperability formats.
A contract is usually a short text file kept in the producer's repository next to the code. Its content looks like a list: dataset name and version, owner, a schema block with each column (name, type, whether it may be null, whether it is a key, a description and a privacy tag), a quality block (for example "null rate of campaign_id at most 0.1%", "impression_id unique"), a service-level block (freshness 20 minutes, completeness 99.5%), and a terms block (support channel, notice period for breaking changes, retention). Because it is a file in version control, a change to the contract is itself a reviewed pull request, and the consumers can subscribe to it. Tools read the same file to generate the table definition, the CI check and the monitors, so there is one source for the promise and for its enforcement.
Consumption works through the catalog. A consumer finds a product, reads its contract and SLO, requests access (approved by the owner, enforced by the platform), and reads it by a stable address. Sharing across organisations or clouds can use open protocols such as Delta Sharing. Cross-domain products ("customer 360") are built by consumers combining the domain products, and these are published as products too, with their own owners. Lineage and usage metrics show who depends on what, so owners know whom a change affects.

example: Take an ad-tech company with a pixel (impression) service owned by team P, and 12 downstream consumers: billing, reporting, three ML pipelines, segment builds and others.
Before the mesh. Team P writes raw events to a data lake. A central data team builds a cleaned impressions table. When team P renames campaign_id to cmp_id in its events, the central job silently produces nulls, and 12 consumers see wrong numbers. Suppose it takes 6 engineer-hours to find the cause in each affected consumer and there are 4 such incidents per year: 12 × 4 × 6 = 288 engineer-hours per year, plus the trust damage when a billing number is wrong.
With a product and a contract. Team P publishes impressions_v2 as a Delta table with this contract: columns impression_id (string, not null, unique), event_ts (timestamp, not null), campaign_id (string, null rate at most 0.1%), device_id_hash (string, personal-data class "pseudonymous"), cost_micros (bigint, units of one millionth of a dollar). Freshness: 95% of events visible within 20 minutes. Completeness: at least 99.5% of the count seen by the ingest layer. Owner: team P, on-call alias. Change policy: breaking changes only as a new major version, with the previous version kept for 90 days.
Now repeat the rename. Team P opens a pull request that renames the field. CI compares the new schema with the registered contract and fails: "column campaign_id removed: breaking change". Team P has two options. It can keep campaign_id and add cmp_id (compatible), or publish impressions_v3 with the new name and keep v2 running, fed by the same pipeline, for 90 days. A notification goes to the 12 registered consumers from the catalog's lineage. Each moves during the 90 days.
Run time. One night a deployment bug leaves campaign_id null in 4% of rows. The write-time check sees 4% above the 0.1% limit and quarantines the batch instead of publishing it, and pages team P. Consumers see a delayed table with a "freshness breached" status instead of silently wrong data. The cost is that team P now spends some effort on tests and on-call: perhaps 1 engineer-week to set up and a few hours per month. Compared with 288 hours of firefighting, it is a good trade at this size. For a company with two teams and one consumer, the same effort would not pay.
intuition: Treat data like an API that other teams depend on, owned by the people who understand it best.
When an organisation grows, a central team cannot know every domain, so knowledge and responsibility drift apart. Software solved this by giving each service an owner and an interface, and by making breaking changes visible through versioning and tests. Data mesh applies the same solution: put responsibility next to knowledge, make the interface explicit, and let machines enforce it. The platform and governance parts exist because independence without shared tools and rules would give a hundred incompatible dialects.
mistakes: People often think data mesh is a technology that you can buy. In fact it is an organisational and operating model. Tools such as catalogs, contract checkers and platforms support it, but a vendor cannot give you domain ownership or product thinking.
People often think a data mesh means no central team. In fact the central role changes from "building all pipelines" to "building the platform and the guardrails". Without a strong platform team, each domain reinvents tooling, and the result is a mesh of inconsistent islands with duplicated cost.
People often think a data contract is only a schema. In fact schema is the easy part. A contract also states meaning (units, time zone, definitions), quality, freshness, privacy class, ownership and the change process. A column with the right type and the wrong meaning breaks consumers as badly as a missing one.
People often think a contract file protects you by existing. In fact a contract that nothing enforces is documentation that drifts. It must be checked in CI, at write time and at run time, and violations must have an owner who is paged.
People often think every company needs data mesh. In fact the pattern solves the scaling problem of many domains and one overloaded central team. With fewer than about five domain teams and one capable data team, a well-run central platform is simpler and cheaper. Adopt the parts that match your pain, for example contracts for the ten most-used datasets.
friction: The cost is mostly human. Domain teams must have data skills and time, and their priorities (features) compete with data-product work. Product thinking has to be rewarded in planning and performance reviews, or data products decay. Hiring data engineers into every domain is costly, and a thin embedded model often leaves a single person as owner of a critical product.
Consistency is harder across domains. The same word ("customer", "active device") may mean different things in different domains, and federation needs people to agree on shared identifiers and definitions, which is slow politics more than technology. Cross-domain joins, lineage and cost allocation are still needed, and answering a question that spans five products can be harder than querying one central model.
Tooling is young. Contract standards exist, but formats and tools differ, runtime enforcement for batch tables is less mature than for streams with schema registries, and support for business semantics (units, definitions) is mostly documentation. Versioning has a real price: running v2 and v3 together for 90 days doubles the cost of the product, and consumers that never migrate force the owner to keep old versions for years.
There is a risk of over-engineering: metadata for its own sake, review boards that become the new bottleneck, and a catalog nobody reads. Finally, security and compliance must be designed into the platform, because decentralised ownership spreads the places where personal data can leak, and requests such as deletion must reach every data product that holds the data.
practice: Start small and start with pain. Pick the 5 to 10 datasets that many teams depend on, or that have caused the most incidents. For each, write a contract with a named owner: schema, field meanings, keys, freshness target, quality rules, privacy class, and the change policy. Publish it in the catalog next to the data.
Automate enforcement from the first day. Add a CI check that compares schema changes against the contract and blocks breaking ones. Add write-time validation with a quarantine path for bad records. For streams, use a schema registry with a compatibility mode. For tables, use constraints and expectations (Delta constraints, dbt contracts, Great Expectations, Soda). Add freshness and volume monitors that page the owner, not the consumers.
Build the platform as a product with domain teams as customers. Make the right way the easy way: a template that creates a table, registers it in the catalog, applies tags and access policies, adds default quality checks and lineage in one step. Measure time from "new product idea" to "published product" and drive it down. Show each domain its storage and compute cost.
Define a small number of global rules: identifier standards, personal-data tags and handling, naming, time and currency conventions, retention. Implement them in the platform (tag-based masking, row filters) so that they apply without reviews. Keep the governance group small and give it a regular, short meeting.
For change management, give every breaking change a deprecation notice from the catalog's lineage, a parallel-run window (for example 60 to 90 days), and usage tracking so you can see who still reads the old version. Track a few outcome measures: incidents caused by upstream changes, time to onboard a new consumer, and share of key datasets with an enforced contract.
tradeoffs: Choose a data mesh approach when you have many domain teams (roughly ten or more), the central data team is a bottleneck with queues of weeks, and incidents come from upstream changes that nobody owns. You accept an organisational change, platform investment and the need for data skills in domains. Choose a central data platform with a strong team when the company is small, domains are few, or data skills are scarce. It is cheaper, more consistent, and easier to govern, and you give up scalability of ownership.
Choose full mesh (ownership, products, platform, governance) when you can fund the platform team and have leadership support for domain accountability. Choose "contracts only" when the main pain is breakage at boundaries: you get most of the incident reduction with little reorganisation. A common path is contracts on key datasets first, then domain ownership, then self-service, then federated governance.
Choose strict, machine-enforced contracts for datasets with money or legal consequences (billing, compliance) and many consumers. Choose lighter contracts (schema plus owner plus freshness) for exploratory or low-use datasets, because heavy process on every table slows teams and breeds workarounds.
Choose a shared enterprise catalog and one platform (for example a single lakehouse with its governance layer) when you want simple access control and lineage. Choose federation across several platforms and clouds with open protocols (Delta Sharing, Iceberg REST catalogs) when mergers, regulations or cost force data to remain in separate systems. The second gives freedom and costs more integration, and lineage and policy become harder to keep uniform.
Choose a long parallel-run window for breaking changes when consumers are many and slow to change (90 days or more); choose a short window and fast migration when consumers are few and in the same organisation. A longer window protects consumers and costs the producer storage, compute and attention.
interview: Where would you enforce a data contract, and why in more than one place? || In CI (a producer's change is compared with the registered contract and breaking changes fail), at write time (validate records and quarantine bad data before it is published), and at run time (monitor freshness, volume and quality and alert the owner). CI catches planned changes, write checks catch bugs and bad inputs, and run-time monitors catch drift and late data. Each covers a failure the others cannot.
A producer wants to rename a column used by 30 pipelines. Walk through the process. || Treat it as breaking. Either add the new column and keep the old one (compatible), or publish a new major version of the product. Use catalog lineage to find and notify the 30 consumers, run both versions in parallel for an agreed window, track who still reads the old one, then retire it on the published date. Do not change the old version in place.
What is the difference between a data catalog and a data contract? || A catalog is the index: where data products are, who owns them, what they contain, how they are classified and who uses them. A contract is the promise about one product: schema, semantics, quality, service levels and change rules, with checks that can fail a build or a run. A catalog without contracts is a directory of unreliable data, and contracts without a catalog are not discoverable.
Why can a data mesh fail without a good platform team? || Each domain would have to build storage, scheduling, access control, quality checks and catalog integration itself, which duplicates effort, produces different conventions, and puts heavy demands on domain teams. The platform provides paved paths and enforces global policy by code, which is what makes decentralisation cheap and consistent.
How do you handle a request to delete a person's data in a mesh? || Maintain classification tags and lineage so that you know which products hold the person's identifiers. Run the deletion through the platform to every tagged product (for example by a shared deletion service and a registry of products with personal data), prove completion with an audit record, and design products to hold the minimal personal data, with keys that allow targeted deletion.
How do you decide whether a company should adopt data mesh? || Look at symptoms: number of domains, queue time of the central team, incidents from upstream changes, and whether domains have capacity for data work. If the pain is mostly boundary breakage, begin with contracts. If the central team is the bottleneck and domains are mature, add ownership and a platform. If the company is small, keep a central team and revisit later.
quiz: What are the four principles of data mesh? || Domain-oriented ownership, data as a product, a self-serve data platform, and federated computational governance.
Which kinds of schema changes are usually compatible, and which are breaking? || Adding an optional column (with a default or nullable) is compatible. Removing, renaming or changing the type of a column, or changing its meaning, is breaking and needs a new version.
Why should a contract be checked in CI and at write time? || CI stops an incompatible change before deployment, while write-time validation catches bad data produced by bugs or unexpected input that no schema review could predict. They detect different failures.
A producer runs v2 and v3 of a product in parallel for 90 days. What does this cost and what does it buy? || It costs extra storage, compute and maintenance for two versions, and it buys consumers time to migrate without breakage, so the change is not forced on them at once.
When is a central data team a better choice than a mesh? || When the organisation is small, with few domains and a capable central team, because the central model is simpler, cheaper and consistent. The mesh pays off when the number of domains makes the central team a bottleneck.

value: A Dynamo-style store keeps a huge table of keys and values on many machines, so that reads and writes keep working even when machines fail or the network splits. Every key is copied to several nodes (typically three). Any replica that is up can accept a write, there is no single leader that must be reachable, and the copies are allowed to disagree for a short time and are repaired in the background. This is "always say yes to a write, reconcile later". The trade is clear: you get very high availability, low and predictable latency, and nearly linear scaling by adding nodes; you give up joins, multi-row transactions and, by default, the guarantee that every read sees the latest write.
The design came from a business problem. Amazon in the mid 2000s found that its shopping cart must accept "add to cart" even during a data-centre failure, because each failed request costs money. A relational database with a single primary and synchronous replication chooses consistency during failures, which means refusing writes. Dynamo (2007) chose the other side of the CAP theorem. Its ideas live on in Apache Cassandra, ScyllaDB, Riak, Voldemort, and in the partitioning and replication of Amazon DynamoDB, MongoDB's sharding, and many others. In ad tech, these stores hold user profiles, frequency-cap counters, segment membership and device graphs, where millions of tiny reads and writes per second must complete in single-digit milliseconds.
Many lower-layer ideas combine here. Consistent hashing (L2) assigns keys to nodes so that adding a node moves only a small share of the data. Quorums (L2) let you tune how many replicas must answer. Gossip (L2) spreads membership and failure information without a central coordinator. Vector clocks (L2) and CRDTs (L2) detect or merge concurrent updates. Read repair and anti-entropy (L2) make replicas converge. LSM trees (L2) make each node's writes sequential and fast. CAP (L2) and consistency models (L2) explain what is promised. The layer above, Cassandra, DynamoDB, ScyllaDB and MongoDB (L0), packages these into products.
terms: Replication factor (N) — the number of nodes that store a copy of each key; commonly 3.
Partition key — the part of the primary key that is hashed to decide which nodes own the row.
Token ring — the circle of hash values, with each node owning one or more ranges (virtual nodes).
Quorum (R, W) — the number of replicas that must answer a read (R) or acknowledge a write (W); if R + W > N, every read set overlaps every write set.
Coordinator — the node that receives a client request and forwards it to the replicas.
Hinted handoff — a stand-in node stores a write meant for an unreachable replica and delivers it when the replica returns.
Read repair — fixing stale replicas when a read notices that they differ.
Anti-entropy (Merkle trees) — a background comparison of replicas using hash trees, to find and fix differences without comparing every row.
Tombstone — a marker that records a delete, kept for a while so the delete reaches all replicas.
Last-write-wins (LWW) — resolving conflicts by keeping the value with the newest timestamp.
history: Relational databases scaled up (bigger machines) for decades. In the early 2000s, web companies hit limits. Google published the Bigtable paper (OSDI 2006): a sorted, sparse, multi-dimensional map stored on GFS, with a memtable and immutable SSTable files (the LSM design). Amazon published Dynamo (SOSP 2007, DeCandia and others): a key–value store built for the shopping cart that used consistent hashing, N/R/W quorums, vector clocks, sloppy quorums with hinted handoff, gossip membership, and Merkle-tree repair. It was designed to meet a latency target at the 99.9th percentile (the paper quotes a 300 ms goal at 500 requests per second), not the average.
Facebook's Avinash Lakshman, who had worked on Dynamo, and Prashant Malik built Cassandra (2008) by combining Dynamo's distribution with Bigtable's data model and storage, and open-sourced it; it became an Apache top-level project in 2010. Other Dynamo-inspired systems followed: Riak (Basho, with vector clocks and later CRDTs), Voldemort (LinkedIn), and ScyllaDB (2015), a C++ rewrite with a shard-per-core design that keeps Cassandra's data model. The word "NoSQL" came from a 2009 meetup about these systems. Document stores such as MongoDB (2009) joined the group, although MongoDB uses a leader per replica set.
Amazon DynamoDB (2012) is often confused with the Dynamo paper. It is a managed service that kept the name and some ideas (partitioning, replication across three availability zones) but is different in design: each partition has a leader chosen through consensus, and writes go through it, which gives strong consistency on request. The 2022 USENIX ATC paper describes it. Later years brought transactions into these systems (DynamoDB transactions 2018, Cassandra lightweight transactions using Paxos, and newer consensus-based transaction work in Cassandra), a sign that pure "eventual and no transactions" was too limiting for many applications. Distributed SQL databases (the neighbouring concept) took the opposite choice, consistency first.
how: Start with data placement. A key's partition key is hashed (Cassandra uses Murmur3) to a number on a ring, for example 0 to 2⁶⁴. Each node owns several ranges of the ring, called virtual nodes. The node that owns the range containing the hash is the primary replica; the next N − 1 distinct nodes clockwise (preferably in different racks or availability zones) hold the other copies. This is consistent hashing (L2). When you add a node, it takes a few ranges from many existing nodes, so only about 1/(number of nodes) of the data moves, and when a node leaves only its ranges move. Hashing spreads keys evenly, so load is balanced without a central directory. A client may contact any node. That node becomes the coordinator and uses the ring map, which every node holds, to find the replicas.
Writes. The coordinator sends the write to all N replicas and waits for W acknowledgements. Each replica appends the write to a commit log on disk (sequential, durable), adds it to an in-memory sorted structure (the memtable), and acknowledges. When the memtable is full (for example 100s of MB), it is written as an immutable sorted file (SSTable), and background compaction merges files. This LSM design (L2) turns random writes into sequential ones, which is why a node can accept tens of thousands of writes per second per core-set. A delete writes a tombstone, since an immutable file cannot be edited; tombstones are removed by compaction after a grace period (10 days by default in Cassandra) that allows all replicas to hear about the delete.
Reads. The coordinator asks R replicas (often one for the data and others for digests), compares the answers and returns the newest. Each node reads its memtable and the SSTables that may hold the key; Bloom filters (L2) skip files that cannot contain the key. If the digests differ, the coordinator performs read repair: it sends the newest value to the stale replicas. The quorum rule is simple: if R + W > N, the replicas that answered a read must include at least one that took part in the last successful write, so the read sees the newest value. With N = 3, W = 2, R = 2 you get that overlap and can tolerate one node down. With W = 1, R = 1 you get speed and availability but may read stale data. You choose per request, which is the "tunable consistency".
Failures. Nodes learn about each other by gossip (L2): every second, each node exchanges state with a few random peers, and information about joins, leaves and suspected failures spreads through the cluster in a logarithmic number of rounds. A failure detector (Cassandra uses the phi accrual detector) marks a node as suspect when heartbeats are late. If a replica is down during a write, the coordinator keeps a hint, a stored copy of the write, and delivers it when the node returns (hinted handoff). In Dynamo's sloppy quorum, another healthy node outside the usual replica set can take the write temporarily, to keep writes available. Hints are best effort, so a second mechanism catches what is missed: anti-entropy repair, in which replicas build Merkle trees (hash trees) of their key ranges, compare the roots and descend only into subtrees that differ, so they find a few differing rows among billions without sending all data.
Conflicts. Two clients may write the same key through different coordinators at the same time. Dynamo used vector clocks (L2): each version carries a map of counters per node, so the system can tell whether one version descends from another or whether they are concurrent. For concurrent versions it returned all of them (siblings) to the application, which merged them (for a cart, take the union). Cassandra and DynamoDB chose simplicity: last-write-wins by timestamp, per column. This is easy but can lose a concurrent update, and it depends on reasonably synchronised clocks. CRDTs (L2) are a better answer for some data: counters, sets and maps whose merge operation is commutative and idempotent, so replicas converge without losing updates (Riak provides them).
Data model. These systems are queried by key, so you design tables around queries. In Cassandra, the primary key is (partition key, clustering columns): the partition key picks the node, and clustering columns sort the rows within the partition on disk, so a range query inside a partition is a sequential read. There are no joins, so data is denormalised: the same fact is written to several tables, one per query. This moves the cost from read time to write time, which fits an LSM store that is good at writes.

example: Use a small ring with four nodes. Tokens are A = 25, B = 50, C = 75, D = 100, and each node owns the range up to its token (A owns 1 to 25, B owns 26 to 50, C owns 51 to 75, D owns 76 to 100). The replication factor is 3.
Placement. A key "u42" hashes to 62. It falls in C's range, so C is the primary, and the next two nodes clockwise, D and A, hold the other copies. Key "u7" hashes to 7, and its replicas are A, B and C.
Quorum write and read. Use N = 3, W = 2, R = 2. A client writes u42 = "gold" with timestamp 1000. The coordinator sends it to C, D and A. A is down. C and D acknowledge, so W = 2 is met and the client sees success. The coordinator stores a hint for A. Later a client reads u42 with R = 2 and the coordinator picks C and A (A has just come back, before the hint arrives). C returns "gold" at 1000, and A returns the old "silver" at 500. The newest timestamp wins, and the client gets "gold". Read repair sends "gold" to A. This works because R + W = 4 > 3, so any two replicas must include at least one of the two that took the write.
Weak setting. With W = 1 and R = 1, a write that reached only C is later read from A: the client gets "silver". There is no violation, only a different promise: 1 + 1 is not greater than 3, so stale reads are allowed until repair.
Capacity. You need 1 million writes per second, each stored on 3 replicas, which means 3 million replica writes per second. If one node sustains about 20,000 writes per second with compaction running, you need 3,000,000 / 20,000 = 150 nodes at full load, and about 200 to keep around 25% headroom. Storage: 10 TB of unique data with RF = 3 is 30 TB. If you keep disks under about 50% full to leave room for compaction, and each node has 4 TB, you need 30 / (4 × 0.5) = 15 nodes for space, so writes, not storage, set the cluster size here.
Clock hazard. Two writes to the same column arrive 2 ms apart, from different data centres whose clocks differ by 5 ms. The later one carries an earlier timestamp and is silently discarded as "older". This is the cost of last-write-wins, which is why counters or sets should use CRDT-like types, or a compare-and-set (lightweight transaction) when order matters.
intuition: Always say yes to a write somewhere, and reconcile the copies later.
Think of several clerks who each keep a copy of the same notebook, and a customer can speak to any clerk who is present. If one clerk is away, the others continue. When the clerk returns, the clerks compare notes and copy what is missing. The hard part is deciding what to do when two clerks wrote different things at the same time. The design is natural because it moves the cost of a failure from "refuse service" to "reconcile later", and for many data types (carts, profiles, counters, sessions) reconciling later is cheap and refusing service is expensive.
mistakes: People often think eventual consistency means data may be wrong forever. In fact it means that, if no new writes arrive, all replicas converge. The window is usually milliseconds to seconds, and quorum settings can narrow it. What you must handle is the window itself, and conflicting concurrent writes.
People often think NoSQL means no schema or no SQL. In fact Cassandra has typed schemas and a SQL-like language (CQL). What is missing is joins, flexible ad hoc queries and cross-partition transactions. The "no" is about access patterns, not about structure.
People often think R + W > N gives linearizability. In fact it gives a guarantee only in simple cases. With sloppy quorums, concurrent writes, clock-based conflict resolution, or a failed write that was partially applied, a read can still return stale or even flip-flopping values. Dynamo-style quorums are weaker than consensus; if you need linearizable compare-and-set, use the store's transaction feature, which uses Paxos or Raft.
People often think DynamoDB is the Dynamo paper as a service. In fact DynamoDB is a different system, with leaders per partition, strongly consistent reads as an option, and global secondary indexes, but it shares the key-value model and partitioning idea. Facts from one do not carry over to the other.
People often think that adding nodes always fixes performance. In fact a hot partition (one very popular key, or a key that grows without limit) stays on three nodes however many you add. Capacity grows only if keys are spread evenly and partitions stay small, with a common guideline in Cassandra of keeping partitions below roughly 100 MB.
friction: The application carries the burden. You must model data around queries, handle denormalised copies, decide on consistency per operation, and resolve or avoid conflicts. Changing the access pattern later often means a new table and a backfill. There is no ad hoc analytics, so teams export data to a warehouse or lakehouse for that.
Operations need care. Repair must run regularly (in Cassandra, within the tombstone grace period of 10 days), or deleted data can come back from a replica that never heard of the delete ("zombie data"). Compaction uses disk and I/O in bursts, so latency can spike; you need spare disk space (often 50% free for some strategies) and monitoring of pending compactions. Tombstones that accumulate (for example from queue-like patterns, or from deleting many rows) make reads scan many markers and can time out. Large partitions, hot keys, and wide rows cause uneven load. Adding or removing nodes (streaming data) takes hours and affects performance. Garbage-collection pauses in JVM-based systems hurt tail latency, which is one reason for C++ rewrites such as ScyllaDB.
Semantics are subtle. Last-write-wins loses updates under clock skew. Lightweight transactions are several times slower than normal writes because they run a consensus round (Paxos needs multiple round trips). Counters and secondary indexes have weaker or special behaviours. Multi-region setups bring cross-region latency into any quorum that spans regions (LOCAL_QUORUM avoids this, at the price of weaker cross-region guarantees). Cost is also an issue: replication factor 3 means 3 times the storage and write work, and managed pricing models (DynamoDB capacity units) can surprise teams that scan or hot-spot.
practice: Start from the queries. List every read and write your application needs, with the key it knows, the ordering it needs, and the expected rate. Design one table per query, with a partition key that has many distinct values and spreads load (for example user_id, or a (user_id, day) bucket for data that grows) and clustering columns that give the order you read in. Keep partitions bounded in size: add a time bucket to the key when needed.
Choose consistency per operation. A common Cassandra pattern is LOCAL_QUORUM for both reads and writes with RF = 3 in each data centre: it tolerates one node down per data centre and avoids cross-region latency. Use ONE for data where staleness is harmless (counters for metrics, caches) and use lightweight transactions or a transactional database for uniqueness and money. In DynamoDB, use eventually consistent reads (half the cost) unless a read must see the last write, and conditional writes for compare-and-set.
Prevent hot partitions and tombstone traps. Avoid sequential or low-cardinality keys, avoid using the store as a queue, set TTLs deliberately (TTL expiry also creates tombstones) and use time-window compaction for time series. Run repair on a schedule, monitor pending compactions, tombstones per read, p99 latency, dropped mutations and disk usage, and test node loss and restore before production.
Plan capacity from write rate times replication factor, with 30 to 50 percent headroom, and test with realistic data sizes. Keep a separate analytical copy (CDC or batch export to your lakehouse), and keep relational databases for relational workloads. Use TTL and data retention to bound the size. For global tables or multi-region writes, decide how conflicts resolve and test it with concurrent writes.
tradeoffs: Choose a Dynamo-style store when you need writes that always succeed, with single-digit millisecond latency, at very high rates (hundreds of thousands per second), and across multiple data centres, and the access pattern is by key. User profiles, session data, frequency caps, device graphs, time series and activity feeds fit. You give up joins, ad hoc queries and multi-row transactions. Choose a distributed SQL database (the neighbouring concept) when you need transactions across rows and strong consistency and can pay a consensus round trip on every write; choose a single PostgreSQL with replicas when data fits one machine (up to a few TB and a few thousand writes per second), which is simpler than any distributed store.
Choose quorum consistency (LOCAL_QUORUM, R + W > N) for data that users see and expect to be current, such as profiles after an update. Choose ONE or eventual consistency for high-volume, forgiving data such as counters, recommendations and caches. Quorum reads and writes cost an extra replica round trip and fail if two replicas are unavailable; ONE is faster and survives more failures but allows stale reads.
Choose last-write-wins when updates replace whole values and occasional loss of a concurrent update is tolerable. Choose CRDT types, version vectors with application merge, or transactions when updates must combine (counters, sets, carts) or when ordering matters. LWW is simple and cheap; the alternatives cost complexity and metadata.
Choose Cassandra or ScyllaDB when you want to run the system yourself or on any cloud, with control over data placement and tuning, and ScyllaDB when per-node efficiency matters (fewer, larger nodes; lower tail latency). Choose DynamoDB when you want no operations at all and can accept pricing per request and lock-in to AWS. Choose MongoDB when documents with rich queries and secondary indexes matter more than leaderless writes, accepting a leader-based design with failover pauses of seconds.
Choose a higher replication factor (5) for critical data across three or more regions where you can lose a region and still have a quorum; choose 3 for most cases. Each extra replica adds storage and write cost and slightly slows quorum operations.
interview: Explain how consistent hashing with virtual nodes helps when a node is added or fails. || Keys and nodes are mapped onto a ring, with each node owning many small ranges. A new node takes a few ranges from many nodes, so only about 1/N of the data moves, and the load is shared among many sources. When a node fails, its ranges are covered by many different neighbours rather than one, so no single node takes the whole extra load. Plain modulo hashing would remap almost every key.
With N = 3, what are the failure and consistency properties of W = 2, R = 2 versus W = 1, R = 1? || With W = R = 2, R + W = 4 > 3, so every read quorum intersects the last write quorum and reads see the latest acknowledged write in the normal case; it tolerates one node down for both reads and writes. With W = R = 1 there is no overlap guarantee, so reads can be stale until repair, but it tolerates two nodes down and is the fastest. Neither is linearizable under concurrent writes or sloppy quorums.
Why can a deleted row reappear in Cassandra, and how do you prevent it? || A delete writes a tombstone. If a replica was down when the delete happened and the tombstone is purged after gc_grace_seconds before that replica is repaired, the replica still holds the old row, and repair then copies it back as if it were new. Prevent it by running repair on every node more often than the grace period, and by not letting nodes stay down longer than the grace period without being rebuilt.
Your cluster shows a few nodes at 95% CPU while others idle. What do you check? || A hot partition or hot key (a popular entity, a low-cardinality key, or an unbounded partition), uneven token ownership, a bad client load balancing policy, or heavy compaction or repair on some nodes. Look at per-partition metrics, change the key (add a bucket or salt), and spread the access, rather than adding nodes.
When is last-write-wins acceptable and when is it dangerous? || Acceptable when each update replaces the full value and the newest information is the right one, and clock skew is small relative to the update rate. Dangerous for counters, sets, and read-modify-write patterns, because concurrent updates are dropped silently and skewed clocks can make an older write win. Use CRDTs, conditional writes or a transaction instead.
How does a Merkle tree make anti-entropy cheap? || Each replica hashes its rows into leaves, and hashes of children into parents up to a root. Two replicas compare roots; if equal, they hold the same data. If not, they compare children and descend only into mismatching subtrees, so they transfer just the differing ranges. The cost is the tree build over the data, and the benefit is network traffic proportional to the differences, not to the data size.
quiz: What does R + W > N guarantee? || That a read quorum and a write quorum share at least one replica, so a read contacts at least one node that saw the latest acknowledged write, and can return it by comparing timestamps or versions.
Why are tombstones needed in an LSM-based replicated store? || Files are immutable and replicas may be down, so a delete must be written as a marker that wins over older data and is kept long enough to reach every replica; deleting the data physically would let an out-of-date replica resurrect it.
With N = 3 and 1.5 million writes per second, how many replica writes per second must the cluster absorb? || 3 × 1.5 million = 4.5 million replica writes per second.
Two clients update the same shopping cart on different replicas at the same time. How does a Dynamo-style system with vector clocks handle it, and how does last-write-wins differ? || With vector clocks the system detects that the versions are concurrent and keeps both for the application to merge (for example by union). With last-write-wins it keeps only the one with the newer timestamp, so one update is lost.
When should you prefer a distributed SQL database over a Dynamo-style store? || When you need multi-row transactions, joins and strong consistency, and can accept a consensus round trip on writes; Dynamo-style stores are better when always-on writes and low latency across regions matter more.

value: A distributed SQL database (often called NewSQL) gives you what a single relational database gives you, SQL, secondary indexes and ACID transactions with strong isolation, but across many machines and often across regions. The data is cut into pieces (ranges or tablets) of a few hundred megabytes. Each piece is copied to three or five machines and kept in sync by a consensus protocol (Raft or Paxos). A transaction that touches several pieces commits atomically through a distributed commit protocol, and a timestamp scheme gives every transaction a place in one global order. To the application, it looks like one very large PostgreSQL or MySQL.
The problem it solves is the gap that Dynamo-style stores left. Those stores scale and stay available but push consistency, joins and transactions onto the application. Sharded MySQL scales but leaves cross-shard transactions, schema changes and resharding to the team. Google lived this with AdWords, which ran on a manually sharded MySQL for years; the answer was Spanner and the F1 database on top of it (about 2012). Spanner showed that a database can offer external consistency (the strongest form, strict serializability) at global scale. CockroachDB, TiDB, YugabyteDB and others brought the idea outside Google, and FoundationDB offers a strictly serializable key–value core.
These systems are assembled from many ideas below them. Raft (L2) and Paxos (L2) replicate each range so that it survives failures and has one agreed history. TrueTime (L2) and hybrid logical clocks (L2) give timestamps that make a global order of transactions possible. MVCC (L2) keeps old versions so that readers do not block writers and can read a consistent snapshot. Two-phase commit (L2) makes a transaction over several ranges all-or-nothing. CAP (L2), PACELC (L2) and consistency models (L2) describe what is chosen and what it costs. Deterministic simulation testing (L2) is how builders find rare bugs in this much concurrency. Above them are Spanner, CockroachDB, TiDB and FoundationDB (L0).
terms: Range (tablet, region) — a contiguous slice of the key space, a few hundred MB, that is the unit of replication and movement.
Replica group — the set of 3 or 5 copies of a range that run a consensus protocol.
Leader (leaseholder) — the replica that currently coordinates writes for a range and often serves consistent reads.
Serializable — transactions behave as if they ran one at a time in some order.
Strict serializability (external consistency) — serializable, and the order respects real time: if transaction A finishes before B starts, A comes first.
Timestamp ordering — assigning each transaction a timestamp, and using it to decide which versions it may see.
TrueTime — Google's clock API that returns an interval [earliest, latest] guaranteed to contain true time.
Hybrid logical clock (HLC) — a timestamp that combines the local physical clock with a logical counter, so it follows real time and also respects causality.
Commit wait — waiting out clock uncertainty before a transaction becomes visible.
Write intent — a provisional version of a row written by an uncommitted transaction.
history: Early scale-out relational efforts in the 2000s either partitioned by hand (sharded MySQL at Facebook and others) or gave up transactions to scale. Google's Percolator (OSDI 2010) added snapshot-isolation transactions on top of Bigtable by using a timestamp oracle and a two-phase commit with locks stored in table cells; it powered Google's incremental web indexing. Megastore (CIDR 2011) offered synchronous replication with Paxos but only inside small entity groups. Calvin (Thomson et al., SIGMOD 2012) proposed ordering transactions in advance so that nodes can run them deterministically.
Spanner (Corbett et al., OSDI 2012) brought it together: data in tablets, each tablet replicated with Paxos, read–write transactions with two-phase locking and two-phase commit across tablets, and TrueTime, a clock API built from GPS receivers and atomic clocks in each data centre, which exposes the clock uncertainty (about 1 to 7 ms, average around 4 ms in the paper). With it, Spanner can give snapshot reads at a timestamp without locks and external consistency for writes. F1 (VLDB 2013) put a SQL layer on it, and AdWords moved from MySQL. Cloud Spanner, the managed service, reached general availability in 2017. The term "NewSQL" was coined by Matthew Aslett (451 Group) around 2011, and Pavlo and Aslett's paper "What's Really New with NewSQL?" (2016) analysed the category.
Outside Google, former Google engineers started CockroachDB (2014 onward), using Raft per range and hybrid logical clocks because ordinary servers do not have atomic clocks. PingCAP started TiDB (2015) with TiKV, a Rust key–value layer using Raft and a Percolator-style transaction protocol with a central timestamp service, plus TiFlash for columnar analytics. YugabyteDB (2016) reuses the PostgreSQL query layer over its own storage. FoundationDB (2013, acquired by Apple 2015, open source 2018) is known for its deterministic simulation testing. Amazon Aurora DSQL (announced late 2024) added a multi-region, active-active design built on synchronised clocks. The trend is clear: SQL-compatible interfaces, Raft-replicated ranges, and cloud-managed offerings.
how: Data layout. A table is stored as a sorted map from key to row (primary key order), which is then cut into contiguous ranges. In CockroachDB, a range is a few hundred megabytes by default; in TiDB a "region" is on the order of 100 MB; in Spanner they are "splits". When a range grows beyond the limit or receives too much traffic, it is split in two, and when it is small and quiet it can be merged. A placement component (CockroachDB's allocator, TiDB's PD, Spanner's placement driver) moves ranges between nodes to balance size and load, and to follow placement rules such as "keep this table's European customers in Europe".
Replication. Each range is a Raft group (CockroachDB, TiKV, YugabyteDB) or Paxos group (Spanner) of three or five replicas, in different failure domains (zones or regions). One replica is the leader. A write goes to the leader, which appends it to its log and sends it to followers; once a majority has stored it, it is committed and applied. With three replicas, a write needs one follower to acknowledge, so the latency is the round trip to the nearest follower plus a disk write. If the leader fails, the remaining replicas elect a new one, typically within seconds (Raft election timeouts), and no committed data is lost. That is how these databases choose consistency over availability in the CAP sense: a range that has lost its majority stops accepting writes, while the other ranges continue.
Storage inside a node uses an LSM tree (RocksDB in TiKV and Yugabyte, Pebble in CockroachDB) or similar, and MVCC: every write creates a new version of the key tagged with the transaction's timestamp. A reader at timestamp T sees, for each key, the newest version with timestamp ≤ T. Because old versions are retained for a while, readers never block writers, and long read-only queries can run on a consistent snapshot. Old versions are garbage-collected after a configured time.
Timestamps and ordering. For correct ordering across nodes, each transaction needs a timestamp that respects real time. Spanner assigns a commit timestamp from TrueTime and then waits, "commit wait", until that timestamp is certainly in the past on every clock (about the uncertainty interval, \( 2\varepsilon \)) before releasing locks and acknowledging. Any transaction that starts after this one is acknowledged will get a larger timestamp, so the order matches real time. CockroachDB uses hybrid logical clocks: each timestamp is (physical time, logical counter), updated by messages, so causality is respected without special hardware. Because the physical clocks are only loosely synchronised, a reader may encounter a value that was written at a time within the maximum clock offset (500 ms by default) above its timestamp, and it must then retry at a later timestamp ("uncertainty restart"), and a node whose clock drifts too far shuts itself down to protect correctness. TiDB gets timestamps from a central timestamp oracle in PD, which is simple and strict but adds a round trip, and is a single logical point. FoundationDB uses a sequencer that hands out versions.
Distributed transactions. A transaction that writes keys in several ranges uses a form of two-phase commit across Raft groups. The coordinator writes provisional "write intents" on each range (each replicated by consensus), records the transaction as committed in a transaction record (which is one atomic Raft write), and then asynchronously resolves the intents. Because the commit decision lives in a replicated record, the classic problem of two-phase commit, a coordinator crash leaving participants blocked, is reduced: another node can look at the record and finish or abort the transaction. CockroachDB's "parallel commits" lets the commit record and the intents be written in the same round, saving a round trip. Conflicts are handled by lock waiting or by timestamp pushing and retries; at serializable isolation a transaction that cannot be ordered gets a retryable error and the client must retry.
SQL layer. A SQL gateway on each node parses, plans and executes queries. It maps tables and indexes to key ranges, finds the ranges involved, and sends sub-queries to the leaseholders (distributed execution), pushing filters and aggregations to the data. Secondary indexes are themselves ranges, so a write that changes an indexed column touches more than one range and becomes a distributed transaction. Schema changes run online in stages without blocking reads and writes.
Testing. A system with this much concurrency fails in rare interleavings. FoundationDB pioneered deterministic simulation testing (L2): the whole cluster, network and disks run in one process with a seeded random scheduler and injected faults, so any failure can be replayed exactly. Jepsen tests from outside check isolation claims. Both are now expected of serious databases.

example: Take a three-region deployment: us-east, us-west and europe. Round trip times are roughly 65 ms between east and west, 80 ms between east and Europe, and 140 ms between west and Europe. A table of accounts is replicated in all three regions with Raft, so the database survives the loss of a whole region. The leaseholder for a range is in us-east.
A single-row write from a client in us-east. The leader appends to its log and needs one follower to confirm, which is a majority of 3. The nearest follower is us-west at 65 ms. So the write takes about 65 ms plus disk time, say 70 ms. A same-region write would have taken 2 to 5 ms. This is the PACELC cost (L2): even with no failure and no partition, durability across regions costs one wide-area round trip. If you instead place replicas in three zones of one region, a write needs about 2 ms and survives a zone failure but not a regional outage.
A read from us-east of the same row, at the leader (leaseholder), needs no consensus round: the leaseholder knows it holds the lease and serves from local data, so it is about 1 ms. A client in Europe reading the same row pays about 80 ms to reach the leader, unless the application accepts a slightly stale read from a local follower ("follower read" at a timestamp a few seconds in the past), which costs a few ms.
A transfer between two accounts in different ranges, with 10 from account 150 (range 7) to account 850 (range 12), both led in us-east. The transaction takes a timestamp, writes intents on both ranges in parallel (each needs a majority, about 65 ms), and writes the commit record. With parallel commits the commit decision is nearly implied by the staged record, so the client sees success after roughly one to two consensus rounds, about 65 to 130 ms. If the two ranges had leaders in different regions, the coordinator would also pay the wide-area hop between them. Compare this with a single PostgreSQL primary where the same transfer costs about 1 ms, but where the whole database is one machine and one failure domain.
Commit wait. With TrueTime uncertainty ε = 4 ms, a Spanner write waits about \( 2\varepsilon \) = 8 ms after choosing the commit timestamp. This overlaps with the Paxos replication, so with replicas in other zones the added cost can be small. The benefit: if client X sees its commit succeed and then tells client Y to read, Y is guaranteed to see X's write, even if Y talks to a replica in another continent.
Contention. If a table has a sequential primary key (an auto-increment id or a timestamp), every insert lands in the last range and one leader handles all inserts: the cluster of 30 nodes behaves like 1 node for that table, and inserts max out at the capacity of one range, perhaps a few thousand per second. Using a random UUID or a hash-prefixed key spreads writes over all ranges.
intuition: Many small replicated databases stitched together into one logical database, with a clock that decides who came first.
The design is natural because it solves one problem at each level with a known tool. Failure of a machine: replicate the range with consensus. Size: split the key space into ranges and spread them. Atomicity across ranges: commit protocol with a durable decision record. Ordering across the world: timestamps that respect real time. Each tool is well understood, and the novelty is making them work together fast enough. The cost is also natural: every step that needs agreement waits for a message round trip, and the speed of light sets the minimum.
mistakes: People often think NewSQL removes the CAP trade-off. In fact it chooses consistency. If a range loses its majority (for example, a network split isolates one region holding two of three replicas), writes to that range stop on the minority side. What these systems do is reduce unavailability to the affected ranges and recover fast, not avoid the choice.
People often think distributed SQL is faster than a single node database. In fact each write pays at least one consensus round trip, and cross-range transactions pay more. It is built to scale throughput beyond one machine, to survive failures without downtime and to span regions. For a workload that fits one PostgreSQL, a single node is faster per query and much simpler.
People often think you need atomic clocks. In fact only Spanner's TrueTime relies on special hardware to bound the uncertainty tightly. CockroachDB uses HLCs with a configured maximum offset, YugabyteDB uses HLC, and TiDB uses a central timestamp service. They pay with uncertainty restarts or an extra network hop instead. Cloud time services have narrowed the gap, since AWS offers a time service with microsecond-level accuracy and published error bounds on its instances.
People often think the isolation level is always serializable. In fact defaults differ: Spanner and CockroachDB give serializable isolation by default, but TiDB's default "repeatable read" is snapshot isolation, which allows write skew, and several systems offer weaker read committed modes for compatibility. Check the isolation level and the anomalies it permits.
People often think a MySQL or PostgreSQL compatible database behaves exactly like the original. In fact wire compatibility is not behaviour compatibility: features such as some locking behaviours, sequences, triggers, extensions and performance characteristics differ. Test your application, in particular long transactions and hot rows.
friction: Latency is the main cost. Every write waits for consensus, and cross-region writes pay the wide-area round trip, which is tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Multi-statement transactions chat with the database several times, so an interactive transaction with 10 statements, each with 2 ms of latency, costs 20 ms before the commit. Applications need to send fewer, larger statements, use stored procedures or batch operations, and keep hot data near its writers (regional tables, leader placement, follower reads).
Contention is the second cost. Hot rows (counters, popular items) serialize on one range, and transactions that conflict have to wait or retry. A retry loop is a part of the client contract. Sequential keys create hot ranges. Secondary indexes make writes distributed. Large transactions (millions of rows) hit limits.
Operations are heavier than for one node: you manage several nodes, clock health, range rebalancing, upgrades with mixed versions, backups across ranges, and costs for cross-zone and cross-region network traffic. Debugging performance means reading distributed traces and range hotspots. Licences and cloud lock-in also matter: some systems have changed from open source to source-available or commercial licences, and managed services price per request or per capacity.
Open problems include cheaper cross-region transactions (reducing round trips, geo-partitioned designs), mixing transactional and analytical workloads without hurting each other (HTAP; TiDB uses a separate columnar replica), and testing: even with simulation and Jepsen, subtle bugs in isolation and recovery are found regularly.
practice: Choose distributed SQL only when one machine is not enough, or when you need survival of zone or region loss with no manual failover, or multi-region writes with strong consistency. Otherwise a well-tuned PostgreSQL with replicas is simpler and faster; a surprisingly large workload (several TB, tens of thousands of transactions per second) fits.
Design keys for distribution. Do not use sequential integers or timestamps as the leading primary-key column; use UUIDs, or hash-sharded or bucketed keys. Put the column that groups related rows first so that they are in the same range (for example (tenant_id, order_id)), so that common transactions stay within one range, which avoids the distributed commit.
Keep transactions short and few. Batch statements, avoid interactive transactions across the network, retry on serialization errors with backoff (the client driver should do this), and avoid hot-row counters (split a counter into N rows and sum them). Use follower reads or stale reads for dashboards and listings that tolerate seconds of staleness. Place leaders near the writers (regional by row, or locality-aware placement) and test cross-region latency for each write path.
Test failure. Kill a node, a zone and, if you claim regional resilience, a region, and observe latency and error rates. Check clock sync health (chrony, cloud time services) and alert on offset. Watch range count and hot ranges, replica imbalance, and transaction retry rates. Practise backup and restore, including point-in-time recovery, and rehearse upgrades.
Check the isolation level you really have, and what anomalies are possible. Measure with your actual workload and data volume, not a vendor benchmark. Consider whether a simpler design, such as per-tenant sharded PostgreSQL, or a Dynamo-style store for the key-value parts, gives you what you need at a lower cost.
tradeoffs: Choose distributed SQL when you need relational transactions with strong consistency and also horizontal scale or multi-region survival, for example payments, inventory, user accounts for a global service, or an ad-platform metadata store that must remain correct during zone failures. You pay in write latency (a consensus round trip), operational complexity and cost. Choose a Dynamo-style store when the access pattern is by key, latency of a few milliseconds at huge write rates matters most, and you accept weaker consistency. Choose a single PostgreSQL/MySQL with a standby when the data fits one machine and a few seconds or minutes of failover is acceptable.
Choose Spanner (or Cloud Spanner) when you are on Google Cloud and want the strongest guarantees, global scale and managed operations; its clocks give external consistency with low overhead. Choose CockroachDB or YugabyteDB when you want a PostgreSQL-flavoured interface and portability across clouds or your own data centres, and accept clock-offset limits and licensing terms. Choose TiDB when MySQL compatibility and a mixed transactional and analytical workload (TiFlash) matter. Choose FoundationDB when you want to build your own data model on a strictly serializable key–value store and can accept its transaction limits (about 5 seconds and 10 MB per transaction).
Choose replicas in three zones of one region when your goal is zone-failure survival and low latency; choose three regions when you must survive a region outage and can afford 50 to 150 ms writes. Choose "regional by row" or geo-partitioned data when users are tied to a region and most transactions are local: you get local latency and data residency, and you give up cheap cross-region transactions.
Choose serializable isolation when correctness matters more than avoiding retries, and snapshot or read committed when you need compatibility with an existing application and can audit it for write skew. The weaker levels reduce retries and wait time and can allow anomalies.
interview: Why do distributed SQL systems need synchronised clocks or a timestamp oracle? || Each transaction needs a timestamp that orders it, so that MVCC snapshots and strict serializability work across nodes. If a later transaction could get a smaller timestamp than an earlier, acknowledged one, reads would miss committed writes. Spanner bounds uncertainty with TrueTime and waits it out. CockroachDB uses HLCs and restarts reads that fall inside the uncertainty window. TiDB asks a central oracle, at the cost of an extra hop.
Explain commit wait in Spanner. || After choosing a commit timestamp s at least as large as TrueTime's latest bound, the leader waits until TrueTime says s is in the past on all clocks, about \( 2\varepsilon \). Only then does it make the writes visible and acknowledge the client. This guarantees that any transaction starting after the acknowledgement receives a later timestamp, which gives external consistency. The wait overlaps with replication, and smaller ε means shorter waits.
A table with an auto-increment primary key shows one hot node and poor throughput. Why, and what do you change? || Inserts always go to the highest key, so one range and its leader take all the writes, regardless of cluster size. Use random UUIDs, hash-sharded indexes or a bucket prefix to spread inserts, or lead with a column such as tenant. Accept that range scans in key order become scatter-gather.
How does two-phase commit behave differently here from the textbook version? || The coordinator's decision is stored in a replicated transaction record, itself protected by consensus, so a coordinator crash does not block participants: another node reads the record and completes or aborts. Participants are replicated Raft groups, so a participant failure is also handled by failover. Optimisations such as parallel commits overlap the prepare and commit steps and save a round trip.
Your cross-region write latency is 150 ms. What are your options? || Place leaders near the main writers (regional-by-row or locality settings), use geo-partitioned data so most transactions stay in one region, reduce the number of round trips per transaction (batching, single statement transactions), use follower or stale reads for reads, and consider a configuration where only a nearby majority is needed. If strong cross-region consistency is not required, relax to eventual consistency with a different system.
When would you advise against distributed SQL? || When the data and load fit a single node with replicas, when the team cannot operate a distributed database, when latency per statement matters more than horizontal scale, when the workload is heavy in hot rows, or when licence or lock-in is unacceptable. A single PostgreSQL is simpler and faster, and sharding or caching may give enough scale.
quiz: What replicates each range in CockroachDB or TiKV, and what does a write need to commit? || A Raft group of three or five replicas. A write commits when a majority of replicas have stored it in their logs.
What does TrueTime return, and why does it help ordering? || An interval [earliest, latest] guaranteed to contain true time. Knowing the bound lets Spanner pick a commit timestamp and wait until it is certainly in the past, so later transactions get later timestamps.
A three-replica Raft group has replicas 1 ms, 65 ms and 80 ms from the leader. About how long does a write take to commit (ignoring disk)? || The leader needs one follower for a majority of 3, so the nearest follower matters: about 1 ms round trip. The 65 and 80 ms replicas are not on the critical path for this write.
Why does an auto-increment primary key hurt a distributed SQL database? || All inserts hit the last range, so a single leader handles all writes, and cluster size does not add insert throughput. Random or hash-prefixed keys spread the load over many ranges.
When is a single PostgreSQL a better choice than distributed SQL? || When the data and write rate fit one machine, and brief failover is acceptable. It has lower latency, fewer operational problems and fewer semantic surprises.

value: A graph database stores data as nodes (things) and edges (relationships between things), and answers questions by walking along the edges. In a relational database, a relationship such as "person A knows person B" is a row in a join table, and finding "friends of friends of friends" means joining that table with itself three times. In a graph database the relationship is a stored object that points directly from one node to the next, so you start at a person, follow their edges, then follow the edges of those nodes, and so on. The cost of such a walk depends on how much of the graph you touch, not on how big the whole database is.
This is useful when the questions are about connections: who is linked to whom through what, how far apart two things are, which cluster a node belongs to, what is the shortest or cheapest path. Typical uses are fraud rings (accounts that share devices, phones and addresses), recommendations ("people who bought this also bought"), network and infrastructure dependencies ("which services break if this one fails"), knowledge graphs, access-control hierarchies, and identity graphs that connect devices, households and accounts. In ad tech, an identity graph that links a connected-TV device, a phone and a household through shared signals is exactly this shape of data, although at billions of nodes it is usually processed in batch rather than in a graph database.
The layers below supply the machinery. Graph algorithms (L3) are what the database actually runs: breadth-first and depth-first traversal, shortest paths (Dijkstra, A*), PageRank, connected components, community detection and subgraph pattern matching. Hashing and hash tables (L3) provide the entry points: a hash or B-tree index turns "the account with this email" into a starting node in one lookup, and from there pointers do the rest. The layer above is a product such as Neo4j (L0), which implements the storage, the query language and the transactions.
terms: Node (vertex) — an entity in the graph, such as a person, account or device; it can have labels and properties.
Relationship (edge) — a directed, typed connection between two nodes, such as (:Account)-[:USES]->(:Device); it can have properties too.
Property graph — the model used by Neo4j and most graph databases: nodes and relationships carry key–value properties.
RDF triple — the other graph model: statements of the form (subject, predicate, object), queried with SPARQL.
Traversal — walking from a start node along edges, hop by hop, to find connected nodes or paths.
Index-free adjacency — each node stores direct pointers to its relationships, so moving to a neighbour needs no index lookup.
Cypher / GQL — declarative graph query languages that describe a pattern of nodes and edges to match; GQL is the ISO standard (2024) that Cypher strongly influenced.
Supernode (dense node) — a node with a very large number of edges, such as a popular celebrity or a shared public IP address.
Connected component — a set of nodes that are all reachable from each other through edges.
history: Graph theory is old (Euler, 1736, the Königsberg bridges), and graph-shaped data was stored in the 1960s in network databases (CODASYL) and later hierarchical systems, before the relational model (Codd, 1970) displaced them. Relational databases handled most business data well for decades. Graph questions that needed many joins stayed awkward.
Three things renewed interest. First, web scale: Google's PageRank (Page and Brin, 1998) ranked pages by link structure, and social networks made "friends of friends" a daily product feature. Second, the semantic web: the W3C standardised RDF (1999) and SPARQL (2008), and knowledge bases such as DBpedia and Wikidata grew around triples. Third, a practical property graph product: Emil Eifrem and colleagues created Neo4j (first release about 2007), which stored relationships as first-class records, and the Cypher language (2011), made open as openCypher in 2015. Apache TinkerPop and its traversal language Gremlin gave a vendor-neutral API (used by JanusGraph and Amazon Neptune, launched 2018), and TigerGraph (GSQL) targeted deep analytics on large graphs.
Graph processing at scale developed in parallel with databases. Google's Pregel (2010) introduced the "think like a vertex" model, followed by Apache Giraph, GraphX on Spark and GraphFrames, and PowerGraph (2012), which showed that real-world graphs with skewed degree distributions are hard to partition. In 2023 the SQL standard added property graph queries (SQL/PGQ in ISO/IEC 9075-16), and in April 2024 ISO published GQL (ISO/IEC 39075:2024), the first new ISO database language since SQL. Recently, "GraphRAG" systems combine knowledge graphs with language models to answer questions that span many documents. Embedded and in-memory graph engines have also appeared for analytics inside applications.
how: Data model. In a property graph, a node has one or more labels (Person, Account) and properties (name, email). A relationship has exactly one type (KNOWS, USES, PURCHASED), a direction, a start node and an end node, and optional properties (since, weight). A schema is optional but constraints (unique email on Account) and indexes are common. There are no join tables; adding a new kind of relationship needs no schema migration of existing data, which makes graph models easy to evolve.
Storage and index-free adjacency. In Neo4j's classic store format, nodes and relationships are fixed-size records in separate files (roughly 15 and 34 bytes), so a record's position in the file follows from its ID, and finding it needs only arithmetic. A node record holds a pointer to its first relationship. Each relationship record holds the IDs of its start and end node, its type, and pointers to the previous and next relationships of both nodes, forming doubly linked lists. To list a node's neighbours, the engine reads the node record, jumps to the first relationship, and follows the chain, never touching an index. To go one hop further, it jumps to the neighbour's node record and repeats. Each hop costs a few memory or page reads, regardless of the graph's total size. This is what "index-free adjacency" means: the hash or B-tree index is used only once, to find the start node, and then pointers take over. Other systems store adjacency lists in sorted arrays or compressed sparse row form, which has the same effect with better cache behaviour. Hash tables (L3) give the entry points: lookups of nodes by property such as email or ID.
Query execution. In Cypher, you describe a pattern and the planner finds matches. For example, MATCH (a:Account)-[:USES]->(d:Device)<-[:USES]-(b:Account) WHERE a.id = 42 AND a <> b RETURN b finds other accounts that share a device with account 42. The planner chooses a start (an index seek on Account.id), then expands relationships step by step (an "expand" operator), applying filters as early as possible, and finally returns rows. Variable-length patterns such as -[:KNOWS*1..3]- run a breadth-first or depth-first traversal up to a depth. Shortest path searches use bidirectional BFS or Dijkstra for weighted edges. Pattern matching in general is subgraph isomorphism, which is NP-hard in the worst case, so the planner relies on selective start points and on the fact that real queries match small neighbourhoods.
Planning needs statistics. A cost-based planner keeps counts of nodes per label, relationships per type, and the selectivity of indexes. For a pattern with two possible starting points, say an Account with a unique id and a Device label with 8 million nodes, it estimates how many rows each choice produces and starts from the more selective one, then expands outward. It can also expand from both ends of a long path and meet in the middle, which turns a search of \( b^d \) nodes (\( b \) is the branching factor, \( d \) the depth) into two searches of \( b^{d/2} \): with b = 50 and d = 4, that is 2 × 2,500 = 5,000 nodes instead of 6.25 million. This is the same idea as bidirectional breadth-first search from the graph algorithms layer, and it is why shortest-path queries between two known nodes are cheap even in large graphs.
Graph algorithms library. Beyond pattern queries, analytical algorithms run over the whole graph or a large projection of it: PageRank (importance), connected components (clusters, such as identity resolution), community detection (Louvain, label propagation), centrality (betweenness), node similarity, and node embeddings for machine learning. Neo4j Graph Data Science, TigerGraph and Spark GraphFrames offer them. They are iterative and touch every edge several times, so they run in memory on a projected copy.
Transactions and scale. Neo4j and most property graph databases offer ACID transactions on a single machine or a cluster with a leader and read replicas (replicated by Raft). Large graphs need partitioning, which is the hard part: assigning nodes to machines so that few edges cross machines is a balanced graph partitioning problem, NP-hard, and real graphs with hubs (power-law degree distributions) have no clean cuts. Systems either keep the graph on one big machine with replicas for reads (simple, works to billions of edges with enough memory and fast disks), or shard it (Neo4j's composed databases and Fabric, TigerGraph, Neptune clusters, JanusGraph on Cassandra) and pay for network hops during traversal.
RDF stores follow a different model: everything is a triple, stored with several permutation indexes (SPO, POS, OSP) so that any pattern is a range scan, and queried by SPARQL. They suit data integration and inference over ontologies. Property graphs suit application data and traversal.

example: Consider a fraud team with 5 million accounts, 8 million devices and 30 million USES edges. A ring of fraudulent accounts shares a small set of devices. The team wants all accounts within two hops of a known bad account, that is, accounts that share a device with it, and the accounts that share a device with those.
In a graph database. The query starts with an index lookup on the bad account (one hash or B-tree lookup, microseconds). It then expands to the account's devices (say 3), and for each device to the accounts that use it (say 20 on average), giving about 60 accounts, then expands again: each of those has about 3 devices with 20 accounts each, so about 60 × 3 × 20 = 3,600 paths. The database touches a few thousand records. At roughly a microsecond per cached hop, the query takes a few milliseconds, even though the graph holds 38 million records. The cost follows the neighbourhood size.
In a relational database. The same query is a self-join of the USES table through the device column: USES u1 join USES u2 on device, then again. With an index on (device_id) and on (account_id), the engine does index nested-loop joins: each step is an index lookup of about log₂(30,000,000) ≈ 25 comparisons, but B-tree pages are often cached, so the same 3,600 paths cost tens of milliseconds. So a well-indexed relational database is not hopelessly slow at two hops. The difference grows with depth and fan-out. At depth 6 with an average of 50 neighbours, a naive traversal has up to 50⁶ ≈ 15.6 billion paths, which no engine can enumerate; both approaches need pruning (filters, depth limits, visited-sets). What graph storage gives is a lower constant per hop, a query language where a variable-length path is one line instead of six joins, and an optimiser built for traversals.
Supernode problem. Suppose one shared public IP address node is linked to 2 million accounts. Any query that passes through it expands 2 million edges. Fixes are to model it differently (a high-degree "IP" node is excluded from traversals by a filter such as WHERE d.degree < 1000), to store those edges grouped by type or by property so that they can be skipped, or to treat them as a feature (a count) rather than as a path.
Identity graph in batch. With 2 billion device nodes and 6 billion edges, you do not load that into a transactional graph database. You compute connected components as a Spark job (GraphFrames or a label-propagation / union-find approach), write a table (device_id, component_id), and serve lookups from a key–value store. The graph appears only as an algorithm.
intuition: When the relationships are the data, store them as first-class citizens, so that following a relationship costs a pointer read instead of a join.
The design is natural because many questions are about paths, and a path is a sequence of local steps. If the storage lets you take a local step cheaply, long questions become long walks over a small part of the data. Relational storage is organised by sets (tables) and finds connections by matching values; graph storage is organised by connections and finds sets by walking. Each is better when the question matches its organisation.
mistakes: People often think graph databases are much faster than relational databases for all connected data. In fact the advantage is for deep, variable-length traversals and path queries over highly connected data. For one or two joins with good indexes, a relational database is competitive, and for aggregation over many rows (sums, group by) it is far better.
People often think a graph database scales out like Cassandra. In fact scaling a graph across machines is hard because traversals cross partitions, and every cross-partition hop costs a network round trip. Many deployments use one large machine plus read replicas, and only the largest need sharding.
People often think graph analytics must run inside a graph database. In fact large analytics (PageRank, connected components on billions of edges) usually run in distributed batch engines such as Spark with GraphFrames or specialised systems, on data stored in a lakehouse. The graph database serves low-latency queries over a connected working set.
People often think a graph database needs no modelling. In fact the model decides query performance: what is a node and what is a property, which relationships exist, and their direction and type. Putting a high-cardinality value (a date, a country) as a node can create supernodes. Modelling takes as much thought as a relational schema.
People often think "graph" means knowledge graph and ontologies. In fact RDF with ontologies is one branch. Most application graph databases use property graphs without formal reasoning, and a knowledge graph in an LLM application is usually just a property graph with extracted entities.
friction: The main limit is scale-out. A single machine holds a graph of billions of edges if it has hundreds of GB of RAM and fast NVMe, but beyond that, partitioned traversal costs network hops and partitioning quality dominates. Hubs (power-law degrees) make balanced partitions impossible, and supernodes make some queries explode in cost regardless.
Query cost is hard to predict. The result size can grow exponentially with depth, so an innocent query can touch millions of nodes. Production systems need depth limits, timeouts, and memory limits per query, and developers need to understand the query plan. The planners are younger than relational optimisers, and there are fewer tools for monitoring and tuning.
The ecosystem is fragmented. Query languages differed (Cypher, Gremlin, GSQL, SPARQL), and although GQL (2024) and SQL/PGQ now exist, support is only beginning to spread; moving between vendors is still work. There are fewer engineers with graph experience, less tooling for reporting, and weaker support for bulk analytics compared with data warehouses. Loading and keeping a graph in sync with source systems (change data capture from relational stores or Kafka) is a pipeline to maintain, and it risks becoming a second copy of the truth.
Finally, many "graph problems" are solved well by relational joins, recursive SQL (WITH RECURSIVE), or batch graph processing, so the case for a separate database must be made on the actual queries. Licensing also varies: Neo4j's community and enterprise editions have different capabilities (for example clustering), and some engines have changed licences.
practice: First write down the real queries and their expected depth, fan-out, latency target and update rate. If they are mostly one or two joins or aggregations, use your relational database or lakehouse. Consider a graph database when queries are variable-depth traversals, path finding, pattern matching or recursive hierarchies, and the answers are needed in milliseconds on interactive paths.
Model for the questions. Make entities nodes, connections relationships with a clear type and direction, and facts that describe a node properties. Avoid generic relationships (RELATED_TO) that hide meaning. Be careful with high-degree nodes: measure the degree distribution before loading, cap or filter the traversal through hubs, and consider storing very common values as properties.
Index the entry points (unique constraints on IDs, indexes on lookup properties), constrain traversals with relationship types, directions and depth limits, and read query plans (PROFILE or EXPLAIN in Cypher) to see how many records each step touches. Put limits on query time and memory. Load data in bulk with the import tool for the initial load and then incrementally with CDC or batch upserts; keep the graph rebuildable from the system of record.
For large graph analytics, process the graph in your lakehouse (Spark GraphFrames, connected components, PageRank) and publish results (component IDs, scores) back to a key–value or relational store, or into the graph database as properties. Use the graph database for the interactive neighbourhood queries. For retrieval with language models, a graph can provide structured relationships to complement vector search, but test whether it improves answers on your questions before building it.
Plan capacity from the working set: the hot part of the graph should fit in memory (page cache). Test failover and backup of the cluster, and check which edition provides clustering and security features you need.
tradeoffs: Choose a graph database when the value of the application lies in multi-hop questions (fraud rings, dependency impact, recommendations by shared connections, access hierarchies), the working graph fits on one machine or a small cluster, and responses are needed in milliseconds. You give up the maturity of SQL tooling and cheap analytics, and you take on a second system to keep in sync. Choose relational (with recursive queries or a few indexed joins) when connections are shallow, data is already in SQL, and your team knows it; PostgreSQL with WITH RECURSIVE or the new SQL/PGQ-style features can cover moderate cases without a new system.
Choose a property graph (Neo4j, Neptune, Memgraph) for application data with rich properties and traversal. Choose RDF and SPARQL when you integrate data from many sources, use shared vocabularies and inference, or publish linked data. RDF gives standards and federation, at the cost of more verbose modelling (edges cannot have properties without extra constructs, though RDF-star addresses this).
Choose a single-machine graph database with read replicas up to a few billion edges, because it avoids partition costs and keeps traversal fast. Choose a sharded graph (TigerGraph, JanusGraph on Cassandra, Neptune clusters) when the graph is beyond memory of a large machine and queries are mostly local to a partition. Expect more latency and more design effort.
Choose batch graph processing (Spark, GraphFrames) when the question is global (communities, PageRank, identity resolution over all devices) and a result table refreshed every few hours is enough. Choose an online graph database when the question starts from one entity and must return in milliseconds.
Choose a graph in front of a language model (GraphRAG) when the answers need relationships across many documents (who is connected to what) and you can afford extraction and maintenance; choose plain vector search when questions are answered by one or a few passages. Graph construction costs a lot of model calls and effort, and quality depends on entity resolution.
interview: Explain index-free adjacency and what it does and does not buy. || Each node record points directly to its first relationship, and relationship records link to the next ones, so a hop is a few pointer reads, with no index lookup per hop. Traversal cost follows the size of the neighbourhood visited, not the size of the database. It does not remove the exponential growth of paths with depth, makes aggregation over all nodes no faster, and does not help distribution, since pointers across machines become network calls.
How would you find fraud rings sharing devices among 10 million accounts? || Model Account and Device nodes with USES edges. For interactive investigation, query the neighbourhood of a suspect with bounded depth and a degree filter to skip public devices. For detection over everything, compute connected components or run community detection in batch on a filtered graph (ignoring high-degree devices), score components by size and shared attributes, and push the flagged accounts to the online store. Validate with labelled fraud.
A Cypher query that worked in testing times out in production. What do you look at? || Run PROFILE to see rows and database hits per operator. Likely causes: no index on the start node so it scans a label, an unbounded variable-length pattern, a supernode in the path, a cartesian product from disconnected patterns, or a filter applied too late. Fix by anchoring with an indexed property, limiting depth, filtering by relationship type and degree, and restructuring the pattern.
Why is horizontally scaling a graph database harder than scaling a key–value store? || In a key–value store, requests touch one key and partition by hash, so the work is independent. In a graph, a query follows edges that may lead to any partition, and high-degree hubs and power-law structure prevent partitions that cut few edges. So traversals become chains of network calls. Hence most systems scale up first, add read replicas, and only shard when forced.
When would you use SQL recursive queries or a lakehouse instead of a graph database? || If the traversal is shallow or needed only in batch, if the data is already in SQL or Delta tables and the result is a table, if there is no millisecond latency requirement, or if the team lacks capacity to run another database. WITH RECURSIVE handles hierarchies and moderate depth, and Spark handles whole-graph algorithms.
What is a supernode and how do you handle it? || A node with a huge number of relationships, such as a shared IP or a popular product. Traversals through it fan out to millions of edges. Handle it by excluding it from traversals with a degree filter, grouping its relationships by type or property so that most can be skipped, or modelling the shared value as a property or a count instead of a node.
quiz: What does a node record store in index-free adjacency? || A pointer to its first relationship, whose record links onward to the node's other relationships, so neighbours are found by following pointers instead of looking up an index.
Why does traversal cost not depend on the total size of the graph? || The engine starts at an indexed node and only follows edges from nodes it has reached, so the work grows with the neighbourhood it visits, not with the number of nodes in the database.
With an average of 40 neighbours per node, how many paths of length 4 start from one node (upper bound)? || 40⁴ = 2,560,000 paths, showing why depth limits and filters are necessary.
Why is graph partitioning difficult? || Placing nodes on machines so that few edges cross machines is a balanced minimum-cut problem, which is NP-hard, and real graphs with hubs have no good cuts, so many traversal hops will cross machines.
When is a relational database or Spark the better choice than a graph database? || When queries are shallow joins or aggregations, or when the analysis covers the whole graph in batch (components, PageRank), so that a result table refreshed periodically is enough.

value: A storage engine is the part of a database that decides how bytes are laid out on disk and in memory, and that does the actual reading and writing. Above it sit the parts users see: the SQL parser, the query planner, the network protocol, replication. Below it is the file system and the disk. The engine answers the questions that decide speed and cost: how a key is found, how a write becomes durable, what happens when the power fails in the middle of a write, how old versions are removed, and how data is compressed and cached.
The engine matters more than most users realise, because it fixes the shape of a database's performance. The same SQL on MySQL's InnoDB (B-tree) and on MyRocks (LSM tree) can differ by several times in write throughput, disk space and SSD wear. Most databases today are built from a handful of engines, and many are reusable libraries: RocksDB runs inside TiKV, YugabyteDB (as a fork), Kafka Streams, Flink state, MySQL (as MyRocks) and Spark Structured Streaming's state store; SQLite is embedded in phones and browsers; WiredTiger stores MongoDB. Learning the few designs gives you a model of almost every database.
The layers below supply the building blocks. The B-tree (L2) keeps sorted pages that are cheap to search and update in place, which suits reads and range scans. The LSM tree (L2) buffers writes in memory and writes them as sorted immutable files, which suits heavy ingestion. The Bloom filter (L2) lets an LSM engine skip files that cannot hold a key. The write-ahead log (L2) makes each change durable before it reaches the main structure, so a crash can be recovered. The layer above is a database or a library such as RocksDB (L0), which packages these parts into something you embed.
terms: Page (block) — the fixed-size unit (commonly 4 to 16 KB) that an engine reads and writes on disk; B-trees are built from pages.
Buffer pool (block cache) — the memory region where the engine keeps recently used pages or blocks, managed by the engine itself.
Write-ahead log (WAL) — an append-only file where each change is recorded and flushed before the data structure is changed.
Memtable — the in-memory sorted structure that receives writes in an LSM engine before they are flushed to disk.
SSTable (sorted string table) — an immutable file of sorted key–value pairs with an index and filters.
Compaction — background work that merges sorted files, drops overwritten and deleted entries, and keeps reads fast.
Write / read / space amplification — the ratio of bytes physically written, read, or stored to the bytes the application logically wrote, read, or holds.
Checkpoint — a point at which the engine makes all earlier changes durable in the main files, so the log before it can be discarded.
MVCC — keeping several versions of a row so that readers see a consistent snapshot while writers continue.
history: The first generation were B-tree engines. Bayer and McCreight invented the B-tree in 1970, and by the 1980s every relational database had a page-based engine with a buffer pool and a log. The ARIES recovery algorithm (Mohan et al., IBM, 1992) became the standard way to combine a write-ahead log with steal/no-force buffer management. Berkeley DB (1990s) offered such an engine as an embeddable library, and SQLite (2000) put a B-tree database in a single file, which became the most widely deployed database in the world. MySQL (1990s) made engines pluggable: MyISAM first, then InnoDB (transactions, row locks, clustered index), which became the default in MySQL 5.5 (2010). MongoDB adopted WiredTiger as its default engine in version 3.2 (2015).
The LSM idea came from Patrick O'Neil and colleagues (1996), and it entered practice with Google's Bigtable (2006), then LevelDB (2011, Jeff Dean and Sanjay Ghemawat). Facebook forked LevelDB into RocksDB (2012 to 2013) to use many CPU cores and fast flash, with column families, multi-threaded compaction, rate limiting and tuning options. It became the default embedded engine for new systems: TiKV, YugabyteDB (a fork), Kafka Streams, Flink, Spark's state store, and MyRocks at Facebook. CockroachDB wrote Pebble in Go, compatible with RocksDB file formats, to avoid the cost of calling C++ from Go.
Research then worked on amplification. The RUM Conjecture (Athanassoulis et al., 2016) argued that an access method cannot minimise Read, Update and Memory overheads together. WiscKey (Lu et al., FAST 2016) separated values from keys in the LSM tree to cut write amplification for large values. Monkey (Dayan et al., SIGMOD 2017) showed how to assign Bloom filter bits across levels optimally. Newer filters (ribbon) and learned indexes reduce memory. LMDB showed that a copy-on-write B+tree with memory-mapped files is simple and very fast for read-heavy loads.
how: An engine has the same main parts whatever its structure: an index that maps keys to data, a cache, a log for durability, a concurrency control scheme, and background maintenance. The index structure is the first choice.
B-tree engines (InnoDB, SQLite, WiredTiger by default, LMDB, PostgreSQL's heap with B-tree indexes) keep data in fixed-size pages arranged as a balanced tree. Internal pages hold keys and child pointers; leaf pages hold the rows (in InnoDB the leaf level is the table itself, a clustered index on the primary key). A lookup walks from the root to a leaf. With 16 KB pages and a fan-out of around 500, a tree with 3 internal levels holds hundreds of millions of rows, and the top levels stay in memory, so a point lookup costs one or two disk reads. Updates change the page in place in the buffer pool and mark it dirty. Because a crash could leave the on-disk page half-written or newer than the log, durability needs the write-ahead log (L2): before a page is changed, a redo record is appended to the log and flushed (fsync) at commit. After a crash the engine replays the log from the last checkpoint. InnoDB adds a doublewrite buffer to protect against torn pages, and an undo log for rollback and MVCC. Dirty pages are written back lazily, and a checkpoint bounds the log length. Range scans follow linked leaf pages and are fast and sequential.
LSM engines (RocksDB, LevelDB, Pebble, Cassandra, WiredTiger's LSM mode) never update in place. A write goes first to the WAL (sequential append, fsync according to the durability setting), then into the memtable (a skip list or similar sorted structure). When the memtable reaches its size limit (64 MB by default in RocksDB), it is frozen and flushed as an immutable SSTable file at level 0, and the WAL segment can be discarded. Files accumulate, so compaction merges them into larger sorted files at deeper levels. In leveled compaction each level is about 10 times larger than the one above (RocksDB defaults: level 1 target 256 MB, multiplier 10), and the files inside a level (below level 0) do not overlap in key range, so a key lives in at most one file per level. A read checks the memtable, then level-0 files (which may overlap), then one file per deeper level. Each file has an index block and a Bloom filter (L2), and with about 10 bits per key a file that does not contain the key is skipped 99% of the time. A deleted key is a tombstone that is dropped when compaction reaches the bottom level. Compaction also removes older versions of overwritten keys.
These choices set three amplification factors. Write amplification is the physical bytes written per logical byte. In a B-tree, changing a 100-byte row dirties a whole 16 KB page, plus the log record, so the raw ratio can be above 100 for random updates (reduced when several updates hit the same page before it is written back). In leveled LSM it is typically 10 to 30, because data is rewritten as it moves down levels, but all of it is sequential. Size-tiered compaction merges similar-sized files, lowers write amplification, and raises space and read amplification. Read amplification is the number of places that must be consulted: 1 to 2 page reads for a B-tree point lookup, and for an LSM up to one per level plus the memtable (filters make the real number of disk reads close to 1). Space amplification is stored bytes over live bytes: about 1.1 for leveled LSM, up to 2 or more for size-tiered, and 1.3 to 2 for B-trees with half-empty pages after splits. The RUM conjecture is exactly the observation that improving one costs another.
Concurrency control decides how readers and writers coexist. Most engines use MVCC (L2): each write creates a new version with a transaction timestamp or sequence number, and a reader uses the snapshot at its start. In an LSM engine this is natural, since overwrites are new entries with higher sequence numbers. In InnoDB, old versions are rebuilt from the undo log. Row locks or optimistic validation resolve write conflicts. Caching has two layers: the engine's own cache (InnoDB buffer pool, RocksDB block cache) and the operating system page cache; engines often bypass the latter with direct I/O so that they control eviction. Compression (LZ4, Zstd, Snappy) is applied per block, usually stronger at deeper levels where data is older and larger.
Operational mechanics matter. If compaction cannot keep up, level-0 files pile up; RocksDB first slows writes (default at 20 files) and then stops them (36), which appears as write stalls. Backups use consistent snapshots or checkpoints (hard links to immutable files). Recovery after a crash replays the WAL into a memtable (LSM) or redoes the log from the checkpoint (B-tree). Embedded engines run inside your process: the application calls Get, Put and iterators, and has to manage memory, threads and file layout itself, which is why RocksDB has hundreds of options.

example: Compare the two designs on a workload of 100,000 random updates per second to 100-byte values in a table with 1 billion keys (100 GB of live data), on one NVMe SSD.
B-tree. Each update changes a leaf page of 16 KB. The keys are random and the table is far larger than memory, so two updates seldom hit the same page before it is written back. Worst case the engine writes 100,000 × 16 KB = 1.6 GB per second of pages, plus about 100,000 × (100 B + overhead) ≈ 15 MB/s of log. A single NVMe drive may sustain 1 to 2 GB/s of writes, so it is at its limit, and the write amplification is about 160. Reads: a lookup costs about two disk reads if the top levels are cached.
LSM tree. The same updates go to the WAL (10 MB/s of logical data) and the memtable. Flushes write about the same 10 MB/s. Leveled compaction rewrites data as it moves down: with a fan-out of 10 and about 5 levels the total write amplification is roughly 15 in practice, so the disk sees about 10 MB/s × 15 = 150 MB/s, ten times less than the B-tree's worst case. The cost appears elsewhere: a point read may check up to about 9 filters (4 level-0 files plus 5 levels) and read one or two data blocks; background compaction uses CPU and I/O; space amplification is about 1.1 (110 GB for 100 GB live).
SSD wear. A 3.84 TB drive rated for 1 drive-write-per-day tolerates about 3.84 TB per day. The LSM at 150 MB/s writes 150 MB/s × 86,400 s ≈ 13 TB per day, which is 3.4 drive-writes per day: too high for that drive class, so you would need a higher endurance drive or a lower write rate, or a size-tiered strategy with a write amplification near 5 (about 4.3 TB per day). The B-tree worst case of 1.6 GB/s would be 138 TB per day, impossible; in practice the buffer pool coalesces many updates and the numbers are lower, but the direction holds for random small writes.
Sizing the filter. With 1 billion keys and 10 bits per key, the Bloom filters need 10 billion bits = 1.25 GB of memory. A lookup for a missing key would cost up to 9 file checks without filters; with a 1% false positive rate per file it costs about 0.09 expected extra block reads. Dropping to 5 bits per key halves the memory to 625 MB but raises the false positive rate to about 9% per file, which is around 0.8 extra block reads on average for misses.
Choice. If this workload is mostly random updates and point reads, the LSM wins on write cost. If it were 95% reads with range scans and updates of few hot rows, the B-tree would give lower and more predictable read latency, with no compaction stalls.
intuition: The engine is the warehouse layout; the database is the business running on top.
A warehouse can be organised for fast picking (every item at a known shelf, updated in place) or for fast receiving (put every delivery in a new pile at the door, and tidy up at night). The first is a B-tree: easy to find things, but each change walks to a shelf. The second is an LSM tree: arrivals are very cheap, finding things needs a look at several piles, and the night shift (compaction) is real work that must keep up. There is no layout that is best at receiving, picking and floor space at once, which is the RUM conjecture in plain words. A log in front of either makes it safe to crash.
mistakes: People often think LSM engines are always faster than B-trees. In fact they are faster for write-heavy loads and make smaller files, but a point read may check several levels and range scans merge many files, so read latency is less predictable and compaction can cause stalls. For read-heavy workloads with moderate writes, a B-tree is often better.
People often think a write is safe once the application call returns. In fact it is safe only when the log record has reached stable storage. Many engines let you choose: fsync on every commit (safe, slower, about 0.1 ms on enterprise NVMe and 1 ms or more on network disks), grouped commits (fsync shared by many transactions), or periodic sync (fast, but a crash can lose the last second). Check the setting you actually run.
People often think write amplification is a flaw of LSM trees alone. In fact B-trees have it as well, from whole-page rewrites, the log, and the doublewrite buffer. The difference is its shape: B-trees amplify with random I/O in place, LSM trees with sequential background rewrites. Both are costs to measure, not to assume.
People often think the choice of engine is a detail hidden by the database. In fact it shows up in your bills and incidents: disk space, SSD wear, tail latency during compaction, behaviour under deletes (tombstones), and recovery time. Know which engine your database uses and what its metrics mean.
People often think an embedded engine such as RocksDB is a database. In fact it has no network server, no query language, no replication and no schema. It is a library for ordered key–value storage with transactions on one machine. Those other parts must be built around it, which is what TiKV, CockroachDB and others did.
friction: There is no universally best engine. The RUM trade-off means you tune for one workload and pay in another; an LSM tuned for fast ingestion may have slow range scans, and a B-tree tuned for reads suffers on random writes. Parameters are numerous: RocksDB has hundreds of options (memtable sizes, level sizes, compaction style, filter bits, compression per level, rate limits), and defaults suit a generic workload. Good tuning needs measurement of write, read and space amplification, and tests with realistic data sizes (much larger than memory) and key distributions.
Compaction is the hardest operational part. If background merging falls behind the ingest rate, read performance degrades (more files to check) and eventually writes stall. Compaction consumes I/O and CPU, which causes latency spikes at the 99th percentile. Deletes are delayed: tombstones occupy space and slow scans until compaction reaches the bottom level, so queue-like workloads and mass deletes can hurt. Space needs headroom (compaction needs temporary space, up to the size of the files being merged).
B-trees have their own failure modes: page splits and fragmentation leave space half-empty, long scans pollute the buffer pool, the log and checkpoints must be tuned to avoid stalls, and write-heavy random updates saturate I/O. Crash safety depends on details: torn writes, fsync semantics of file systems, and disks that acknowledge writes before they are durable. Real bugs have appeared in all major engines, so crash-recovery tests with fault injection matter. Hardware has changed the assumptions too: NVMe drives have microsecond latency, so CPU, locking and memory allocation matter more than disk seeks, and persistent memory and zoned storage (ZNS) require new designs. Finally, upgrades of on-disk formats and compatibility between engine versions can be a long-lasting risk for embedded use.
practice: Pick the engine by workload and then verify it. For read-heavy, range-scan and predictable-latency workloads, use a B-tree engine (InnoDB, WiredTiger, SQLite, LMDB). For ingestion-heavy, write-heavy and large-data workloads, particularly on flash, use an LSM engine (RocksDB and its relatives). For state in a stream processor, the RocksDB state backend is the usual choice for state bigger than memory, with the state in memory (heap) for small state.
Benchmark with your own key and value sizes, read–write mix, key distribution (uniform, skewed), and a data set at least several times larger than memory, run long enough for compaction to reach steady state (hours, not minutes). Watch the right metrics: write amplification, read amplification (blocks read per get), space amplification, p99 latency, pending compaction bytes, level-0 file count, write stall time, block cache hit rate, and disk utilisation.
For RocksDB, start with the defaults of a modern version, use Bloom (or ribbon) filters at 10 bits per key for point reads, choose compression (LZ4 for upper levels, Zstd for the bottom level), size the block cache to your working set, put the WAL on its own device if you can, limit compaction I/O with a rate limiter so foreground traffic is not starved, and consider key–value separation (BlobDB) for large values. Use prefix extractors if your reads are prefix scans.
Set durability explicitly: understand fsync policy, group commit, and what a crash can lose. Test recovery by killing the process and the machine in the middle of load. Leave 30 to 50% free disk for compaction. Monitor SSD wear (percentage used, writes per day) against the drive's endurance rating. Keep the engine version and on-disk format under control during upgrades, and take consistent checkpoints for backups. When you only need a local key–value store inside a service, prefer an embedded engine to running a separate database server.
tradeoffs: Choose a B-tree engine when reads and range scans dominate (more than about 80% reads), when you need predictable latency and low space overhead, and when updates are moderate or touch hot rows that the buffer pool absorbs. You accept higher write amplification on random writes and page-level fragmentation. Choose an LSM engine when writes dominate or data is large and compresses well, when the storage is flash and sequential writes matter for wear and throughput, and when you can afford background compaction. You accept variable read latency, stalls under pressure, and 1.1 to 2 times space for compaction.
Choose leveled compaction when you need low space amplification and good read performance, and can afford write amplification of 10 or more; this suits read-mostly or space-constrained data. Choose size-tiered (or universal) compaction when ingestion throughput and low write amplification matter more, and you can spend extra disk space and accept more files to read. Choose time-window compaction for time-series with TTL, where whole files expire at once.
Choose more Bloom filter bits (10 to 14 per key) when most lookups miss and disk reads are expensive; choose fewer (or none on the bottom level, as RocksDB can do) when the data set is huge, almost all lookups hit, and memory is limited. Each bit per key costs memory but each extra 4.8 bits divides false positives by ten.
Choose fsync on every commit when losing an acknowledged write is unacceptable (payments, metadata); choose group commit to keep that safety at higher throughput; choose periodic sync only for caches, derived data or anything rebuildable. The cost of per-commit fsync is latency, and with group commit it is a small added wait.
Choose an embedded engine inside your service when state is local, you control the process, and you want microsecond access with no network hop; choose a separate database server when many services share the data, you need a query language and operational tooling, or you need replication handled for you. Embedding gives speed and control and gives you the operational burden of the engine.
interview: Why is a B-tree write more amplifying than an LSM write for small random updates, and when is it not? || The B-tree rewrites an entire page (for example 16 KB) to change 100 bytes, plus the log record and possibly a doublewrite, so the raw ratio exceeds 100. The LSM batches updates in memory and writes them in large sequential runs, then rewrites them during compaction, giving a ratio of about 10 to 30. It is not true when several updates hit the same page before it is flushed (hot pages), when values are large, or when keys are inserted in order, where B-trees append cheaply.
Walk through an LSM point lookup and say how Bloom filters change its cost. || Check the active memtable and any immutable memtables, then the level-0 files from newest to oldest (they overlap), then at most one file per deeper level, using the file's key range to choose it. Each candidate file's Bloom filter is checked first; a negative skips the file. With 1% false positives, the expected number of useless block reads is small, so a lookup costs about one block read when the key exists and almost none when it does not.
What does fsync have to do with durability, and what is group commit? || Data returned from a write call is only in the OS cache. fsync forces it to the device. The WAL must be fsynced before the commit is acknowledged, or a crash can lose acknowledged data. Group commit lets many concurrent transactions share one fsync, so throughput scales while each commit waits a small amount for the batch.
Your RocksDB service shows write stalls every few minutes. Diagnose. || Stalls come from level-0 file count reaching the slowdown or stop trigger, too many immutable memtables, or pending compaction bytes passing limits. The cause is that compaction cannot keep up with ingest. Check compaction throughput and disk I/O, increase background compaction threads and the rate limit, enlarge memtables, review the compaction style and level sizes, reduce write rate or value size, and verify that the disk has enough bandwidth and free space.
How would you choose between InnoDB and an LSM engine for a MySQL-compatible service with 5 TB of data? || Measure with production-like traffic. If the workload is read-heavy with range queries and moderate updates, InnoDB is predictable. If writes are heavy, data compresses well and storage cost matters, an LSM engine such as MyRocks can cut space and SSD writes by large factors, at the cost of read variability and operational differences (compaction, fewer tools). Decide with measured write and space amplification, p99 latency, and recovery behaviour.
Why does an LSM engine need free disk space and what happens when it runs out? || Compaction writes new files before deleting the old ones, so it needs temporary space up to the size of the merged files, and tombstones and old versions stay until compaction reaches them. If the disk fills, compaction cannot proceed, writes stop or the engine goes read-only, and recovery may need manual deletion. Keep 30 to 50% headroom and alert early.
quiz: What does the WAL guarantee, and when must it be flushed? || That a committed change can be redone after a crash. It must be flushed (fsync) to stable storage before the commit is acknowledged, before the data pages or SSTables carry the change.
In leveled compaction with level 1 at 256 MB and a multiplier of 10, how large is level 3? || Level 2 is 2.56 GB and level 3 is 25.6 GB.
Why can a B-tree point lookup need only one or two disk reads on a billion-row table? || The fan-out is hundreds per page, so the tree has only three to four levels, and the root and upper internal pages stay in the buffer pool, leaving about one internal page and one leaf page to read.
An LSM engine has write amplification 20 and the application writes 5 MB/s. How much does the SSD receive per day? || 5 MB/s × 20 = 100 MB/s, and 100 MB/s × 86,400 s ≈ 8.6 TB per day.
When would you choose size-tiered over leveled compaction? || When ingest throughput and low write amplification matter more than space and read performance, and you have spare disk space, as in write-heavy ingest or time-series data.
