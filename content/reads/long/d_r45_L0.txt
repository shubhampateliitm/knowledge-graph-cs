@@ vast
value: VAST (Video Ad Serving Template) is an XML format that an ad server returns to a video player. It answers the questions the player cannot answer by itself: which video file should I play, how long is it, where does a click go, and which URLs must I call when the ad starts, reaches 25%, 50%, 75%, finishes, is skipped, or fails? VMAP (Video Multiple Ad Playlist) is a companion XML format that answers a different question: where in the content are the ad breaks, and which ad tag should fill each break. The IAB Tech Lab publishes both. VAST 4.3 (December 2022) is the newest full VAST release, with a CTV Addendum published in July 2024.
Before VAST, every ad server had its own way to talk to every player, so each pair of ad server and player needed custom integration work. VAST made the contract public. Any compliant player can play any compliant ad, and any ad server can report to any buyer in a way the buyer can count. This is why VAST sits at the end of almost every video ad transaction: the OpenRTB bid response carries VAST markup (or a URL to it), the exchange passes it on, and the player or the stitching server finally executes it.
VAST gives the layers above it three things: playback instructions (the media files and their formats), measurement hooks (the tracking events that become billable impressions and quartile reports), and a place for third parties (verification scripts, interactive creatives). The layers below make it work. HTTP carries the XML and every beacon. Programmatic advertising (adtech) decides which bidder's VAST gets used. Video streaming (mediastream) defines what a "media file" is and how the player buffers it. Server-side ad insertion (ssai) moves the execution of VAST from the device to a server in the cloud, which changes who fires the tracking calls.
terms: Linear ad — a video ad that plays in the timeline of the content and pauses the content while it plays (pre-roll, mid-roll, post-roll).
Wrapper — a VAST document that contains no media, only a `VASTAdTagURI` that points to the next VAST document and its own tracking URLs; wrappers can be chained.
InLine — a VAST document that holds the final ad: media files, duration, click URLs and tracking.
Tracking event — a named moment (`start`, `firstQuartile`, `midpoint`, `thirdQuartile`, `complete`, `skip`, `mute` and others) with one or more URLs that the player must request when the moment happens.
VMAP — Video Multiple Ad Playlist; XML that lists ad breaks (`AdBreak`) with a time offset and an ad source for each.
Mezzanine — a high-quality source file of the creative, offered so that a server or CTV platform can transcode it to its own formats.
SIMID / OMID — SIMID is the Secure Interactive Media Interface Definition for interactive ad creatives; OMID is the Open Measurement Interface Definition for third-party viewability and verification code.
history: The IAB released VAST 1.0 in 2008, when online video was growing and every publisher's player had a private ad protocol. The first goal was small: one XML shape that tells the player what to play. VAST 2.0 (2009) added wrappers, which let an ad server redirect to another ad server. This was needed because agencies and verification companies wanted to sit in the middle of the chain to count impressions on their own.
VAST 3.0 (2012) added the features that mobile and long-form video needed: skippable ads (`skipoffset`), ad pods with a sequence, and more error and tracking detail. A separate document, VMAP, describes ad breaks inside content. Alongside these, VPAID (Video Player Ad-Interface Definition) let an ad creative be a piece of JavaScript or Flash that ran inside the player. It allowed rich ads, but it also allowed slow, buggy and unsafe code, and it could not run on connected TVs.
VAST 4.0 (2016) changed direction. It introduced a standard way to attach verification vendors (`AdVerifications`) and to pass a "mezzanine" file for server-side use. VAST 4.1 (the IAB page lists 2017 in one place and November 2018 in another) and VAST 4.2 (June 2019) continued this, and 4.2 introduced SIMID, the replacement for VPAID: the interactive part runs in a sandboxed iframe and talks to the player through a message channel, so it cannot reach into the player. OMID took over the measurement job. VPAID is now deprecated.
VAST 4.3 appeared in December 2022 as a smaller update. In July 2024 the IAB Tech Lab published the VAST CTV Addendum, which says how connected TV players and server-side stitching systems should handle VAST. The driver in every step is the same: video moved from desktop plugins to mobile apps to connected TVs and server-stitched streams, and each move broke an assumption of the previous version.
how: Step 1: the request. The player (or, in SSAI, a stitching server) builds an ad request. This is an HTTP GET to an ad tag URL, or an OpenRTB request to an exchange. The URL carries macros such as cache-busting numbers, player size, content metadata and consent strings. Because the transport is plain HTTP, the usual HTTP rules apply: redirects, cookies (or the lack of them), timeouts, and TLS. The response body is the VAST XML.
Step 2: the parse. The root element is `<VAST version="4.2">`. It contains zero or more `<Ad>` elements. Each `<Ad>` has either an `<InLine>` child or a `<Wrapper>` child. An empty `<VAST/>` means "no ad": the player should continue with the content. A `Wrapper` does not carry a creative. It carries a `<VASTAdTagURI>` and its own `<Impression>` and `<Tracking>` elements. The player fetches the next URL, parses that document, and repeats. The player keeps a list of all the tracking URLs it has seen along the chain, because every wrapper in the chain (the agency's ad server, the verification vendor, the exchange) expects its own beacons to fire when the ad plays. Players limit the chain, commonly to 5 wrappers, and report error code 302 when the limit is hit and 301 when a wrapper request times out.
Step 3: choose the media. An `<InLine>` ad holds a `<Linear>` creative with a `<Duration>` and a list of `<MediaFile>` elements. Each one names a URL, a MIME type such as `video/mp4`, a width, a height, a bitrate and a delivery method (`progressive` for a direct file, `streaming` for an HLS or DASH manifest). The player picks the file that best fits its screen, codec support and network. This is where mediastream knowledge matters: a file encoded with a codec the player cannot decode causes error 403 ("could not find a supported media file"). Advertisers therefore ship several renditions, for example H.264 at 360p, 720p and 1080p. For streaming delivery the file is an HLS or DASH manifest, and the player then picks segments itself, as it does for content. A `Mezzanine` element offers a high-quality master that a server can transcode for its own needs.
Step 4: play and track. When the first frame is shown the player requests every `<Impression>` URL. As playback passes 25%, 50% and 75% of the duration, and at completion, it requests the URLs listed under the matching `<Tracking event="...">`. If the user clicks, it opens `<ClickThrough>` and requests every `<ClickTracking>` URL. If anything fails, it requests the `<Error>` URL after replacing the macro `[ERRORCODE]` with a standard number: 100 for an XML parsing error, 303 for no ad after a wrapper, 402 for a media file timeout, 405 for a problem displaying the media. These beacons are plain HTTP GET requests, usually returning a 1x1 pixel, and they are fire and forget.
Step 5: VMAP, the break schedule. VMAP wraps the question "when". A `<vmap:VMAP>` document contains `<vmap:AdBreak>` elements. Each one has a `timeOffset` (`start`, `end`, a clock time like `00:10:00`, a percentage like `50%`, or a position like `#2`), a `breakType` and an `<vmap:AdSource>` that either embeds a VAST document or gives a tag URL. The player reads the VMAP once, schedules the breaks on the content timeline, and calls each tag at the right moment. Many modern players skip VMAP and let their own ad SDK (for example Google's IMA SDK or a publisher's own rule engine) decide break times, and use VMAP only for interoperability.
Step 6: SSAI and verification. In VAST 4, `<AdVerifications>` lists JavaScript from measurement vendors (OMID), and `<InteractiveCreativeFile>` carries SIMID creatives; both need a JavaScript engine in the player. In server-side ad insertion, the device never sees VAST. The stitching server requests VAST from an ad decision service, picks a media file, transcodes it to match the content stream, writes the ad segments into the HLS or DASH manifest, and fires the impression and quartile beacons itself, usually when the ad segments are requested or at the calculated play times. The device sees one continuous stream. This is why VAST 4 and the CTV Addendum define how a server should forward client signals (such as the viewer's IP address and device ID, which would otherwise show the server's address) and how to avoid firing the same beacon twice.

Now follow a CTV player through it. It reads a 30-second ad with `skipoffset` of 5 seconds, so the skip button appears at 00:05. The player fetches the 720p file at 2,500 kbit/s. A 30-second file at that bitrate is about 30 × 2,500 / 8 ≈ 9.4 MB. At 10 Mbit/s of home bandwidth the download takes about 7.5 seconds, so the player must buffer a few seconds of the ad before showing the first frame, or the ad stalls. When the first frame renders (t = 0 of the ad) it requests the impression URL. At 7.5 seconds (25%) it calls the firstQuartile URL if one exists; at 15 seconds (50%) it calls the midpoint URL, and at 30 seconds the complete URL.
Now count the work in a wrapper chain. Suppose the exchange returns a wrapper from the agency ad server (A), which points to a verification wrapper (B), which points to the final InLine (C). Each hop is one HTTP round trip of about 100 to 250 ms, so the player waits about 0.3 to 0.75 s just to reach the InLine, before it downloads any video. On the first frame the player must fire the Impression URLs of A, B and C: three beacons. At completion it fires three complete beacons. With 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% quartiles plus start, one ad view makes roughly 3 × 6 = 18 beacon requests. For a platform that serves 100 million ad views per day, that is about 1.8 billion beacon requests per day on the buyer side, and each of them is a place where a number can go missing.
intuition: VAST is a shopping list and a receipt book in one: it tells the player what to play, and it lists everyone who needs a stamped receipt when the ad is played.
The design is natural once you see who has to trust whom. The player belongs to the publisher, the creative belongs to the advertiser, and the money moves according to counts that neither side can check directly. A plain, public XML document that lists the exact URLs to call at the exact moments turns the player into a witness that everyone can read. Wrappers are the same idea applied to a chain: each party that must be paid or must verify inserts itself by adding its own beacons. Because the document is data and not code, a server (an SSAI stitcher) can run it just as well as a phone can.
mistakes: People often think VAST is a video format or a protocol that streams ads. In fact it is only a description in XML. The video itself is a normal file or manifest that the player fetches with HTTP, and the beacons are normal HTTP requests.
People often think a VAST impression means the viewer saw the ad. In fact the `Impression` event fires when the player starts to render the ad. Whether anyone could see it is a separate question that the viewability standard (at least 50% of the pixels in view for 2 continuous seconds for video, defined by the Media Rating Council) and OMID verification answer. In SSAI, the server fires the beacon with no knowledge of the screen at all.
People often think VPAID is still the way to build interactive video ads. In fact VPAID is deprecated. It ran third-party code with full access to the player, which was a security and performance problem and did not work on most TVs. SIMID (interactive) and OMID (measurement) replaced it.
People often think that if the VAST is valid the ad will play. In fact valid XML only means the document parses. The player can still fail on an unsupported codec, a media file that times out, a wrapper chain that is too long, or a CDN that does not allow the request. Error codes exist so that each failure has a reported reason.
friction: Wrapper chains are the main source of latency and loss. Every hop adds a round trip, and each party in the chain adds its own beacons. When the chain times out, nobody gets paid and the viewer sees a black screen or a spinner. Players protect themselves with a time budget and a chain limit, which means a slow ad server loses the auction even if its bid was the highest.
Beacons are an honour system. They are plain GET requests that anyone can call. Fraud operations fake the calls to produce impressions that never happened, and in SSAI the server cannot prove that a human saw anything. Third-party verification (OMID, sell-side attestations) and server-to-server checks reduce the risk but do not remove it.
There is also the privacy and identity problem. VAST says nothing about consent or identity. Those travel in the ad request (OpenRTB fields, consent strings), and every beacon URL in a wrapper chain can receive the user's IP address and device data. Each added vendor is another recipient of personal data that you must account for.
tradeoffs: Choose client-side VAST execution when you need rich interaction (SIMID), real viewability measurement and per-device signals, and your players are modern browsers or apps that you control. Choose SSAI when ad blockers or the stream's stability matter more: the ad is part of the stream, so there is no buffer between content and ad and nothing to block. You give up per-device measurement and interactivity, and you need a server that fires beacons correctly.
Choose a shallow wrapper chain (one or two hops) when latency and fill matter. Choose deeper chains only when contracts require third-party counting. Each extra hop adds roughly 100 to 250 ms and a chance of timeout, so the revenue you gain from an extra verification party must be compared with the impressions you lose.
Choose to return the VAST document inline in the OpenRTB bid (`adm`) when you want the shortest path; choose a URL (`nurl` or a VAST tag URI) when the creative must be assembled late, at play time, for example to insert fresh prices. The inline route is faster but fixes the content at auction time; the URL route adds a round trip but allows late decisions.
practice: Validate every creative before it enters rotation: run the IAB VAST validator or your player's test tool, and check each media file URL, codec and bitrate. Serve at least two or three renditions (for example 360p, 720p and 1080p with H.264) and keep the audio loudness within the platform's limit.
Log the error codes. A dashboard of the `[ERRORCODE]` values, split by player, exchange and ad server, shows quickly whether a drop in fill comes from timeouts (301, 402), unsupported media (403) or empty responses (303). Keep the wrapper limit at 5 or lower, and set separate timeouts for the wrapper fetch and the media fetch.
Compare counts at every boundary. Count requests, VAST responses, impressions and completions in your own system and compare them with the partner's. When the impression gap is larger than about 10%, test with a known device: play one ad and check that each beacon in the chain appears exactly once. In SSAI, check that the beacon uses the viewer's IP and user agent, not the stitcher's.
For CTV, read the 2024 CTV Addendum and test on real devices: the oldest TV models in your audience, not only the newest ones. If you run programmatic video, check that the VAST version in the bid is accepted by your SSP and that your OpenRTB response sets the right fields for video (`mimes`, durations, protocols).
interview: A VAST ad plays but billing shows fewer impressions than the player logs. How do you debug it? || Compare counts at each hop: request, VAST response, first frame, beacon sent, beacon received. Typical causes are beacon timeouts, the player closing before the request, macros that were not replaced, a wrapper that was not followed so that a party's URL never fired, and a different impression definition. Replay one device and check that each URL fires once.
Why do wrapper chains have a limit, and what happens when it is exceeded? || Each hop costs a round trip and another failure chance, and chains can loop. Players cap the depth (often 5) and time budget, and report error 302 or 301. The ad is dropped and the player continues with content, so a long chain lowers fill rate.
How does SSAI change where VAST is executed, and what breaks? || A server fetches the VAST, stitches the media into the manifest and fires the beacons. There is no device to run OMID or SIMID, the beacon IP and user agent are the server's unless forwarded, and the server's timing is an estimate of what the viewer saw. You need signal forwarding, deduplication and server-side verification.
Design the ad break logic for a 90-second mid-roll on a CTV app. || Use VMAP or SDK rules to place the break, request a VAST pod with a total duration and slot structure, ask for several short ads with a `sequence` order, apply brand and competitive separation in the ad server, set tight time budgets with a fallback filler, and report errors for every unfilled slot. Decide client or server stitching based on the device mix.
quiz: What are the two XML formats around video ad serving, and what does each decide? || VAST decides what plays in one ad: media files, click URLs and tracking events. VMAP decides when and where ad breaks occur in the content and which ad source fills each break.
Why does a wrapper chain of four ad servers hurt fill rate more than the extra beacons suggest? || Each hop is a separate HTTP round trip (about 100 to 250 ms) with its own timeout risk, and the player has a fixed time budget. The delay can exceed the budget, so the whole ad is dropped.
A 30-second ad at 2,500 kbit/s: about how large is the file, and how long to download at 10 Mbit/s? || 30 × 2,500 / 8 ≈ 9.4 MB, so about 7.5 seconds at 10 Mbit/s, which is why players buffer before the first frame.
Three parties are in a chain and every one wants impression and quartile beacons (start, 25%, 50%, 75%, complete). About how many beacon calls does one ad view create? || Six events (impression plus five tracking events) times three parties is about 18 requests.
When would you choose SSAI over client-side VAST playback? || When ad blockers, stream stability or devices with weak ad SDKs are the main problem. You give up device-level viewability, interactivity and some measurement accuracy.

@@ prebid
value: Prebid is open-source software that runs an auction for an ad slot before the publisher's ad server decides what to show. The page (Prebid.js) or a server (Prebid Server) sends a bid request to many demand partners at the same time, waits for a fixed time such as 1,000 ms, picks the best bids, and hands the winning price to the ad server as a key-value pair. The ad server then compares that price with its own direct deals. This pattern is called header bidding, because the first versions put the code in the `<head>` of the HTML page.
The problem it solved is the "waterfall". Before 2014, an ad server called demand partners one after another: first the direct deals, then exchange A, then exchange B, and so on, each with a price floor. Whoever answered first with a price above the floor won, even if a later partner would have paid more. Header bidding turned this sequence into one parallel auction, so every partner sees every impression and the highest bid wins. Publishers reported clear increases in revenue per thousand impressions (CPM) when they adopted it. The cost is latency and complexity: the page now waits for many network calls before it can show an ad.
Prebid is the open standard for this. It was started in 2015 and is now run by the non-profit Prebid.org, with hundreds of adapters, one for each demand partner. Prebid.js (version 11.x in October 2026) runs in the browser. Prebid Server (written in Go) runs the same auction in the cloud, which is how apps and connected TV (CTV) use it, because they have no page to hold JavaScript. The layers below supply the pieces: ad auctions (the rules for who wins and what they pay), programmatic advertising (the real-time bidding ecosystem of exchanges), HTTP (the transport of every bid request), OpenRTB (the bid request format Prebid Server uses), and consent (the privacy signals that decide which partners may bid).
terms: Header bidding — running a parallel auction among demand partners before the ad server is called.
Waterfall — the older method where partners are called one at a time in a fixed order, and the first to clear its floor wins.
Adapter (bidder adapter) — Prebid code for one demand partner; it builds that partner's request and parses its response.
Ad unit — a description of one ad slot: its sizes, media types (banner, video, native) and the list of bidders with their parameters.
Price granularity — the rule that rounds a bid to a bucket such as $1.80, so that the ad server needs only a finite set of line items.
Targeting keys — key-values such as `hb_pb` (price bucket), `hb_bidder` and `hb_adid` that Prebid sets on the ad server call.
Prebid Server — a server-side auction host that accepts one request from a client, calls many bidders over OpenRTB, and returns merged bids.
Prebid Cache — a store that holds winning creative markup, mainly VAST for video, so that the ad server can point to it by a short ID.
Floor — the minimum price a publisher accepts; bids under it are dropped.
history: Header bidding began around 2014 as a hack. Large publishers added several exchanges' JavaScript tags to the top of their pages, then used custom ad server line items to compare the results. Each publisher built its own wrapper, and each exchange asked for its own integration, which scaled badly. AppNexus released its wrapper as open source in 2015 and called it Prebid. The reason was simple: a shared wrapper means a demand partner writes one adapter and every publisher can use it.
In 2017 Prebid.org was formed as an independent non-profit so that no single company owned it. That year Prebid Server started, because mobile apps could not run page JavaScript, and a page with ten bidders had to download ten adapters and open ten sets of connections on the user's device. Server-side bidding moved those calls to a data centre with good bandwidth. A weakness of the server-side route is user identity: the server has no browser cookies, so partners match users with less accuracy. Prebid added user ID modules to carry identifiers across.
Around 2017 to 2019 the industry also moved from second-price to first-price auctions: the winner pays what it bid. Header bidding made this move easier to see, because publishers could compare prices outside their ad server, and Google moved its exchange to a first-price auction in 2019. Prebid added features for it, such as price floors (a module for dynamic floors) and bid adjustments.
After 2020, three forces shaped Prebid: privacy laws (GDPR, CCPA and later US state laws), which added consent modules for the IAB's TCF and GPP strings; the loss of third-party cookies in Safari and Firefox and the uncertain path in Chrome; and the growth of video and CTV, which brought video bids, Prebid Cache and Prebid Server SDKs for apps. The project releases often: Prebid.js 11.44.0 was published on 24 October 2026.
how: Step 1: setup. The publisher defines ad units in JavaScript. Each unit lists its sizes and a list of bidders, each with its own parameters (for example a placement ID for that exchange). The publisher loads a Prebid.js build that contains only the adapters and modules it needs; this keeps the file small, because each adapter adds code that the page must download and parse.
Step 2: the page calls `pbjs.requestBids({ timeout: 1000 })` before it asks the ad server for ads. Prebid first checks consent. A consent management module reads the TCF, GPP or US privacy string from the page's consent platform and decides which bidders may be called and which user IDs may be sent. This is the only layer that makes the auction lawful, so it runs first.
Step 3: parallel bids. For each bidder Prebid builds a request, usually an HTTP POST with JSON in the partner's own or OpenRTB format, and sends all of them at once. Browsers allow a limited number of parallel connections per host, but bidders have different hosts, so the calls run in parallel. Each response is parsed, converted to Prebid's common bid object (price, currency, size, creative), checked against the floor, and adjusted if the publisher set a bid adjustment (for example to account for different fees).
Step 4: close. The auction ends when all bidders have answered or the timeout fires. Late bids are ignored: a bid that arrives at 1,150 ms in a 1,000 ms auction is thrown away, whatever its price. So the timeout is a direct trade of revenue against page speed. Under the hood this is the auction concept in its simplest form: a sealed-bid auction with a deadline, where the rule is first price (the winner pays its own bid) and the seller sets a reserve (the floor). Prebid does not run the payment; it only finds the best sealed bid, and the winner is billed after the ad renders, through its billing URL or the beacons in its creative.
Step 5: hand-off. For each ad unit, Prebid picks the highest bid, rounds its price down to a bucket according to the price granularity, and sets targeting keys on the ad server request (`pbjs.setTargetingForGPTAsync()` for Google Ad Manager). The ad server holds one line item per bucket, each with a matching key-value rule and the bucket price as its value. When the ad server runs its own selection, the Prebid line item competes with direct deals and with the exchange demand inside the ad server. If the Prebid line item wins, the ad server returns a small creative that calls Prebid's render function with `hb_adid`, and Prebid draws the winning bidder's creative in the slot. If anything else wins, Prebid does nothing.
Step 6: Prebid Server and reporting. Instead of calling each bidder, the client sends one request to Prebid Server. The server builds an OpenRTB request for each bidder (this is where OpenRTB, the common bid request format, pays off: one internal format, many partners), calls them with a time budget, and returns all bids in one response. For video and CTV, the server stores the VAST markup in Prebid Cache and returns a cache URL, so the ad server can reference a short URL instead of a large XML body. The connected TV device sends one call, and the server does the fan-out. For example, a TV app asks for a break of four 30-second slots. The server sends OpenRTB requests to six bidders with a budget of 800 ms, each bidder returns VAST in the `adm` field, and the server keeps each winning VAST in the cache and returns its URL. Because the server is a long-lived process in a data centre, it can keep warm connections to bidders with HTTP keep-alive and compress responses, so each bidder call costs less than a cold call from a phone. The server also applies the same consent rules before it calls any bidder, using the consent string the app passes in the request (the `regs` and `user.ext` fields of OpenRTB). Many publishers run a hybrid: a few strong partners client-side (better user matching) and the rest server-side (fewer browser connections).

example: A page has one banner slot and a timeout of 1,000 ms. Five bidders answer as follows. A bids $1.20 at 320 ms. B bids $1.85 at 480 ms. C bids $0.90 at 250 ms. D bids $2.10 at 1,150 ms. E returns no bid. The auction closes at 1,000 ms, so D is dropped although it is the highest price. The best remaining bid is B at $1.85.
Price granularity is "medium": $0.10 steps up to $20. Prebid rounds $1.85 down to $1.80, so it sets `hb_pb=1.80`, `hb_bidder=B`. The ad server has a line item with the rule `hb_pb = 1.80` and a rate of $1.80. A direct line item for a campaign is priced at $1.60 with equal priority, and the exchange's dynamic demand returns $1.70. The Prebid line item at $1.80 wins. The publisher is paid B's real bid of $1.85 in the first-price model, but the ad server saw only $1.80, so the 5 cents is "bucket leakage". A finer granularity ($0.01 steps) removes this loss but needs about 2,000 line items for a $0 to $20 range instead of 200, which makes the ad server slower to configure.
Now the timeout cost. Suppose the median bidder responds in 300 ms and the 90th percentile response is 900 ms. A timeout of 1,000 ms captures about 90% or more of the bids. Reducing it to 500 ms speeds up the page by up to 500 ms for pages where slow bidders hold the auction open, but may lose perhaps 30 to 40% of the bids, mostly from the partners that tend to bid the highest because they run heavier models. The right number comes from your own logs: plot revenue and page speed against timeout and look for the knee.
intuition: Header bidding is an open-outcry auction in a room instead of a series of phone calls where the first buyer to say yes wins.
The idea is natural because the old order was arbitrary. Nobody knew whether the fifth partner on the list would have paid twice as much, so the seller never saw the true price. Asking everyone at once and taking the highest bid is the simplest way to find a price. The cost is that you must wait for the slowest bidder you are willing to wait for, so the design is a trade between price discovery and speed. Putting the wrapper in the open makes it fair as well: the logic that picks the winner is public, so a publisher can check that no bidder is favoured.
mistakes: People often think header bidding is only a JavaScript trick in the browser. In fact the same pattern also runs on servers (Prebid Server, or Google's Open Bidding) and in apps and CTV, where there is no page at all.
People often think Prebid sends the winning ad to the user. In fact Prebid only decides a price and a bidder. The ad server still makes the final decision, with its own direct deals and demand, and only if the Prebid line item wins is the Prebid creative shown.
People often think a longer timeout is always better for revenue. In fact late bids help only up to a point; beyond it, the slow page lowers viewability, causes users to leave, and delays other ad slots. Common settings are 800 to 1,500 ms, but the right value depends on the audience and the bidders.
friction: Latency is the first cost. Every bidder is a network call, every adapter is JavaScript, and the auction must finish before the ad server is called. Ads load later and the page may shift or show an empty slot for a while. Lazy loading and parallel requests for several slots help, but the page is still slower than one with no header bidding.
Line item sprawl is the second. A Google Ad Manager setup needs line items for each price bucket, and sometimes for each size and media type, which means hundreds or thousands of items to create and maintain. Granularity that is too coarse loses money; too fine makes the setup slow.
Transparency is the third. The ad server still takes the final decision, and a header bid competes through a rounded price. Publishers must trust the ad server's comparison. Some use an "unified pricing rules" setting or server-side decision logic to make the competition fair. Also, in first-price auctions, bidders began to "shade" their bids, so the clearing price is not a simple measure of value.
User identity and privacy are the fourth. Server-side bidding means bidders see no browser cookies, so match rates fall and bid prices fall with them. Consent strings have to be read correctly and sent to every partner, and an error here is a legal risk, not only a revenue risk. Prebid supports consent modules, but the publisher still decides the policy.
tradeoffs: Choose client-side Prebid.js when user matching matters most and you have a modest list of bidders (say 5 to 8). Cookies and browser IDs raise bids, and the page can show you which bidders time out. Choose Prebid Server when you have many bidders, an app or CTV inventory, or a slow mobile web audience; you give up some user-match rate, usually seen as lower CPMs from the bidders that depend on cookies.
Choose a shorter timeout (about 500 to 800 ms) when your content is mobile-first and speed affects revenue through viewability and bounce. Choose a longer timeout (1,200 to 1,500 ms) for desktop audiences and long-lived pages, where the higher bids from slow bidders pay for the delay. Decide from your own revenue-versus-timeout curve.
Choose Prebid over a closed alternative (Google Open Bidding, Amazon's solutions) when you want to see the bids, set your own rules, and switch partners without code from one vendor. You give up the vendor's managed service and need your own team to maintain the build and the line items. Many publishers run both.
practice: Build only the adapters you use, and remove partners that rarely win. Measure each bidder's response rate, timeout rate, win rate and revenue share every week, and drop or reduce any partner that costs more in latency than it earns.
Set the timeout from data: collect the response-time distribution, then choose the timeout where extra revenue per added 100 ms becomes small. Use `pbjs.requestBids` for all slots in one call, so that one slow bidder does not hold several auctions separately. Lazy-load below-the-fold slots so they do not join the first auction.
Send consent first. Use the TCF, GPP and US privacy modules and test with consent denied, so that no bidder or ID module fires without permission. For CTV, use Prebid Server with Prebid Cache, keep the VAST chain short (see the VAST read) and set `tmax` to leave time for the ad server and the stitcher after the auction.
Use the floors module for dynamic floors by device, geography and size, and check bid adjustments for fee differences. Pin the Prebid.js version in your build, and read release notes: the project ships often (11.44.0 on 24 October 2026), and a change in a module can shift auction behaviour. Test new builds against a copy of the page before you roll them out.

@@ bigtable
value: Bigtable is a database that stores a huge sorted table across thousands of machines. You give it a row key, a column and a timestamp, and it gives you the bytes stored there. It does not have SQL joins, secondary indexes or multi-row transactions. In return it gives you something a single-server database cannot: a table of petabytes that grows by adding machines, with single-row reads and writes that take a few milliseconds, at hundreds of thousands to millions of operations per second.
Google built Bigtable in 2004 to 2006 for its own products, such as the web index, Google Earth, Google Analytics and personalised search, which all needed to store very large, sparse tables keyed by things like URL or user. The 2006 paper (Chang et al., OSDI) made the design public. It inspired Apache HBase (open source, 2007, a Hadoop project) and Apache Cassandra, and it is now sold as Cloud Bigtable on Google Cloud. In ad tech and data platforms, this family holds user profiles, device graphs, counters and event timelines: workloads with a very high write rate, lookups by key, and scans of one key range.
The design is a careful assembly of lower-layer ideas. An LSM tree (lsm) turns random writes into sequential ones. A write-ahead log (wal) makes those writes durable before they reach disk files. Partitioning (partitioning) splits the sorted key space into row ranges and spreads them across servers. The distributed key-value model (nosql) defines the simple, sparse data model. A distributed file system (GFS/Colossus for Bigtable, hdfs for HBase) stores the files so that servers hold no irreplaceable state, and a coordination service (Chubby for Bigtable, zookeeper for HBase) elects the master and tracks which servers are alive.
terms: Row key — the primary key; all rows are stored in sorted byte order by this key, and it is the only index.
Column family — a group of columns declared in the schema (for example `profile`); settings such as compression and the number of versions kept are set per family.
Cell — the value stored at one (row, column, timestamp); a column can hold several timestamped versions.
Tablet (HBase: region) — a contiguous range of rows; the unit of assignment to a server and the unit of splitting.
Tablet server (HBase: region server) — a process that serves reads and writes for the tablets assigned to it.
Memtable (HBase: MemStore) — the in-memory sorted buffer where recent writes go.
SSTable (HBase: HFile) — an immutable, sorted file of key-value cells with a block index, written when a memtable is flushed.
Compaction — merging several SSTables into fewer, larger ones to cut read cost and remove deleted or old cells.
Hotspot — a tablet that receives most of the traffic, usually because many consecutive keys (such as timestamps) fall in the same range.
history: In the early 2000s Google's products needed storage that was larger than any relational database could hold on commodity machines. Google already had GFS (2003) for files and MapReduce (2004) for batch computing. Bigtable, built by Fay Chang, Jeff Dean, Sanjay Ghemawat and colleagues, added structured, low-latency storage on top of GFS. It went into production around 2005 and the paper appeared at OSDI in 2006. The paper's main idea was to keep the data model small (a sorted map) and let storage details such as compression, caching and locality groups be tunable by the user.
Apache HBase began in 2007 at the company Powerset as a Bigtable clone on top of Hadoop's HDFS. It became a Hadoop subproject in 2008 and a top-level Apache project in 2010. Facebook used it for its messaging system around 2010, and many companies adopted it for time series and profile stores. Cassandra (2008, from Facebook) combined Bigtable's data model and SSTable storage with Dynamo's peer-to-peer replication, which removed the master.
Google offered the service to others as Cloud Bigtable, generally available in 2015, and separated storage from compute more completely over time. Today a Cloud Bigtable node holds only pointers to tablets; the data lives in Google's distributed file system, Colossus. Apache HBase continues as an open-source project: its downloads page in October 2026 lists the 2.5 and 2.6 lines (both updated on 1 October 2026) and HBase 3.0.0, released on 5 August 2026. Newer designs, such as Spanner and the cloud object-store-based lakehouse tables, have taken some of the workloads, but the sorted-map-over-LSM idea lives on in many systems, from RocksDB-based stores to Cassandra and ScyllaDB.
how: The data model. Bigtable is "a sparse, distributed, persistent multi-dimensional sorted map": a map from (row key, column, timestamp) to an uninterpreted byte string. A column is written `family:qualifier`. Families are declared in the schema and are few; qualifiers are created freely, so each row can have a different set of columns, and an empty cell costs nothing (sparse). Rows are sorted by key in byte order, so a scan of a key range reads neighbouring rows from the same place on disk. Reads and writes of a single row are atomic, however many columns they touch. There are no transactions across rows. This one rule is what makes the system easy to scale.
Partitioning. The sorted key space is cut into tablets, ranges of consecutive rows. In the paper a tablet is about 100 to 200 MB; HBase splits a region at 10 GB by default and Cloud Bigtable splits and merges dynamically. A tablet server holds tens to hundreds of tablets. A master process assigns tablets to servers, balances load, and handles server failures. Clients do not go through the master for data: they look up which server holds a row in a special METADATA table (HBase calls it `hbase:meta`), cache the answer, and talk to the server directly. In the paper the lookup has three levels (a file in Chubby points to a root tablet, which points to METADATA tablets, which point to user tablets), enough to address about 2^34 tablets. Partitioning by range, not by hash, is what allows ordered scans. It is also why a bad key can create a hotspot.
The write path (the LSM tree). When a write arrives at the tablet server, it first appends the mutation to a commit log (the write-ahead log) in the distributed file system, and then inserts it into the in-memory memtable. The log append is sequential and is synced for durability; the memtable insert is in memory. When the write is acknowledged it is safe even if the server crashes, because the log can be replayed. When the memtable reaches a size limit (128 MB by default in HBase), it is frozen and written to the file system as a new SSTable. This is called a minor compaction. SSTables are never changed after they are written.
The read path. A read must look in the memtable and in every SSTable of the tablet and merge the results, newest first. Without help this would touch many files. Three things limit the work. An SSTable has a block index in memory so a lookup reads one block (64 KB by default). A Bloom filter per SSTable (see the Bloom filter read) says "this row is definitely not in this file" for most files, so a point read usually touches one or two files. And a block cache keeps recently used blocks in memory. Over time the number of SSTables grows, so the server runs merging compactions, and occasionally a major compaction that rewrites everything in a tablet into one SSTable and finally removes deleted cells. Deletes are written as markers called tombstones, because files cannot be changed; the tombstone hides older versions until a major compaction removes them.
Recovery. Tablet servers keep no irreplaceable state. If a server dies, the master notices (through a lost Chubby lock in Bigtable, through ZooKeeper in HBase) and reassigns its tablets. The new server reads the SSTables from the shared file system and replays the part of the commit log that was not yet flushed. Nothing is copied between servers, so recovery takes seconds to minutes, and the requests for those key ranges fail or retry meanwhile. Durability comes from the file system's replication (three copies in HDFS), not from the tablet server.
How the layers help. The LSM tree gives the high write rate and sequential disk use. The write-ahead log gives durability and fast crash recovery. Range partitioning gives the scaling: add servers, move tablets. Chubby or ZooKeeper give agreement about who is master and who is alive, which is a hard distributed problem that Bigtable avoids solving itself. The shared file system makes the servers disposable.

example: Take a device-event table for an ad platform. Which row key do you choose? A first idea is `timestamp#device_id`. All new events have the current time as a prefix, so every write goes to the last tablet, the one that holds the highest keys. One server takes all the writes while the others are idle. This is a hotspot. If that server can do 20,000 writes per second and you need 200,000, the design fails whatever the cluster size.
A better key is `device_id#reverse_timestamp`, with the device ID hashed or evenly distributed. Writes now spread over all tablets, because different devices hash to different ranges. The reverse timestamp (a large constant minus the time in milliseconds) makes the newest event sort first, so "the last 10 events of device X" is a short scan that starts at the prefix `device_id#`. The same data now supports one query well (events of one device, newest first) and another one badly (all events in the last hour), which needs a full scan or a second table keyed by time bucket. This is the basic rule of Bigtable design: the row key is your only index, so you choose it for the query you must serve fast.
Now the numbers. A 1 TB table with tablets of about 150 MB has about 1,000,000 MB / 150 MB ≈ 6,700 tablets. On 100 tablet servers that is about 67 tablets each. If each server can sustain 10,000 small writes per second (Google's performance guide gives about this order of magnitude for a Cloud Bigtable SSD node), the cluster takes about 1,000,000 writes per second when the load is even. A write of 1 KB costs one sequential log append and one memtable insert. With a 128 MB memtable, a server writing 10 MB per second flushes a new SSTable roughly every 13 seconds, so over an hour a tablet could have hundreds of small files without compaction. That is the read cost that compaction and Bloom filters pay down: with 10 files and 1% Bloom filters, a read of a missing row touches about 0.1 files on average instead of 10.
intuition: A giant sorted address book split into volumes, where each librarian holds a few volumes, scribbles new entries in a notebook first, and files them into the volumes in batches.
The design is natural because it accepts one limit and turns it into a strength. A disk or a network is slow at random updates but fast at sequential ones, so every change is first appended (the log) and later merged in bulk (compaction). A table that is sorted by one key can be cut anywhere, so it can be spread over any number of machines without coordination between them. Refusing multi-row transactions removes the need for distributed locking on the hot path: each row lives in exactly one tablet on exactly one server, so a single-row update is an ordinary local operation. Everything hard, such as replication and failure detection, is pushed to the file system and the lock service, which are built once and reused.
mistakes: People often think Bigtable or HBase is a relational database with a different syntax. In fact there are no joins, no foreign keys and no secondary indexes. The row key is the only index, and the schema is designed backward from the queries.
People often think that scaling out always spreads the load. In fact range partitioning spreads load only if the keys do. A key that starts with a timestamp, a sequence number or a customer ID with a few huge customers puts most traffic on one tablet, and adding servers does not help.
People often think a major compaction or a delete frees space at once. In fact deletes are tombstones until a compaction removes them, and expired versions stay on disk until then. Compaction also uses disk and network, so a major compaction at a busy time can raise latency.
People often think that Cloud Bigtable stores data on its nodes, so a node failure loses data. In fact data is in the shared file system, and a node holds only pointers and caches. This is why resizing a cluster takes minutes and mostly moves metadata.
friction: The hardest problem is the row key. You get one sort order; every other access pattern needs a second table that you keep in step yourself, with no transaction across the two. Time-series and counters tempt you to put time first, which makes hotspots. The usual cures (hash or salt prefix, reversed domain, field promotion) trade away easy range scans over the original order.
Tail latency is the next. Most reads take a few milliseconds, but compactions, garbage collection pauses (a serious issue for HBase region servers running on the JVM), cache misses and tablet moves produce slow requests at the 99th percentile. Clients need timeouts, retries with backoff and hedged reads. Both systems also have a small window when a tablet is being moved or recovered and requests fail until it is online again.
Consistency is limited. Single-row operations are atomic and strongly consistent in one cluster. Across clusters, Cloud Bigtable replication is eventually consistent by default, and you can configure read-your-writes or stronger behaviour by routing with app profiles. HBase offers region replicas with "timeline" consistency. Multi-row atomicity needs a layer such as Percolator-style transactions, Apache Phoenix or Omid, which is a different system.
tradeoffs: Choose Bigtable or HBase when you have very high write rates, key-based lookups and range scans over one key, and a data size that no single server can hold (many terabytes or more). Choose a relational database such as PostgreSQL or Aurora when you need joins, several indexes and multi-row transactions, and your data fits on a big server or a few shards. You give up the flexible queries of SQL to gain horizontal scale.
Choose Cloud Bigtable when you are on Google Cloud and want no operations work; choose Apache HBase when you must run on your own hardware or in another cloud, or you already operate Hadoop. You give up some operations convenience with HBase, and with Cloud Bigtable you give up portability and the ability to look inside.
Choose Cassandra over HBase when you need multi-region writes with no single master and tunable consistency; choose HBase or Bigtable when you want strong consistency for a row and ordered scans with a simple model. Cassandra gives you availability across regions but weaker guarantees per row; HBase and Bigtable give you simpler semantics but depend on a master and a file system.
practice: Design the row key first. Write down the three queries you must serve, then choose a key where each is a point read or a short scan. Avoid a leading timestamp. Use a hashed or well-distributed prefix, put the entity ID next, and use a reversed timestamp at the end if you want the newest first. Test with the Bigtable key visualizer (Cloud Bigtable) or the HBase region load page to find hotspots before launch.
Keep keys, family names and qualifiers short, because they are stored with every cell. Use few column families (one to three), because each family has its own memtable and files. Set the number of versions and a time-to-live per family so old data is removed during compaction. Pre-split a new table into many regions or tablets if you plan a large bulk load, otherwise the first hours hit a single server.
Monitor tail latency, the CPU load per node, the number of SSTables per tablet (HBase), the compaction queue and the size of the largest rows. Keep rows small (well under 100 MB; Google advises values typically no larger than 10 MB) because a huge row cannot be split across tablets. For HBase, run a current stable line (2.5 or 2.6, or 3.0 when your distribution supports it), size heaps with care, and use off-heap caches to limit garbage collection pauses.
interview: A Bigtable table receives timestamp-keyed writes and one server is at 100% CPU. What is wrong and what do you change? || A monotonic key creates a hotspot: all new rows fall in the last tablet. Walk through what happens on a write and what makes it durable. || The server appends the mutation to the commit log in the shared file system, which is replicated, then inserts it into the memtable and acknowledges. Durability comes from the log; the memtable is only a cache of recent writes. When it fills it is flushed into an immutable SSTable.
Why can a read be slow, and how is it kept fast? || It must merge the memtable and several SSTables. Block indexes, per-file Bloom filters, block caches and compaction (which reduces the file count) limit the work. Tombstones and many versions slow scans until compaction removes them.
When would you pick Bigtable over Cassandra, or the reverse? || Bigtable or HBase when you need strong row-level consistency, ordered scans and a managed Google service. Cassandra when you need multi-region active-active writes, no master and tunable consistency.

Why are SSTables immutable, and how are deletes handled? || Immutable files allow sequential writes, simple caching and cheap concurrent reads. A delete is written as a tombstone marker that hides older data until a compaction removes both.
Your table has 600 GB and tablets of about 150 MB. About how many tablets, and how many per server on 50 servers? || 600,000 MB / 150 MB = 4,000 tablets, which is 80 per server.
Why does a tablet server crash not lose acknowledged writes? || Each write is appended to a replicated commit log in the shared file system before it is acknowledged, so a new server can replay it.
When would you choose salted row keys over time-ordered keys? || When write throughput matters more than ordered time scans, because time-ordered keys put all new writes in one tablet. You give up simple range scans and run one scan per salt bucket.

@@ gfxapi
value: A graphics API is the programming interface through which an application tells a GPU what to draw. The application cannot send instructions to the GPU directly: each vendor's chip has its own machine code and its own hardware details, and the operating system must share the GPU between programs. The API (OpenGL, Direct3D 12, Vulkan, Metal, WebGPU) defines a common way to create buffers and textures, write shader programs, record draw and compute commands, and present the finished image on the screen. The driver then translates these calls into the commands of the actual chip.
The reason the family keeps changing is the cost of the CPU side. A modern game or browser draws thousands of objects per frame, 60 or 120 times a second, which leaves 16.7 ms or 8.3 ms for each frame. Older APIs such as OpenGL hid many details: the driver tracked state, checked errors, managed memory and compiled shaders when you drew. This was easy for programmers but made one CPU thread the bottleneck. The newer APIs (Direct3D 12, Vulkan, Metal) move this work to the application. You record commands in advance into command buffers, on many threads, and you manage memory and synchronisation yourself. WebGPU brings the same style to browsers, with a safer surface.
What this gives the layer above is the ability to turn a scene into pixels at a predictable cost. Game engines, browsers (page compositing, WebGL, WebGPU), design tools, video players and machine learning runtimes sit on it. The layers below supply the model. Computer graphics (graphics) defines the problem: transform a 3D scene to an image. The rasterisation pipeline (raster) gives the fixed sequence of stages that the API exposes: vertex processing, primitive assembly, rasterisation, fragment processing, depth test and blending. Shading and physically based rendering (shading) give the content of the shader programs. GPU architecture (simt) explains why those programs run as thousands of lock-step threads and why the API asks for work in large batches.
terms: Command buffer — a list of GPU commands (set pipeline, bind resources, draw, dispatch) that the application records on the CPU and submits to a queue once.
Queue — the channel through which command buffers reach the GPU; GPUs usually have graphics, compute and copy queues that can run in parallel.
Pipeline state object (PSO) — an immutable bundle of shaders and fixed-function settings (blending, depth test, vertex layout) compiled ahead of time.
Shader — a small program that runs on the GPU for every vertex, pixel or compute work item.
Descriptor / bind group — an object that tells shaders where their buffers, textures and samplers are.
Barrier / fence / semaphore — synchronisation tools; a barrier orders GPU operations, a fence lets the CPU wait for the GPU, a semaphore orders work between queues.
Swapchain — a set of images that the application renders into in turn while the display shows another one.
SPIR-V / WGSL / HLSL / MSL — shader languages and formats: SPIR-V is Vulkan's binary shader format, WGSL is WebGPU's language, HLSL is DirectX's, MSL is Metal's.
Draw call — one command that asks the GPU to render a set of triangles with the current state.
history: Graphics APIs started with the need to make 3D hardware usable across vendors. Silicon Graphics created IRIS GL for its workstations, and in 1992 turned it into the open OpenGL specification. OpenGL stayed the cross-platform standard for 25 years; its last major version, 4.6, appeared in 2017. Microsoft's Direct3D (1996, as part of DirectX) took the Windows game market and went through many versions. Both used a "state machine" model where the driver did a great deal of hidden work.
By 2010 GPUs had become fast, but CPUs were the limit: a single driver thread had to validate state, track resource hazards and compile shaders on demand. Game developers on consoles, who had direct access to the hardware, showed what was possible. In 2013 AMD announced Mantle, a low-level API for PC. Apple released Metal in 2014 for iOS and Microsoft released Direct3D 12 with Windows 10 in 2015. Khronos Group, which manages OpenGL, took Mantle as the starting point for Vulkan, released as version 1.0 in February 2016.
Vulkan then grew by steady versions: 1.1 (2018), 1.2 (2020), 1.3 (2022), which made dynamic rendering and the improved synchronisation API part of the core, and 1.4 (December 2024), which promoted 16 extensions into the core and raised the minimum limits and required features. Apple's Metal reached Metal 4 in 2025 with tools to run machine-learning networks in the command stream and with more efficient shader compilation. Direct3D 12 added ray tracing (DXR), mesh shaders and work graphs through its Agility SDK.
WebGPU was designed from 2017 by Apple, Google, Mozilla and Microsoft in the W3C. It is not a thin copy of any one native API: it takes the common ground of Vulkan, Metal and Direct3D 12 and adds safety checks so web pages cannot crash or read the memory of other programs. Chrome and Edge shipped it in version 113 on Windows, macOS and ChromeOS, and later on Android. Firefox added Windows support in version 141 and Apple-silicon Macs in 145, and Safari 26 supports it on Apple's 26 platforms. WebGPU also gives browsers general-purpose compute shaders, which is why it is used for machine learning in the browser.
how: Step 1: connect to the device. The application asks the API for the available GPUs (physical devices or adapters), picks one, and creates a logical device and one or more queues. In Vulkan this means creating an instance, listing physical devices and their queue families, and enabling the features and extensions you want. Nothing is hidden: if you do not enable a feature, you cannot use it. This explicit set-up is what lets the driver skip checks later.
Step 2: create resources and memory. Buffers hold vertices, indices and constants. Images and textures hold pixels. In Vulkan and Direct3D 12 you allocate large blocks of GPU memory yourself and place resources in them, because allocating per object is slow. A staging buffer in CPU-visible memory is copied to fast GPU-only memory by a copy command. WebGPU and Metal manage more of this for you; the application still chooses usage flags and whether memory is shared with the CPU, which matters on integrated and Apple-silicon GPUs where memory is unified.
Step 3: build pipelines. Shaders are written in a high-level language (GLSL or HLSL for native APIs, WGSL for WebGPU, MSL for Metal) and compiled to an intermediate form (SPIR-V, DXIL, Metal's AIR). A pipeline state object bundles shaders with the state that used to be set one call at a time. The driver compiles it to the GPU's machine code once, at creation, which can take milliseconds to hundreds of milliseconds. The shading concept matters here: a material, with its lighting equation and textures, becomes a shader and a set of bindings.
Step 4: record commands. For each frame the application begins a command buffer (WebGPU: a command encoder), begins a render pass that names the colour and depth targets, binds a pipeline and the resources, and issues draw calls. Recording is cheap: it appends bytes to a buffer and does not touch the GPU. Because each command buffer is independent, several CPU threads can record in parallel. This is the main performance gain over OpenGL, where one thread owned the context.
Step 5: submit and synchronise. The application submits the command buffers to a queue. From then on, the GPU runs them without the CPU. The programmer must say which operations depend on which. A barrier says that writes by one pass must finish before the next pass reads them, and may also change the memory layout of an image. A fence lets the CPU wait until a frame is done so that it can reuse buffers. Typical engines keep two or three frames in flight: the CPU records frame N+1 while the GPU draws frame N. If you forget a barrier, you get flicker or corruption that depends on the hardware, which is why validation layers exist.
Step 6: the GPU runs the pipeline. The GPU front end reads the commands and starts the stages of the rasterisation pipeline. Vertex shaders run once per vertex and compute screen positions. Fixed hardware assembles triangles and rasterises them into fragments (candidate pixels). Fragment shaders run once per fragment and compute colour. The depth test (z-buffer) keeps the nearest surface, and blending combines the colour with the target. Every shader runs as many threads in groups (32 or 64 threads executing the same instruction at once, the SIMT model), so the GPU is fast when neighbouring pixels do similar work and slow when they branch differently.
Descriptors and layouts. Shaders do not receive pointers to resources. The application builds descriptor sets (Vulkan), root signatures (Direct3D 12) or bind groups (WebGPU) that list which buffer and texture sit in each shader slot, and binds the set before a draw. Changing the whole set for every draw is slow, so engines group resources by how often they change: per frame, per material and per object. In the same spirit the API asks you to declare image layouts, because the GPU stores textures in different internal arrangements for rendering and for sampling.
Step 7: present. The finished image goes to the swapchain and is shown at the next display refresh. The API also exposes compute-only pipelines, with no triangles, for general work such as post-processing, physics, video and neural networks. A browser uses the same machinery: it draws the page into layers with the platform API (Metal, Direct3D or Vulkan through a translation layer), composites them and presents the result.

example: Here is the shape of a minimal WebGPU frame. It draws one triangle. The code is short, but every line is one of the steps above.
```js
const adapter = await navigator.gpu.requestAdapter();
const device = await adapter.requestDevice();
const ctx = canvas.getContext("webgpu");
const format = navigator.gpu.getPreferredCanvasFormat();
ctx.configure({ device, format });

const module = device.createShaderModule({ code: `
  @vertex fn vs(@builtin(vertex_index) i: u32)
      -> @builtin(position) vec4f {
    var p = array(vec2f(0,.5), vec2f(-.5,-.5), vec2f(.5,-.5));
    return vec4f(p[i], 0, 1);
  }
  @fragment fn fs() -> @location(0) vec4f {
    return vec4f(1, 0.4, 0, 1);
  }` });

const pipeline = device.createRenderPipeline({
  layout: "auto",
  vertex: { module, entryPoint: "vs" },
  fragment: { module, entryPoint: "fs", targets: [{ format }] },
});

const enc = device.createCommandEncoder();
const pass = enc.beginRenderPass({ colorAttachments: [{
  view: ctx.getCurrentTexture().createView(),
  loadOp: "clear", storeOp: "store" }] });
pass.setPipeline(pipeline);
pass.draw(3);
pass.end();
device.queue.submit([enc.finish()]);
```
Count the work. The vertex shader runs 3 times. The triangle covers a part of a 1920 × 1080 canvas, which has 2,073,600 pixels; if the triangle covers a quarter of the screen, the fragment shader runs about 518,000 times. On a GPU these run as groups of threads, so the cost is small. The CPU did very little: it recorded about five commands and submitted once.
Now the cost model for a real scene. Suppose a scene has 20,000 objects. If each draw call costs 2 microseconds of CPU time in a thin API such as Vulkan, recording takes 40 ms on one thread, which already misses the 16.7 ms budget. Split across 4 threads, each records 5,000 draws in 10 ms. These numbers are illustrative, but they show why engines also reduce the number of draws with instancing (one draw call for many copies), indirect draws that the GPU fills itself, and culling on the GPU. In an older API with a higher per-draw cost, the practical limit was a few thousand draws per frame.
intuition: Instead of telling a chef each step while you stand in the kitchen, you hand over a complete written order and a list of which dishes must wait for which.
The design is natural once you see where the time goes. The GPU is a wide, deep pipeline that wants to be kept busy with large batches, and it runs far from the CPU, across a bus. Talking to it one call at a time, with the driver checking everything each time, wastes the CPU and leaves the GPU idle. Recording work into command buffers makes the cost of describing work separate from the cost of running it, so it can be spread across threads and prepared early. The price is that the API stops protecting you: you now carry the duty to order the work, place the memory and compile the pipelines ahead of time. The newer APIs are low level because the old ones were too smart in the wrong place.
mistakes: People often think a newer API such as Vulkan is always faster than OpenGL. In fact it only removes driver overhead and gives control. A program with good batching in OpenGL can beat a badly written Vulkan program, and a GPU-bound scene runs at the same speed in both.
People often think the graphics API is the GPU's language. In fact the driver translates API calls and shader intermediate code into the real machine code, which differs between vendors and even between chip generations. This is why shaders are compiled on first use and cached.
People often think WebGPU is just WebGL with a new name. In fact WebGL is OpenGL ES with its state machine. WebGPU is a different design with pipelines, command encoders, bind groups and compute shaders, and it maps onto Vulkan, Metal and Direct3D 12 underneath.
friction: The first problem is complexity. A Vulkan program of about 1,000 lines may be needed to show a triangle, and you must handle memory, synchronisation, swapchain re-creation and device loss yourself. Most teams use an engine, a helper library (for example the Vulkan Memory Allocator) or a framework, and few write raw Vulkan.
The second is fragmentation. Windows uses Direct3D 12 and Vulkan, Apple platforms use Metal, the web uses WebGPU, and consoles have their own APIs. Cross-platform engines keep several back ends, or translate shaders (SPIR-V to MSL or HLSL) with tools such as SPIRV-Cross, Naga or Slang. Subtle differences in behaviour and driver bugs make testing on many GPUs mandatory.
The third is shader and pipeline compilation. Creating a pipeline can take tens to hundreds of milliseconds. If an engine creates it during a frame, the player sees a stutter. Pipeline caches, precompilation at load time, and (for the web) asynchronous pipeline creation reduce it, but the combinatorial growth of shader variants keeps it a live problem.
The fourth is correctness and safety. Missing barriers cause bugs that appear on one GPU only. Drivers can crash or hang on bad input. WebGPU adds validation and bounds checks, which helps security but costs some speed, and its feature set is a safe subset of what native APIs offer.
tradeoffs: Choose Vulkan or Direct3D 12 when you build an engine that must use many CPU cores, control memory, and run on a known set of platforms, and you have the people to maintain it. You give up simplicity and gain control and predictable CPU cost. Choose OpenGL or a higher-level engine when the scene is modest (thousands of draws) and speed of development matters more.
Choose Metal when you target Apple devices only: it is the only first-class API there, tightly tied to Apple's GPUs and tools. Choose Vulkan on Android and Linux. If you need both, plan for a back end for each or a layer such as MoltenVK or wgpu, which loses some performance and the newest features.
Choose WebGPU for new web graphics and for compute on the web: it has modern features, compute shaders and a safer design. Choose WebGL when you must support older browsers and devices, because its reach is wider. WebGPU is available in current Chrome, Edge, Firefox (on supported platforms) and Safari 26, but the supported hardware set is still narrower than WebGL's, so check your audience.
Choose a ready engine (Unreal, Unity, Godot) or a thin wrapper when graphics is not your product. Write against the raw API when you are building the engine, a browser, a video tool or a visualisation product with special needs. You trade control for engineering time either way.
practice: Measure on the GPU, not by guessing. Use the platform's tools: RenderDoc and Nsight Graphics for Vulkan and Direct3D, PIX on Windows, Xcode's GPU tools for Metal, and the browser's WebGPU timestamp queries. Check whether you are CPU bound (command recording takes longer than the frame) or GPU bound (the GPU busy time is the limit) before you optimise.
Reduce state changes and draws: sort by pipeline, use instancing, merge static meshes, and use indirect draws or GPU culling for large scenes. Create pipelines at load time, cache them on disk, and keep the number of shader variants under control. Allocate memory in big blocks and reuse buffers. Keep two or three frames in flight, and avoid waiting on the GPU in the middle of a frame.
Turn on validation layers (Vulkan), the debug layer (Direct3D 12) or Metal's API validation during development; they find missing barriers and wrong usage. Test on at least one GPU from each major vendor, and on the oldest hardware you promise to support. For the web, handle the case where `navigator.gpu` is missing or the adapter request fails, and fall back to WebGL or a simpler view.
interview: Why are Vulkan and Direct3D 12 called explicit APIs, and what does the programmer take over? || The driver no longer tracks state, hazards or memory for you. The programmer allocates memory, records command buffers, builds pipelines ahead of time and inserts barriers and fences. In return the CPU overhead per draw is low and recording scales across threads.
Your game stutters for the first minutes of play. What do you check? || Pipeline or shader compilation during frames. Look for pipeline creation on the render path, missing pipeline cache, many shader variants, and driver shader caches that are cold. Fix by precompiling at load, caching pipelines, and creating them asynchronously.
What is a frame in flight and why do you need more than one? || The CPU records the next frame while the GPU draws the current one, with fences protecting shared buffers. One frame in flight serialises CPU and GPU. Too many adds input latency and memory use. Two or three is typical.
How would you tell whether a slow frame is CPU or GPU bound? || Compare CPU time to record and submit against the GPU time from timestamp queries or a profiler. If the GPU is idle waiting for commands, the CPU is the limit (reduce draws, use threads). If the GPU is busy all the frame, reduce shading cost, overdraw, resolution or bandwidth.
Why does WebGPU not expose the exact feature set of Vulkan? || It must run safely on Vulkan, Metal and Direct3D 12, including their differences, and must stop a page from reading other programs' memory or crashing the GPU. So it offers the common subset, validates all input, and adds features only when they work on all back ends.
quiz: What does a command buffer hold and why is recording it cheap? || A list of GPU commands such as bind pipeline, draw and dispatch. Recording only appends data on the CPU and does not touch the GPU, so it can be done on many threads before submission.
Why do modern APIs make pipeline state objects immutable? || The driver can compile and validate the whole state once at creation, so a draw call does not need checks or recompilation. The cost moves to load time.
A scene has 30,000 draws and each costs 1.5 µs to record. How long on one thread and on 6 threads, and is 16.7 ms met? || 30,000 × 1.5 µs = 45 ms on one thread, which misses 16.7 ms. On 6 threads it is about 7.5 ms, which fits.
What does a barrier do? || It orders GPU work: writes from an earlier pass must finish and become visible before a later pass reads them, and it can change the image's memory layout. Without it you get flicker or corruption.
When would you choose WebGL over WebGPU? || When you must support older browsers and devices that lack WebGPU, or when the content is simple. You give up compute shaders and the modern design.

@@ tlaplus
value: TLA+ is a language for writing down how a concurrent or distributed system is supposed to behave, and a set of tools that check the description for mistakes before any code exists. You describe the system as a state machine: what the variables are, what the starting states are, and what steps may change the state. Then you state the properties that must always hold, for example "two nodes are never both leader in the same term". The model checker TLC tries every reachable state, and if some sequence of steps breaks a property, it prints the shortest such sequence.
The problem is that the hardest bugs in distributed systems are not coding errors. They are design errors that appear only when messages are delayed, reordered or lost, and nodes crash at an unlucky moment. Tests rarely reach these interleavings, and a code review cannot enumerate them. Writing the design as a precise model and exploring it by machine finds such errors in hours, at the design stage, when fixing them costs a diagram edit and not a production incident. Amazon engineers described this in a 2014 report and a 2015 article: they found serious bugs in S3, DynamoDB and other services that would have been very hard to find by testing.
TLA+ gives the layer above it designs that are known to be correct under a stated model, and a precise document that engineers can read and argue about. The layers below make it work. Formal verification (formalverif) provides the idea of proving or checking a system against a specification. Model checking and temporal logic (modelcheck) provide the algorithm: breadth-first exploration of all reachable states, and the logic for properties over infinite behaviours (always, eventually). Propositional and first-order logic with set theory (logic) provide the language of the specification itself: sets, functions, quantifiers (for all, there exists) and connectives. TLA+ is the most widely used formal specification tool in industry. It is open source and developed under the TLA+ Foundation, with TLC and the symbolic checker Apalache as the main checkers.
terms: State — an assignment of a value to every variable of the specification at one moment.
Behaviour — an infinite sequence of states; the specification describes the set of allowed behaviours.
Action — a formula over a current state and a next state (written with primed variables such as `x'`) that describes one kind of step.
`Init` / `Next` — `Init` says which states are allowed at the start; `Next` is the disjunction of all actions: any one of them may happen as the next step.
Invariant — a property that must hold in every reachable state; it is a safety property ("nothing bad happens").
Liveness property — a property about the future, such as "every request is eventually answered"; it needs fairness assumptions and the temporal operators always (`[]`) and eventually (`<>`).
Stuttering step — a step in which no variable changes; TLA+ always allows it, which makes it possible to describe one system as an implementation of a simpler one.
TLC — the explicit-state model checker; it enumerates every reachable state of a finite model.
PlusCal — a pseudo-code language for algorithms that is translated into TLA+; each labelled statement is one atomic step.
history: Leslie Lamport, who had worked on distributed systems since the 1970s (logical clocks, Paxos, Bakery algorithm), found that algorithms written as code or prose hid their errors. In 1994 he published the Temporal Logic of Actions (TLA), a logic where the system and its properties are formulas in the same language, and refinement ("the implementation is a correct version of the design") is plain logical implication. TLA+ was the full language: TLA plus ordinary set theory, so that data structures can be described with sets and functions and no new theory is needed.
Tools followed. Yuan Yu, with Panagiotis Manolios and Lamport, wrote TLC in 1999 at a Compaq research lab. Lamport wrote the book "Specifying Systems" (2002), which is still the main reference, and added PlusCal in 2009 for people who think in algorithms. TLAPS, a proof system, was built at the Microsoft Research–Inria Joint Centre for cases where a proof for all sizes of a system is needed, not only a finite check.
Industry adoption began when engineers at Amazon Web Services started to use TLA+ in 2011. Chris Newcombe and colleagues reported the results in 2014 and in the Communications of the ACM in 2015: the specifications found subtle bugs in services that were already in design review, one of them in a fault-tolerant protocol that needed a sequence of 35 steps to appear. Microsoft used it for Azure Cosmos DB's consistency models, MongoDB for its replication protocol, and Confluent, Elastic and others for their replication and consensus designs. These examples made TLA+ the formal method that working engineers actually choose.
In 2023 the TLA+ Foundation was created under the Linux Foundation, with Amazon, Microsoft and Oracle among the founding members, to look after the tools and the community. The Apalache symbolic checker (from Informal Systems, using the SMT solver Z3) was added to the ecosystem. The release notes of the TLA+ tools now say that the original Toolbox IDE is no longer actively maintained, and recommend the TLA+ extension for VS Code or running TLC from the command line.
how: Step 1: model the system as a state machine. You choose the variables that matter, for example for a lock service: the lock holder and the state of each client. You leave out everything else. The art is in the abstraction: a model of a database replication protocol might have no disks, no network packets and no clocks, only a set of messages in flight. `Init` is a logical formula that is true exactly for the starting states. For example `lock = FALSE /\ pc = [p \in Procs |-> "start"]`, where `/\` is "and" and `[p \in Procs |-> ...]` defines a function.
Step 2: define the steps. Each action is a formula that relates the current values of the variables to the next values, which are written with a prime. `lock' = TRUE` says that in the next state the lock is true. `UNCHANGED x` says `x' = x`. An action also has an enabling condition: if its condition is false in a state, the action cannot happen there. `Next` is the "or" of all actions, usually with an existential quantifier such as `\E p \in Procs : Acquire(p)` ("some process p takes this step"). Nondeterminism comes from the "or": the model allows every choice, so you check all schedules, not one. A common style is to write one action per kind of event in the real system (send a message, receive it, time out, crash, restart), so failures are just more actions that the checker is free to choose. The logic layer supplies all of this: `/\`, `\/`, `~`, `\A`, `\E`, and sets and functions from set theory.
Step 3: write the specification and its properties. The full spec is `Init /\ [][Next]_vars`, meaning "start in `Init`, and always do a `Next` step or leave `vars` unchanged" (stuttering). Safety properties are invariants like `MutualExclusion`. Liveness properties use `<>` and fairness conditions such as weak fairness `WF_vars(Action)`: if an action stays enabled, it eventually happens. Without fairness, a model that simply never moves would satisfy "nothing bad happens", so liveness checks need these assumptions, and they should be written with care.
Step 4: write a finite model. TLC cannot check an infinite set of states, so you assign values to constants: three servers, two values, at most four messages. This is a deliberate choice: most protocol bugs show up with 2 to 4 participants, a pattern sometimes called the small scope hypothesis. You also set what to check (the invariants and properties) and optional limits such as state constraints.
Step 5: TLC explores. This is the model checking algorithm. TLC computes all initial states, puts them in a queue, and then repeatedly takes a state, computes all successor states by evaluating `Next`, and adds each new state to the queue. To recognise states it has seen, it stores a 64-bit fingerprint (a hash) of each state in a large set, which needs memory proportional to the number of states, and it can spill to disk. Several worker threads run in parallel, and the state set can be kept on disk when it no longer fits in memory. Because the search is breadth-first, the first violation it finds comes with a trace of minimum length, which is the most readable one. For liveness it builds the state graph and looks for cycles where the property fails.
Step 6: read the counterexample. TLC prints each state in the path from `Init` to the bad state, with every variable value. You see the exact interleaving that breaks the invariant, you fix the design (or the spec, if the spec was wrong), and run again. When the model passes, you have not proved the design for all sizes, but you have checked it exhaustively for the model. For all sizes you can use TLAPS to write a proof, or Apalache to check bounded runs of a larger model symbolically with an SMT solver.
Step 7: connect to code. This step is optional but valuable. A spec is not code. Teams link the two in several ways: by using the spec to generate test traces for the real implementation, by checking logs of the real system against the spec, or by keeping the spec as the design document that reviewers read. MongoDB's work on "model-based trace checking" of its replication protocol is an example.

example: Here is a lock that two processes use, with a mistake that is easy to make. Each process first tests the lock, then sets it in a second step.
```text
---- MODULE Lock ----
VARIABLES lock, pc
Procs == {1, 2}
Init == lock = FALSE /\ pc = [p \in Procs |-> "start"]
Test(p) == /\ pc[p] = "start" /\ lock = FALSE
           /\ pc' = [pc EXCEPT ![p] = "set"] /\ UNCHANGED lock
Set(p)  == /\ pc[p] = "set" /\ lock' = TRUE
           /\ pc' = [pc EXCEPT ![p] = "cs"]
Exit(p) == /\ pc[p] = "cs" /\ lock' = FALSE
           /\ pc' = [pc EXCEPT ![p] = "start"]
Next == \E p \in Procs : Test(p) \/ Set(p) \/ Exit(p)
Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_<<lock, pc>>
MutualExclusion == ~(pc[1] = "cs" /\ pc[2] = "cs")
====
```
The state space is small: `lock` has 2 values and each process has 3 positions, so at most 2 × 3 × 3 = 18 states, and 13 of them are reachable. TLC finds all 13 almost at once and reports that `MutualExclusion` is violated. The shortest trace has 5 states. State 1: lock is false, both processes at "start". State 2: process 1 tests the lock and moves to "set" (the lock is still false). State 3: process 2 also tests the lock, sees false, and moves to "set". State 4: process 1 sets the lock and enters "cs". State 5: process 2 sets the lock (it was already true) and also enters "cs". Both are in the critical section. The bug is that the test and the set are separate steps.
The fix is one atomic action: `Acquire(p) == pc[p] = "start" /\ lock = FALSE /\ lock' = TRUE /\ pc' = [pc EXCEPT ![p] = "cs"]`. After this change only 3 states are reachable (both idle; process 1 in the section; process 2 in the section), and TLC reports no error. In PlusCal the same mistake would be two statements with two labels, and the fix one label for the pair, because a label is an atomic step.
Real systems are bigger. A model of a consensus protocol with 3 servers, 2 values and a few rounds can have millions or billions of reachable states. TLC may then need hours and tens of gigabytes. Engineers shrink the model with symmetry sets (servers are interchangeable, so states that differ only by renaming a server are treated as one) and with state constraints that cut off the unlikely deep corners, and they split the spec in parts.
intuition: Writing a TLA+ spec is like building a board game for your system and letting a tireless player try every legal move to find a way to cheat.
The idea is natural because distributed bugs are about order, and order is a thing a machine can enumerate while a person cannot. If you describe the system as a set of allowed steps, "every possible schedule" becomes "every path in a graph", and graph search is easy. The surprise is how little you need to write. A page of logic captures the heart of a protocol, because you leave out the code, and the checker supplies the patience. The value is greatest when the thinking is hardest: it forces you to say exactly what the protocol promises, which often reveals that the team had different promises in mind.
mistakes: People often think TLA+ is a programming language that produces a program. In fact a spec is a mathematical description and does not run as a service. It is a design tool. Connecting it to code takes extra work such as trace checking and test generation.
People often think a passing TLC run proves the algorithm correct. In fact it proves that no violation exists in the finite model you chose, for the properties you wrote. A wrong abstraction, a missing property or a model that is too small can all hide a bug. To prove the property for all sizes you need a proof system such as TLAPS.
People often think TLA+ is only for experts in logic. In fact most engineers need only a small part: sets, functions, `/\`, `\/`, `\E`, primes and `UNCHANGED`. Lamport's video course is written for programmers, and PlusCal gives a familiar syntax for the first specs.
friction: State explosion is the main limit. The number of states grows exponentially with the number of processes, values and message slots. A model that is too big will not finish, and a model that is small enough may miss bugs that need larger numbers. Choosing the model size, symmetry and constraints is a skill that comes from practice. Apalache and distributed TLC runs help, but they do not remove the problem.
The second limit is the gap to the code. The spec and the implementation can drift apart. Unless the team checks traces or generates tests from the model, the spec becomes an out-of-date document. Few teams do this well, and tool support is still young.
The third is learning cost. The notation looks like mathematics. Engineers must learn to think in states and actions, to write fairness correctly and to read counterexamples. Common errors include writing a spec that allows no behaviour at all (so everything is vacuously true), or forgetting `UNCHANGED` for a variable and getting wild behaviours. Typing is also loose: TLA+ is untyped, so you write a `TypeOK` invariant, and mistakes show up at run time.
tradeoffs: Choose TLA+ when the design involves concurrency, failure and several participants, and a bug would be costly: replication, consensus, transaction protocols, cache coherence, lock managers, workflows. Choose unit and integration tests, property-based tests or fault injection when the risk is in coding details. A spec gives you exhaustive coverage of the design in a small model; tests give broad coverage of the real code. Most mature teams use both.
Choose TLC (explicit state) when your model is finite, small enough to enumerate, and you want clear shortest traces and liveness checking. Choose Apalache (symbolic) when your model has large integers or large parameters and you want bounded checks, or inductive invariants. @@ aurora
value: Amazon Aurora is a relational database service from AWS that speaks the MySQL or PostgreSQL protocol but stores its data in a different way. A normal MySQL server writes pages, a redo log, a binary log and a double-write buffer to a local or network disk, and a replica repeats all of that work. Aurora keeps the SQL engine (parsing, planning, transactions, the buffer cache) in the database instance, and sends only the redo log records to a separate, shared storage service. That service keeps six copies of every piece of data in three Availability Zones, builds the data pages from the log by itself, and accepts a write when four of the six copies confirm it.
The problem it solves is the network. In a cloud database, storage is across the network, and a classic engine produces many synchronous writes per transaction, each mirrored to a second copy. The 2017 Aurora paper showed that the bottleneck is no longer the disk or the CPU; it is the number and size of the network round trips on the write path. By shipping only the log (hundreds of bytes per change instead of 16 KB pages), and letting the storage layer do the page work, Aurora cut the writes per transaction a great deal. AWS's documentation claims up to 6x the throughput of stock MySQL and of stock PostgreSQL on similar hardware; treat this as a vendor claim that depends on the workload.
The layer above gets a drop-in database: applications, ORMs and tools written for MySQL or PostgreSQL work unchanged, and they get more availability, up to 15 read replicas that share one storage volume, storage that grows by itself to 256 TiB, fast crash recovery and backup without pausing. The layers below do the real work. The write-ahead log (wal) is the unit that crosses the network. Quorums (quorum) make a write safe with four of six acknowledgements. Replication (replication) puts the copies in three zones. Separating storage from compute (sepstorage) lets the instance be replaced, resized or restarted without moving data. OLTP transaction semantics (oltp) are preserved by the engine, and the MySQL (InnoDB) and PostgreSQL engines are modified, not rewritten.
terms: Redo log record — a small record that describes a change to a page ("at byte offset X of page P, write these bytes"); replaying records rebuilds the page.
LSN — log sequence number; a number that increases with every log record and orders all changes in the database.
Quorum — the minimum number of copies that must take part in an operation; Aurora writes need 4 of 6 and reads need 3 of 6.
Protection group — a 10 GB segment of the volume with its six copies on six storage nodes.
Availability Zone (AZ) — an isolated data-centre group in a region; Aurora puts two copies in each of three zones.
Cluster volume — the shared, auto-growing storage of an Aurora cluster, visible to the writer and all readers.
Writer / reader instance — the single instance that accepts writes, and the read-only instances (up to 15) that read the same volume.
Volume durable LSN (VDL) — the highest LSN up to which the database knows that all changes are durable on enough copies.
Aurora Serverless v2 — a mode in which compute capacity, measured in Aurora capacity units, scales up and down with load.
history: Aurora was announced in 2014 and became generally available in 2015 as a MySQL-compatible engine; the PostgreSQL-compatible version followed in 2017. The authors, a team at AWS (Alexandre Verbitski, Anurag Gupta and colleagues) published the design at SIGMOD 2017, and a second paper in 2018 explained how Aurora avoids distributed consensus for I/O, commits and membership changes. The reasoning in the paper is clear: in the cloud, compute and storage fail independently, and the network between them is the shared bottleneck, so the log should be the only thing that crosses it.
Before Aurora, the standard way to make MySQL highly available on AWS was RDS Multi-AZ, which used synchronous block-level mirroring between two volumes in two zones. Every write went through a chain of mirrored steps, and the cost was high latency and low throughput. Aurora replaced this chain with the quorum log service.
After the first design, AWS added features around the same storage layer: Global Database (2018) for cross-region replication done by the storage layer, Serverless (2018, with a second version later) for elastic compute, the I/O-Optimized storage option (2023) with no per-I/O charge, and Limitless Database for horizontal scaling of PostgreSQL-compatible writes. In May 2025 AWS made Aurora DSQL generally available. DSQL is a different system, though it shares the brand: a serverless, distributed SQL database with active-active writes in several regions, designed for 99.99% availability in a single region and 99.999% across regions. In October 2026 the main Aurora product is still the MySQL- and PostgreSQL-compatible one described here, and DSQL is the new line for multi-region SQL.
how: Step 1: split the database. The engine (a modified MySQL InnoDB or PostgreSQL) runs on the writer instance and keeps its buffer pool, lock manager and transaction manager. When a transaction changes a page in memory, the engine creates redo log records, but it does not write the page to storage, ever. The engine sends only log records. There is no double-write buffer, no checkpointing of dirty pages from the engine, and no binary log needed for replication.
Step 2: ship the log to six copies. The volume is divided into 10 GB segments. Each segment is a protection group of six storage nodes: two in each of three Availability Zones. A log record goes to all six nodes of the segment that owns the page. Each node writes the record to its on-disk log, acknowledges, and applies it to the page in the background. The writer considers the record durable when four of six nodes acknowledge. The write quorum is 4 and the read quorum is 3, so 4 + 3 > 6 (every read quorum overlaps every write quorum) and 4 + 4 > 6 (two conflicting writes cannot both succeed). The system survives the loss of a whole zone without losing the ability to write, and the loss of a zone plus one more node without losing data (reads still have 3 copies).
Step 3: commit without distributed consensus. In the usual design, a distributed commit needs a protocol like two-phase commit or Paxos. Aurora does not need that for writes, because only one writer exists and it assigns the LSNs. The writer tracks, for each segment, the highest LSN that the segment has acknowledged without gaps. It marks certain records as consistency points (the end of a mini-transaction, such as a B-tree page split), and computes the volume durable LSN: the highest consistency point such that everything below it is durable. A committing transaction waits (asynchronously, in a group-commit style, so that threads are not blocked) until the VDL passes its commit LSN. This is the quorum idea (quorum) applied to a single, ordered log (wal).
Step 4: reads. When the engine needs a page not in its buffer pool, it asks one storage node, not a quorum. This works because the writer knows which nodes are complete up to the needed LSN (it tracks their acknowledgements), so it can pick one that has everything and is closest. The storage node returns the page as of the requested LSN by applying the log records to its last stored version. Read quorums are needed only in recovery.
Step 5: recovery. After a crash, a new instance reads from the storage nodes to find the highest durable LSN with a read quorum, and tells the storage layer to throw away the records above it. Because storage nodes already apply the log all the time, the instance does not replay a long redo log. The paper reports recovery in about ten seconds or less, instead of the minutes of a standard InnoDB redo replay.
Step 6: repair and growth. If a node or disk fails, the missing 10 GB segment is re-copied from the other five copies to a new node. At 10 Gbit/s a 10 GB copy takes about 10 seconds, and because many nodes hold different segments, repairs run in parallel and in small units. Small segments shorten the time in which a second failure could hurt. Storage grows in 10 GB steps to 256 TiB, and the storage fleet is shared by many customers.
Step 7: replicas. A reader instance uses the same cluster volume, so it needs no copy of the data and no replica storage. The writer sends it the log stream and the reader applies the records only to pages in its own cache; others are read from storage on demand. Replica lag is typically tens of milliseconds, and adding a reader takes minutes. If the writer fails, one reader is promoted (failover typically takes tens of seconds), and it already sees the same volume.

example: Take one small transaction that changes one 16 KB page. In a classic mirrored MySQL setup the engine writes the redo log, the binary log, the changed page, and a second copy of the page for the double-write buffer. Each of these goes to the primary volume and is copied to a standby in another zone, in sequence. A rough count of data is 16 KB × 2 for the page and its double-write copy, and then all of this twice for the mirror, so over 64 KB cross the network, plus the log and the metadata, in several ordered steps. In Aurora the engine creates a redo record of perhaps 200 bytes. It sends it in parallel to six nodes: 6 × 200 B = 1.2 KB. That is about 50 times fewer bytes, and there are no ordered steps because the six writes are parallel. The Aurora paper reported that a write-heavy benchmark ran about 35 times more transactions in the same time than mirrored MySQL, with roughly eight times fewer I/Os per transaction; the exact figures depend on the workload.
Now the latency of a commit. The six acknowledgements arrive after 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3 and 9.0 ms (the last node has a slow disk). The writer waits for the fourth fastest, which arrives at 1.2 ms. If it had to wait for all six, the commit would take 9.0 ms. Because only four out of six are needed, a slow or failing node does not set the latency. This is how a quorum cuts tail latency.
Now failures. Normal state: 6 healthy copies. A whole zone fails: 4 copies remain, writes need 4, so writes continue with no pause (but no spare). One more node in the other zones fails: 3 copies remain; writes stop, since 3 is below 4, but reads work with a read quorum of 3, no data is lost, and the missing copies are rebuilt from the surviving ones within seconds. Only when 4 of 6 copies are lost at once (for example two zones) is data at risk.
intuition: A newspaper does not mail its readers the printing plates; it sends the story text and lets each print shop typeset it locally.
The design is natural when you see where the cost is. A page is big and the change is small. The database engine would repeat the same page-level work (double writes, flushes, checkpoints) that exists only because a single disk can fail halfway through a write. Once the log is replicated to a durable quorum, those protections are not needed: the log is the truth, and any page can be rebuilt from it at any time. The storage service is then free to do the work in the background, in parallel, on many machines. Quorums turn many unreliable disks into one reliable device, and a single writer gives the log a single order without a heavy consensus protocol.
mistakes: People often think Aurora is MySQL or PostgreSQL running on faster disks. In fact the storage layer and the write path are different: the engine is modified so that it ships redo records and never writes pages. It keeps the SQL behaviour, but internals, such as the binary log use for replicas and the checkpoint behaviour, differ.
People often think six copies means six times the storage cost. In fact the six copies are an internal detail of the storage service, and you pay for the logical size of your volume (plus I/O in the standard billing mode). The price is still higher than a plain RDS volume per GB.
People often think Aurora is multi-writer or a distributed SQL database that scales writes. In fact an Aurora cluster has one writer instance; the replicas only read. Writes scale up by using a larger instance. Limitless Database and DSQL are separate products that scale writes out.
People often think Aurora readers are always up to date. In fact reader instances apply the log asynchronously, so they can be behind by milliseconds to more. A read right after a write on another instance may not see the write. Use the writer endpoint for read-your-writes.
friction: There is one writer. Write throughput is limited by the largest instance, and a long failover or maintenance window affects all writes. Aurora Global Database adds cross-region replicas, but only one region accepts writes, with a typical replication lag of about a second. Writing in several regions needs DSQL or an application-level design.
The system is proprietary and tied to AWS. You cannot run it on your own hardware, and its internals can be seen only through papers, metrics and documentation. Features differ between the MySQL- and PostgreSQL-compatible versions, and some extensions or settings that work in community PostgreSQL are not allowed. Version support also lags the community: new major versions appear in Aurora some months after the community release.
Cost is not simple. You pay for instances, storage, and (in standard mode) per I/O request; a scan-heavy or write-heavy workload can produce a large I/O bill, which is why the I/O-Optimized option exists, with higher instance and storage rates and no I/O charge. A read replica is a full instance cost.
Performance has limits. A cold read from storage costs a network round trip (around a millisecond or less within a zone), slower than a local NVMe disk. Cross-zone traffic adds latency to every commit, because at least two zones take part in a 4-of-6 write. Large sorts and temporary tables use local instance storage, not the shared volume.
tradeoffs: Choose Aurora over standard RDS (MySQL or PostgreSQL on EBS) when you need fast failover, many read replicas, large storage that grows by itself, or heavy write loads where the redo-only path shows its gain. Choose standard RDS when the database is small and steady, the cost per GB matters, and the extra features are not needed. You pay more per unit with Aurora and gain availability and read scale.
Choose Aurora Serverless v2 when load is spiky or unknown and you want capacity to follow demand; choose provisioned instances (possibly with reserved pricing) when load is steady and you can plan, because it costs less for steady use. With serverless you give up some predictability of cost and cache warmth.
Choose Aurora when one writer is enough (most OLTP systems are). Choose Aurora DSQL or a distributed SQL system such as CockroachDB or Spanner when you need writes in several regions with strong consistency, and you accept transaction retries on conflict and a subset of PostgreSQL features. Choose a sharded design (Limitless, Citus, Vitess) when a single writer cannot handle the write rate and you can partition your data.
practice: Size the writer for peak write load and memory for the working set, because a larger buffer pool avoids storage reads. Use reader instances for read-heavy traffic through the reader endpoint, and keep application sessions that need read-your-writes on the writer. Put readers in different zones from the writer so that a zone loss still leaves capacity.
Watch the metrics that Aurora exposes: `AuroraReplicaLag`, `VolumeReadIOPs` and `VolumeWriteIOPs`, buffer cache hit ratio, and commit latency. Use Performance Insights to find expensive queries. Reduce I/O by indexing well, avoiding wide scans and keeping transactions short. Check the monthly bill split between instances, storage and I/O before choosing the storage mode.
Plan for failover. Use the cluster endpoint, set client timeouts and retries, and keep DNS caching short, so applications reconnect within seconds after a promotion. Test failover on a schedule. Use cloning for test environments: a clone shares pages with the source and copies them only when they change, so it appears in minutes whatever the size. Test major version upgrades on a clone first.

value: Amazon SQS (Simple Queue Service) is a managed message queue. A producer sends a message to a queue; a consumer asks for messages, processes them, and then deletes them. Between those steps SQS keeps the message safely stored on several servers, so a consumer crash does not lose work. Amazon SNS (Simple Notification Service) is the companion publish-subscribe service: a publisher sends one message to a topic, and SNS copies it to every subscriber, which can be an SQS queue, a Lambda function, an HTTP endpoint, an email address, a phone or a mobile app.
The problem they solve is coupling in time and in load. If service A calls service B directly, A fails when B is down, and B is overwhelmed when A sends a burst. A queue between them absorbs the burst, lets B work at its own speed, and lets either side restart without the other noticing. SQS adds a second feature that matters just as much: because the consumer must delete a message to finish it, a consumer that dies leaves the message in the queue to be tried again. This gives at-least-once delivery without any code on your side to track work.
SQS was the first AWS service (beta in 2004, general availability in 2006) and is still one of its most used building blocks. The layers above it include AWS Lambda (functions are often triggered by SQS queues), batch pipelines, order processing, email sending, and ad-tech work such as buffering events before a slow downstream system. The layers below provide the ideas. The message queue (mqueue) is the data structure and contract. Publish-subscribe (pubsub) is what SNS adds. Delivery guarantees (deliverysem) define what "at least once" means and why duplicates happen. Retries, backoff and jitter (retries) are built into the visibility timeout and the dead-letter queue. The cloud (cloud) supplies the replicated storage and the scaling that you do not have to run.
terms: Visibility timeout — the time during which a received message is hidden from other consumers; if the consumer has not deleted it by then, the message becomes visible again.
Receipt handle — a token returned with each receive; the consumer needs it to delete the message or to change its visibility timeout.
Dead-letter queue (DLQ) — a second queue where SQS moves a message after it has been received `maxReceiveCount` times without being deleted.
Long polling — a receive request that waits up to 20 seconds for a message to arrive, instead of returning at once with an empty answer.
Standard queue — the default queue type: nearly unlimited throughput, at-least-once delivery, best-effort ordering.
FIFO queue — a queue with strict order inside each message group and deduplication of messages with the same ID within 5 minutes.
Message group ID — a label (such as a customer ID) that defines which messages must stay in order in a FIFO queue.
Topic — an SNS channel; publishers send to it and subscribers receive from it.
Fan-out — publishing one message to a topic so that several queues or functions each get their own copy.
history: Amazon launched SQS in beta in November 2004 and in production in 2006. It was the first AWS service, and it grew out of the need to connect services reliably at Amazon's scale. SNS followed in 2010. For years SQS had only what is now called the standard queue: very high throughput, but with duplicates and no strict ordering. The trade was deliberate, because storing a message on many servers and letting any server answer a receive request is easier to scale than holding a global order.
Customers kept asking for order and no duplicates, so in 2016 AWS added FIFO queues, with message groups for ordering and a five-minute deduplication window. FIFO queues started at 300 transactions per second per API action and were raised through batching and then a high-throughput mode (2021), which now allows tens of thousands of messages per second per queue in large regions. In the same period AWS added server-side encryption, dead-letter queue redrive (move messages back to the source queue from the console or API), and larger limits.
Two recent changes matter. The maximum message size grew from 256 KiB to 1 MiB, so many payloads that needed the S3 extended client library now fit directly. And "fair queues" were added to standard queues: if you set a message group ID on a standard queue, SQS tries to treat each group fairly when delivering, so one tenant that floods the queue does not delay the others. SNS gained filter policies (subscribers receive only matching messages), FIFO topics (2020), and delivery to more targets. Alongside both services, Amazon EventBridge (events from AWS and partner sources with rules) and Kinesis and MSK (streams with replay) cover cases where a queue is too simple.
how: Step 1: store. When a producer calls `SendMessage`, SQS writes the message to multiple servers in multiple Availability Zones before it returns success. The message stays there for the retention period, which is 4 days by default and can be set from 60 seconds to 14 days. A message can be up to 1 MiB, in batches of up to 10 per request, and can have up to 10 attributes. A delay of up to 15 minutes can be set so that a message is hidden at first.
Step 2: receive. A consumer calls `ReceiveMessage` and asks for up to 10 messages. SQS picks messages that are visible, returns them with a receipt handle, and starts the visibility timer (30 seconds by default, up to 12 hours). The message is not removed. It is hidden. For a standard queue, the data is spread on many servers, so a short poll (`WaitTimeSeconds` of 0) asks only a sample of them and may return nothing even though the queue holds messages. Long polling (up to 20 seconds) queries all servers and waits, which gives fewer empty responses and fewer requests.
Step 3: process and delete. The consumer does the work, and then calls `DeleteMessage` with the receipt handle. Only then is the message gone. If the work is not finished before the visibility timeout, the message becomes visible again and another consumer (or the same one) can receive it, and its `ApproximateReceiveCount` increases. If the work is long, the consumer can extend the timeout with `ChangeMessageVisibility`, a heartbeat. This is the whole delivery guarantee: the message is delivered at least once, and a duplicate is possible whenever a consumer is slow, crashes after the work but before the delete, or when the system sends a message twice on its side. That is why consumers must be idempotent, which is the heart of the delivery-guarantee idea.
Step 4: retry and the dead-letter queue. Redelivery after a failure is the retry mechanism: the visibility timeout is the delay between attempts. For a message that always fails (a poison message), a redrive policy on the source queue sets `maxReceiveCount`, for example 5. On the sixth receive, SQS moves the message to the dead-letter queue instead of delivering it. Operators watch the DLQ, fix the bug, and redrive the messages back. Exponential backoff is not automatic in SQS: a consumer can call `ChangeMessageVisibility` with a longer timeout after a failure to build backoff and jitter by itself.
Step 5: FIFO. A FIFO queue keeps messages in order inside each message group. While a message of a group is in flight (received, not deleted), SQS gives no later message of the same group to any consumer, so order is kept; other groups can proceed in parallel. This means parallelism equals the number of active groups. A deduplication ID (or a hash of the body) makes SQS ignore a second send with the same ID in the next 5 minutes, which gives exactly-once processing as long as the consumer deletes within the visibility timeout. Throughput is limited to 300 API calls per second per action in normal mode (3,000 messages per second with batches of 10) and far higher in high-throughput mode.
Step 6: SNS and fan-out. A publisher calls `Publish` on a topic. SNS stores the message and delivers a copy to each subscription, with retries for each protocol. A subscription can have a filter policy so that it receives only some messages, for example `{"event": ["order_created"]}`. The usual pattern is SNS to several SQS queues: each consumer service has its own queue, so it can process at its own speed, fail and retry without affecting the others, and keep messages while it is down. SNS is the splitter and SQS is the buffer. A FIFO topic can feed FIFO queues to keep order.
Step 7: Lambda and scaling. A Lambda event source mapping polls the queue for you with long polling, invokes your function with a batch, and deletes the messages if the function succeeds. If it fails, the whole batch becomes visible again, unless the function returns the IDs of the failed items (partial batch response), which avoids reprocessing the successful ones. AWS advises setting the queue's visibility timeout to at least six times the function timeout, so that a message does not return while a slow invocation still works on it.

example: A queue holds image-processing jobs. The average job takes 20 seconds, but 5% of jobs take longer than 30 seconds because they are large. The visibility timeout is the default 30 seconds. About 5% of jobs therefore return to the queue while a first consumer is still working. If 1,000,000 messages arrive per day, about 50,000 are delivered a second time while the first run continues, and the second run may also be slow, so the waste can be larger. If the results are written twice, you may get duplicate output or double billing.
There are two fixes. The first is to set the timeout longer than the slowest job, for example 120 seconds. This is simple, but a crashed consumer now delays the retry by 2 minutes. The second is a heartbeat: set the timeout to 30 seconds and extend it by 30 seconds every 20 seconds while the job runs. Then a crash delays the retry by at most 30 seconds, and a long job is never duplicated. Both need an idempotent consumer in any case, for example by writing the result with the message ID as the key, so a repeated delivery overwrites the same result.
```python
import boto3
sqs = boto3.client("sqs")
URL = "https://sqs.us-east-1.amazonaws.com/123456789012/jobs"

while True:

        QueueUrl=URL, MaxNumberOfMessages=10,
        WaitTimeSeconds=20, VisibilityTimeout=60)

            pass
        else:

        sqs.delete_message(QueueUrl=URL,
                           ReceiptHandle=m["ReceiptHandle"])
```
Now the poison message. The redrive policy has `maxReceiveCount = 3` and the timeout is 30 seconds. A bad message is received, fails, hidden for 30 seconds, received again, and so on. After 3 failed receives (about 90 seconds in total) it moves to the DLQ. The main queue is no longer blocked. Without a DLQ the message would cycle until the retention period ended (4 days by default). Set the DLQ retention at least as long as the source queue's. Long polling also cuts cost: one consumer on an empty queue sends 86,400 short-poll requests per day, against 4,320 with 20-second long polling, which is 20 times fewer.
intuition: A restaurant order rail: the ticket stays on the rail until the cook marks it done, and if the cook walks away, the ticket is picked up again by someone else.
The design is natural because failure is the normal case in a distributed system. A message that disappears when it is received would be lost if the consumer crashed. A message that is only hidden, and removed after a confirmed delete, can never be lost by a consumer failure, at the cost of possible duplicates. The visibility timeout is a lease: you own the message for a limited time, and silence returns it. That one rule gives you retries, crash recovery and load balancing between consumers without a coordinator. SNS adds the other half: the publisher does not know its readers, so adding a new consumer means adding a subscription, not changing the producer.
mistakes: People often think SQS delivers each message exactly once. In fact a standard queue is at-least-once, and duplicates are normal during timeouts and retries. Only FIFO queues dedupe, and only within a 5-minute window; your consumer should still be idempotent.
People often think messages in a standard queue come out in the order they went in. In fact the order is best effort. Messages are spread over many servers, so order can change. If order matters, use FIFO with a message group ID, or carry a sequence number in the message.
People often think a received message is removed from the queue. In fact it is only hidden until the visibility timeout ends, and it stays until you delete it. Forgetting `DeleteMessage` makes every message come back and eventually go to the DLQ.
People often think SNS keeps messages for slow subscribers. In fact SNS retries delivery for a limited time, per protocol, and then drops the message unless a subscription DLQ is set. To buffer for a slow consumer, put an SQS queue between SNS and the consumer.
friction: The first limit is ordering and duplicates. If you need strict order and no duplicates at high throughput, standard queues cannot give it and FIFO has throughput limits per message group and per queue. FIFO also needs the group design: one hot group (one big customer) becomes a bottleneck because only one message per group is processed at a time.
The second is that SQS has no replay or consumer groups. Once a message is deleted it is gone, and you cannot read the same message with a new consumer later. If you need replay, history or many independent readers of one stream, a log such as Kafka, Kinesis or MSK fits better.
The third is visibility timeout tuning. Too short gives duplicates; too long delays recovery. Job durations change over time, so timeouts that were right once become wrong. A heartbeat helps but adds code and calls.
The fourth is hidden failure. A queue that slowly grows or a DLQ that fills up does not raise an error by itself. You must alarm on `ApproximateAgeOfOldestMessage`, queue depth and DLQ depth. A related effect: large backlogs can mask a slow consumer for hours. Also, `ApproximateNumberOfMessages` is approximate.
tradeoffs: Choose SQS standard when throughput and cost matter, work items are independent, and your consumer is idempotent. Choose FIFO when order within a key or exactly-once processing is a real requirement (payments for one account, state changes of one object). You give up throughput headroom and take on the group design. Many systems use standard queues with idempotent workers and keep FIFO for the few flows that need it.
Choose SNS to SQS fan-out when several services need the same event and each should process on its own schedule and keep messages while down. Choose SNS direct to Lambda or HTTP for simple, low-volume notifications where buffering is not needed. You give up retry control and backlog visibility in the direct case.
Choose SQS over Kafka, Kinesis or MSK when you need a simple work queue with per-message acknowledgement, no capacity planning and no operations, and each message is consumed once. Choose a log when you need replay, many independent consumers on the same data, strict per-partition order at very high volume, or stream processing. Queue means "work to do"; log means "history of facts".
Choose a longer visibility timeout plus a heartbeat when jobs vary a lot in duration; choose a short fixed timeout when jobs are short and uniform, because recovery is then fast. Choose a small `maxReceiveCount` (3 to 5) for work that fails for permanent reasons, and a larger one when failures are mostly transient and you rely on the queue as the retry loop.
practice: Make every consumer idempotent. Use the message ID or a business key to detect work already done, and make writes upserts. Delete a message only after the work is durable. Handle partial failures inside a batch: delete the messages that succeeded, and leave only the failed ones, or return the failed item IDs from Lambda.
Always attach a DLQ with a sensible `maxReceiveCount`, and alarm when the DLQ is not empty. Set the DLQ retention to the maximum (14 days) so that you have time to react. Add alarms on the age of the oldest message, which tells you directly how late you are, and on queue depth. Use these to scale consumers: scale on backlog per consumer, not on CPU.
Use long polling (`WaitTimeSeconds=20`) and batches of 10. Set the visibility timeout from measured job durations (for example the 99th percentile times a safety factor), and extend it with a heartbeat for long jobs. With Lambda, set the queue timeout to at least six times the function timeout and enable partial batch responses.

@@ webrtc
value: WebRTC (Web Real-Time Communication) lets two programs, usually two browsers, exchange live audio, video and arbitrary data directly, with a delay of a few hundred milliseconds and no plugin. A web page asks for the camera and microphone, creates a connection object, and the browser does the rest: it finds a network path between the two sides even when both are behind home routers, encrypts the media, adapts the bitrate to the network, and plays the stream with echo cancellation and a jitter buffer. It is the technology behind browser-based video calls (Google Meet and many others), cloud gaming and remote desktop tools, live-auction and telehealth products, and peer-to-peer file transfer.
The problem is that real-time media is hard. Live conversation needs end-to-end delay below roughly 200 to 400 ms, but the Internet is a network of private addresses behind NATs and firewalls, where most devices cannot accept incoming connections, and where capacity changes from second to second. TCP, with retransmission and in-order delivery, is wrong for live video: a lost packet makes everything behind it wait. WebRTC therefore uses UDP, tolerates loss, and adds the pieces on top: NAT traversal, encryption, congestion control and media handling. Compare this with HTTP streaming (HLS or DASH), which gets 2 to 30 seconds of delay in return for scale on ordinary CDNs.
What WebRTC gives the layer above is a built-in, standardised real-time stack. A developer writes about fifty lines of JavaScript, and the browser supplies hundreds of thousands of lines of tested media code. The layers below make this possible. UDP and QUIC (quic) give the unreliable datagram transport. NAT and firewalls (nat) are the obstacle that ICE, STUN and TURN overcome. TLS (tls) provides the model for DTLS, the encryption handshake that runs on UDP. Congestion control (congestion) is what stops one video stream from filling the link and queueing everything. Video codecs such as H.264, VP8, VP9 and AV1 (codecs) compress the pictures, and the browser (browser) hosts the whole stack, so that it is available on billions of devices.
terms: Peer connection — the browser object (`RTCPeerConnection`) that holds one connection between two endpoints, with its media tracks and data channels.
Signaling — the exchange of setup messages (who to call, which codecs, which addresses) that WebRTC does not define; apps use WebSockets or HTTP for it.
SDP — Session Description Protocol; a text format that lists the media, codecs, encryption fingerprints and network candidates of one side.
ICE — Interactive Connectivity Establishment; the procedure that gathers possible addresses (candidates), tests pairs and picks a working path.
STUN — a simple server that tells a client its public address and port as seen from the Internet.
TURN — a relay server that forwards the media when no direct path works.
DTLS-SRTP — DTLS performs the key exchange on the UDP path, and the keys then protect the RTP media packets (SRTP).
SFU — Selective Forwarding Unit; a server that receives each participant's stream once and forwards it to the others without decoding it.
Data channel — a message channel (`RTCDataChannel`) over SCTP on DTLS, reliable or unreliable, ordered or not.
history: Real-time voice over IP existed long before browsers, but it needed plugins (Flash, Java) or separate applications such as Skype. In 2010 Google bought Global IP Solutions (GIPS), a company with strong audio and video engines for echo cancellation, jitter buffering and codecs, and in May 2011 it released the code as the open-source WebRTC project. The goal was to make real-time communication a normal capability of the web. Within a year the IETF (for the protocols) and the W3C (for the JavaScript APIs) started working groups.
The standards took long, because the group had to settle hard disputes: which codecs every browser must support (the result was Opus for audio, and both VP8 and H.264 for video), how signaling should work (the answer was to leave it to the application), and how to use SDP (the offer/answer model was kept, with JavaScript Session Establishment Protocol, JSEP). Chrome and Firefox interoperated in 2013. Safari added support in 2017, and Edge in its Chromium version.
The W3C published WebRTC 1.0 as a Recommendation in January 2021, with the IETF RFCs in the same period (for example RFC 8825 for the overview and RFC 8831 for data channels). The W3C document was updated again as a Recommendation on 13 March 2025. WebRTC has kept growing: AV1 and simulcast and scalable video coding, insertable streams for custom processing and end-to-end encryption, and in 2025 the IETF published RFC 9725, WHIP (WebRTC-HTTP Ingestion Protocol), which gives a standard way to send a live stream into a media server with one HTTP request. A matching design for playback, WHEP, is being standardised in the IETF. These make WebRTC a streaming tool as well as a calling tool.
how: Step 1: capture. The page calls `navigator.mediaDevices.getUserMedia()`. The browser asks the user for permission, opens the camera and microphone, and returns tracks. Before anything leaves the machine, the browser can run acoustic echo cancellation, noise suppression and automatic gain control on the audio, which is one reason a browser call sounds better than a naive recording.
Step 2: signaling. The two sides must exchange a description of what they can do. Side A creates an `RTCPeerConnection`, adds its tracks, and calls `createOffer()`. The result is an SDP text with codecs, header extensions, an ICE username and password, and a fingerprint of its DTLS certificate. A sends it to B through the application's own channel, usually a WebSocket to a server. B sets it as the remote description, creates an answer, and sends it back. WebRTC defines no signaling protocol, because applications differ: a call centre, a game and a doctor visit all have different ways of finding and ringing the other side.
Step 3: find a path with ICE. Each side gathers candidates, possible address and port pairs. A host candidate is a local interface address. A server-reflexive candidate is the public address and port that a NAT assigned, learned by sending a STUN request to a public server and reading the reply, which says "I saw your packet from 203.0.113.5:54321". A relay candidate is an address on a TURN server that will forward packets for you. Candidates are sent to the other side (trickle ICE sends them as they are found, which saves time). Each side then forms pairs of its candidates with the remote candidates and sends STUN connectivity checks on them in order of priority. When a check succeeds in both directions, the pair works. The nat concept explains why this succeeds: when A sends a packet to B's public address, A's NAT creates a mapping that lets B's reply back in, and if B does the same at the same time, both NATs open (hole punching). Direct paths work for most sessions; a minority, often quoted as 10 to 20%, where a NAT is strict or a firewall blocks UDP, must use the TURN relay, which carries all the media.
Step 4: encrypt with DTLS-SRTP. On the chosen path, the peers run a DTLS handshake (the TLS idea, adapted for datagrams that can be lost or reordered). Each side presents a certificate whose fingerprint was in the SDP, so a man in the middle must also control the signaling channel to impersonate. The handshake gives keys, and from them the peers derive keys for SRTP, which encrypts and authenticates the RTP media packets. Encryption is mandatory in WebRTC; there is no unencrypted mode. Note the limit: a TURN server or an SFU can see packet headers, and an SFU terminates the encryption unless insertable streams add end-to-end encryption on top.
Step 5: send media with RTP and RTCP. The encoder (VP8, VP9, H.264 or AV1 for video, Opus for audio) produces frames, which are cut into packets of about 1,200 bytes and sent over UDP as RTP, with sequence numbers and timestamps. The receiver puts packets in a jitter buffer that smooths delay variation and reorders them. RTCP runs next to it and carries feedback: receiver reports, requests to resend a lost packet (NACK), and requests for a new key frame (PLI) when a frame cannot be decoded. Forward error correction can add redundancy for audio. Because loss is expected, the receiver conceals small gaps, and the sender only retransmits what is still useful.
Step 6: control congestion. The sender must not push more than the path can carry, or queues build up and the delay grows. Browsers use Google Congestion Control: the receiver (or the sender, with transport-wide feedback) measures how packet arrival times drift. A growing delay means a queue is filling, so the rate is cut before packets are lost; loss also reduces the rate. Between these signals the sender estimates the available bandwidth every fraction of a second and tells the encoder a target bitrate. The video then changes resolution or frame rate to fit. This is the congestion-control concept applied to media, and it has to be fast and gentle, because a TCP-style sawtooth would be visible as freezing.
Step 7: scale with servers. A pure peer-to-peer mesh needs N−1 uploads per participant. In an SFU each client sends one stream (often as 2 or 3 simulcast layers of different quality), and the server forwards the right layer to each receiver, depending on its bandwidth and screen size. The SFU does not decode, so it is cheap and adds few milliseconds. An MCU decodes and mixes streams into one, which is easy for receivers but costs a lot of server CPU. Data channels use SCTP over DTLS and carry chat, game state or files, with options for reliability and order.

example: Alice is on a home network with the private address 192.168.1.10. Bob is on a mobile network. Alice's browser gathers three candidates. The host candidate is 192.168.1.10:50000. A STUN request shows that her router maps her traffic to 203.0.113.5:54321, which becomes the server-reflexive candidate. A TURN allocation gives a relay candidate, 198.51.100.9:49152.
ICE gives each candidate a priority from its type: priority = 2^24 × type preference + 2^8 × local preference + (256 − component). With type preferences host 126, server-reflexive 100 and relay 0, a typical host candidate scores 126 × 16,777,216 + 65,535 × 256 + 255 = 2,130,706,431. The server-reflexive candidate scores 1,694,498,815 and the relay 16,777,215. Pairs are tried from the highest combined priority, so a direct path always beats a relay. With 3 local and 3 remote candidates there are 9 pairs. Suppose host/host fails (different networks) but srflx/srflx succeeds after one STUN round trip of 40 ms. The call then uses the direct path, and the relay is never used. Setup is one signaling round trip (about 100 ms through the app server), the ICE check (a few tens of ms), and the DTLS handshake (one to two round trips), so media usually starts within a second.
Now bandwidth. A 720p stream at 1.5 Mbit/s with Opus audio at 32 kbit/s needs about 1.55 Mbit/s of upload. In a 4-person mesh, each person uploads 3 copies, about 4.7 Mbit/s, which breaks most home connections. With an SFU each person uploads one stream, about 1.55 Mbit/s (a little more with simulcast layers: perhaps 2.5 Mbit/s for three layers), and downloads 3 streams, 4.7 Mbit/s. The server carries 4 × 1.55 = 6.2 Mbit/s in and 4 × 4.65 = 18.6 Mbit/s out. Scale to 1,000 such meetings: about 6 Gbit/s in and 19 Gbit/s out, which is why call providers run large server fleets.

intuition: Two people call each other through a locked building: STUN is asking a passer-by what the street address looks like from outside, and TURN is a doorman who passes the messages when no one can get through.
The design is natural once you accept the Internet as it is. Addresses are hidden behind NATs, so peers must discover how they look from outside and try many paths in order. Packets are lost, so live media must be loss tolerant and adapt its bitrate rather than wait. Everything runs over UDP, since it adds no delay, and the missing parts (security, congestion control, ordering when needed) are added back, one by one, only as much as the media requires. The browser becomes the best place for the stack, since it already has the camera, the audio system and the security model.
mistakes: People often think WebRTC is peer-to-peer only, with no servers. In fact it always needs a signaling server, almost always STUN, a TURN relay for the minority of failures, and usually an SFU for group calls. Only the media path of a simple one-to-one call is direct.
People often think WebRTC is a protocol. In fact it is a set of browser APIs and a bundle of protocols: ICE, STUN, TURN, DTLS, SRTP, RTP, RTCP, SCTP and SDP. Signaling, which every app needs, is left out on purpose.
People often think the media is end-to-end encrypted in all cases. In fact the encryption is hop-by-hop between peers and servers. A TURN server only relays encrypted packets, but an SFU terminates the encryption to read headers and forward packets. For end-to-end encryption between users through an SFU you need extra work such as insertable streams.
People often think WebRTC is only for video calls. In fact data channels move arbitrary bytes with unordered, unreliable options, used for game state, file transfer and remote control, and WHIP lets broadcasters send live streams into servers with sub-second delay.
friction: Connectivity is the first headache. A minority of sessions need TURN, which carries all the traffic and costs real bandwidth and money: a relayed 1.5 Mbit/s call moves 3 Mbit/s through your server (both directions). Corporate firewalls may block UDP entirely, so TURN over TCP or TLS on port 443 is needed as a last fallback, with worse delay.
Scale and operations are the second. Large meetings need SFU clusters, with cascading across regions, load balancing and recording. Building this yourself is serious work; many teams use a hosted service or an open-source SFU.
Quality is the third. Packet loss, Wi-Fi jitter, bufferbloat and the bandwidth estimator's reactions can cause freezes or a drop in resolution, and debugging needs per-call statistics (`getStats()`), not logs. Different browsers have small differences in codec support, simulcast and statistics; Safari and mobile devices have been the hardest to support.
Fourth, it does not fit broadcast economics. WebRTC delivers each viewer a separate connection from a media server, so serving 100,000 viewers costs far more than HTTP streaming on a CDN, which serves identical cached files. Sub-second delay at huge scale is still an open and expensive problem; solutions combine WebRTC edges with CDNs, or use new designs such as Media over QUIC.
tradeoffs: Choose WebRTC when interaction needs sub-second delay: calls, auctions, remote control, cloud gaming, live Q&A. Choose HLS or DASH (with low-latency modes) when you have many viewers and 2 to 6 seconds of delay is acceptable, since a CDN makes it cheap. You pay more per viewer with WebRTC, and gain interaction.
Choose a mesh only for two or three participants and short calls, where server cost matters most. Choose an SFU for groups from about four to hundreds, with simulcast so that weak receivers get a lower layer. Choose an MCU only when receivers are very weak (legacy devices, telephone bridges) and you can pay for the CPU.
Choose a hosted platform (a CPaaS service) when real-time is not your product and you want to ship in weeks; choose an open-source SFU such as LiveKit, mediasoup or Janus when you need control of cost, data location and features, and you can run it. You trade money for operations effort.
practice: Always deploy your own TURN server (for example coturn) or use a managed one, with short-lived credentials and TLS on port 443 as a fallback. Test with realistic networks: a corporate proxy, a phone on 4G, a hotel Wi-Fi. Measure the share of sessions that use relay candidates, and watch the bandwidth bill.
Use `getStats()` to collect round-trip time, jitter, packet loss, frame rate, resolution and the quality-limitation reason, per call, and send it to your metrics system. These numbers, not logs, tell you whether a complaint was caused by the network, the CPU or your code. Treat a rise in freezes, or in time to first frame, as an incident.
For group calls use an SFU with simulcast or scalable video coding, subscribe only to the streams that are visible, and lower the resolution of small tiles. Keep audio on Opus with in-band forward error correction and discontinuous transmission. Limit the encode load on phones by choosing resolution 360p to 720p, not 1080p.
interview: A one-to-one call works at the office but fails for a customer on a corporate network. What do you check? || ICE candidate types and connection state in `getStats()`. UDP may be blocked, so no srflx pair succeeds and a relay is needed. Check that TURN is reachable over TCP or TLS on 443, credentials are valid, and the proxy does not break WebSockets for signaling. Add TURN/TLS as a fallback and log relay use.
How does ICE find a path, and why does it prefer host over relay candidates? || Both sides gather host, server-reflexive (via STUN) and relay (via TURN) candidates, pair them, and run STUN checks in priority order. Priorities favour direct paths because relays add delay, cost and server load. The first pair that works in both directions is nominated.
Compare mesh, SFU and MCU. || Mesh: each peer uploads N−1 streams; fine for 2 to 3 people. SFU: one upload per client, the server forwards without decoding, scales to many participants with simulcast. MCU: server decodes and mixes, simple for clients but very CPU heavy.
Why does WebRTC use UDP and not TCP, and how does it cope with loss? || TCP retransmission and ordering add delay (head-of-line blocking), which is worse than a small glitch in live media. WebRTC uses RTP over UDP, with NACK for useful retransmissions, key-frame requests, FEC, jitter buffers, concealment, and congestion control that adapts the bitrate.
How would you deliver a live event to 200,000 viewers with low delay? || Pure WebRTC to each viewer is expensive. Ingest with WHIP or RTMP, transcode to ladders, and serve through a CDN with low-latency HLS or DASH (2 to 5 s). Offer WebRTC through an SFU tier only to the viewers who need sub-second delay, such as bidders or presenters.
Is WebRTC media end-to-end encrypted? || Hop by hop it is always encrypted (DTLS-SRTP). With a direct or TURN path, only the two peers hold the keys. With an SFU, the server terminates SRTP, so end-to-end needs insertable streams or SFrame-style encryption on top.
quiz: What does STUN tell a client and what does TURN do? || STUN tells a client its public address and port as seen from the Internet. TURN relays all media through a server when no direct path can be established.
Why must WebRTC apps provide their own signaling? || The standard leaves out how peers find each other and exchange offers, answers and candidates, because applications differ. Any channel such as a WebSocket can carry the SDP and ICE messages.
In a 5-person mesh, each person sends video at 1.5 Mbit/s. How much does each upload, and how much with an SFU? || Mesh: 4 copies × 1.5 = 6 Mbit/s. SFU: one stream, 1.5 Mbit/s (more with simulcast layers).
What are the three ICE candidate types in priority order? || Host (local address), server-reflexive (public address learned from STUN) and relay (TURN address). Direct paths are preferred because relays cost delay and bandwidth.
When would you choose HLS over WebRTC for a live stream? || When the audience is large, a delay of a few seconds is fine and cost per viewer matters, since a CDN caches and serves the same segments. You give up interactivity and sub-second delay.
