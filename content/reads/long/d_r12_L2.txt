@@ lsh
value: Locality-sensitive hashing (LSH) finds items that are similar to each other without comparing every pair. Ordinary hashing sends different items to different buckets and only equal items collide. LSH turns this around: it uses hash functions that are designed so that similar items collide with high probability and dissimilar items collide with low probability. To find the neighbours of an item, you hash it and look only at the items in the same buckets.
The reason this matters is arithmetic. With one billion documents there are about 5 × 10¹⁷ pairs. Even at one billion comparisons per second, checking all of them would take about 16 years. LSH reduces the work to a few hash lookups per item plus exact checks on a small list of candidates. It is used for near-duplicate detection in web crawls and in ad and content pipelines, for matching audio and video fingerprints, for clustering similar users or products, and as an early form of approximate nearest-neighbour search for embedding vectors.
For the layer above, LSH gives a way to cut a quadratic problem down to a nearly linear one, at the price of controlled errors. Three layers below make it work. Hashing and hash tables (L3) supply the buckets: candidate pairs are simply items that land in the same bucket, found with a group-by on the bucket key. Random projections and the Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma (L4) supply the hash functions for cosine and Euclidean similarity: a random hyperplane or a random direction preserves distances well enough to hash on. Probability (L4) supplies the tuning: a short formula tells you how many hashes to AND together and how many tables to OR together so that pairs above your similarity threshold almost always collide and pairs below it almost never do.
terms: Similarity — a number that says how alike two items are, for example Jaccard similarity of two sets (size of the intersection divided by size of the union) or cosine of the angle between two vectors.
Shingle — a short piece of a document, for example every run of 5 words; a document becomes the set of its shingles.
MinHash — a hash for sets: apply a random hash to every element and keep the smallest value; two sets give the same MinHash with probability equal to their Jaccard similarity.
Signature — a short vector of many MinHash (or other LSH) values that stands for one item; 100 to 200 values is typical.
Band — a group of r consecutive rows of the signature; two items become candidates if they agree on all r values of at least one band.
b and r — the number of bands and the number of rows in each band; the signature has b × r values.
Candidate pair — two items that share a bucket in at least one band; they still need an exact check.
SimHash — an LSH for cosine similarity: each bit says on which side of a random hyperplane the vector lies.
history: The idea has two roots in 1997–1998. Andrei Broder, working on AltaVista, needed to find near-duplicate web pages. In "On the resemblance and containment of documents" (1997) and the paper "Syntactic clustering of the Web" (Broder, Glassman, Manasse and Zweig, 1997), he turned each page into a set of shingles and showed that keeping only the minimum hash value gives an unbiased estimate of the Jaccard similarity. This is MinHash. It let a crawler compare short signatures instead of whole pages.
In 1998 Piotr Indyk and Rajeev Motwani published "Approximate nearest neighbors: towards removing the curse of dimensionality" (STOC 1998), and named and defined the general idea of locality-sensitive hashing. Their point was theoretical but strong: exact nearest-neighbour search in high dimensions cannot beat a linear scan in practice, because tree indexes degrade as dimension grows (the curse of dimensionality). If you accept an approximate answer, hashing gives sub-linear query time. Gionis, Indyk and Motwani (VLDB 1999) made it practical, with many hash tables and a tuning recipe.
Moses Charikar (STOC 2002) gave the random-hyperplane hash for cosine similarity, now called SimHash. Datar, Immorlica, Indyk and Mirrokni (2004) gave LSH for Euclidean distance using projections onto random lines with stable distributions. Andoni and Indyk (FOCS 2006) gave near-optimal hashing for Euclidean space, and Andoni and Razenshteyn (2015) reached the optimal exponent with data-dependent hashing. In industry, Manku, Jain and Das Sarma (WWW 2007) described Google's use of 64-bit SimHash fingerprints to find near-duplicate pages in a crawl of billions of pages, treating pages as duplicates if their fingerprints differ in at most 3 bits.
After about 2016, graph-based indexes such as HNSW became the default for dense embedding search, because they reach higher recall at the same query cost. LSH did not disappear. It remains the standard tool for near-duplicate detection over sets and text, where MinHash plus banding scales to billions of items with plain group-by operations, and Spark MLlib ships it as MinHashLSH and BucketedRandomProjectionLSH.
how: Start with a similarity measure and a hash family. A family is locality-sensitive if, for a random hash h from the family, the chance that h(x) = h(y) is high when x and y are close and low when they are far. For sets with Jaccard similarity J, the family is MinHash. Take a random permutation of all possible elements, or in practice a random hash function. h(S) is the element of S that comes first in that order. Look at the union of two sets A and B. The first element of the union in the random order is equally likely to be any element of the union. Both sets have the same MinHash exactly when that first element lies in the intersection. So P[h(A) = h(B)] = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| = J(A, B). One MinHash is a single noisy coin flip with success probability J. To reduce the noise, use many independent hashes. With k = 100 hashes, the fraction of agreeing positions estimates J with a standard error of √(J(1 − J)/100), which is 0.05 at J = 0.5.
For cosine similarity of vectors, the family is random hyperplanes. Draw a random vector r (each coordinate from a normal distribution) and let h(x) be the sign of r·x, one bit. Two vectors at angle θ get different bits with probability θ/π, so the chance of the same bit is 1 − θ/π. Here the Johnson–Lindenstrauss idea does the work: random projections keep angles and distances, so one random direction is an unbiased probe of the geometry. Concatenating 64 such bits gives a SimHash fingerprint, and the Hamming distance between two fingerprints estimates the angle. For Euclidean distance, hash h(x) = ⌊(r·x + c)/w⌋ for a random direction r, random offset c and bucket width w.
A single hash is too weak: for J = 0.8 it matches only 80% of the time, and for J = 0.3 it still matches 30% of the time. Banding sharpens the difference. Compute a signature of b × r values. Cut it into b bands of r rows. In each band, combine the r values into one bucket key (hash the tuple). Two items are candidates if they share a bucket in at least one band. For two items with similarity s, the chance that a given band matches completely is s^r (an AND of r independent events), and the chance that at least one of b bands matches is 1 − (1 − s^r)^b (an OR). This is the S-curve. It is flat near 0, rises steeply around the threshold t ≈ (1/b)^(1/r), and is flat near 1. Probability theory (L4) is exactly this calculation. You choose b and r so that t sits at the similarity you care about. A larger r makes the curve steeper and shifts it right; a larger b shifts it left and costs more tables.
The signatures are small: 100 values of 4 bytes are 400 bytes per document, so a billion documents have about 400 GB of signatures, far less than the documents themselves. The full pipeline for a distributed engine is short. First, shingle each document and compute its signature (a map step). Second, for every band emit the pair (band number, bucket key) with the document ID, and group by that key (a shuffle, which is the hash table of L3 done at cluster scale). Third, every group with more than one document yields candidate pairs. Fourth, verify candidates with an exact similarity on the original sets or vectors, which removes all false positives. Finally, if you want clusters rather than pairs, feed the verified pairs into union–find (the neighbouring L2 concept) to get connected components. Only the first three steps are approximate; false positives are removed by verification, but a similar pair that never collided is lost for good, so b and r control recall.
The theory sums these ideas into one exponent. If near points collide with probability p₁ and far points with probability p₂, an LSH index with n^(1+ρ) space answers queries in about n^ρ time, where ρ = ln(1/p₁) / ln(1/p₂) < 1. For Hamming distance and approximation factor c, ρ ≤ 1/c. For Euclidean distance the best data-independent exponent is about 1/c². Smaller ρ is better, and ρ < 1 is what makes the search sub-linear.

example: First a tiny MinHash by hand. Let the universe be the elements 0 to 5. Set A = {0, 2, 3, 5} and set B = {0, 2, 4, 5}. The intersection is {0, 2, 5}, the union is {0, 2, 3, 4, 5}, so the true Jaccard similarity is 3/5 = 0.6. Use six random orders. Each order is a ranking of the elements, and the MinHash of a set is the lowest-ranked element it contains. Take these rankings, listed for elements 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5: order 1 = [3, 0, 5, 1, 4, 2], order 2 = [1, 4, 0, 5, 2, 3], order 3 = [5, 2, 3, 0, 1, 4], order 4 = [2, 5, 1, 4, 0, 3], order 5 = [4, 1, 2, 3, 5, 0], order 6 = [0, 3, 4, 2, 1, 5]. For order 1, the ranks of A's elements 0, 2, 3, 5 are 3, 5, 1, 2, so the minimum rank is 1 (element 3); for B's elements 0, 2, 4, 5 the ranks are 3, 5, 4, 2, minimum 2 (element 5). They differ. Doing the same for all six orders, the signatures (minimum ranks) are A = [1, 0, 0, 1, 0, 0] and B = [2, 0, 1, 0, 0, 0]. They agree at positions 2, 5 and 6, which is 3 of 6, an estimate of 0.5. The true value is 0.6, and the error comes from using only six hashes.
Now bands with b = 3 and r = 2. Band 1 holds positions 1–2: A gives (1, 0), B gives (2, 0). Different buckets. Band 2 holds positions 3–4: A gives (0, 1), B gives (1, 0). Different. Band 3 holds positions 5–6: both give (0, 0). Same bucket, so A and B become a candidate pair. Notice that the pair was caught by only one of three bands, even though its similarity is 0.6.
Now a realistic setting: 1 billion documents, signatures of 100 values, b = 20 bands and r = 5 rows. Threshold t ≈ (1/20)^(1/5) = 0.55. A pair with s = 0.8 is a candidate with probability 1 − (1 − 0.8⁵)²⁰ = 1 − 0.672²⁰ ≈ 99.96%. A pair with s = 0.5 is a candidate with probability 1 − (1 − 0.03125)²⁰ ≈ 47%. A pair with s = 0.3 is a candidate with probability about 5%, and with s = 0.2 only 0.6%. The shuffle moves 20 × 10⁹ small records (a band number, a bucket key, a document ID, about 20 bytes each, so roughly 400 GB), which a Spark cluster handles easily, instead of 5 × 10¹⁷ pair comparisons. If you instead want a sharper cut, b = 10 and r = 10 gives 1% at s = 0.5 and 68% at s = 0.8, which is a threshold near 0.79 but misses many true pairs around 0.8.
intuition: A post office that sorts mail by rough area codes: letters addressed to nearby places often land in the same bin, so you only search your own bin.
The design is natural once you see that exact equality is the only thing a hash table can test, and that hash tables are the only thing that is fast at billions of items. So instead of changing the data structure, change what equality means. A hash that ignores small differences makes "equal after hashing" mean "close". One such hash is noisy, so you combine several: AND them inside a band to make false matches rare, and OR the bands to make true matches likely. Two cheap probabilistic operations give you a dial between precision and recall, and the S-curve shows exactly where the dial is set.
mistakes: People often think LSH finds the exact nearest neighbours. In fact it returns a candidate list that contains the true neighbours only with some probability. Pairs near the threshold can be missed. You raise recall by adding bands, and you get exactness only by verifying candidates, never by trusting the hash.
People often think LSH is the same as ordinary hashing with a different function. In fact a good ordinary hash is designed to destroy similarity (a one-bit change in the input changes about half the output bits). An LSH family is designed to preserve it. A cryptographic hash such as SHA-256 is useless for this purpose.
People often think a single MinHash of a document is enough. In fact one MinHash is a single coin flip with success chance J. Only a signature of many MinHashes, combined with banding, gives a usable estimate and a steep cut-off.
People often think LSH is obsolete because HNSW is better for embeddings. In fact HNSW usually wins for dense vector search with high recall, but LSH is still the right tool for set and text similarity at very large scale, because it needs no index that must sit in memory, runs as a group-by on a cluster, is trivially incremental (new documents just add bucket entries), and has provable guarantees.
friction: The first problem is the memory and compute cost of recall. To catch most pairs close to the threshold you need many bands, and each band is a full copy of the item set keyed by bucket. For dense vectors with high recall targets, LSH can need tens or hundreds of tables, which makes it larger and slower than graph indexes at the same recall. This is why it lost ground in embedding search.
Second, the S-curve is never a step. Pairs just below the threshold still get through at a measurable rate and pairs just above it are missed at a measurable rate. For deduplication, a pair at similarity 0.85 that is missed stays in the corpus, and a pair at 0.45 that is caught costs a verification. You must choose which error you can live with and test it on labelled pairs.
Third, skew hurts. Common shingles (boilerplate, licences, headers, templates) make huge buckets in which every document is a candidate for every other, so the group-by key has a few giant groups and the candidate pairs inside them explode quadratically. Production systems remove boilerplate, cap bucket sizes, or sample inside large buckets.
Fourth, the choice of similarity is a modelling decision that LSH does not make for you. Jaccard on 5-word shingles finds copied text but misses paraphrases, and cosine on embeddings finds meaning but also finds topical neighbours that are not duplicates. Finally, the hash family must match the metric. There are good families for Jaccard, cosine, Hamming and Euclidean distance, but for arbitrary learned metrics there is no general recipe.
practice: Use MinHash with banding for near-duplicate detection on text, URLs, device or household fingerprints represented as sets, and use SimHash (64 bits, Hamming distance of 3 or less, with permuted tables for lookup) when each item is a single vector and you want a compact fingerprint. For embeddings with high recall requirements, use HNSW or IVF instead and keep LSH for pre-filtering or cheap clustering.
Choose parameters from the S-curve, not by guessing. Decide the similarity threshold t that defines "duplicate" for your business, fix the signature length (128 is a common default, since it is a power of two and gives a standard error near 0.04), and pick (b, r) with b × r = 128 so that (1/b)^(1/r) is close to t, for example b = 32 and r = 4 gives a threshold near 0.42 and b = 16 and r = 8 near 0.71. Plot 1 − (1 − s^r)^b and check it against a few hundred labelled pairs before running at scale.
Always verify candidates with the exact similarity. Cap or sample large buckets. Use 64-bit hashing for the MinHash values to avoid accidental collisions between different elements. In Spark, MLlib's MinHashLSH and approxSimilarityJoin work for moderate sizes, but at billions of items many teams write the signature and band steps by hand so they control the shuffle and the bucket caps. For the clusters, run connected components on the verified pairs, and keep a representative per cluster. Track the number of candidates per document as a health metric: if it rises sharply, a template or a bug is flooding a bucket.
tradeoffs: Choose LSH (MinHash plus banding) when the items are sets or text, the corpus has hundreds of millions to billions of items, you can express the work as a group-by, and a recall of about 90 to 99% above the threshold is acceptable. Choose exact methods, such as an inverted index with prefix filtering or a plain sort-based join on normalised content, when you only need exact or very-near-exact duplicates (for example identical after normalisation), because a content hash is cheaper, exact and simpler. You give up recall with LSH and flexibility with exact hashing.
Choose graph-based or quantised indexes (HNSW, IVF with product quantisation) when the items are dense embedding vectors, queries come one at a time with a latency target in milliseconds, and recall of 95 to 99% is needed with the smallest memory. Choose LSH when you need a batch self-join, easy incremental inserts, a distributed implementation with no shared index, or provable error bounds. You give up some recall per unit of memory with LSH, and you give up easy updates and simple scale-out with the graph index.
Choose more bands with small r (a low threshold, high recall) when a miss is expensive, for example when removing duplicates is a legal or billing requirement and you can afford more candidate verification. Choose fewer bands with large r when verification is expensive or when false matches would merge unrelated items, for example when the output feeds an identity graph where one wrong link merges two households. You pay with CPU and shuffle volume in the first case and with missed pairs in the second.
Choose longer signatures when you also need a similarity estimate, not only candidates; the standard error falls as 1/√k, so quadrupling k halves the noise. Choose shorter signatures with b-bit or one-permutation variants when memory per item matters more than precision.
interview: Why can't you just hash each document and group by the hash to find near-duplicates? || A normal hash makes any small change produce a completely different value, so only exact duplicates collide. Near-duplicates (one changed sentence) need a hash that preserves similarity, such as MinHash over shingles, where the collision probability equals the Jaccard similarity. Exact hashing is still the first step to remove identical copies cheaply.
Derive the probability that two documents with Jaccard similarity s become candidates with b bands of r rows. || One band matches only if all r MinHash values agree, probability s^r. At least one of the b independent bands matching has probability 1 − (1 − s^r)^b. The curve is an S-shape with its steep part near (1/b)^(1/r); I choose b and r so that this point is the similarity threshold, and I check the false-positive and false-negative areas against the business cost.
Your LSH job runs for hours because three reducers get almost all the data. What happened and what do you do? || Boilerplate shingles or near-identical templates create giant buckets, and every document in a bucket pairs with every other, which is quadratic. I would remove common shingles by document frequency, cap the bucket size or sample inside it, add a second salted band key, and compare the candidate count per document against a baseline.
How does LSH for cosine similarity work, and where does random projection come in? || Pick a random hyperplane through the origin and record on which side a vector lies (one bit). The chance that two vectors differ in this bit is θ/π, where θ is their angle, so Hamming distance between many such bits estimates the angle. Random projection preserves angles and distances with high probability (the Johnson–Lindenstrauss idea), which is why a random direction is a fair probe.
When would you not use LSH for nearest-neighbour search? || For dense embeddings with high recall and low latency needs I would use HNSW or IVF-PQ, which reach the same recall with far fewer tables and less memory. I would also skip it when only exact duplicates matter, because a content hash does that exactly and cheaply, and when the dataset is small enough (up to a few million) for a brute-force scan on a GPU or with SIMD.
How do you evaluate an LSH deduplication pipeline in production? || Build a labelled sample of true and false pairs around the threshold, measure recall of the candidate stage (what share of true pairs collided) and precision after verification. Then monitor candidates per document, bucket size distribution and the duplicate rate over time. Recall is the number you cannot see without labels, so I would periodically run a brute-force check on a small slice.
quiz: What property must a hash family have to be locality-sensitive? || The collision probability must be high for similar items and low for dissimilar ones, and it must depend on the similarity. For MinHash it equals the Jaccard similarity exactly.
Why does banding with b bands of r rows give an S-shaped curve, and where is the steep part? || A band matches with probability s^r (all r values must agree, an AND), and any of b bands matching has probability 1 − (1 − s^r)^b (an OR). The steepest part is near s ≈ (1/b)^(1/r).
Why does a random hyperplane give a hash for cosine similarity? || The bit is the sign of r·x for a random r. Two vectors at angle θ get different bits with probability θ/π, so smaller angles collide more often, and the collision probability is 1 − θ/π.
With b = 20 and r = 5, what is the probability that a pair with Jaccard similarity 0.5 becomes a candidate? || One band matches with 0.5⁵ = 0.03125. The chance that at least one of 20 bands matches is 1 − (1 − 0.03125)²⁰ ≈ 0.47.
You need a sharper threshold but cannot afford more signature values. What would you change, and what do you lose? || Increase r and decrease b (for example b = 10 and r = 10 instead of 20 and 5). The curve becomes steeper and moves right, so fewer false candidates but more true pairs near the threshold are missed.

@@ suffixarrays
value: A suffix array is an index over one long string that answers substring questions. Where does the pattern "GATTACA" occur in a 3-billion-letter genome? How many times does it occur? What is the longest piece of text that appears twice? What do two documents have in common? A word index cannot answer these, because genomes, source code, logs with no spaces, and many languages have no word boundaries. A suffix array answers them by treating every position of the text as the start of a suffix and keeping those suffixes in sorted order.
The key observation is simple. Every substring of the text is a prefix of some suffix. In sorted order, all suffixes that begin with the same pattern sit next to each other, so finding a pattern means finding one contiguous range in the array. A binary search does it in O(m log n) steps for a pattern of length m in a text of length n, and with extra information it takes O(m + log n). The array itself stores only n integers. A suffix tree stores the same information as a tree and supports more operations in linear time, but takes several times more memory.
Three lower layers do the work. Sorting (L3) is how the array is built: sorting all suffixes naively costs too much because comparing two suffixes can take O(n), so the construction algorithms use prefix doubling or induced sorting to sort in O(n log n) or O(n). Trees and tries (L3) give the suffix tree, which is a compressed trie of all suffixes, and the array is that tree read in depth-first order. Range-query structures (L2) turn the array into a full toolkit: the longest common prefix of any two suffixes is the minimum of an LCP array over a range, which a range-minimum structure answers in O(1). The layers above are the inverted index for non-word text, genome aligners built on the FM-index (a compressed form of the suffix array), and compressors that use the Burrows–Wheeler transform.
terms: Suffix — the part of a string from some position to the end; the string "banana" has 6 suffixes, "banana", "anana", "nana", "ana", "na", "a".
Sentinel — a special last character, often $, smaller than all other characters, so that no suffix is a prefix of another suffix.
Suffix array (SA) — an array of the n start positions of the suffixes, in the sorted order of the suffixes themselves.
LCP array — for each neighbouring pair in the suffix array, the length of their longest common prefix; LCP[i] compares the suffixes at SA[i−1] and SA[i].
Suffix tree — a compressed trie of all suffixes, where every path of single-child nodes is merged into one edge labelled with a substring.
Burrows–Wheeler transform (BWT) — the string whose i-th letter is the character just before the i-th sorted suffix; it groups similar contexts together and is the basis of bzip2 and the FM-index.
FM-index — a compressed index made of the BWT plus small rank tables; it counts and locates patterns in a space close to that of the compressed text.
Rank — the number of occurrences of a symbol in a prefix of a string; the main operation behind backward search in the FM-index.
history: Peter Weiner described the suffix tree in 1973 under the name "position tree" and built it in linear time, in a paper called "Linear pattern matching algorithms". Edward McCreight (1976) gave a simpler and more memory-efficient construction, and Esko Ukkonen (1995) gave the online version that reads the text from left to right and is the one most textbooks teach. For decades the suffix tree was the standard tool in string algorithms, and it was said to be too large to use on real data.
In 1990 Udi Manber and Gene Myers (SODA 1990; journal version SIAM Journal on Computing, 1993) introduced the suffix array as a space-saving replacement. Their motivation was practical: a suffix tree on a large text needed about 10 to 20 bytes per character, and a suffix array needed 4. They built it in O(n log n) time by prefix doubling and searched it with binary search plus an LCP array. Kasai and colleagues (2001) showed how to compute the LCP array in O(n) time, and Abouelhoda, Kurtz and Ohlebusch (2004) showed that a suffix array with LCP and a few extra tables can simulate every suffix tree algorithm (the "enhanced suffix array").
The next question was whether the array can be built in linear time without building a suffix tree first. Kärkkäinen and Sanders (ICALP 2003) answered yes with the DC3 (skew) algorithm, and Nong, Zhang and Chan (2009) gave SA-IS, a fast, memory-light linear algorithm that is the basis of most modern libraries (libdivsufsort is a fast practical alternative).
In parallel, Michael Burrows and David Wheeler (1994) invented the transform that sorts the rotations of a string, and Paolo Ferragina and Giovanni Manzini (2000) showed that it can be searched directly without storing the text or the array, which gives the FM-index. This ended the memory problem for genomics: tools such as Bowtie (2009) and BWA (2009) align billions of short reads against the human genome with an index of a few gigabytes.
how: Build the array first. Take the text T of length n, ended by a sentinel $ that is smaller than every other character. For each i from 0 to n − 1 the suffix T[i..] is a candidate. SA lists the values i in the order of the suffixes. For T = "banana$" the sorted suffixes are $, a$, ana$, anana$, banana$, na$, nana$, so SA = [6, 5, 3, 1, 0, 4, 2]. The sentinel makes every suffix distinct and the order well defined.
Sorting n suffixes with an ordinary comparison sort needs O(n log n) comparisons, and each comparison may cost O(n) in a repetitive text, so a naive build can reach O(n² log n). Prefix doubling avoids that. Round 1 ranks every position by its first character. In round k you know the rank of every length-2^(k−1) prefix, and the rank of the length-2^k prefix at position i is the pair (rank[i], rank[i + 2^(k−1)]). Sort these pairs (a radix sort makes it O(n)), assign new ranks, and stop when all ranks are different. This takes at most log₂ n rounds, so O(n log n) with radix sort, which is the Manber–Myers and Larsson–Sadakane idea. SA-IS goes further: it classifies each suffix as smaller or larger than the next one (S-type or L-type), sorts a small set of special suffixes recursively, and then induces the order of all the others in two linear scans.
Search is a binary search over the array. To find a pattern P of length m, compare P with the suffix at the middle position, looking at only the first m characters. If P is smaller, go left; if larger, go right. Run the search twice, once for the lowest suffix that starts with P and once for the highest. All positions between them are occurrences: the count is hi − lo, and the positions are SA[lo..hi−1]. The cost is O(m log n) character comparisons, and an LCP-based variant that remembers how many characters the left and right ends already match brings it to O(m + log n).
The LCP array adds the extra power. LCP[i] is the length of the common prefix of the suffixes at ranks i − 1 and i. The longest common prefix of any two suffixes at ranks i < j is min(LCP[i+1..j]). That is a range-minimum query, so a sparse table or Fischer–Heun structure (the rangeq concept) answers it in O(1) after O(n) to O(n log n) preprocessing. Kasai's algorithm builds LCP in O(n) with one observation: if the suffix at position p shares h characters with its predecessor in sorted order, the suffix at position p + 1 shares at least h − 1 with its own predecessor, so the scan never moves back by more than one and total work stays linear.
With SA and LCP you can solve many problems with a single scan. The longest repeated substring is the maximum LCP value. The number of distinct substrings is n(n+1)/2 minus the sum of all LCP values. The longest common substring of two strings S and T is found by building the array of S#T and finding the largest LCP between adjacent suffixes that come from different strings. A suffix tree answers the same questions by walking nodes, and each internal node of the tree corresponds to a maximal run in the LCP array, so tree algorithms translate directly. The tree takes 10 to 20 bytes per character, against 4 or 8 for the array plus 4 for LCP.
Finally, compression. The BWT is derived from the array as BWT[i] = T[SA[i] − 1], the character before each sorted suffix. For "banana$" it is "annb$aa". Equal characters that follow the same context end up close together, which makes the BWT compress well (bzip2 uses this), and a clever trick, the LF-mapping, lets you walk backwards through the text using only counts. Backward search matches the pattern one character at a time from its end, keeping a range of rows in the sorted order, and each step costs two rank queries. A pattern of length m is counted in O(m) rank queries, and locating each match uses a sampled copy of SA. This is the FM-index, and it needs roughly the size of the compressed text instead of 4n bytes plus the text.

Check prefix doubling for the middle step. Round 1 ranks by first character, with $ = 0, a = 1, b = 2, n = 3, so positions 0 to 6 get ranks 2, 1, 3, 1, 3, 1, 0. Round 3 uses pairs (rank[i], rank[i + 2]) and breaks the two ties: position 3 has (2, rank[5] = 1) and position 1 has (2, rank[3] = 2), so 3 comes before 1; position 4 has (5, rank[6] = 0) and position 2 has (5, rank[4] = 5), so 4 comes before 2. The final order is 6, 5, 3, 1, 0, 4, 2, which matches.

Middle row 3 is "anana$", whose first three characters are "ana", so it is not smaller; set hi = 3. Middle row 1 is "a$", which is smaller than "ana" because $ < n; set lo = 2. Middle row 2 is "ana$", not smaller; hi = 2. The first row is 2. Now the first row whose first three characters are greater than "ana": lo = 0, hi = 7. Row 3 matches, so lo = 4. Row 5 is "na$", greater; hi = 5. Row 4 is "banana$", greater; hi = 4. The end is row 4. Rows 2 and 3 match, so "ana" occurs twice, at positions SA[2] = 3 and SA[3] = 1. In "banana" these are indeed positions 1 and 3 (they overlap, and the index finds overlapping matches without extra work).
Other answers from the same arrays. The longest repeated substring is the maximum of LCP, which is 3, so "ana". For scale: a 1 GB text needs a 4 GB suffix array with 32-bit entries (8 GB once positions exceed 2³², as in a large corpus) and 4 GB more for LCP. A suffix tree for the same text needs roughly 10 to 20 GB. An FM-index for the 3.1-billion-letter human genome fits in a few gigabytes.
intuition: The index at the back of a book, but for every starting point: sorted endings of the text, so any phrase is the beginning of some ending.
The design is natural because of one fact that can be stated in a sentence: a substring is a prefix of a suffix. That turns "find a substring anywhere" into "find a prefix among sorted strings", which is exactly what binary search on a sorted list does. The cost of sorting is paid once at build time, and each query then needs only the pattern length times a logarithm. The suffix tree and the suffix array are two layouts of the same sorted order, one pointer-based and fast to navigate, the other flat and small.
mistakes: People often think a suffix array stores all the suffixes, which would be n²/2 characters. In fact it stores only the n start positions, and the text itself stays in place; every comparison reads the text through the position.
People often think suffix trees are always better because they support more operations. In fact the enhanced suffix array (array plus LCP plus range-minimum structure) supports almost the same operations in O(n) words with a much smaller constant, and for large inputs the memory difference of 3 to 5 times decides what is possible.
People often think the build is cheap because binary search is cheap. In fact the build dominates. Naive sorting of suffixes is quadratic on repetitive text such as "aaaa…a", and even linear-time algorithms are memory-bound, with random access over the whole text. For hundreds of gigabytes you need external-memory or distributed construction.
People often think a suffix array can be updated like a B-tree. In fact an insertion at the front shifts all positions and changes the order of many suffixes. Practical systems rebuild per immutable segment, or use an index per chunk and merge results.
friction: Memory and build time are the first limits. The array needs 4 to 8 bytes per character plus the text and LCP, so a 100 GB text needs hundreds of gigabytes of index. Linear-time construction in main memory has large constants and random access patterns, and for inputs beyond RAM one needs semi-external algorithms or splitting the text into blocks. Compressed indexes (FM-index, compressed suffix arrays) cut memory to a fraction of the text size, but queries become slower by a factor of the sampling rate because locating an occurrence needs several steps through the BWT.
Second, the index is static and exact. It handles one text; any change means rebuilding, which is why log search engines use inverted indexes with segment merging, and why a suffix array fits archives, reference genomes and code repositories better than live data. It also does exact matching: approximate matching with mismatches or gaps needs backtracking or seed-and-extend strategies, and the search cost grows quickly with the number of errors allowed.
Third, text with a large alphabet or unusual structure, such as Unicode with multi-byte characters, needs care: the array can be built over bytes or over code points, and a pattern can match in the middle of a character if you choose bytes. Fourth, the search time depends on the output: reporting millions of occurrences costs millions of reads, and the positions are returned in text order only after sorting. Finally, the array gives positions, not documents. For a collection of many documents, a document array or a generalized suffix array with separators is needed, and relevance ranking is not part of the structure.
practice: Use a suffix array when the text is large, fixed, and has no natural word boundaries, and queries are arbitrary substrings: genome and protein search, plagiarism and clone detection in source code, finding long repeated sequences, deduplication of very large text corpora (the same repeat-finding idea is used to remove duplicated passages from language-model training sets), and compression. For word-based search with ranking, use an inverted index. For genomic read alignment, use an FM-index tool (BWA, Bowtie 2, minimap2 uses minimizers instead) rather than your own code.
Build it with a library, not by hand. libdivsufsort and SA-IS implementations build arrays for text of hundreds of megabytes in seconds to minutes on one core. Keep the text as bytes with a sentinel, store positions as 32-bit values when the text is under 4 GiB and 64-bit otherwise, and compute LCP with Kasai's algorithm. If memory is tight, build an FM-index and sample the suffix array every 16 or 32 positions, which trades locate speed for space.
When one text is too big, split it into blocks, index each, and merge results, taking care that matches crossing a block border are not lost (overlap blocks by the pattern length). Test the code on pathological inputs, such as a string of one repeated letter, a long period such as "abababab", and random data, because performance on natural text hides bad constants.
tradeoffs: Choose a suffix array when you need arbitrary substring search over a static text of up to a few billion characters, you can afford 4 to 8 bytes per character plus the text, and queries are frequent enough to repay the build. Choose a suffix tree only when you need operations that are awkward on the array and memory is not the limit (for example many online, left-to-right text updates, or suffix links for certain algorithms). The array is smaller and cache-friendlier; the tree is more direct to navigate.
Choose an FM-index when the text is too large for 4 bytes per character, as with a whole genome or a very large corpus, and you can accept slower locate queries (microseconds instead of nanoseconds per occurrence) for an index of about the size of the compressed text. Choose the plain array when query latency matters more than memory and the text fits in RAM.
Choose an inverted index when users search words or phrases and need ranking, stemming and fast updates; choose a suffix-based index only when the query is "any substring", because it solves a harder problem at a larger cost. Choose an n-gram or trigram index (as in code search tools) when you want substring search with near-index-size memory and tolerance for some false candidates that you verify afterwards; you give up exactness at the index level and gain simplicity and incremental updates.
Choose Kasai plus a sparse table for repeated LCP queries (O(n log n) words, O(1) per query), and Fischer–Heun or a bit-vector based structure when memory matters more (about 2n + o(n) bits, O(1) query). You pay with implementation complexity for the compact version.
interview: Why is every substring a prefix of a suffix, and how does that give a search algorithm? || A substring that starts at position i is the first m characters of the suffix starting at i. If suffixes are sorted, all suffixes that start with a given pattern are adjacent, so two binary searches find the range. The cost is O(m log n) comparisons, and the answer is the count and the positions SA[lo..hi).
What is the cost of building a suffix array and why is naive sorting bad? || Comparing two suffixes can take up to O(n), so a naive comparison sort can cost O(n² log n) on repetitive text. Prefix doubling gives O(n log n) with radix sort, and SA-IS or DC3 give O(n). In practice build time and memory dominate, not query time.
How would you find the longest substring that occurs at least twice, and the longest common substring of two strings? || For repeats, build SA and LCP and take the maximum LCP. For two strings S and T, build the array on S, a separator, and T, and take the largest LCP between adjacent suffixes that come from different strings. Both are a single scan after O(n) construction.
Why do genome tools use an FM-index instead of a plain suffix array? || A plain array for 3.1 billion bases needs about 12 GB (4 bytes each) plus the text. The FM-index stores the BWT with rank tables and a sampled array in a few gigabytes, and backward search counts a pattern in O(m) rank steps. The cost is slower locate, because each reported position needs several LF steps to reach a sampled entry.
You need substring search over a log stream that grows all day. Would you use a suffix array? || Not directly: the array is static, and rebuilds are expensive. I would index immutable segments (one array or n-gram index per segment), search all segments, and merge segments in the background. For word queries an inverted index is much simpler.
How does an LCP array plus a range-minimum structure replace suffix tree operations? || The longest common prefix of any two suffixes is the minimum of the LCP values between their ranks, an RMQ answered in O(1). Internal nodes of the suffix tree correspond to intervals of the array defined by LCP values, so the tree can be traversed implicitly. That is the enhanced suffix array.
quiz: What does the suffix array of "banana$" look like, and what does the LCP array contain? || SA = [6, 5, 3, 1, 0, 4, 2], from the sorted suffixes $, a$, ana$, anana$, banana$, na$, nana$. LCP = [0, 0, 1, 3, 0, 0, 2], the common prefix lengths of neighbouring suffixes.
Why does a search for pattern P of length m cost O(m log n) on a suffix array? || The array has n sorted entries, so binary search makes about log₂ n probes, and each probe compares P with the suffix over at most m characters.
Why does prefix doubling finish in at most log₂ n rounds? || After round k, positions are ranked by their first 2^k characters, by combining two ranks of length 2^(k−1). When 2^k ≥ n, no two suffixes can share the same ranked prefix (the sentinel makes them distinct), so all ranks differ.
A text has 2 billion characters. How much memory does a 32-bit suffix array need, and how much for a suffix tree at 15 bytes per character? || The array is 2 × 10⁹ × 4 B = 8 GB (plus 8 GB for LCP if you keep it). The tree is about 2 × 10⁹ × 15 B = 30 GB.
When is an inverted index better than a suffix array, and when is it worse? || Better when queries are words or phrases, results need ranking, and documents change often. Worse when the query is an arbitrary substring, the language has no word boundaries (genomes, code, some scripts), or you need repeats and common-substring analysis.

value: Union–find, also called the disjoint-set data structure, keeps track of a collection of groups that only ever merge. It supports two operations: find(x), which tells you which group x belongs to (by returning one representative element of the group), and union(a, b), which merges the groups of a and b. Two items are in the same group exactly when their find results are equal. With two small tricks, both operations cost almost constant time: the amortized cost per operation is O(α(n)), where α(n) is at most 4 for any n that could ever be stored.
This is the right tool whenever connectivity grows over time and you need to ask "are these two connected?" after each change. It runs Kruskal's minimum spanning tree algorithm, labels connected regions in an image, finds connected components in a stream of edges, checks whether adding an edge creates a cycle, drives type unification in compilers and equality reasoning in solvers, and clusters records in data pipelines: after a locality-sensitive hash proposes similar pairs, or after a data source says two device IDs belong to one household, union–find merges them into clusters.
Three lower layers supply the parts. Trees (L3) give the shape: each group is a tree stored with parent pointers, and the root is the representative, so find is "walk up to the root". Amortized analysis (L3) gives the proof that the operations are cheap: a single find can take O(log n) steps, but path compression and union by rank make the total cost over m operations O(m α(n)), where α is the inverse of the Ackermann function. Graph algorithms (L3) give the use: connected components, spanning trees and cycle detection are all graph problems in which edges arrive and groups merge, and union–find answers them in near-linear time without ever building adjacency lists.
terms: Disjoint sets — a partition of items into groups with no overlap; every item is in exactly one group.
Representative (root) — the one element chosen to name a group; the root of the tree.
find(x) — returns the representative of the group that contains x.
union(a, b) — merges the group of a with the group of b; does nothing if they are already the same group.
Union by rank — when merging two trees, attach the root of the shallower tree under the root of the deeper one, so trees stay shallow.
Path compression — during find, make every visited node point directly to the root, so later finds on those nodes take one step.
Rank — an upper bound on the height of a tree; it grows by one only when two trees of equal rank are merged.
Inverse Ackermann function α(n) — a function that grows so slowly that it is at most 4 for any n you could ever store.
history: The structure comes from a very practical problem: the compiler problem of EQUIVALENCE statements in Fortran, where variables declared equal had to share storage. Bernard Galler and Michael Fischer described a forest-based algorithm in "An improved equivalence algorithm" (CACM, 1964). Their version used trees with parent pointers and gave good running time in practice, but the exact complexity was unknown.
Hopcroft and Ullman (1973) proved that with union by rank and path compression the total time for m operations on n elements is O(m log* n), where log* is the number of times you must take a logarithm before reaching 1. Robert Tarjan (1975), in "Efficiency of a good but not linear set union algorithm", then proved the tight bound O(m α(n)), and showed that the bound is not linear: the inverse Ackermann function really appears. It was a surprising result, since the algorithm is only a few lines long.
The lower bound came later. Fredman and Saks (1989) proved that any structure for this problem in the cell-probe model needs Ω(α(n)) amortized time per operation, so Tarjan's structure is optimal. Tarjan and van Leeuwen (1984) analysed the variants used in practice, path halving and path splitting, which do the compression in a single upward pass without recursion or a second pass, and showed that they have the same bound.
Later work moved to parallel and distributed machines. Wait-free concurrent union–find (Anderson and Woll, 1991) uses compare-and-swap on the parent array. For clusters, Kiveris and others at Google (SoCC 2014) described MapReduce algorithms for connected components that take a logarithmic number of rounds, and graph systems such as Pregel, GraphX and GraphFrames compute components with many synchronized rounds, since the pointer-chasing of union–find is hard to spread over a network.
how: The data structure is one array parent[0..n−1]. Initially parent[i] = i for every i, so every element is a group of its own and a root. A root is an element with parent[x] = x. The tree of a group is formed by the parent pointers, and the root names the group. A second array, rank[0..n−1], starts at 0. That is the whole structure, 4 to 5 bytes per element.
find(x) starts at x and follows parent pointers until it reaches a root. With path compression, it then makes a second pass (or a recursive return) that sets parent of every node on the walk to the root. The cost of one find is the length of the path, but the work is not wasted: all nodes on the path now point straight to the root, so the next find from any of them costs one step. A cheaper variant is path halving: while parent[x] ≠ x, set parent[x] = parent[parent[x]] and move x to its new parent. It makes one pass and reaches the same bound.
union(a, b) calls find on both elements to get roots ra and rb. If ra = rb, they already are in one group, so return false (this is also the cycle test in Kruskal). Otherwise link one root under the other. With union by rank, compare rank[ra] and rank[rb] and make the root with the higher rank the parent. If the ranks are equal, pick either one and increase its rank by one. Rank is not exactly the height after compression has changed the trees, but it stays an upper bound on the height, and that is all the proof needs. The idea of linking by rank has a clean consequence: a root of rank r has at least 2^r nodes in its tree, so rank is at most log₂ n. Union by size works equally well: attach the smaller tree under the larger.
Neither trick alone gives the best bound. Union by rank alone guarantees trees of height at most log₂ n, so find costs O(log n) in the worst case. Path compression alone, with arbitrary linking, gives O(log n) amortized per operation, though a single find may take O(n). Together, Tarjan proved the total is O(m α(n)) for m operations on n elements. The function α(n) is the inverse of the Ackermann function A(n, n), which grows faster than any tower of exponentials; α(n) ≤ 4 for every n up to a number that has far more digits than there are atoms in the observable universe. In practice a find is a few memory reads, and the cost is dominated by cache misses on the parent array, not by the number of steps.
The amortized argument (the L3 idea of paying for expensive steps with a potential) has a simple core. Ranks strictly increase along any path from a node up to the root, and at most n / 2^r nodes can ever have rank r. Group the ranks into blocks such as {1}, {2}, {3 … 4}, {5 … 16}, {17 … 65536}, where each block starts at the tower of the previous one. A step along a path that leaves one block pays for itself, because there are very few blocks (log* n of them). A step that stays inside a block is paid by the node, because path compression moves its parent to a higher rank, and a node can do this only a bounded number of times before its parent is in a higher block. Tarjan's sharper accounting replaces blocks by the Ackermann hierarchy and gets α(n).
The graph connection is direct. To compute the connected components of a graph with V vertices and E edges, start with V singleton sets and call union(u, v) for every edge. The final partition is the set of components, in O(E α(V)) time and O(V) memory, and the edges can arrive in a stream in any order. If you want to answer "are u and v connected?" at any time, call find on both. Kruskal's algorithm sorts the edges by weight and adds an edge to the spanning tree exactly when union returns true, in time O(E log E) dominated by the sort.
What union–find cannot do is split a group. Deleting an edge may disconnect a component, and nothing in the parent pointers tells you whether it does. If you need deletions, you need a different structure, such as link–cut trees or Euler tour trees (dynamic connectivity in polylogarithmic time), or an offline method: process the edges in a divide-and-conquer over time and use a union–find with rollback (union by size, no path compression, each union logged so it can be undone), which gives O(log n) per operation and handles known-in-advance deletions.

example: Take 8 elements, 0 to 7, parent = [0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7], all ranks 0. Do union(0, 1): the roots are 0 and 1 with equal rank, so link 1 under 0 and raise rank[0] to 1. parent = [0, 0, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Do union(2, 3): link 3 under 2, rank[2] = 1. Do union(0, 2): roots 0 and 2 have equal rank 1, so link 2 under 0 and set rank[0] = 2. parent = [0, 0, 0, 2, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Do the same on the right side: union(4, 5), union(6, 7), union(4, 6) give parent = [0, 0, 0, 2, 4, 4, 4, 6] with root 4 of rank 2 and 7 below 6.
Now union(3, 7). find(3) walks 3 → 2 → 0 and reaches root 0; compression sets parent[3] = 0. find(7) walks 7 → 6 → 4 and reaches root 4; compression sets parent[7] = 4. The ranks of 0 and 4 are both 2, so link 4 under 0 and set rank[0] = 3. The final array is parent = [0, 0, 0, 0, 0, 4, 4, 4]. Now find(5) walks 5 → 4 → 0 (two steps) and sets parent[5] = 0. All items are in one group, and the deepest remaining node is at depth 2. The maximum possible rank for 8 elements is log₂ 8 = 3, and it was reached.
Now see why compression matters. Suppose a careless union linked the root of its first argument under the root of its second, and you performed union(0, 1), union(1, 2), …, union(6, 7). Then 0 points to 1, 1 points to 2, and so on: a chain of depth 7 with root 7. The first find(0) walks 7 steps. With path compression, it sets every node on the path to point at the root, so the next find(0) takes one step, as do find(1), find(2) and the rest. The one expensive operation has paid for the cheap ones that follow. That is amortization at work: the total over m operations is small even though one operation is not.
For scale, a data pipeline with 2 billion device IDs needs a parent array of 2 × 10⁹ × 4 bytes = 8 GB plus 2 GB for ranks stored as one byte each, which fits in memory on one large machine. Processing 10 billion edges at roughly 50 to 100 nanoseconds per edge (mostly cache misses) takes about 10 to 20 minutes on one core. The same job as a distributed connected-components computation on Spark takes dozens of rounds of shuffles, each moving all remaining edges.
intuition: Each club has a president; you ask "who is your president?" and two people are in the same club when they name the same one.
The structure is natural because it stores only what is needed. A group is just "everyone who names the same boss," so merging two groups means making one boss answer to the other, which costs one pointer change. Questions are answered by asking up the chain. The tricks keep chains short: when people merge, the smaller organization joins the bigger one, so any single person's distance to the top grows at most log₂ n times, and when you have asked the chain once, you remember the top boss and stop asking the middle managers. Each trick is cheap, and together they make the chains almost flat.
mistakes: People often think union–find is O(1) per operation. In fact it is O(α(n)) amortized, which is practically constant but not exactly so, and a single find can still take O(log n) steps. The guarantee is on the total, not on each call, which matters for latency-sensitive uses.
People often think you can delete an item or remove an edge. In fact there is no efficient way to undo a union once path compression has rearranged pointers. If your problem has deletions, use a dynamic connectivity structure, or rebuild components periodically from the edge list, or use the offline rollback technique.
People often think path compression alone or union by rank alone is enough. In fact each gives only O(log n) in the worst case. The α(n) bound needs both, and the proof of the combined bound is one of the best known in amortized analysis.
People often think union–find tells you the structure of the connection. In fact it says only whether two items are in the same group. It does not store the path between them, the number of edges, or which edge made the link. If you need to explain why two IDs were merged, keep the evidence edges separately.
friction: The deepest problem is that merging is irreversible and monotone. One wrong link merges two groups for good, and all later queries about those groups are wrong. In identity graphs this is the "mega-cluster" failure: a shared device, a public IP address, a carrier-grade NAT or a family tablet connects thousands of unrelated people, and the component grows to millions of IDs. Because union–find has no memory of why a link exists, you cannot remove the bad edge without recomputing from the edge list. Real systems cap node degree, ignore high-fanout identifiers, require a minimum evidence weight for a link, and monitor the size distribution of components.
Distribution is the second problem. The array has random access, and each union depends on the result of earlier finds, so the algorithm is sequential in nature. A cluster computes components by rounds: label propagation (each node takes the minimum label of its neighbours) needs a number of rounds equal to the graph diameter, which can be dozens or hundreds on real graphs; star contraction and similar algorithms need O(log n) rounds but are subtle. Each round reshuffles the edges, so the job costs far more than the single-machine version. GraphFrames, for example, requires a checkpoint directory because the lineage becomes long.
Third, memory and cache behaviour decide the speed. The parent array is accessed at random, so for billions of elements a find is dominated by cache and TLB misses, around 100 ns each. Packing rank and parent into one word, using path halving, and sorting edges by one endpoint to improve locality help in practice. Fourth, concurrent updates need atomic compare-and-swap or locks, and naive parallel versions can produce cycles in the parent array. Finally, the structure supports no iteration over the members of a group; to list members you need an extra linked list or a final grouping pass over the array.
practice: Use union–find for offline or streaming connectivity, clustering the output of pairwise matching, Kruskal and minimum spanning forests, cycle detection in undirected graphs, region labelling, and equivalence classes in compilers and checkers. For deduplication, the typical pipeline is: candidate pairs (from LSH or blocking keys), verify pairs, union the verified pairs, and keep one representative per final component.
Write it with path halving and union by size, in an array of 32-bit ints, with the root storing the negative size (a trick that uses one array for both parent and size). Test it by comparing with a simple BFS on random graphs. Add iteration safety: make find iterative, not recursive, because recursion on a long chain overflows the stack in languages without tail-call elimination.
For identity resolution at scale, run the clustering in two stages. First, compute components on a graph that includes only high-confidence edges. Second, limit the degree of each node and drop identifiers that connect more than a set number of devices. Track the largest component size, the histogram of component sizes, and the churn of cluster IDs from day to day, and alert if the largest component grows by an order of magnitude. Use the minimum ID (or a stable hash) as the label so that the cluster ID remains the same as the cluster grows, which keeps downstream joins stable.
On Spark, use GraphFrames connectedComponents with a checkpoint directory for graphs up to billions of edges, but when the graph is a pre-reduced edge list, a few iterations of min-label propagation with a join can be simpler to operate and debug. Process in the driver only if the component graph is small, such as the clusters formed after blocking.
tradeoffs: Choose union–find when edges only arrive (never leave), the question is "same component?", and the data fits in one machine's memory (up to a few billion elements). It beats a graph traversal because it never builds adjacency lists, and it handles streams in order of arrival. Choose BFS or DFS over an adjacency list when you need the actual paths, the members of each component in one pass, or other graph analysis, and you can afford the adjacency memory.
Choose distributed connected components (GraphFrames, GraphX, MapReduce-style star contraction) when the edges do not fit on one machine or the rest of the pipeline is already on a cluster. You give up speed: it is typically many times slower per edge than in-memory union–find. A common middle road is to pre-reduce edges on the cluster (dedupe, drop hubs), then collect the reduced edge list to one machine and run union–find there.
Choose union by size with path halving for general use, because it is simple and fast. Choose union by rank with full compression when you want the textbook guarantee. Choose union with rollback (no compression) when you must undo operations, such as in offline dynamic connectivity or backtracking search, and accept O(log n) per find.
Choose a dynamic connectivity structure (link–cut trees, Euler tour trees, or the Holm–de Lichtenberg–Thorup structure) only when edges truly get deleted online and queries must stay correct; you pay polylogarithmic time and a large implementation cost. If deletions are rare and a delay is acceptable, rebuilding components in a nightly batch is far simpler.
interview: Explain union by rank and path compression, and what each one buys. || Union by rank keeps trees shallow (height at most log₂ n), so find is O(log n) in the worst case. Path compression flattens the path on each find, so repeated finds on the same nodes become one step. Alone each is O(log n); together the amortized cost is O(α(n)), which is at most 4 in practice.
How would you use union–find to check whether adding an edge creates a cycle, and where does Kruskal use this? || For an undirected edge (u, v), if find(u) = find(v), the two ends are already connected and the edge closes a cycle. Otherwise union them. Kruskal sorts the edges by weight and keeps an edge exactly when it joins two different components, giving a minimum spanning forest in O(E log E).

I would find the nodes with extreme degree, exclude them or require extra evidence, rebuild the components from the edge list, and add monitoring of the maximum component size.
Why can't you support edge deletion, and what can you do if you need it? || Unions are recorded as parent pointers, and path compression rewrites them, so the structure does not remember which edge caused a merge or whether another path connects the parts. Options: offline divide-and-conquer with a rollback union–find, an online dynamic connectivity structure, or rebuilding periodically from the edge list.
How would you compute connected components for a graph with 50 billion edges? || Not with a single array, unless a reduced edge list fits on a large machine. On Spark, I would first dedupe and prune edges, then run a star-contraction or min-label propagation algorithm with checkpointing, watching the number of rounds and the shuffle volume per round. If the component count shrinks quickly, I would collect the remaining small graph and finish with union–find on one node.
Why is the cost described as "amortized", and is that a problem in a real-time system? || An individual find can take O(log n) steps, and the first find after a long chain walks the whole chain. The bound holds for the sum over many operations. For hard real-time limits, I would use union by rank without compression, which bounds each find by log₂ n, or accept the rare long find.
quiz: What are the two operations and what does each return? || find(x) returns the representative (root) of the group containing x. union(a, b) merges the groups of a and b and, in typical implementations, returns whether a merge actually happened.
Why does union by rank keep the height at most log₂ n? || A root of rank r has at least 2^r nodes, because rank rises only when two trees of equal rank merge, which at least doubles the size. So rank, an upper bound on height, is at most log₂ n.
What is the final parent array if you start with 4 singletons and do union(0,1), union(2,3), union(0,2) with union by rank, linking the second root under the first on ties? || After union(0,1): parent = [0,0,2,3]. After union(2,3): [0,0,2,2]. After union(0,2), ranks tie at 1, so 2 goes under 0: parent = [0,0,0,2], and rank[0] = 2.

Would you use union–find or BFS to answer "are u and v connected" after each of a million edge insertions and a few deletions? || With only insertions, union–find. With deletions in the mix, plain union–find breaks. Use an offline rollback approach if the whole sequence is known in advance, or a dynamic connectivity structure if it is online; rebuilding per deletion with BFS is too slow.

@@ cuckoo
value: Cuckoo hashing is a hash table in which every key has exactly two possible homes, chosen by two hash functions. A lookup checks those two slots and nothing else, so lookup time is bounded in the worst case: 2 memory probes, never a long chain. Ordinary hash tables cannot promise this. Chaining and linear probing give good averages, but a few unlucky keys may need many probes. Cuckoo hashing pays for its guarantee at insert time: when both homes of a new key are full, the key takes one slot and kicks out the old occupant, which moves to its own other home, and so on until a free slot is found.
The same idea gives the cuckoo filter, a Bloom-filter replacement. Instead of storing keys, the table stores short fingerprints (for example 12 bits per key). The filter answers "maybe in the set" or "definitely not", like a Bloom filter, but it also supports deleting items, and at low error rates it uses a little less space. Cuckoo tables are used in network packet processing (flow tables in software switches and DPDK), in key-value caches (MemC3, a faster memcached), in in-memory databases and in filters for storage and deduplication.
Two lower layers explain how it works. Hash tables (L3) provide the base design: a table of slots, a hash function, and a collision policy. Cuckoo hashing is open addressing with two choices per key, and a bucket usually holds several slots (4 is common), which fits in one cache line. Randomized algorithms (L3) provide the guarantee: if the hash functions behave like random functions, the chain of evictions is short in expectation, and the rare cycle is handled by choosing new hash functions and rebuilding, a standard Las Vegas style argument. The layer above, the Bloom filter, is the structure that cuckoo filters compete with.
terms: Slot — one place in the table that holds one key (or one fingerprint).
Bucket — a group of slots, usually 2 to 8, that sit together in memory; a key may go into any free slot of either of its buckets.
Two choices — each key has two candidate positions h₁(key) and h₂(key); a lookup checks only these.
Eviction (kick) — when both candidate buckets are full, the new key replaces a resident, which is then moved to its alternate position.
Insertion path — the chain of evictions caused by one insert; a long or cyclic path signals that the table is too full.
Stash — a small extra area (for example 4 entries) that holds keys that failed to find a slot, so rare failures do not force a rebuild.
Fingerprint — a short hash of a key (8 to 16 bits) stored instead of the key itself in a cuckoo filter.
Partial-key cuckoo hashing — computing the alternate bucket from the current bucket and the fingerprint, so a stored fingerprint can be moved without knowing the original key.
Load factor — the fraction of slots in use; the main limit on how full the table can get before inserts fail.
history: Rasmus Pagh and Flemming Friche Rodler presented cuckoo hashing at ESA 2001 (journal version in the Journal of Algorithms, 2004). The name comes from the cuckoo bird, which lays its egg in another bird's nest and pushes out the existing egg. Their result was a hash table with worst-case constant lookup time and expected constant-time insertion, using space linear in the number of keys, and with practical performance close to the best known hash tables at the time. They used two separate tables, and showed that the scheme works while the table is less than half full.
The follow-up work raised the load factor. Fotakis, Pagh, Sanders and Spirakis (2003) generalised to d hash functions, and Dietzfelbinger and Weidling (2005) showed that buckets of several slots allow much higher fill. Kirsch, Mitzenmacher and Wieder (2008) added a tiny stash, which reduces the failure probability from about 1/n to about 1/n^(s+1) for a stash of size s. These results gave tables that can be 90 to 95% full with constant-time inserts in expectation.
The practical turn came from systems. Fan, Andersen and Kaminsky (NSDI 2013) built MemC3, which replaced memcached's chained table with an optimistic cuckoo table of 4-slot buckets and 1-byte tags, so readers do not take locks. The same team's Cuckoo Filter paper (CoNEXT 2014, by Fan, Andersen, Kaminsky and Mitzenmacher) introduced partial-key cuckoo hashing and showed that the filter beats Bloom filters on speed and on space at error rates around 3% and below, while adding deletion.
Since then there have been many variants: libcuckoo (a concurrent table from the same CMU group), Morton filters (2018) that compress buckets for higher speed, and static filters such as xor filters (2019) and ribbon filters (2021) that beat the cuckoo filter on space when the set never changes.
how: Start with the table. Allocate m buckets of b slots each, and two hash functions h₁ and h₂ that map a key to a bucket number. For the classic version b = 1 and the table has m slots. A lookup of key x checks bucket h₁(x) and bucket h₂(x). If x is in either, it is found; otherwise it is not in the table. This is the whole lookup, and it is why the worst case is 2 probes. With b = 4, each probe reads one bucket, which is usually one cache line, so a lookup costs at most 2 cache misses, and the two probes are independent and can be issued in parallel.
Insertion is where the work is. To insert x, first check whether x is already present. If h₁(x) or h₂(x) has a free slot, put x there and stop. Otherwise both buckets are full. Pick one of them (alternating or random) and a victim y in it. Put x in the victim's slot, and now y needs a new home. The only place for y, other than its current bucket, is its other candidate bucket. If that has a free slot, y goes there and the insert is done. If not, y evicts one resident z, and so on. Repeat until a free slot appears or a limit is reached (500 kicks in the cuckoo filter paper, O(log n) in the analyses). If the limit is reached, the insertion has probably hit a cycle or an overfull region. A stash may absorb the final key; otherwise the table is rebuilt with new hash functions, or doubled.
Why does the loop usually end? Draw a graph whose vertices are buckets and whose edges are keys, each edge joining the key's two candidate buckets. An insertion walks along this graph. With b = 1 slot per bucket, a connected component of the graph with k vertices can hold at most k keys, so a component that has more edges than vertices cannot be stored at all. When the load is below 50%, random graphs with m vertices and fewer than m/2 edges consist mostly of small trees and a few cycles, and a component with only one cycle can still be stored. Randomness (the L3 randomized-algorithms idea) is what makes long components rare, so the expected insertion cost is constant while the load stays under the threshold. This threshold is 50% for 2 choices with 1 slot, about 92% for 3 choices with 1 slot, about 90% for 2 choices with 2 slots, and above 95% for 2 choices with 4 slots, where practical tables are run at about 95%.
Deletion is easy: find the key in one of its two buckets and clear the slot. No tombstones are needed, which is another advantage over open addressing with linear probing.
A cuckoo filter keeps only a fingerprint of f bits per key. The problem is relocation: when a fingerprint is evicted, the table does not hold the key, so it cannot compute the second hash h₂(x). Partial-key cuckoo hashing solves this. Compute i₁ = hash(x) and fp = fingerprint(x). Define the alternate bucket as i₂ = i₁ XOR hash(fp). Since XOR is its own inverse, i₁ = i₂ XOR hash(fp) as well. Knowing only the current bucket and the stored fingerprint, you can always compute the other bucket. (The number of buckets must be a power of two for the XOR to stay in range.) A lookup of y computes i₁, fp, i₂ and checks both buckets for fp. If any slot holds it, the answer is "maybe present". If not, y is certainly absent.
This gives the false-positive rate. A query for an absent item can match only if some fingerprint in one of its two buckets equals its own fingerprint. With b slots per bucket, there are at most 2b stored fingerprints to compare, and each matches with probability 1/2^f, so the rate is about 2b/2^f. For b = 4 and f = 12, that is 8/4096 ≈ 0.2%. Each item costs about f/α bits, where α is the load factor, near 0.95, so 12.6 bits per item. A Bloom filter needs 1.44 × log₂(500) ≈ 12.9 bits for 0.2%. Plain cuckoo filters beat Bloom only below about 0.4% error; the paper's semi-sorting trick (storing the fingerprints in a bucket sorted, which saves one bit per entry) moves that break-even to about 3%.
Duplicates and deletion have rules. Two different keys with the same bucket pair and fingerprint are indistinguishable. A bucket pair can hold at most 2b copies of the same fingerprint, so inserting the same item 2b + 1 times fails. Deleting an item removes one matching fingerprint, which is correct only if the item was really inserted; deleting a never-inserted item can remove somebody else's fingerprint and create a false negative.

example: Part 1, the table. Take 7 slots and keys with candidate slots A = (1, 4), B = (1, 2), C = (2, 5), D = (4, 2), E = (1, 6), F = (5, 4). Insert in the order A, B, C, D, E, F, always trying the first candidate first. A goes to slot 1. B wants slot 1, which holds A, so B takes slot 1 and A moves to its other slot 4. C goes to slot 2 (free). D wants slot 4, which holds A; D takes slot 4 and A moves to its other slot 1, which holds B; A takes slot 1 and B moves to its other slot 2, which holds C; B takes slot 2 and C moves to its other slot 5, which is free. That insert caused 3 evictions, and the table is [ _, A, B, _, D, C, _ ]. Insert E = (1, 6): E takes slot 1, evicting A; A moves to slot 4, evicting D; D moves to slot 2, evicting B; B moves to slot 1, evicting E; E moves to its other slot 6, which is free. Four evictions, and the table is [ _, B, D, _, A, C, E ]. Now any key is found by checking only its two slots: for example D = (4, 2) is in slot 2.
Insert F = (5, 4). The chain goes around in circles and never ends: F evicts C, C evicts D, D evicts A, A evicts B, B evicts C again, and so on. It cannot succeed. The reason: A, B, C, D and F together can use only the four slots 1, 2, 4 and 5, but they are five keys. No arrangement exists, so no amount of kicking helps. The program stops after the kick limit and rebuilds with new hash functions. At 6 keys in 7 slots, the load was 86%, far over the 50% safe level for this design.
Part 2, the filter. Use 8 buckets of 2 slots, 4-bit fingerprints, and H(fp) = (5 × fp) mod 8. Insert "cat" with i₁ = 3 and fp = 11. H(11) = 55 mod 8 = 7, so i₂ = 3 XOR 7 = 4 (binary 011 XOR 111 = 100). Put 11 in bucket 3. Insert "dog" with i₁ = 4 and the same fp = 11: i₂ = 4 XOR 7 = 3. The pair is again {3, 4}; put 11 in bucket 4. Insert "pig" with i₁ = 3 and fp = 9: H(9) = 45 mod 8 = 5, so i₂ = 3 XOR 5 = 6; bucket 3 has a free slot, so it becomes [11, 9]. Now query "emu" with i₁ = 3 and fp = 11: its buckets are {3, 4}, which hold 11. The filter says "maybe", a false positive, because "emu" shares its fingerprint and bucket pair with "cat" and "dog".
Eviction: suppose bucket 3 is full ([11, 9]) and a new item with i₁ = 3 and fp = 5 also finds its other bucket full. Pick the victim 9 in bucket 3 and move it to its alternate: 3 XOR H(9) = 3 XOR 5 = 6. We never needed the original key of "pig". Deletion: delete "cat" by removing one 11 from bucket 3. "dog" is still found, because its 11 is in bucket 4. But deleting "emu", which was never inserted, would remove an 11 and break "cat" or "dog". Finally, the error rate: with b = 2 and f = 4, the rate is about 2b/2^f = 4/16 = 25%, which is why real filters use 8 to 16 bits.
intuition: A nest with two possible rooms for every guest: a newcomer may take a room and push the occupant to the occupant's second room.
Why is this a good idea? Lookup is the hot operation in most tables, and cuckoo hashing moves all the difficulty into insertion, where it can be absorbed. Because every key is always in one of exactly two places, a reader never searches. Because the eviction chain is a walk on a random graph, and random graphs at moderate density are made of small trees, the walk almost always ends quickly. The design says: pay occasionally at write time to get a hard bound at read time.
mistakes: People often think cuckoo hashing makes inserts constant time in the worst case. In fact lookups and deletes are worst-case O(1), but an insert can trigger a long eviction chain or a full rebuild. Inserts are constant in expectation while the load stays below the threshold.
People often think a cuckoo table can be filled completely. In fact with one slot per bucket and two choices, inserts begin to fail around 50% load. Four-slot buckets reach about 95%, but chains grow longer as the table fills, so insertion latency has a long tail near the limit.
People often think a cuckoo filter can remove any key the way a table can. In fact it can only delete items that were inserted, because it holds fingerprints that other keys may share. Deleting a key that was never inserted can erase another key's fingerprint and cause a false negative, which a Bloom filter never has.
People often think a cuckoo filter can grow by doubling like a hash table. In fact the table cannot be rehashed, because the stored fingerprint and bucket index do not contain enough bits to compute the new position in a larger table. You must rebuild from the original keys, or use a variant that adds new filters in a chain (like scalable Bloom filters).
friction: The first problem is the sharp capacity limit. Past 90 to 95% load, the number of kicks per insert rises rapidly and eventually an insert fails. A system must keep spare room (planning for 80 to 90% load) and handle a failed insert, either by rebuilding in the background, by using a stash, or by chaining a second filter. A Bloom filter, by contrast, degrades smoothly when overfilled.
The second problem is concurrency. An insert moves several keys, and a concurrent reader may look at both buckets while a key is in the middle of moving, and miss it. MemC3 and libcuckoo solve this by finding the whole eviction path first, then moving keys backwards from the free slot so that each key is always present in at least one of its homes, and by using version counters for readers. This works, but it complicates the code compared with a simple chained table.
Third, cuckoo hashing needs good hash functions. If the two functions are correlated, the random-graph argument fails and the table jams at low load. Fourth, cuckoo filters cannot be merged with a simple OR, as Bloom filters can, and they cannot be resized in place. They also have the duplicate limit of 2b copies. Finally, the memory access pattern is two random cache lines per lookup, which is the same as a blocked Bloom filter but worse than a linear-probing table, whose probes are contiguous. For read-only data, a static filter (xor or ribbon) is smaller and simpler.
practice: Use a cuckoo hash table when lookups dominate, latency must be predictable, and the key set changes at a moderate rate: packet and flow tables, session caches, in-memory dictionaries with a hot read path. Use buckets of 4 slots, run at 85 to 90% load for headroom, keep the keys' tags in the bucket so a lookup compares tags before touching full keys, and rebuild in the background when an insert fails. Prefer a tested library (libcuckoo, DPDK's rte_hash) to your own.
Use a cuckoo filter when you need a membership filter with deletion: tracking which items a cache or a downstream store currently holds, expiring items from a deduplication filter, or any filter where the set has real removals. Pick the fingerprint size from the error target: f = log₂(2b / ε), which is 12 bits for 0.2% with b = 4, 8 bits for 3%. Size the table for the maximum item count at 90% load. Redis Stack's cuckoo filter (CF.ADD, CF.DEL) is a ready-made option.
If your set is built once and never changes (SSTable files, immutable partitions), use a Bloom, xor or ribbon filter instead: they are smaller and need no eviction logic. If you only add items and want merge by OR, Bloom is the simplest. And never use a cuckoo filter's delete on an item that you are not sure was inserted. Track the live item count and the insert-failure count as metrics.
tradeoffs: Choose a cuckoo hash table over linear probing or chaining when you need the worst-case lookup bound (2 cache lines), you read much more than you write, and you can afford to leave 5 to 15% of slots empty. Choose linear probing with a good hash when lookups mostly hit one cache line anyway, inserts are frequent, and simplicity matters more than a hard bound; it also has better locality. You give up the guarantee with probing, and you give up insert speed and simplicity with cuckoo.
Choose a cuckoo filter over a Bloom filter when you must delete items and the false-positive target is below about 1%, when you want lookups that touch at most 2 cache lines, or when a lower error rate matters. Choose a Bloom filter when items are only added, when you want to merge filters with OR, when the number of items is uncertain and a graceful degradation is better than a hard failure, or when the target error is above a few percent. You give up deletion and exact planning with Bloom; you give up smooth degradation and mergeability with cuckoo.
Choose an xor or ribbon filter over a cuckoo filter when the set is known in advance and fixed (an immutable file or a dictionary built once): they use close to the theoretical minimum of 1.23 × log₂(1/ε) bits or less per item, versus about 1.05 × (log₂(1/ε) + 3) for the cuckoo filter, but they need a full rebuild to change. Choose the cuckoo filter when you need to change the set, even slowly.
Choose bucket size 4 and 2 choices as the default; use bucket size 8 when your fingerprint is small and cache lines are 64 bytes, as it gives the highest load factors; use 3 choices with 1 slot only when you need very high load and can afford three probes.
interview: Walk through inserting a key into a cuckoo hash table when both of its buckets are full. When does it fail and what do you do then? || Place the key in one of its buckets, evict a resident, and move the resident to its other bucket; repeat. If the chain exceeds a limit (about 500 kicks, or O(log n)), there is likely a cycle or too little free space. Use a small stash for the leftover key, or rebuild with new hash functions or a larger table.
How does a cuckoo filter find the alternate bucket of a fingerprint without the original key? || It stores i₂ = i₁ XOR hash(fp). XOR is its own inverse, so from any bucket index and the fingerprint you can compute the other bucket. This is partial-key cuckoo hashing. It also needs a power-of-two number of buckets.
Derive the false-positive rate of a cuckoo filter. || A negative query compares its fingerprint against up to 2b fingerprints in its two buckets, each matching with probability 1/2^f, so the rate is about 2b/2^f. With b = 4 and f = 12 it is about 0.2%.
Why can a cuckoo filter produce false negatives if used badly? || It stores fingerprints, which different keys share. If you delete a key that was never inserted but whose fingerprint and bucket pair match another key, you remove that other key's fingerprint, and that key is then reported missing. Deletion is safe only for items known to be inserted.
You see insert latency spikes in a cuckoo table at 94% load. What do you check and change? || The eviction chain length is growing as the table nears its threshold. I would look at the load factor and the kick-count distribution, check the hash quality, increase the table size to run at 85 to 90%, add a stash, and make rebuilds incremental or background so the p99 does not include a full rehash.
Why prefer a cuckoo filter over a Bloom filter in a deduplication cache, and what do you lose? || Items expire, and a Bloom filter cannot delete, so it would have to be rebuilt or use counting (4× memory). A cuckoo filter deletes in place and gives two-cache-line lookups. I lose mergeability, smooth degradation when over capacity, and the ability to grow without rebuilding.
quiz: How many memory locations does a cuckoo hash lookup check, and why? || Two (one bucket each for h₁ and h₂), because every key is stored in one of its two candidate buckets at all times.
Why does the eviction process usually end quickly when the load is low? || The evictions follow edges of a random graph whose vertices are buckets and whose edges are keys. At low density the components are small trees or have a single cycle, so a free slot is found after a few steps. Above the threshold large cyclic components appear and failures occur.
Why must a cuckoo filter store the alternate bucket as i₂ = i₁ XOR hash(fp) instead of hashing the key again? || The filter keeps only the fingerprint, not the key, so it cannot recompute a hash of the key during an eviction. XOR with a hash of the fingerprint lets either bucket be computed from the other.
A cuckoo filter has 4 slots per bucket and 10-bit fingerprints. What false-positive rate do you expect at full load? || About 2b/2^f = 8/1024 ≈ 0.8%.
You need a membership filter for 500 million items that never change after being built, and also one for a cache with constant insertions and removals. Which filter for each? || Static set: an xor or ribbon filter (smallest, built once). Cache with removals: a cuckoo filter, because it supports deletion and constant-time updates; a Bloom filter would need to be rebuilt or made into a larger counting filter.

value: A range query asks one question about a stretch of an array: what is the sum of positions 100 to 5,000, or the smallest value, or the largest, or the greatest common divisor? A plain scan answers it in time proportional to the length of the range. If you ask millions of such questions on an array of millions of values, scanning is far too slow, so range-query structures precompute partial answers and assemble each answer from a few of them. The three classic structures are the sparse table (O(1) query for minimum and maximum, static data), the segment tree (O(log n) for any combinable quantity, with updates) and the Fenwick tree, also called the binary indexed tree (O(log n) prefix sums with updates, in a few lines of code).
These structures appear all over data systems under other names. A time-series store that keeps per-minute values and also per-hour and per-day rollups is a segment tree whose levels have been given names. A database that stores min/max statistics for each block of rows lets a query skip blocks, which is the same idea, and the star-tree index in Apache Pinot pre-aggregates along dimensions in the same spirit. Inside the suffix array, the longest common prefix of any two suffixes is a range-minimum query over the LCP array, which is why the suffix array's toolkit depends on this concept. Rank and quantile estimators and arithmetic coders keep cumulative frequencies in a Fenwick tree.
Two lower layers shape these structures. Trees (L3) provide the shape: a segment tree is a balanced binary tree over the positions, each node owning an interval, and a Fenwick tree is an implicit tree hidden in the binary representation of array indexes. Dynamic programming (L3) provides the build: the sparse table is a table in which each entry for length 2^k is computed from two entries of length 2^(k−1), the standard way of reusing subproblem answers. In both cases the central idea is to store answers for intervals at several scales, so that any interval is a union of a few stored ones.
terms: Range query — a question whose answer is an aggregate (sum, minimum, maximum, gcd) over positions l to r of an array.
Associative operation — an operation where grouping does not matter, such as (a + b) + c = a + (b + c); needed so that partial answers can be combined in any grouping.
Idempotent operation — an operation where combining something with itself changes nothing, such as min(x, x) = x; this lets two overlapping ranges be combined safely.
Prefix sum — the sum of the first i elements; the difference of two prefix sums gives any range sum.
Sparse table — a table st[k][i] holding the aggregate of the 2^k elements that start at position i.
Segment tree — a binary tree in which each node stores the aggregate of an interval, and the children split the interval in half.
Fenwick tree (binary indexed tree) — an array in which entry i holds the sum of the last lowbit(i) elements ending at i, where lowbit(i) is the lowest set bit of i.
Lazy propagation — a segment tree technique that postpones range updates, keeping a note on a node and pushing it to the children only when needed.
Range minimum query (RMQ) — a query for the smallest value, or its position, in a range; the standard example behind the LCA and LCP problems.
history: Range queries grew out of computational geometry and string algorithms. Jon Bentley introduced the segment tree in the 1970s (a technical report, 1977, and Bentley and Wood 1980) to solve stabbing and rectangle problems, where the question is which intervals cover a point or which rectangles overlap. It quickly became the general tool for aggregate queries on arrays.
Peter Fenwick published "A new data structure for cumulative frequency tables" in Software: Practice and Experience in 1994. His problem was arithmetic coding in data compression, where the coder needs the cumulative frequency of symbols below a given one and must update counts after every symbol. A plain array gives O(1) update and O(n) query, or the reverse. Fenwick found a layout that gives O(log n) for both using only n entries and a few lines of bit manipulation. It has been a favourite of programming contests ever since because the code is so short.
The static case, where the array never changes, had a longer theory. Harel and Tarjan (1984) showed that the lowest common ancestor in a tree can be found in O(1) after O(n) preprocessing, and Bender and Farach-Colton (2000) gave a simpler version, "The LCA problem revisited", that works by reducing RMQ to LCA and back (through the Cartesian tree) and using blocks of size about (log n)/2 with a lookup table for all small cases. Fischer and Heun (2006, SIAM J. Computing 2011) reduced the space to 2n + o(n) bits, close to the information-theoretic minimum, and made RMQ practical for compressed text indexes.
The sparse table is older folklore: the idea of covering a range with two overlapping power-of-two blocks is used in many early algorithm texts. Its modern importance comes from the suffix-array literature, where Abouelhoda, Kurtz and Ohlebusch (2004) showed that the LCP array plus a few extra tables (including RMQ) can replace the suffix tree, and from string indexes for genomes, where RMQ structures run on billions of values.
how: Start with the problem split in two. If the array never changes, a prefix-sum array P with P[i] = a[1] + … + a[i] answers any range sum as P[r] − P[l−1] in O(1), after one O(n) pass. This works because addition has an inverse (subtraction). Minimum has no inverse: knowing min(a[1..r]) and min(a[1..l−1]) tells you nothing about min(a[l..r]). So minimum needs other structures. If the array changes, a prefix-sum array costs O(n) per update. The structures below cover these gaps.
The sparse table is for static minimum, maximum, gcd and similar. Define st[0][i] = a[i]. For k ≥ 1, define st[k][i] = min(st[k−1][i], st[k−1][i + 2^(k−1)]), the minimum of the 2^k elements starting at i. That recurrence is a dynamic-programming table over lengths that double: log₂ n levels, n entries each, so O(n log n) time and space to build. To answer min(l..r), let k = ⌊log₂(r − l + 1)⌋, the largest power of two that fits inside the range. Two blocks of length 2^k, one starting at l and one ending at r, cover the range together and may overlap. The answer is min(st[k][l], st[k][r − 2^k + 1]). Overlap is harmless for min because min(x, x) = x (idempotence). It would double count in a sum. The query is two lookups, O(1).
The segment tree works for any associative operation, including sums, products modulo p, min with position, and matrix products, and it supports updates. Build a complete binary tree with the n elements as leaves. Each internal node stores the aggregate of its two children, so the root holds the aggregate of the whole array. A point update changes a leaf and recomputes the aggregate of each ancestor on the way to the root: log₂ n + 1 nodes, O(log n). A range query on [l, r] starts at the root. If a node's interval lies fully inside [l, r], take its stored value. If it lies fully outside, skip it. If it partly overlaps, descend into both children. At most 2 nodes per level are partly overlapped, and at most 2 are taken per level, so the query visits O(log n) nodes and combines at most about 2 log₂ n stored values. A compact version stores the tree in an array of size 2n (leaves at positions n to 2n − 1, node i with children 2i and 2i + 1) and runs the query loop bottom-up with no recursion.
Lazy propagation extends the segment tree to range updates such as "add 7 to every element in [l, r]". A node fully covered by the update gets its stored aggregate changed in one step (a sum grows by 7 times the node's length) and keeps a tag saying "add 7 to all children, not yet applied". When a later operation must look inside the node, it pushes the tag down to the children first. Each update and query still touches O(log n) nodes.
The Fenwick tree is a trade: less general (it needs an invertible operation such as + or XOR for ranges) but smaller and faster. Index the array from 1. Entry tree[i] stores the sum of a block that ends at i and has length lowbit(i) = i & (−i), the value of the lowest set bit. So tree[6] (binary 110, lowbit 2) holds a[5] + a[6], and tree[8] (binary 1000, lowbit 8) holds a[1] + … + a[8]. To get the prefix sum up to i, add tree[i], then subtract lowbit from i, and repeat until i is 0. Each step clears one set bit, so there are at most log₂ n steps. To add a value d at position i, add d to tree[i], then add lowbit(i) to i and repeat while i ≤ n. Each step moves to the next block that contains i, again at most log₂ n steps. A range sum is prefix(r) − prefix(l − 1). The tree uses n numbers (a segment tree uses 2n) and the loops are tiny, which is why it is usually faster than a segment tree in practice.
For the static minimum with the least space, the O(n)/O(1) RMQ structures rely on a decomposition. Cut the array into blocks of size about log n. Build a sparse table over the block minimums (n / log n entries, so O(n) space). For queries inside one block, use a lookup table that is shared by blocks with the same shape (the "Four Russians" trick), since only a few block shapes exist. A query takes a bounded number of lookups: the tail of one block, the head of another, and a sparse-table query over the blocks between them.

example: Use the array of the diagram, a[1..8] = 5, 2, 6, 3, 8, 1, 4, 7 (positions from 1).
Fenwick sum. The array tree = [5, 7, 6, 16, 8, 9, 4, 36] for positions 1 to 8. Ask for the sum of positions 3 to 6. Compute prefix(6): i = 6 (binary 110), add tree[6] = 9; remove the lowest bit, i = 4 (100), add tree[4] = 16, total 25; remove the lowest bit, i = 0, stop. Compute prefix(2): i = 2, add tree[2] = 7; i = 0, stop. The range sum is 25 − 7 = 18. Check by hand: 6 + 3 + 8 + 1 = 18. Two steps and one step. Now add 5 to position 4. i = 4: tree[4] becomes 16 + 5 = 21; i = 4 + lowbit(4) = 8: tree[8] becomes 41; i = 8 + 8 = 16 is beyond n, stop. Only two entries changed, and prefix(6) is now 9 + 21 = 30, as it should be (25 + 5).
Segment tree sum. The tree has root 36, children 16 and 20, then 7, 9, 9, 11, then the leaves. Query the sum of positions 3 to 6. The root partly overlaps, so descend. Node [1..4] partly overlaps, node [5..8] partly overlaps. In node [1..4], child [1..2] is outside; child [3..4] is inside, so take its value 9 (6 + 3). In node [5..8], child [5..6] is inside, so take 9 (8 + 1); child [7..8] is outside. Total 9 + 9 = 18, using two stored values after visiting 7 nodes. The same tree, with min in place of sum, would give min(3, 1) = 1 for this range, where the stored minimums are 3 and 1.
Sparse table minimum. Query min over positions 2 to 7, which holds 2, 6, 3, 8, 1, 4. The length is 6, so k = ⌊log₂ 6⌋ = 2 and blocks have length 4. The first block starts at 2 and covers positions 2 to 5: st[2][2] = min(2, 6, 3, 8) = 2. The second block ends at 7 and starts at 7 − 4 + 1 = 4, covering 4 to 7: st[2][4] = min(3, 8, 1, 4) = 1. The answer is min(2, 1) = 1. Two lookups, and positions 4 and 5 were covered twice without harm. Doing this with a sum would count a[4] + a[5] twice, which is why sparse tables are for idempotent operations.
Scale. For n = 10⁶ elements of 4 bytes: a Fenwick tree needs 4 MB and about 20 steps per operation; a segment tree needs 8 MB and about 40 node visits per query; a sparse table for minimum needs 20 levels × 4 MB = 80 MB and answers in two memory reads. If you ask 10⁷ queries, plain scanning of ranges of average length 250,000 would take 2.5 × 10¹² steps, about 40 minutes even at a billion steps per second, while the structures take a few seconds at most.
intuition: Keep running totals at several zoom levels, so any stretch can be assembled from a few ready-made pieces.
The idea works because every interval of an array can be written as a union of a few intervals taken from a small, fixed family, as every whole number can be written as a sum of a few powers of two. The family needs only about n log n possible intervals (sparse table), about 2n intervals arranged as a tree (segment tree), or n intervals chosen by the binary digits of the index (Fenwick). Once you store the answer for each interval in the family, a query is a short combination, and an update touches only the intervals that contain the changed position, and these are also few for the same reason.
mistakes: People often think the sparse table works for sums. In fact it needs an idempotent operation (min, max, gcd, bitwise and/or), because its two blocks overlap. For sums you must use disjoint blocks, which needs O(log n) pieces, or a prefix-sum array when the data are static.
People often think a Fenwick tree can answer any aggregate, such as range minimum. In fact it supports prefix queries with an invertible operation. For the sum, range = prefix(r) − prefix(l−1). For min there is no inverse, so a Fenwick tree gives only prefix minimums (and only if values never increase). Use a segment tree for general aggregates.
People often think segment trees are only for competitive programming. In fact the same structure is behind block statistics in column stores, hierarchical rollups in time-series databases, interval trees in schedulers and memory allocators, and order-statistics queries in rate limiters and sliding-window percentile services.
People often think a segment tree needs 4n memory and recursion. In fact the 4n figure is a safe bound for a recursive tree with arbitrary n, and a bottom-up version needs 2n and no recursion. In production code the tree is stored as a flat array to keep memory access local.
friction: Each structure has a narrow sweet spot. The sparse table is static and costs O(n log n) memory, which is large for big arrays (80 MB for one million 4-byte values, about 1 GB for ten million). The Fenwick tree needs an invertible operation and supports no range updates without a second tree trick. The segment tree is general but has the highest constants: about twice the memory of a Fenwick tree, and a query visits dozens of nodes with poor cache locality, since parents and children are far apart in a big array.
Range updates and queries together are subtle. Lazy propagation works only if the update and the aggregate fit together (a "range add" works with sum and min; "range assign" needs a different tag; "range add" with "range max position" needs care). Composition of tags must be associative, and bugs in tag pushing are among the most common errors in this area.
Outside one machine, things get harder. On disk, a binary tree of height 20 means 20 random reads, so databases use wide trees (B-trees with aggregates in the nodes) or block-level statistics (a zone map: min and max per block of 100,000 rows). In a distributed system, the same idea becomes a hierarchy of rollups (hourly, daily) maintained by streaming jobs, with the usual problems of late data and backfills. Finally, the structures answer only the aggregates they were built for. A new aggregate, such as a median, needs another structure (a merge-sort tree, a wavelet tree, or a quantile sketch).
practice: Choose by the operations. Static array, sums: prefix sums. Static array, min or max: sparse table (or the O(n)/O(1) RMQ structure when memory matters). Sums or counts with point updates: Fenwick tree. Anything else with updates, or aggregates that are not invertible: segment tree. Write the Fenwick tree and the bottom-up segment tree from memory once, because they are short and appear in interviews.
Use them in data work wherever you keep both detail and rollups: store per-block min/max statistics so that scans skip blocks (Parquet and Delta do this), or maintain a Fenwick tree over bucket counts to compute running ranks and percentiles with updates. In ranking and rate-limiting services, a Fenwick tree over timestamps' buckets gives sliding-window counts in O(log n). For string indexes, build an RMQ structure over the LCP array to get fast longest-common-prefix queries.
Test against a brute-force scan on random arrays and random operations, including ranges of length 1, ranges equal to the whole array, and n that is not a power of two. Use 64-bit integers for sums; a sum of a million values near 2³¹ overflows 32 bits. Remember the index base: Fenwick trees are 1-indexed, because lowbit(0) = 0 would cause an infinite loop. For very large arrays, build the tree in blocks and keep the top levels in cache.
tradeoffs: Choose prefix sums when data is static and the query is a sum, count or other invertible aggregate: O(n) build, O(1) query, nothing simpler. Choose a Fenwick tree when the same sum queries must also accept updates and memory or speed matters; you give up non-invertible operations such as min, and range updates need a trick with two trees.
Choose a sparse table when the data is static and the operation is min, max or gcd with millions of queries: O(1) per query is hard to beat, and the build is only n log n. Choose the O(n)-space block-based RMQ (or Fischer–Heun) when n is large enough that n log n words are too much, such as 10⁸ or more, and you accept a more complex implementation.
Choose a segment tree when you need updates and a non-invertible or compound aggregate (min with position, sum and count together, matrix product), or range updates with lazy tags. You give up about 2 times the memory and some speed compared with the Fenwick tree for the same task, and you take on more code.
Choose square-root decomposition (blocks of size about √n with a precomputed aggregate each) when the operation is awkward for trees, or when you want something simple and cache-friendly for n up to a few million: queries and updates cost O(√n), about 1,000 steps for a million elements, in exchange for much simpler code.
Choose a wider-fanout structure (a B-tree with aggregates, or zone maps) when data is on disk or in columnar files, where the number of block reads, not the number of comparisons, dominates; you give up tight log₂ bounds for far fewer I/Os.
interview: Explain why a sparse table answers range minimum in O(1) and why it cannot answer range sum the same way. || Any range of length L is covered by two blocks of length 2^k, where k = ⌊log₂ L⌋, one at each end, and they may overlap. For min the overlap does not matter because min(x, x) = x. For sums the overlapped elements would be counted twice, so sums need disjoint pieces.
How does a Fenwick tree work and why are updates and queries both O(log n)? || tree[i] holds the sum of the last lowbit(i) elements ending at i. A prefix query repeatedly adds tree[i] and clears the lowest set bit of i; an update repeatedly adds to tree[i] and adds lowbit(i) to i. Each loop changes at most log₂ n bits, so both operations take O(log n).
You need the 95th percentile of a sliding window of request latencies, updated for every request. What structure helps? || Bucket the latency values into a fixed set of bins and keep a Fenwick tree (or segment tree) of counts per bin. Insertion and expiry are point updates, and the 95th percentile is found by descending the tree to the bin where the cumulative count reaches 95% of the total, all in O(log B) for B bins. The cost is bin-width error, so use finer bins or log-scale bins.
How do range minimum queries help a suffix array? || The LCP of two suffixes at ranks i < j equals the minimum of the LCP array between them. A range-minimum structure over the LCP array answers this in O(1) (sparse table or Fischer–Heun), which makes many suffix-tree algorithms possible on the array, such as finding longest common extensions.
Design range add and range sum on an array of 10⁸ values, with 10⁶ operations. || A segment tree with lazy propagation (or two Fenwick trees) gives O(log n) per operation, about 27 steps for 10⁸, for a total of roughly 3 × 10⁷ steps. Memory is the concern: 2 × 10⁸ 8-byte values is 1.6 GB for a segment tree, so I would consider coordinate compression if only 10⁶ positions are touched, or a dynamic (sparse) segment tree that creates nodes on demand.
When would you not use any of these structures? || When the data is small enough to scan (below a few thousand elements), when the query pattern is a few large aggregate scans that a columnar engine handles with vectorised reads, or when you need an aggregate they cannot decompose (exact median or distinct count), where a sketch or a sorted-structure is more appropriate.
quiz: What are the build and query costs of a sparse table, and for which operations is it valid? || Build O(n log n) time and space; query O(1). It is valid for idempotent operations such as min, max, gcd and bitwise and/or, because the two blocks of a query may overlap.
Why does a Fenwick prefix query take at most log₂ n steps? || Each step removes the lowest set bit of the index, so the number of steps equals the number of 1 bits in the index, at most ⌊log₂ n⌋ + 1.
For a = 5, 2, 6, 3, 8, 1, 4, 7, what are tree[4] and tree[6] in the Fenwick tree? || tree[4] covers positions 1 to 4, so 5 + 2 + 6 + 3 = 16. tree[6] covers positions 5 to 6, so 8 + 1 = 9.
You have 10⁶ values and 10⁷ min queries with no updates. Which structure and how much memory? || A sparse table: 20 levels × 10⁶ × 4 bytes ≈ 80 MB, with O(1) per query. It is cheaper in time than a segment tree and the memory is acceptable. For 10⁹ values you would switch to a blocked RMQ structure.
A dashboard needs sums over arbitrary date ranges while new data arrives every minute. Fenwick tree or segment tree? || A Fenwick tree if only sums are needed and updates are point additions, since it is smaller and faster. A segment tree if the dashboard also needs min, max or other non-invertible aggregates over the same ranges.

@@ pagerank
value: PageRank gives every node of a directed graph a score that says how important it is, using only the link structure. The rule is recursive: a node is important if important nodes link to it. A link from a page with a high score, and few other links, counts for much; a link from an obscure page that links to thousands of others counts for little. The scores sum to 1 and can be read as probabilities: PageRank of a page is the long-run chance that a person who clicks random links, and sometimes jumps to a random page, is on that page.
Larry Page and Sergey Brin used it in 1998 to order Google's search results. Before it, search engines ranked pages mainly by the words on the page, which was easy to game by repeating words. Links are harder to fake because they are made by other people. The same computation now ranks users in social networks, important entities in knowledge graphs, influential papers in citation networks, hot spots in fraud and identity graphs, and the datasets that most others depend on in a data catalog. A personalized version, which restarts the walk at chosen nodes, is a standard recommendation tool: "which nodes are close to this user".
Three lower layers make up the method. Markov chains (L4) define what the score means: PageRank is the stationary distribution of the random-surfer chain, the distribution that does not change after one more step. Linear algebra (L4) shows how to compute it: that distribution is the principal eigenvector of the chain's transition matrix, and power iteration (repeated matrix–vector multiplication) converges to it at a known geometric rate. Graph algorithms (L3) supply the object and the engineering: the web is a sparse directed graph with billions of nodes, stored as adjacency lists, and each iteration is one pass over the edges, which maps naturally to MapReduce, Spark or a vertex-centric system. The layer above is the search engine, which uses PageRank as one of many ranking signals.
terms: Link graph — a directed graph with a node for each page and an edge from page u to page v when u contains a link to v.
Out-degree L(u) — the number of links leaving u; a page splits its score equally among them.
Damping factor d — the probability that the surfer follows a link rather than jumping to a random page; 0.85 in the original paper.
Teleportation (random jump) — the step where the surfer jumps to a page chosen uniformly (or from a chosen set), with probability 1 − d.
Dangling node — a page with no outgoing links; the surfer would be stuck, so the model sends the surfer to a random page.
Stationary distribution — a probability vector π that is unchanged by one step of the chain: π = Gπ.
Power iteration — compute π by starting with any distribution and repeatedly applying the transition step until it stops changing.
Personalized PageRank — PageRank in which random jumps go back to a chosen node or set of nodes instead of to a uniform random node.
Link farm — a group of pages created to link to a target page and raise its score.
history: The idea that a link is a vote for importance is older than the web. In the 1950s Leo Katz proposed a status index for social networks that counts paths, and Pinski and Narin (1976) ranked scientific journals by how often important journals cite them, a weighting that is an eigenvector computation. The web made the idea valuable and the computation hard.
In 1996 Larry Page and Sergey Brin, PhD students at Stanford, started the BackRub project to study the link structure of the web, with Rajeev Motwani and Terry Winograd as collaborators. The ranking method was described in the technical report "The PageRank Citation Ranking: Bringing Order to the Web" (Page, Brin, Motwani and Winograd, written in 1998, Stanford InfoLab) and in Brin and Page's paper "The anatomy of a large-scale hypertextual web search engine" (WWW7, 1998). The second paper gives the formula with d usually set to 0.85, describes the random surfer, and describes a working prototype; the PageRank of 26 million pages could be computed in a few hours on a medium-size workstation. Google was incorporated in 1998.
Around the same time, Jon Kleinberg published HITS (Hyperlink-Induced Topic Search; SODA 1998, JACM 1999), which computes two scores per page, hub and authority, but only on a small set of pages relevant to a query. Later work addressed the weaknesses of the basic method. Haveliwala (2002) made it topic-sensitive. Gyöngyi, Garcia-Molina and Pedersen (VLDB 2004) proposed TrustRank, which starts the walk from a hand-checked set of good pages to push down spam. Langville and Meyer's book "Google's PageRank and Beyond" (2006) collected the mathematics.
The algorithm also shaped big-data systems. Iterating over a graph was a main example in Google's Pregel paper (Malewicz and others, SIGMOD 2010), whose vertex-centric model became GraphX and Giraph, and in the Spark RDD paper (2012), where PageRank ran 2.4 times faster than Hadoop on 30 nodes with in-memory caching, and 7.4 times faster with controlled partitioning of the graph. Today PageRank is one signal among very many in web search, but it is the standard first algorithm on any graph engine.
how: Take a graph with N nodes. Each node u has out-degree L(u). The random surfer starts at some node. At each step, with probability d (0.85) the surfer follows an outgoing link of the current node, chosen uniformly; with probability 1 − d (0.15) the surfer jumps to a node chosen uniformly from all N nodes. The PageRank of node v is the long-run fraction of steps the surfer spends at v. Writing PR(v) for this value gives the formula PR(v) = (1 − d)/N + d × Σ over all u that link to v of PR(u)/L(u). Each node passes an equal share of its score to each node it links to, and then every node receives a small base score from the random jump.
The same thing in matrix form. Let M be the N × N matrix with M[v][u] = 1/L(u) if u links to v and 0 otherwise. Each column of M sums to 1, so M is a Markov transition matrix. The random-jump rule gives the Google matrix G = d × M + (1 − d)/N × E, where E is the all-ones matrix. G is also a transition matrix, now with every entry positive. The Markov chain theory (L4) then applies: a chain with all-positive transitions is irreducible (any node reaches any other) and aperiodic, so by the Perron–Frobenius theorem it has exactly one stationary distribution π with Gπ = π, and the chain converges to it from any starting distribution. In linear-algebra terms (L4), π is the eigenvector of G for the eigenvalue 1, which is the largest eigenvalue. The teleportation term is therefore not a detail; without it, the chain might have many stationary distributions or none that is useful, as when part of the graph is a trap that the surfer cannot leave.
Dangling nodes need a rule. A page with no out-links would leak score: its column of M is all zeros, and the total score would shrink at every step. The standard rule is that the surfer on a dangling node jumps to a uniformly random page. In computation this means summing the score of all dangling nodes and distributing it evenly to all nodes at each iteration.
Compute π by power iteration. Start with PR₀(v) = 1/N for all v. Repeat: PR_{t+1} = G × PR_t, computed without forming G. For each node u, send PR_t(u)/L(u) along each out-edge; each node v sums the incoming shares, multiplies by d, and adds (1 − d)/N plus its share of dangling mass. Stop when the change between iterations (the L1 distance) is below a tolerance. A theorem on the second-largest eigenvalue of G (it is at most d in magnitude) says that the error shrinks by about a factor d = 0.85 each iteration. To reduce the error to 10⁻⁸, we need ln(10⁻⁸)/ln(0.85) ≈ 113 iterations; for 10⁻⁴, about 57. This is why the damping factor is a trade-off: a larger d follows the real links more but converges more slowly.
One iteration is one pass over all edges, O(N + E) time and O(N) memory for two score vectors. In a distributed engine such as Spark, the graph is stored as (node, list of out-neighbours), joined with the current ranks, flat-mapped to (target, share) pairs, and summed by key. Partitioning the graph once by node and caching it avoids reshuffling the adjacency lists in each iteration; only the small rank vector moves. In a vertex-centric system (Pregel, GraphX), each superstep has every node send its share to its neighbours and update its own score, and the system handles partitioning and fault tolerance.
Personalized PageRank changes only the jump: instead of jumping to a uniformly random node, the surfer jumps back to a given node s (or a set S) with probability 1 − d. The resulting vector gives each node's proximity to s. It can be computed by the same power iteration, by Monte Carlo (run many short random walks that stop with probability 1 − d at each step and count visits, which needs no matrix at all), or by local push algorithms (Andersen, Chung and Lang, 2006) that touch only the neighbourhood of s. Twitter's who-to-follow service and Pinterest's Pixie recommender use random-walk methods of this family to find accounts and pins close to a user in a large graph.

Scale. The 1998 prototype covered tens of millions of pages. A graph with 10⁹ nodes and 10¹¹ edges stores about 800 GB as 8-byte edge records, and each iteration moves about 10¹¹ shares through a shuffle. At 60 iterations (error about 6 × 10⁻⁵) that is 6 × 10¹² shares. This is why implementations cache and co-partition the adjacency lists, and why approximate and incremental methods are used when the graph changes daily.
intuition: Importance flows through links like water through pipes, and the pages where the water collects are the important ones.
The recursive definition looks circular, but a random walk makes it concrete: if many people wander along links, they will spend more time on pages that many paths lead to, and especially on pages reached from pages where they spend a lot of time. The teleportation step is a safety valve that stops the water from draining into a dead end, and it is also what makes the answer unique and easy to compute. The score is just the equilibrium of a flow, which is what a stationary distribution and an eigenvector of eigenvalue 1 both describe.
mistakes: People often think PageRank is a score for a page's content quality or relevance to a query. In fact it is a query-independent measure of link popularity. A search engine combines it with text relevance and many other signals; a page can have a high PageRank and be irrelevant to your search.
People often think PageRank counts the number of links to a page. In fact each link carries a share of its source's score divided by the source's out-degree. One link from a very important page with few links can be worth more than thousands of links from obscure pages with many links.
People often think the damping factor 0.85 is a law of nature. In fact it is a parameter that trades fidelity against convergence speed and robustness. Smaller d converges faster, makes the scores flatter and weakens the effect of link farms. Larger d follows the structure more but needs more iterations. 0.85 was a practical choice.
People often think PageRank needs the whole matrix in memory. In fact the matrix is never formed: each iteration only walks the edge list, and the vectors have one number per node. This is why it scales to billions of nodes, and why it is a standard example for distributed systems.
friction: The main weakness is manipulation. Because a score is derived from links, people create links for the purpose: link farms, purchased links, comment spam. Search engines respond with spam detection, TrustRank-style seeds, attributes such as nofollow that tell the crawler not to count a link, and many more signals, so that PageRank alone is no longer a defence. On other graphs, the same issue appears as Sybil attacks: fake accounts that follow a target.
It also has structural limits. The score is global and static, so it does not know the user's interest or the time; it favours old, well-linked pages over new ones. The uniform random jump is a modelling choice that has no basis in real behaviour. In graphs where every node has similar degree, or where degree explains most of the variance, PageRank adds little beyond counting in-links. For small, sparse or fragmented graphs, the scores near the floor (1 − d)/N are mostly noise.
The compute cost is real. A full run on a web-scale graph needs tens of iterations over every edge, and the graph changes continuously, so systems use incremental updates, approximate Monte Carlo estimates, or recompute on a schedule. Personalized scores for every user are impossible to store (N vectors of N numbers), so they are computed on demand with local push or random-walk methods, or restricted to a small set of seeds. Finally, the convergence argument assumes the stochastic model: when the graph has huge numbers of dangling nodes or nearly disconnected communities, convergence is still fast, but the results are dominated by how those cases are handled, and it is easy to implement it slightly wrong and still see plausible numbers.
practice: Use PageRank on graphs where an edge means endorsement, citation, dependency or flow: link graphs, follower graphs, citation graphs, call and dependency graphs, transaction graphs. In a data platform, run it on the dataset-dependency graph from your lineage catalog to find the tables that the most important downstream assets depend on, or on a device or account graph to find hubs worth a manual check.
Use a tested implementation: GraphX or GraphFrames pageRank in Spark, or NetworkX for small graphs. Set a fixed number of iterations (for example 20) for batch jobs where exactness is not needed, or a tolerance (10⁻⁶ on the L1 difference) for convergence. Verify that the scores sum to about 1; if they do not, you are losing mass at dangling nodes. Remove duplicate edges and self-links first, because they change the shares. Keep the graph partitioned and cached across iterations; reshuffling the edge list each iteration is the usual performance bug.
For recommendations, use personalized PageRank with Monte Carlo walks or local push, which answer a query in milliseconds without touching the whole graph. Choose d between 0.5 and 0.85 depending on how far from the seed you want results to reach. Always compare the ranking with a trivial baseline (in-degree) on a labelled set of important nodes before deciding that the extra computation helps.
tradeoffs: Choose PageRank when you need a global, query-independent importance score on a large graph, and edges can be read as endorsements; it is simple, has one parameter, and parallelises well. Choose in-degree (or weighted degree) when you need a cheap baseline, because it is one pass and often correlates well; you give up the effect of who is linking. Choose eigenvector-style alternatives (HITS, SALSA) when the task has hubs and authorities roles and you can restrict to a query-specific subgraph.
Choose personalized PageRank when the question is "what is close to this node", as in recommendations and entity search; choose global PageRank when a single ranking for all users is acceptable. Personalized runs cost a local computation per query (milliseconds to tens of milliseconds with push or Monte Carlo), or a precomputed index of fingerprints with a larger memory budget.
Choose a larger damping factor (0.85 to 0.9) when the graph structure is trustworthy and you want it to dominate, accepting slower convergence (113 iterations to 10⁻⁸ at 0.85, about 175 at 0.9). Choose a smaller one (0.5 to 0.7) for faster convergence and more resistance to spam and noisy edges, accepting flatter scores and less use of long paths.
Choose exact power iteration for graphs up to a few billion edges where the job runs on a schedule; choose Monte Carlo or incremental methods when the graph changes continuously and you can accept approximate scores. For learned ranking (neural graph models), PageRank is still a good feature, but it is not a replacement for a model trained on outcomes.

Without teleportation the chain could have traps or several stationary distributions, and dangling nodes would leak mass.
How fast does power iteration converge and what controls it? || The error shrinks by about a factor equal to the second eigenvalue of G, which is at most d in magnitude. At d = 0.85, 10⁻⁸ accuracy needs about 113 iterations. A larger d gives slower convergence; the graph size matters only for the cost of each iteration.

The trap is reshuffling the graph in each iteration or recomputing long lineage; cache and partition the links once and checkpoint the ranks every few iterations.
How do you handle dangling nodes and rank sinks? || Sum the score of dangling nodes each iteration and redistribute it uniformly (or to the personalization set), and keep the teleport term so that a group of nodes with no way out cannot absorb all the score. Verify that the scores still sum to 1 after every iteration.
Someone builds a link farm. How does it affect PageRank and what can you do? || The farm creates many pages that link to a target, so the target collects shares and the farm pages collect only the base score. value: A great deal of computing work is a set of tasks with dependencies: task C needs the output of A, task E needs C and D. DAG scheduling is the method of running such a set. You model the tasks as a directed acyclic graph (DAG): nodes are tasks, an edge from A to C means "C cannot start until A has finished", and "acyclic" means there is no loop of dependencies, so a valid order exists. The scheduler runs every task as soon as all of its inputs are finished and a worker is free. Tasks that do not depend on each other run in parallel; tasks that do depend on each other run in order.
This is the execution model of almost every data and build system. A Spark job is a DAG of stages. An Airflow or Databricks Jobs pipeline is a DAG of tasks. dbt sorts models by their ref() calls. Terraform and other infrastructure tools create resources in dependency order. Build systems such as Make, Ninja and Bazel compile only what is needed, in parallel. ML pipelines chain data preparation, training and evaluation. Medallion layers (bronze, silver, gold) are a pipeline DAG at the table level. The scheduler gives these systems three things: correctness (nothing runs before its inputs exist), speed (independent work overlaps), and a unit of recovery (a failed task can be retried without rerunning the whole pipeline).
Two lower layers carry the idea. Graph algorithms (L3) provide topological sorting and cycle detection: Kahn's algorithm finds a valid order, or proves there is none, in time proportional to nodes plus edges, and longest-path computation on the DAG gives the critical path. Work stealing (L2) provides the way to run the ready tasks on many cores: instead of one central queue, each worker keeps its own queue of ready tasks and idle workers steal from busy ones, which is how fork-join runtimes (Java ForkJoin, Rayon, Tokio) keep all cores busy with little locking. Above this concept sit batch processing, workflow orchestration, declarative pipelines, infrastructure as code and MLOps.
terms: DAG — a directed graph with no cycles; the structure that makes a valid execution order possible.
Topological order — a list of all tasks in which every task appears after all the tasks it depends on; a DAG usually has many.
Ready set — the tasks whose dependencies have all finished and that have not started yet.
Work (T₁) — the total time of all tasks if one worker ran them one after another.
Span or critical path (T∞) — the length of the longest chain of dependent tasks; the time with unlimited workers.
Makespan — the time from the start of the first task to the end of the last, for a given number of workers.
List scheduling — a greedy method: whenever a worker is free, give it a ready task chosen by some priority.
Bottom level — for a task, the length of the longest path from it to the end of the graph; a common priority.
Stage — in Spark, a group of tasks that can run as one pipelined step without moving data between machines; stages are separated by shuffles.
history: The ordering problem is old. Arthur Kahn (CACM 1962) published "Topological sorting of large networks" to order the activities of PERT project plans, a method first built for the Polaris missile programme, and the critical path method (Kelley and Walker, 1959) was developed at DuPont for the same purpose: plan a project whose activities depend on each other and find which chain decides the finish date. Tarjan (1976) gave the depth-first-search version of topological sort that most textbooks teach.
Scheduling on several processors came next. Ronald Graham (1966), in "Bounds for certain multiprocessing anomalies", showed that simple list scheduling always finishes within a factor 2 − 1/p of the optimal time on p identical processors, and that it can behave strangely: adding a processor or shortening a task can make the schedule longer. Coffman and Graham (1972) found an optimal algorithm for the special case of two processors and unit-length tasks. Ullman (1975) proved that the general problem (arbitrary DAG, several processors) is NP-hard, which explains why practical schedulers use heuristics. Brent (1974) gave the bound T_p ≤ T₁/p + T∞ for greedy execution, which is still the way people reason about parallel speedup.
Parallel runtimes made the theory executable. Blumofe and Leiserson (JACM 1999), "Scheduling multithreaded computations by work stealing", proved that randomized work stealing runs a computation in expected time T₁/P + O(T∞) on P processors, and the Cilk system turned it into practice. Topcuoglu, Hariri and Wu (2002) published HEFT, a list-scheduling heuristic for machines of different speeds, which became the standard baseline for scientific workflows.
In data engineering, Make (Stuart Feldman, 1976–79) made the dependency graph an everyday tool. MapReduce (2004) had two levels of tasks, map and reduce. Dryad (2007) and then Spark (2012) allowed arbitrary DAGs of stages, with the DAG scheduler deciding stage boundaries and data locality. Apache Airflow (open sourced by Airbnb in 2015) made DAGs of tasks the common language for pipelines, followed by Luigi, Dagster, Prefect, dbt (2016), and the job DAGs in Databricks.
how: Start with the model. Each node v has a duration w(v) (known or estimated) and each edge u → v means u must finish before v starts. Total work T₁ is the sum of all durations. The critical path T∞ is the weight of the longest path in the DAG. Two bounds follow. With p workers, no schedule can finish faster than T₁/p (all workers busy) or faster than T∞ (the chain must run in order). So T_p ≥ max(T₁/p, T∞). This explains why adding workers helps only until T₁/p drops to T∞. After that, the pipeline is limited by its longest chain.
Step 1 is to get a valid order and detect cycles. Kahn's algorithm keeps an in-degree count for every node (the number of unfinished dependencies). It puts all nodes with count 0 into a queue. It repeatedly removes a node, outputs it, and decreases the count of each of its successors; any successor whose count becomes 0 is added to the queue. If the queue empties before all nodes are output, the remaining nodes lie on or behind a cycle, which is an error to report. The cost is O(V + E). A scheduler does the same thing as a live process: the "output" step is "run the task and wait for it to finish", and the counters are what trigger the next tasks.
Step 2 is the dispatch loop. Maintain the ready set. Whenever a worker is free and the ready set is not empty, choose a task and start it. When a task finishes, decrement the counters of its successors and add the newly ready ones to the ready set. This is list scheduling. It is greedy: a worker is never idle while a ready task exists. Graham's argument shows why this gives the factor-2 guarantee, and Brent's bound T_p ≤ T₁/p + T∞ follows from counting time steps in which all workers are busy (at most T₁/p of them) and steps in which some worker is idle, during which the critical path must progress (at most T∞).
Step 3 is the choice of which ready task to run. Without a priority, any order is allowed, and it matters. A common good choice is the bottom level (longest path from the task to the end): tasks on the critical path go first, because delaying them delays everything. Real schedulers add more factors: data locality (run a task where its input already is), resource needs (a task needs 8 GB or a GPU), fairness among jobs or users, and limits such as pools or concurrency caps so that one pipeline does not starve others or overload a database.
Step 4 is placing the work on workers. In a cluster, a central scheduler hands tasks to executors. In a multi-core runtime, work stealing replaces the central queue: each worker pushes new ready tasks on its own double-ended queue and pops from the same end, which keeps related work on one core and in its cache. An idle worker steals from the other end of a random victim's queue, so it takes the oldest, usually largest, piece of work. Contention is rare because stealing happens only when a worker is idle.
Step 5 is failure handling. When a task fails, the scheduler retries it up to a limit. Tasks must be idempotent, so a retry or a duplicate run produces the same result. If the output of a finished task is lost (a node dies and the shuffle files go with it), the scheduler marks that task not done again and so the successors wait; lineage-based recovery (the next concept in this group) is this rule applied to data partitions. Spark limits retries per task (spark.task.maxFailures, default 4) and retries a whole stage when shuffle data is missing. Airflow keeps each task's state (queued, running, success, failed, up_for_retry, skipped) and uses trigger rules to let a task run when some parents failed, for example a cleanup step.
In Spark, the DAG scheduler turns the lineage of a result into stages. It walks back from the final dataset. Narrow dependencies (a map or filter, where each output partition depends on one input partition) are pipelined into one stage. A wide dependency (a shuffle, such as reduceByKey or join) ends a stage. A stage is submitted when all its parent stages are complete, and each stage becomes one task per partition. The task scheduler then places tasks by locality: first on the process that holds the data, then the same node, then the same rack, waiting up to spark.locality.wait (3 seconds by default) before giving up on a better location. Since Spark 3.0, adaptive query execution looks at real shuffle statistics when a stage finishes and can change the plan for the remaining stages.

Kahn's order. In-degrees: A 0, B 0, C 1, D 2, E 2, F 1. Queue: A, B. Take A: C becomes 0 and D becomes 1. Take B: D becomes 0. Queue: C, D. Take C: E becomes 1. Take D: E becomes 0 and F becomes 0. Queue: E, F. All six tasks come out in the order A, B, C, D, E, F. Another valid order is B, A, D, C, F, E. All orders respect the edges.
Run with two workers, choosing ready tasks by bottom level (A 10, B 7, C 7, D 5, E 3, F 1). At time 0 the ready set is A and B. Worker 1 takes A (0 to 3); worker 2 takes B (0 to 2). At time 2, worker 2 is free, but D needs A, which finishes at 3, so worker 2 waits one unit. At time 3, C and D are ready; worker 1 takes C (3 to 7) because its bottom level is higher, and worker 2 takes D (3 to 5). At time 5, F is ready; worker 2 takes F (5 to 6). At time 7, C is done and D is done, so E is ready; worker 1 runs E (7 to 10). The makespan is 10, equal to the critical path, so this schedule is optimal. With one worker it takes 15. With three workers it still takes 10, because the chain A, C, E cannot be shortened. Speedup 1.5 on two workers, and no more on three.
Why priority matters. Take a second graph with 2 workers: tasks P (1), Q (1) and R (4) are ready at time 0, and S (6) needs R. If the scheduler starts P and Q first, R starts at time 1, ends at 5, and S ends at 11. If it starts R first (bottom level 10 against 1), R runs 0 to 4 on one worker, S runs 4 to 10, and P and Q run on the other worker during 0 to 2. The makespan is 10. A bad order cost 10%. In the worst case, list scheduling is within 2 − 1/p of optimal, so with p = 2 the guarantee is a factor 1.5.
In a real Spark job the same pattern appears. A job reads two tables, joins them, aggregates, and writes. The two scans are independent stages that run at the same time (if there are enough executor cores); the join stage waits for both; the aggregate stage waits for the join. If the job has 200 shuffle partitions after the join and the executors have 100 cores, the stage runs in two waves of tasks, and one slow task in the second wave decides the end of the stage.
intuition: Cooking a dinner with several cooks: dishes that do not need each other cook at once, but the sauce cannot start before the onions are chopped, and the longest chain of waiting steps decides when dinner is ready.
The design is natural because dependencies are the only real constraint. If two tasks have no path between them, nothing prevents them from running together, so a scheduler needs no other knowledge to find parallelism. The graph tells it what may run; the workers tell it what can run now; the only choice left is which of the ready tasks to start first, and "the one with the longest chain behind it" is a sensible answer. The rest is bookkeeping with counters.
mistakes: People often think more workers always give a proportional speedup. In fact the critical path is a hard floor. In the example, three workers are no faster than two. When a pipeline is slow, first compute the longest chain; adding cluster size changes nothing if the chain is the problem.
People often think a topological order is the order in which things run. In fact a topological order is only one valid sequence. A DAG has many, and the scheduler runs independent tasks at the same time. Two runs may start the same tasks in different orders, so tasks must not depend on an accidental order.
People often think the scheduler can find the best possible schedule. In fact the optimal schedule for a general DAG on several workers is NP-hard, and real durations are only estimates. Schedulers use greedy rules with priorities, and the best known simple rule is within a factor 2 − 1/p of optimal.
People often think a cycle can be tolerated if retries are enabled. In fact a cycle means each task in it waits for another, so none can start. Orchestrators reject cyclic DAGs at load time. Loops such as "repeat until converged" must be written as a dynamic expansion of the graph or a task that contains the loop.
friction: Estimating durations is the first problem: priority rules and critical path analysis need task times, but real durations vary with data volume, skew, cluster load and cold caches. Schedulers use history or defaults, and a wrong estimate gives a wrong critical path. A single skewed partition can make a stage as slow as its slowest task, whatever the plan said.
Granularity is the second problem. If tasks are tiny (a few milliseconds), the cost of scheduling and starting them dominates; an orchestrator that takes a second or two to launch each task makes a one-second task pointless, and a Spark stage with millions of small tasks spends its time in scheduler bookkeeping and in small-file reads. If tasks are too large, parallelism is lost and a failure wastes a lot of work. A usual target is tasks that run seconds to minutes.
Third, real clusters add constraints that the pure model leaves out: memory limits, slots and pools, data locality, rate limits on shared databases, and fairness between teams. These turn the problem into a multi-resource packing problem, where greedy rules can leave resources idle (for example a task that needs 64 GB waits while small tasks keep taking the memory). Fourth, state and side effects: a retry that reruns a task with an external side effect (sending an email, appending to a table without a key) causes duplicates, so tasks need idempotent writes. Finally, dynamic graphs (the number of tasks depends on data) and cross-DAG dependencies are hard for static schedulers, and debugging a stuck pipeline means finding which upstream task is holding the ready set empty.
practice: Draw the DAG before you tune the cluster. Compute the work and the critical path, in time units from your own run history. If critical path ≥ work/p, more workers will not help; shorten the chain: split a long task, remove a needless dependency, or move work off the critical path. If work/p is larger, add parallelism or workers.
Keep tasks idempotent (write to a path or partition keyed by the run, use MERGE or overwrite rather than append), set retries with backoff, and set task timeouts. Limit concurrency with pools or queues for shared resources such as a source database. Give tasks resource hints (memory, cores) that match reality, and put heavy tasks on the critical path first by giving them priority. Keep tasks to seconds-minutes at least, and avoid thousands of tiny tasks per run.
In Spark, look at the stage graph in the UI: stages that can run in parallel, the shuffle boundaries, and tasks that take far longer than the median. Reduce the number of shuffles, enable adaptive query execution (default in recent versions) to coalesce small partitions and handle skewed joins, and size the number of partitions to a small multiple of the total core count. In Airflow or Databricks Jobs, set dependencies explicitly rather than relying on timing, use sensors or assets (data-aware scheduling) instead of fixed clock offsets, and alert on the duration of the critical path as well as on failures.
For declarative systems (dbt, declarative pipelines, Terraform), the DAG is derived from references, so keep references accurate and avoid hidden dependencies through side effects such as a table read by raw SQL that the tool does not see. Review the DAG regularly: a pipeline tends to gain dependencies that nobody removes, and the critical path grows.
tradeoffs: Choose static, ahead-of-time DAGs (Airflow-style definitions, dbt projects, Terraform plans) when the work is known in advance and you want visibility, auditability and a plan you can review before running. Choose dynamic task graphs (Dask, Ray, Spark's lazy evaluation, Airflow dynamic task mapping) when the number of tasks depends on data. You give up the reviewable plan with dynamic graphs and some flexibility with static ones.
Choose a central scheduler when the tasks are coarse (seconds or more), you need global policies such as fairness, quotas and priorities, and a modest rate of scheduling decisions is enough. Choose decentralized work stealing when tasks are fine-grained and run in one process or one machine (very high rates of tiny tasks), and you want little coordination. You give up global policy control with stealing, and you give up speed with a central scheduler.
Choose critical-path priority when completion time of a single job matters most. Choose fair-share or first-in-first-out when many jobs share the cluster and predictability for each user matters more than the best makespan for one. You trade a little total throughput for fairness.
Choose fewer, larger tasks when scheduling overhead or small-file costs dominate and failures are rare. Choose more, smaller tasks when you need better load balance, finer-grained retries, and good use of stragglers' idle neighbours. A common target is tasks of 10 seconds to a few minutes, partitions of 100 to 200 MB in Spark, and at least 2 to 4 tasks per core per stage.
Choose to retry a failed task when the failure is transient and the task is idempotent; choose to fail the pipeline fast when the failure is a data or logic error, because retries only delay the alert and waste resources.
interview: How does a DAG scheduler decide which task to run next, and what is the guarantee of the simple greedy approach? || It keeps a ready set (tasks whose in-degree counter is zero) and gives a free worker one of them by priority, often longest remaining path first. Any greedy list scheduler finishes within 2 − 1/p of the optimal time on p workers, and satisfies T_p ≤ T₁/p + T∞. Finding the true optimum is NP-hard.
Your nightly pipeline got slower after doubling the cluster. What do you check? || The critical path and the biggest wait. If the longest chain of dependent tasks or one skewed task dominates, extra workers do nothing. Also check shuffle volume, scheduler delay between tasks, concurrency limits or pools, and whether tasks are queued behind a shared-resource cap.
How does Spark turn a query into stages and tasks? || It walks back from the result through the lineage. Narrow dependencies are pipelined into one stage; every wide dependency (shuffle) ends a stage. A stage is submitted when its parent stages are complete, and has one task per partition. The task scheduler places tasks by locality and retries failed ones.
Explain Kahn's algorithm and how it detects a cycle. || Count unfinished dependencies for every node, queue the nodes with count zero, repeatedly pop a node and decrement its successors, queueing those that reach zero. If fewer than all nodes are popped, the rest are on or behind a cycle. It runs in O(V + E).
How does work stealing differ from a central ready queue, and when is it better? || Each worker owns a deque of ready tasks and works from one end; an idle worker steals from the opposite end of a random victim. There is no shared lock on the fast path, and locality is better. It wins for fine-grained tasks inside a process or machine; a central scheduler is better when you need global policies like fairness and quotas.
What makes a task safe to retry in a DAG, and what breaks it? || Idempotence: the task writes results to a location keyed by the logical run (overwrite a partition, upsert by key) and has no non-repeatable side effects. Appends without keys, sending emails, or calling an external API that charges money break it. Make the side effect conditional on a recorded state or move it into a dedicated final step.
quiz: What are work and span in a DAG, and what lower bound do they give on the running time with p workers? || Work T₁ is the total of all task durations; span T∞ is the longest chain of dependent tasks. Any schedule needs at least max(T₁/p, T∞).
Why does the scheduler reduce an in-degree counter when a task finishes? || The counter is the number of unfinished dependencies of a successor. When it reaches zero, the successor is ready and can be put in the ready set.
In the six-task example, why do three workers not beat two? || The critical path A, C, E takes 3 + 4 + 3 = 10 units, and two workers already reach 10. More workers cannot shorten the chain.
A job has total work 600 core-minutes and a critical path of 40 minutes. What is the least time on 10 cores, and on 100 cores? || On 10 cores: max(600/10, 40) = 60 minutes. On 100 cores: max(6, 40) = 40 minutes. Past about 15 cores the critical path is the limit.
Would you prefer many tiny tasks or fewer larger ones in an Airflow pipeline that runs every 5 minutes, and why? || Fewer, larger tasks. Each Airflow task has scheduling and start-up overhead of seconds, and each adds state to track, so tiny tasks spend more time on overhead than on work. The trade-off is slower retries and less parallelism within a task.

value: Lineage-based recovery is a way to make a large distributed computation survive machine failures without copying the data. The system records how each dataset was produced: from which input datasets, with which transformation. If a machine dies and a piece of a dataset is lost, the system does not need a backup of that piece. It runs the recorded recipe again on the pieces of the parent datasets, and gets the same result. The idea was made famous by Spark's Resilient Distributed Datasets (RDDs), described in a 2012 paper.
Why this matters is a question of cost. The classic way to protect data is replication: keep two or three copies. For data in memory, that triples the memory needed, and every write must cross the network. MapReduce protected every intermediate result by writing it to a replicated distributed file system, which makes a multi-step job slow, since each step reads from and writes to disk. Lineage protects the data at almost no cost during normal operation, because the log is small: a few kilobytes describing transformations, not the gigabytes of data. The price is paid only when a failure happens, by recomputing the lost pieces. Since failures are rare compared with normal work, this is a good bargain for batch analytics, iterative algorithms and interactive queries.
Two lower layers make it possible. Graph algorithms (L3) supply the structure: datasets and their dependencies form a DAG, and recovery is a traversal that walks backwards from the lost partition to the nearest data that still exists (cached data, saved shuffle files, or the source files). Functional programming (L3) supplies the discipline: transformations such as map, filter and reduceByKey are deterministic, side-effect-free functions of their input, so running one again on the same input gives the same output. Without this, a recomputed partition could differ from the lost one, and downstream results would be inconsistent. The layer above is batch processing, which uses it to avoid replicating intermediate results.
terms: Lineage — the recorded chain of parent datasets and transformations from which a dataset can be rebuilt.
Partition — one slice of a distributed dataset, processed by one task; the unit of loss and of recomputation.
RDD (Resilient Distributed Dataset) — Spark's immutable, partitioned collection that knows its parents and the function that builds each partition from them.
Narrow dependency — each output partition depends on at most one partition of each parent (map, filter); recomputation is local.
Wide dependency — an output partition depends on many parent partitions (groupBy, join, reduceByKey), usually through a shuffle.
Shuffle — the step that redistributes records across machines by key; map tasks write shuffle files to local disk and reduce tasks fetch them.
Checkpoint — saving a dataset to reliable storage so its lineage can be cut off; recovery then starts from the saved copy.
Deterministic — always producing the same output for the same input; required so that recomputation reproduces the lost data.
Idempotent write — an output operation that gives the same final state if it runs twice, which makes task retry safe.
history: The problem was set by a mismatch between hardware and software. By 2009 clusters had large memories, and iterative algorithms (machine-learning training, PageRank, graph processing) and interactive analysis kept reusing the same data. Hadoop MapReduce, however, wrote the output of every job to HDFS, replicated three times, and the next job read it back. Matei Zaharia and colleagues at UC Berkeley's AMPLab built Spark in 2009 to keep working sets in memory. The hard question was fault tolerance: how to make an in-memory dataset reliable without replicating it.
Earlier systems for distributed shared memory allowed fine-grained updates to any memory cell, which forced them to replicate state or log every update, costs that kept them from large-scale use. Zaharia's insight, in the paper "Resilient Distributed Datasets: A Fault-Tolerant Abstraction for In-Memory Cluster Computing" (NSDI 2012, a best-paper winner), was to restrict the interface: datasets are immutable, and are built by coarse-grained transformations that apply the same function to many records. Then logging the transformation is enough, because one small log entry covers a whole dataset. The idea of lineage had earlier roots in databases, for example Cui and Widom's lineage tracing for data warehouse views (2000), and in the re-execution model of MapReduce and Dryad, but RDDs made it the main fault-tolerance mechanism for in-memory data.
The paper reported results that explain the interest. Iterative applications ran up to about 20 times faster than on Hadoop. In a k-means run on 100 GB with 75 nodes, an iteration normally took about 58 seconds; when a machine was killed at the start of iteration 6, that iteration took about 80 seconds while the lost partitions were rebuilt from lineage, and later iterations returned to 58 seconds. The lineage graphs in those experiments were each smaller than 10 KB.
Spark became an Apache top-level project in 2014. The model was extended to streaming (discretized streams, SOSP 2013, which recover in parallel from lineage), to DataFrames and SQL (the same lineage is derived from the query plan), and to other systems: Ray reconstructs lost objects by re-running the tasks that created them. Meanwhile stream processors such as Flink took a different route, taking periodic consistent snapshots of operator state, because continuous streams make long recompute chains impractical.
how: Every RDD, and every DataFrame, which compiles down to RDDs, is described by five things (the interface in the paper): its list of partitions; a list of dependencies on parent RDDs; a function to compute a partition from the parents' partitions; optionally a partitioner (how keys map to partitions); and optionally preferred locations (where each partition is best computed, for example the nodes that hold the HDFS block). The program is lazy. Calling map or filter does not run anything; it only creates a new RDD object that points to its parent. Only an action (count, collect, write) triggers computation. The chain of objects is the lineage graph, and it is also the plan.
Dependencies come in two kinds, and the difference decides the cost of recovery. In a narrow dependency, each partition of the child depends on a small, fixed set of parent partitions (usually one). Map and filter are narrow. Narrow transformations can be pipelined: a task reads a block, applies map, filter and the next map in one pass, without storing the intermediate results. Recovering a lost partition means rerunning one task on one input partition. In a wide dependency, each child partition may depend on all parent partitions, which is the case for groupByKey, reduceByKey and non-co-partitioned joins. These need a shuffle: each map task writes its output split by target partition into local files, and each reduce task fetches its piece from every map task.
The scheduler uses this graph for recovery. When a task or executor is lost, the DAG scheduler finds which partitions are missing. For each, it asks: is it cached, or are its shuffle files still on some surviving machine, or is it a source partition? If yes, use it. If not, recompute it, which needs its parents' partitions, and so on backwards, until all required inputs are available. Because narrow chains are pipelined and only the missing partitions are recomputed, the cost of recovering from losing one machine's worth of data is a small fraction of the whole job, and recovery tasks run in parallel across the remaining machines. This is faster than the idea of "restart from the last checkpoint" for the whole job.
Shuffle files change the picture. Map outputs are written to local disk (not memory), so they survive an executor process crash if the node's disks and a shuffle service survive, and then the reduce side only needs to re-fetch. If the node is lost with its disks, the reduce tasks that try to fetch fail with a fetch failure; the scheduler marks those map outputs as lost, reruns just the map tasks that produced them (each reads its source partition), and resubmits the stage. This is why shuffle files act as natural, free partial checkpoints at stage boundaries.
Determinism is the hidden requirement. If a lost partition is recomputed and gives different content (a map that calls random() without a seed, reads the current time, or fetches from a changing external service), then a downstream partition computed from the old version and another computed from the new version are inconsistent. For wide dependencies, even the order of records can differ after a shuffle. Spark treats stages whose output order may change as indeterminate, and when it must retry one it rolls back and reruns dependent stages, but the safest rule is to make transformations deterministic (seed random generators from the partition index, sort before position-dependent operations).
Checkpointing cuts the chain. Long lineage is a problem in two ways: recomputing from the beginning can be very slow, and the lineage graph itself grows (iterative jobs with hundreds of iterations can overflow the driver's stack when serialising the graph). rdd.checkpoint() writes the data to reliable storage (HDFS or cloud storage) and replaces the lineage with a pointer to the saved files. The paper observed that checkpointing pays off for datasets with long lineage and wide dependencies (like the rank vector in PageRank), and does little good for data with narrow dependencies on stable input. Caching, by contrast, is only an optimisation: a cached partition that is evicted or lost is simply recomputed through lineage.
DataFrames and SQL use the same machinery. A query is first a logical plan, then an optimised physical plan, and finally a DAG of RDD stages, so lost partitions are rebuilt by the same lineage walk. Adaptive query execution may change the plan between stages, but the saved shuffle outputs of finished stages still serve as recovery points.

  lineage of "counts" (4 input blocks, 2 reducers)

  block 0 -> map -> filter -> pair --+
  block 1 -> map -> filter -> pair --+--shuffle--> reduce 0

  block 3 -> map -> filter -> pair --+

  Failure cases

  2. a node dies holding shuffle output of map tasks 1 and 3
       -> reduce tasks hit FetchFailed

example: Take a word-count style job on a 512 MB log file stored in 4 blocks of 128 MB. The program is: lines = textFile (4 partitions); parsed = lines.map(parse); errors = parsed.filter(isError).cache(); pairs = errors.map(toPair); counts = pairs.reduceByKey(sum) with 2 output partitions; result = counts.map(format).
The scheduler builds two stages. Stage 1 has 4 tasks, one per block, each reading its block, parsing, filtering, and mapping to pairs in one pipelined pass (all narrow), and writing shuffle files with a partial sum per key, split into 2 pieces. Stage 2 has 2 tasks; reduce task r fetches piece r from all 4 map outputs and sums.
Case 1: the executor that holds the cached partition 2 of "errors" crashes. The lineage of that partition is: block 2 → parse → filter. Only block 2 is read again, by one task on another executor, and the rest of the cache is untouched. If a block takes 12 seconds to process, recovery costs about 12 seconds of one task, not the 12 seconds × 4 of the whole stage. Case 2: stage 1 has finished, and a node that holds the shuffle files of map tasks 1 and 3 is lost. Reduce task 0 tries to fetch and fails. The scheduler marks the outputs of map tasks 1 and 3 as lost. It reruns those two tasks (each reads its HDFS block again), and then reruns the reduce tasks. Map tasks 0 and 2 are not rerun, because their files are intact. Total extra work: 2 map tasks, 2 reduce tasks. Case 3: in stage 2, the output of reduce task 0 is lost after it was cached. Its lineage says: fetch piece 0 of every map output and sum. If those shuffle files survive, only one reduce task reruns. If not, the maps rerun first.
Compare with replication. Suppose each intermediate dataset is 100 GB, as in a multi-stage job. Writing it with 3 replicas moves 200 GB across the network (two extra copies) and writes 300 GB to disk; at about 100 MB/s of disk throughput per node on 50 nodes, writing 300 GB takes about 60 seconds of pure disk time per intermediate dataset. With a 5-stage job that is about 5 minutes spent on safety. Lineage spends 0 extra on the normal path and pays about 12 seconds when a machine fails.
Now see the limit. Suppose an iterative job runs 100 iterations, each a join and a reduceByKey (two shuffles), and nothing is checkpointed. The lineage of iteration 100 is 200 stages deep. If an executor is lost at iteration 100 and shuffle outputs from earlier iterations are gone, the scheduler may have to rerun many earlier stages, since every wide dependency can need data from every partition of the previous stage. With a checkpoint every 10 iterations, the replay is at most 10 iterations. If one iteration takes 30 seconds, the worst case drops from about 50 minutes to about 5 minutes.
intuition: Keep the recipe, not every intermediate dish; if a dish is dropped, cook it again from the ingredients that are still on the shelf.
The method is natural when work is cheap to repeat and the machine failures are rare. In a cluster of 1,000 machines something fails every few days, but most of the time nothing fails, so paying a constant tax (replication) for the rare case is wasteful. A recipe is much smaller than the dish. And because each step is a pure function of its inputs, rerunning the recipe cannot give a different dish. Immutability closes the loop: nobody can have changed the ingredients after the dish was cooked.
mistakes: People often think lineage means the data lineage shown in a data catalog (which table feeds which dashboard). In fact those are different things with the same name. Catalog lineage is metadata for governance and impact analysis. Lineage-based recovery is an execution mechanism inside the engine that rebuilds partitions. They both are graphs of dependencies, but only the second is used to recompute data.
People often think Spark keeps copies of everything, so failures cost nothing. In fact nothing is replicated unless you ask for it (a storage level with replication) or write results to reliable storage. After a failure the lost work is recomputed, which takes time, and a failure late in a long job can be expensive.
People often think any code can be recomputed safely. In fact the guarantee requires deterministic transformations. A random sample without a seed, a call to the current time, a read of a table that changes, or an external API call can produce different data the second time, leading to duplicates, gaps, or rows that no longer match across partitions.
People often think lineage replaces output durability. In fact the final results still must be written to durable storage with idempotent commits. Lineage protects intermediate data inside a job; once the job ends and its executors are gone, nothing can recompute from the lineage.
friction: Recovery cost grows with lineage depth and with the width of dependencies. A lost partition after a long chain of shuffles may need a large part of the whole job to run again, and the recovery time is hard to predict. For this reason long iterative jobs and streaming jobs checkpoint regularly, which brings back the cost of writing to reliable storage, a decision to tune: more checkpoints mean slower normal runs and faster recoveries.
The requirement of determinism is the weakest point in practice. User code with hidden non-determinism, such as unordered iteration over a hash map, floating-point reductions whose order depends on shuffle timing, UDFs that call external services, or row IDs generated after a shuffle, can silently give different results after recovery. Spark has needed special handling of these cases (for example rolling back and rerunning a stage when its output order may differ), and users must still be careful.
It also does not cover everything. The driver, which holds the lineage graph, is a single point of failure; losing it loses the job unless an external mechanism restarts the application and recomputes from scratch or from checkpoints. Data from non-replayable sources (a message queue with short retention, an API) cannot be re-read, so streaming sources need replayable logs. Cloud object stores solve some of the durability problems (source data is safe), but spot-instance loss, shuffle data on local disks, and large clusters with frequent decommissioning make shuffle-file loss common. Systems add shuffle services, remote shuffle storage and migration of shuffle blocks during decommissioning to reduce recomputation. Finally, lineage tracking costs memory in the driver for very large plans with many thousands of partitions.
practice: Treat lineage as the safety net and design for it. Make transformations deterministic: seed random functions from a fixed seed plus the partition index, avoid calls to external services in map functions (read them as a dataset instead), and sort before operations that depend on record order. Make output writes idempotent (overwrite a partition directory or use transactional table formats such as Delta), so that task retries and speculative copies do not duplicate rows.
Cache only what is reused, and prefer the storage level that spills to disk (MEMORY_AND_DISK) so that eviction does not force long recomputation. For iterative algorithms, checkpoint every 10 to 20 iterations, or after any step that follows a heavy shuffle, and set a reliable checkpoint directory. Look at the lineage with toDebugString (RDDs) or explain (DataFrames) when a job recovers slowly: a long chain with many shuffle boundaries is a warning. Keep an eye on stage retries and fetch failures in the Spark UI; frequent fetch failures mean unstable nodes or spot instance loss, and are worth fixing with a shuffle service, push-based shuffle, or more stable nodes for the shuffle-heavy stages.
For long pipelines, break the job at natural points by writing a table (a Delta or Parquet file) and reading it back. That is an explicit checkpoint that also helps debugging and reruns by stage in your orchestrator. In streaming, use a durable checkpoint location for offsets and state, and a source that can replay.
tradeoffs: Choose lineage-based recovery when jobs are batch or micro-batch, transformations are deterministic and coarse-grained, failures are infrequent, and recomputation of a lost partition takes seconds to a few minutes. Choose replication or checkpointing to reliable storage when recomputation is very expensive (long chains, heavy shuffles, slow sources), when the data comes from sources that cannot be re-read, or when recovery time must be tightly bounded. You give up zero-cost safety with replication-based approaches, and you give up predictable recovery time with lineage.
Choose to checkpoint often (every few iterations) when failures are frequent (spot instances, large clusters) or each iteration is heavy; choose to checkpoint rarely when stages are quick to recompute and storage writes are slow. A rule of thumb is to checkpoint so that expected recomputation after a failure is a small multiple of the checkpoint cost.
Choose cache with replication (a storage level with two copies) for a small, hot, expensive dataset that many queries use and that is costly to rebuild, such as a dimension table used by an interactive service. You pay double the memory for a faster recovery.
Choose snapshot-based recovery (Flink-style distributed snapshots) for long-running continuous streams with large mutable state, where there is no bounded batch to recompute, and accept the overhead of periodic snapshots. Choose lineage-based recovery for batch and for micro-batch streaming, where each batch is a bounded deterministic computation.
Choose external shuffle services or remote shuffle storage when nodes are short-lived (autoscaling, spot capacity), since shuffle files on the local disk of a vanished node are the main source of recomputation; you pay extra network hops and cost for the service.

What is the difference between narrow and wide dependencies for recovery? || Narrow: a child partition depends on one parent partition, so only one task reruns on one input. Flink takes consistent snapshots with checkpoint barriers and restores state from the last one, then replays from the source offset. Micro-batch engines can use lineage because each batch is a bounded deterministic computation.
How does lineage-based recovery interact with speculative execution and task retries? || All three rely on tasks being re-runnable. A retried or speculative copy may write output twice, so the commit protocol must pick one result and writes must be idempotent. Deterministic tasks make the duplicates identical, so whichever finishes first is correct.

@@ speculative
value: In a large parallel job, the job finishes only when its slowest task finishes. If 999 of 1,000 tasks take 10 seconds and one takes 100 seconds, the stage takes 100 seconds. The slow task is called a straggler. It may sit on a machine with a failing disk, share its machine with a noisy neighbour, run in a congested network corner, or suffer from a transient fault. Speculative execution deals with it by starting a second copy of the slow task on another machine and using the result of whichever copy finishes first. The other copy is killed.
The same idea for online services is called a hedged request. A client sends a request to one replica. If no answer comes within a short delay, usually the 95th percentile of normal latency, it sends the same request to a second replica and uses the first answer. Because only the slowest few percent of requests are duplicated, the extra load is small, while the slow tail shrinks greatly. Google's "The Tail at Scale" paper reports a benchmark that reads 1,000 keys spread over 100 BigTable servers: with a hedge after 10 ms the 99.9th-percentile latency fell from 1,800 ms to 74 ms, at the cost of only about 2% more requests.
The need grows with scale, because of simple probability from queueing theory (L4): when one user request or one job fans out to many parts, the chance that at least one part is slow approaches 1. If each of 100 servers has a 1% chance of being slow, then 63% of requests that touch all 100 will be slow. The tail of a single machine becomes the typical case of the whole system. Queueing theory also supplies the warning label: duplicates are extra load, and extra load makes queues longer, so the technique works only while the system has spare capacity. It is used inside MapReduce, Hadoop, Spark, Cassandra, HDFS clients, gRPC and most large serving systems, and it is the batch-processing layer's main defence against tail latency.
terms: Straggler — a task that runs much longer than its peers in the same stage.
Tail latency — the slowest few percent of requests, measured at high percentiles such as p99 or p99.9.
Percentile (pXX) — the value below which XX% of observations fall; p99 = 100 ms means 99% of requests finished in 100 ms or less.
Speculative task (backup task) — a duplicate copy of a running task, started on a different machine.
Hedged request — a duplicate of a service request, sent after a short delay if the first has not answered.
Tied request — a duplicate sent at the same time to two servers, each told about the other, so the first one to start work cancels the other.
Median multiplier — the rule "speculate when a task has run longer than k times the median duration of finished tasks"; Spark's default k is 3.
Idempotent — safe to execute twice; required because both copies of a task may run to the end.
Output commit — the step that makes one copy's output the official one and discards the other's.
history: The idea appears in the MapReduce paper by Jeffrey Dean and Sanjay Ghemawat (OSDI 2004), section 3.6, "Backup Tasks". They describe stragglers as machines that take an unusually long time to finish one of the last few map or reduce tasks, and give three causes seen at Google: a bad disk with correctable errors that cut read speed from 30 MB/s to 1 MB/s; competition from other tasks scheduled on the same machine; and a machine-initialisation bug that disabled the processor caches and slowed computation by more than a factor of 100. Their fix is that when the operation is close to completion, the master schedules backup executions of the remaining in-progress tasks. The first copy to complete marks the task done. In the sort benchmark, the job took 891 seconds with backup tasks and 1,283 seconds without them, 44% longer. The paper says the technique typically increases total resource use by only a few percent.
Hadoop copied the mechanism, and its early rule (compare a task's progress score with the average and speculate if it is more than 0.2 behind) turned out to work poorly where machines differ. Zaharia, Konwinski, Joseph, Katz and Stoica addressed this in "Improving MapReduce Performance in Heterogeneous Environments" (OSDI 2008) with the LATE scheduler: estimate each task's remaining time from its progress rate, speculate on the tasks expected to finish last, put copies on fast nodes, and cap the number of copies. They showed up to 2× better response times on a 200-VM cluster. Ananthanarayanan and colleagues described Mantri (OSDI 2010), which identifies the cause of outliers (skew, network, a bad machine) and acts accordingly instead of copying blindly. The Dolly system (NSDI 2013) cloned small jobs outright, because for short jobs waiting to detect a straggler costs more than a duplicate.
Dean and Barroso's "The Tail at Scale" (CACM, February 2013) generalised the lesson to interactive services and named the techniques: hedged requests, tied requests, micro-partitions, and selective replication. They showed that tied requests cut the 99.9th percentile of BigTable reads by about 38% on an idle cluster (98 ms to 61 ms) and 32% with a competing batch job, with less than 1% extra disk use.
Speculation exists in Spark from version 0.6, off by default (spark.speculation=false). Hadoop MapReduce turns it on by default. Databases and clients adopted the hedged form: Cassandra's speculative_retry table option, HDFS hedged reads, and hedging policies in gRPC.
how: Speculative execution has four parts: detect, copy, race and clean up. In batch systems the scheduler runs a monitor on a short timer (Spark checks every 100 ms by default, spark.speculation.interval). For each running stage, it waits until a minimum fraction of the tasks have finished (spark.speculation.quantile, 0.9 in the current documentation, so 90% of the stage's tasks must be complete). It computes the median duration of the finished tasks. Any running task whose runtime exceeds the multiplier times the median (spark.speculation.multiplier, 3 in the current documentation; earlier releases used 1.5 and a quantile of 0.75) and also exceeds a minimum runtime (minTaskRuntime, 100 ms) is a candidate. The scheduler launches a copy of the task on a different executor, preferably on a different host, since copying onto the same bad machine would not help.
The race. Both copies run. The first to finish reports its result; the scheduler marks the task as successful and kills the other copy. The correctness question is the output. In Spark the output commit coordinator lets only one attempt of a task commit its output to the final location, and an attempt that loses is discarded. In MapReduce, the master records the completion of the first one. For this to be safe, task output must go to a temporary location per attempt and be committed atomically, and any side effect of the task (writing to a database, calling an external service) must be idempotent or guarded.
A key design question is what to copy and when. A rule based on the median is simple but crude. LATE estimates the remaining time as (1 − progress)/rate and copies the task with the largest estimate, but only if its rate is below a slow threshold, and keeps the number of concurrent copies under a cap (about 10% of slots) so that speculation cannot flood the cluster. Mantri avoids duplicating tasks that are slow because of data skew (the copy would also be slow) and instead tries to restart tasks that have much more to read than average, or to place them better.
For services the mechanism is similar but runs on the client or a proxy. The client sends a request to replica A. It starts a timer set to a high percentile of the normal latency (say p95 = 20 ms). If A has not answered when the timer fires, the client sends the same request to replica B and takes the first answer. With a delay at p95, about 5% of requests get a duplicate. If the slow response of A and the slow response of B were independent, with p = 1% of requests slow each, the chance that both are slow is p² = 0.01%. A tied request sends to both at once, but includes a note to the second server, so that when one begins to execute the request it tells the other to cancel; the duplicate then wastes almost nothing unless both start at about the same moment.
Queueing theory (L4) sets the limits. Let ρ be the utilisation of a server (the fraction of time busy). In the simplest queue model (M/M/1) the mean waiting time is proportional to ρ/(1 − ρ). If hedging adds 5% to the load, the wait grows by a small factor at ρ = 0.7 (from 2.33 to 2.77 service times, +19%), but at ρ = 0.95 it jumps from 19 to 399 service times, a factor of 21. At high utilisation the extra duplicates create the slowness they were meant to cure, and the system can enter a state where more requests time out, causing more hedges, causing more load. Little's law (L = λW, concurrency = arrival rate × latency) shows the same effect: more requests in flight at the same latency means more concurrency, which the servers must hold. Safeguards: cap hedges to a small percentage of traffic (a retry or hedge budget of 5 to 10%), hedge only idempotent requests, hedge only when the system is healthy, and cancel the loser quickly.
Speculation fails to help in some cases. If the task is slow because its input is larger (data skew), the copy has the same input and the same duration, and it only wastes resources. If the slowness is in a shared resource (the database every task reads from), copies add to the load. If the stage has few tasks, there is no meaningful median. These are why a good implementation delays speculation until most of the stage is done, requires a minimum runtime, and caps the number of copies.

example: A Spark stage has 1,000 tasks, and a normal task takes 10 seconds. One executor has a failing disk, so the one task placed there will need 100 seconds. First, why this is common: if each task has a 1% chance of landing on a slow machine, the chance that at least one of 1,000 tasks is slow is 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰⁰, which is 99.996%. So almost every large stage has a straggler, and its runtime is not 10 seconds but close to the slowest task.
Without speculation the stage takes 100 seconds. The cluster's 1,000 cores are mostly idle from second 10 to second 100: the useful work is about 10,000 core-seconds, but 999 cores sit idle for 90 seconds. With speculation turned on and the defaults of the current Spark documentation (quantile 0.9, multiplier 3, check every 100 ms): the monitor waits until 900 tasks have finished, which happens at about second 10. It computes the median, 10 seconds. A task is a candidate when its runtime exceeds 3 × 10 = 30 seconds, so at about second 30 the monitor flags the slow task and starts a copy on a healthy executor. The copy takes 10 seconds and finishes at second 40. The original is killed. The stage ends at about second 40 instead of 100, a saving of 60 seconds, and the extra work is one 10-second task, 10 core-seconds out of about 10,000, which is 0.1% extra. If you lower the multiplier to 1.5, the copy starts at second 15 and the stage ends at second 25, but a normal task that happens to take 16 seconds also gets copied, so more work is duplicated.
Now a data-skew case. One task has 10 times the data and needs 100 seconds on any machine. The monitor flags it at second 30 and starts a copy. The copy needs 100 seconds too, so it would end at second 130, and the original ends at second 100. The original wins; the copy ran for 70 seconds before being killed. Net effect: no gain and 70 core-seconds wasted. The remedy is to fix the skew (repartition, salting, adaptive skew-join handling), not to speculate.
For a service, take a service that fans out to 100 backends and has a p99 of 1 second per backend. The fraction of user requests with at least one slow backend response is 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ = 63.4%, the number quoted in "The Tail at Scale". Hedge each backend call after 100 ms (about its p95). Now about 5% of backend calls get a duplicate, so the load rises by about 5%. If the two copies are independent, the chance that both exceed 1 s is 0.01 × 0.01 = 0.0001, so the chance of a slow user request falls from 63% to about 1 − (1 − 0.0001)¹⁰⁰ ≈ 1%. In practice, correlation (the same slow disk, the same overloaded rack) leaves the result somewhat worse, which is why the real benchmark reached 74 ms rather than the ideal.
intuition: If one cashier is stuck on a hard customer, open a second lane for that customer and take whichever lane finishes first.
The idea is natural because the cost of waiting is paid by everybody (the whole job waits for the last task), while the cost of a duplicate is paid by a few tasks. When many things are done in parallel, the maximum matters, and the maximum is driven by rare bad events. Rare events are independent across machines, so a second try on another machine is very unlikely to hit the same bad event: the chance of two failures is the square of the chance of one. Trading a small, steady waste for protection from a rare, large delay is exactly what insurance does.
mistakes: People often think speculative execution makes a job faster in general. In fact it removes only delays caused by the machine or its environment. If a task is slow because of its own work (data skew, a huge group, a heavy join key), the copy is just as slow and the cluster does extra work for nothing. The tool for that case is better partitioning.
People often think it is free because the loser is killed. In fact both copies consume CPU, memory, network and disk until the loser stops, and tasks that read from a shared system multiply the load on it. Typical overhead is a few percent in healthy clusters but can be large if the threshold is too low.
People often think duplicate tasks are always safe. In fact only idempotent tasks with an atomic output commit are safe. A task that appends to a table, sends a message or calls a payment API will do it twice. Spark and Hadoop protect their own file outputs with commit protocols, but side effects written in your own code are your responsibility.
People often think hedging is a replacement for capacity. In fact it needs spare capacity. When utilisation is high, the extra requests lengthen all queues, and hedging can turn a slow period into an outage. Production systems cap hedges to a small share of traffic and switch them off under overload.
friction: Detection is the first hard part. Progress is hard to measure: tasks have no reliable progress counter, durations vary naturally with data size, and the median of a heterogeneous stage is a poor yardstick. Waiting for 90% of the stage to finish before speculating (the Spark default) is safe but late; a lower quantile or multiplier finds stragglers sooner and wastes more. There is no setting that is right for all jobs, which is why Spark leaves speculation off by default.
Correctness is the second. Output commit must be exactly-once for files, which was a source of bugs on object stores where rename is a copy, and where two attempts could both write final files. Speculation interacts with non-idempotent sinks, with streaming exactly-once guarantees, and with tasks that hold locks or leases. Modern committers (such as the S3A committers and table formats with atomic commits) reduce the risk but you must use them.
Third, resource effects: copies compete for slots and for shared services, and a cluster at high utilisation may have no free slot to run a copy, so speculation helps least when it is most needed. In a multi-tenant cluster, one job's speculation takes capacity away from others. Fourth, root causes are hidden: speculation masks a bad node or a skewed key, and teams then stop looking. A job whose runtime depends on speculation is a warning sign, and the speculative-task counts in the UI should be monitored. Finally, for services, hedging amplifies load during incidents unless budgeted, and requests that are not idempotent (writes) cannot be hedged without extra protocol.
practice: Turn speculation on for batch jobs where tasks are idempotent and stragglers are a recurring problem (large shuffles on shared or cloud clusters with spot instances), and leave it off where tasks have external side effects or sinks without atomic commit. Start from the defaults (multiplier 3, quantile 0.9 in the current Spark documentation), look at the number of speculative tasks launched and killed in the Spark UI, and tune only if stages show a long tail. Set a minimum task runtime so that short tasks are not copied.
Fix the cause when you can. If the same node keeps producing stragglers, exclude or replace it (Spark's excludeOnFailure / node exclusion features). If one key is heavy, apply adaptive query execution's skew-join handling or salt the key. If slowness comes from small files or an overloaded source, speculation will not help.
For online services, hedge only idempotent reads, delay the hedge to about the p95 of the normal latency (measured, not guessed), cap hedges at 5 to 10% of requests with a budget, propagate cancellation so the loser stops, and disable hedging when the error rate or queue depth rises. Prefer tied requests when your servers support cancellation. In Cassandra, the speculative_retry option (default is a percentile, such as 99p) sets the hedge for reads; in gRPC, a hedging policy sets maxAttempts and hedgingDelay; HDFS hedged reads are enabled with a threshold in milliseconds. Always measure p99 and p99.9 before and after, and the extra load, because a hedging change that helps one percentile can hurt another.
tradeoffs: Choose speculative execution on for batch stages that run longer than a minute and are made of many idempotent tasks on a cluster with some unreliable or shared machines. Choose it off for tasks with side effects, for tiny jobs where detection costs more than it saves, or for clusters at 90% or higher utilisation where there are no free slots and each copy takes a slot from useful work. You give up resources (typically a few percent) when on, and you accept occasional long tails when off.
Choose a conservative threshold (multiplier 3, quantile 0.9) when waste matters, the cluster is shared, and stragglers are rare. Choose an aggressive one (multiplier 1.5, quantile 0.75) for short stages with strict deadlines on an underused cluster. The cost of aggressive settings is wasted work on tasks that are merely a little slow.
Choose proactive cloning for very short jobs (interactive queries, a few seconds) because waiting to detect a straggler would take longer than running a second copy outright; choose reactive speculation for long jobs. Choose hedged requests (delay-based) for the best load profile in services that can tolerate ~5% extra load; choose tied requests when the servers can cancel cheaply and the queueing delay dominates; choose full duplication of every request only when the latency requirement is absolute and the capacity is available.
Choose fixing skew and unhealthy nodes over speculating when the cause is known and persistent. Speculation treats a symptom and suits unpredictable causes; repartitioning or excluding a bad host removes the cause.
interview: How does Spark decide to launch a speculative task, and what are the main settings? || A monitor runs every spark.speculation.interval (100 ms). Once a quantile of the stage's tasks have finished (0.9 in current docs), it takes the median duration of finished tasks and flags running tasks that have exceeded the multiplier (3 in current docs) times that median and a minimum runtime. A copy starts on another executor; the first to finish wins and the other is killed.
When does speculation not help, and what do you do instead? || When the task is slow because of its input (data skew) or because of a shared bottleneck, since a copy is equally slow. Instead I fix the partitioning (salting, skew-join handling, more partitions), remove a bad node, or relieve the shared resource. Speculation is for slowness that belongs to the machine, not to the work.
Why can hedged requests make an overloaded system worse? || A hedge is extra load. When utilisation is high, queueing delay grows steeply (about ρ/(1 − ρ) in a simple model), so extra requests make everything slower, which causes more timeouts and more hedges, a feedback loop. Prevent it with a hedge budget (5 to 10%), hedging only when healthy, and cancellation of the loser.
What has to be true of a task for it to be safely speculated? || It must be idempotent or its output must be committed atomically with only one attempt winning. Outputs go to an attempt-specific temporary location and a commit coordinator publishes one. External side effects (database writes, messages, payments) need idempotency keys or must be moved after the commit.
Derive why fan-out makes tail latency matter more. || If each of n parallel parts is slow with probability p, the request is slow when any part is slow: 1 − (1 − p)ⁿ. With p = 1% and n = 100 that is 63%, and with n = 1,000 it is more than 99.99%. The slow case of one machine becomes the common case of the whole.
How would you decide the delay of a hedged request? || Measure the latency distribution of the call under normal load and set the delay near its p95 (or p99 if capacity is tight). That puts the extra load at about 5% (or 1%) and still catches the slow tail. Re-measure regularly, since the distribution shifts, and cap the total hedge rate.

@@ erasurecoding
value: Erasure coding protects stored data against the loss of disks, servers or racks, using far less extra space than copying. Instead of keeping three full copies of every object, the system cuts the object into k data fragments, computes m extra parity fragments from them, and spreads all k + m fragments over different failure domains (disks, servers, racks). Any k of the k + m fragments are enough to rebuild the object. A common choice is k = 10 and m = 4: the system stores 14 fragments, uses 1.4 times the original size, and survives the loss of any 4 fragments. Triple replication uses 3 times the size and survives the loss of only 2 copies.
At the scale of a data lake or an object store, this difference is the difference in cost. For 100 PB of user data, three copies need 300 PB of raw disk, about 15,000 drives of 20 TB. A 10+4 code needs 140 PB, about 7,000 drives. Object stores such as Amazon S3, Azure Blob Storage and Google Cloud Storage use erasure coding inside (Microsoft has published its scheme, a locally repairable code; the others do not publish their parameters). HDFS 3 supports it (default policy RS-6-3, 1.5 times the size), and Ceph and MinIO use it. It is the reason cloud object storage can be both cheap and highly durable, which is the foundation of the data lake.
Two lower layers explain how it works. Coding theory (L4) supplies the code: Reed–Solomon codes are maximum-distance-separable (MDS), which means they achieve the best possible trade-off: with m parity fragments you can lose any m fragments, and no code can do better for that overhead. Finite fields (L4) supply the arithmetic: bytes are treated as elements of the field GF(2⁸), in which addition is XOR and multiplication follows a table, so that every linear equation can be solved without rounding errors or overflow. Together they turn "rebuild lost data from the rest" into solving a small system of linear equations over bytes. The layer above is the data lake, which relies on this to keep files durable at low cost.
terms: Fragment (shard, chunk) — one of the pieces an object is cut into, or one parity piece; each is stored on a different device.
k and m — k is the number of data fragments and m the number of parity fragments; n = k + m is the total.
Storage overhead — the ratio (k + m)/k of raw to logical size; 1.4 for 10+4, 3 for triple replication.
MDS (maximum distance separable) code — a code where any k of the n fragments rebuild the data; Reed–Solomon is MDS.
Systematic code — a code in which the first k fragments are the original data unchanged, so normal reads need no decoding.
Galois field GF(2⁸) — the field of 256 elements (bytes) in which addition is XOR; multiplication is defined by a polynomial and is done with tables or SIMD instructions.
Degraded read — reading data while some fragments are missing, which requires decoding from the survivors.
Repair (reconstruction) — rebuilding lost fragments from the surviving ones and storing them on new devices.
Repair amplification — the amount of data read to rebuild one fragment divided by the size of the fragment; 10 for a 10+4 Reed–Solomon code, 1 for replication.
Local reconstruction code (LRC) — a code with extra local parities over small groups, so that a single lost fragment can be repaired from a few neighbours.
history: Irving Reed and Gustave Solomon published "Polynomial codes over certain finite fields" in 1960. Their idea was to treat k data symbols as the coefficients of a polynomial of degree less than k and send the values of the polynomial at n different points; any k values determine the polynomial again. Reed–Solomon codes were first used in communication and storage media, such as deep-space probes, compact discs, DVDs and QR codes, to correct scratches and noise.
For disks, the first widely used erasure code was the parity disk of RAID. Patterson, Gibson and Katz (1988) described RAID levels; RAID 5 keeps one XOR parity block per stripe and survives one disk failure, and RAID 6 adds a second parity (Reed–Solomon or EVENODD, Blaum and others 1995) to survive two. James Plank's tutorial (1997) on Reed–Solomon coding for RAID-like systems made the method available to storage engineers. Weatherspoon and Kubiatowicz (2002) compared erasure coding and replication quantitatively, showing that systems with erasure codes reach a mean time to failure many orders of magnitude higher than replicated systems with similar storage and bandwidth.
Large-scale adoption took another decade because disks were not the bottleneck any more: the network was. Rebuilding a lost fragment of a Reed–Solomon code needs data from k other fragments, so repairs generate a lot of traffic. Huang and colleagues at Microsoft published "Erasure Coding in Windows Azure Storage" (USENIX ATC 2012) with local reconstruction codes (LRC), which add local parities so that most single-fragment repairs read only half as many fragments; their (12, 2, 2) code uses 1.33 times the data size. Sathiamoorthy and others at Facebook and USC published "XORing Elephants" (VLDB 2013), an LRC for HDFS. Facebook's f4 BLOB store (Muralidhar and others, OSDI 2014) used Reed–Solomon 10+4 for warm data and reduced the effective replication factor from 3.6 to 2.8, or 2.1 with cross-datacenter XOR.
Open-source systems followed. HDFS added erasure coding in Hadoop 3.0 (2017, HDFS-7285), Ceph supports erasure-coded pools with several plugins, including Intel's ISA-L library for fast coding. Plank and others showed how to do Galois-field arithmetic at memory speed with SIMD instructions (FAST 2013), and Vajha and others built Clay codes (FAST 2018), which cut repair traffic to the theoretical minimum for an MDS code and are available in Ceph.
how: Start with the simplest case, a single parity. Cut an object into k equal fragments d₁ … d_k and compute p = d₁ XOR d₂ XOR … XOR d_k. If any one fragment (data or parity) is lost, XOR the others to get it back. This is RAID 5. It survives one failure with overhead (k + 1)/k. To survive m failures you need m independent equations, and XOR alone gives only one.
Reed–Solomon coding supplies m independent equations. Treat each byte as an element of the field GF(2⁸). Choose an m × k matrix C over the field (a Cauchy matrix or a Vandermonde-derived matrix). Compute parity fragment j as p_j = c_{j1}·d₁ + c_{j2}·d₂ + … + c_{jk}·d_k, applied byte by byte: the byte at offset t in every fragment forms one small system. In matrix terms, the stored vector of n fragments is G·d, where G is the n × k generator matrix made of the identity matrix on top (the data fragments, unchanged: this is the systematic form) and C below. The matrix is chosen so that every set of k rows of G is invertible. That property is exactly what "MDS" means, and it holds for a Cauchy matrix because every square submatrix of a Cauchy matrix is invertible.
Decoding is linear algebra. Suppose any k fragments survive. Take the k rows of G that correspond to them, giving a k × k matrix A. The surviving fragments y satisfy y = A·d, so d = A⁻¹·y. The inverse is computed once per failure pattern by Gaussian elimination, O(k³) field operations, which is negligible for k = 10, and then applied to every byte offset. If the missing fragments are all parity, nothing needs decoding. If a data fragment is missing, the system computes it from k survivors; this is a degraded read. If a parity fragment is missing, it is recomputed from the data fragments by the encode step.
The arithmetic is finite-field arithmetic. In GF(2⁸), addition is XOR. Multiplication by a constant can be done with a 256-entry lookup table, or, much faster, by splitting each byte into two 4-bit halves and using a SIMD byte-shuffle instruction (the PSHUFB trick), which processes 16, 32 or 64 bytes per instruction. Libraries such as Intel ISA-L reach multiple gigabytes per second per core, so coding is rarely the bottleneck; the network and disks are. A field of 256 elements allows up to 256 distinct evaluation points, so n ≤ 256, which is plenty for storage.
An equivalent view helps with intuition. Think of a polynomial p(x) of degree less than k whose coefficients are the k data symbols. The n fragments are p(x₁), …, p(x_n), values at n distinct points. A polynomial of degree less than k is determined by any k points (Lagrange interpolation), so any k fragments rebuild it. The matrix view and the polynomial view are the same code.
Systems design adds a layer. Fragments of one stripe are placed in distinct failure domains (different racks, or different availability zones), so one rack failure removes at most one or two fragments. Reads normally fetch the k data fragments in parallel and concatenate them. Writes encode and write n fragments; to avoid the cost of small writes (see below), systems usually accumulate data into large immutable blocks of tens or hundreds of MB, then encode. Azure, for example, writes new data with replication first, and converts sealed extents to erasure-coded form in the background. Repair is a background job: when a device fails, the system reads k fragments of each affected stripe, recomputes the lost fragment, and writes it elsewhere, at a rate limited so as not to disturb users.
The cost of repair is the main weakness. Rebuilding one lost fragment of a 10+4 Reed–Solomon stripe needs 10 fragments from 10 servers, so repairing a 10 MB fragment reads 100 MB: a repair amplification of 10. With replication, the repair reads one 10 MB copy. Local reconstruction codes lower this: in Azure's (12, 2, 2) code, 12 data fragments are divided into 2 groups of 6, each group has a local parity (the XOR of its group), and there are 2 global parities; a single lost data fragment is rebuilt from the other 5 in its group and the local parity, 6 reads instead of 12. The price is that the code is not MDS: it stores 16 fragments for 12 data fragments (1.33×), and survives any 3 failures but not every 4-failure pattern.

example: A tiny code you can solve with a pen. Work with numbers modulo 7 (the integers 0 to 6 form a field, with addition and multiplication modulo 7; real systems use GF(2⁸), but the algebra is the same). Take k = 2 data symbols d₀ = 3 and d₁ = 5, and m = 2 parity symbols: p₁ = d₀ + d₁ and p₂ = d₀ + 2·d₁. Compute: p₁ = 8 mod 7 = 1, p₂ = 3 + 10 = 13 mod 7 = 6. The four stored fragments are [3, 5, 1, 6], on four different servers. The generator rows are (1,0), (0,1), (1,1), (1,2); every pair of rows has a nonzero determinant modulo 7 (for example rows (1,1) and (1,2) give 1·2 − 1·1 = 1), so any two fragments determine the data.
Lose both data fragments. Survivors: p₁ = 1 and p₂ = 6. The equations are d₀ + d₁ = 1 and d₀ + 2d₁ = 6. Subtract the first from the second: d₁ = 5. Then d₀ = 1 − 5 = −4 ≡ 3 (mod 7). The data (3, 5) is back. Lose d₁ and p₁. Survivors: d₀ = 3 and p₂ = 6. The equation 3 + 2d₁ = 6 gives 2d₁ = 3. Division is multiplication by the inverse: the inverse of 2 modulo 7 is 4 (2 × 4 = 8 ≡ 1), so d₁ = 3 × 4 = 12 ≡ 5. Again (3, 5). This is what decoding is: solve a small linear system using field arithmetic.
Now sizes and costs. A 100 MB object with k = 10 and m = 4: fragments of 10 MB, total stored 140 MB, 1.4× overhead, tolerating the loss of any 4 of the 14 devices. Triple replication stores 300 MB, 3×, and tolerates 2 lost copies. Rebuild one dead 10 MB fragment: erasure code reads 10 × 10 MB = 100 MB over the network; replication reads 10 MB. For a failed 20 TB drive full of fragments, rebuilding means reading about 200 TB across the cluster, against 20 TB for replication. That is why systems spread the rebuild over hundreds of servers, and why repair bandwidth is a first-order design parameter.
Durability, with the assumption that each fragment is independently unavailable at a given moment with probability q. If a drive fails with 2% a year and takes one day to replace, q ≈ 0.02 × 1/365 ≈ 5.5 × 10⁻⁵. Triple replication loses data when all 3 are down: q³ ≈ 1.6 × 10⁻¹³. A 6+3 code loses data when 4 or more of 9 are down: about 1.1 × 10⁻¹⁵, roughly 150 times better, with half the space. A 10+4 code loses data when 5 or more of 14 are down: about 1 × 10⁻¹⁸, about 160,000 times better than replication with less than half the space. The independence assumption is optimistic (correlated failures from power, firmware, bad batches and operator mistakes dominate in practice), but the ordering of the options holds, and it shows why placement across failure domains is as important as the code.
intuition: Spread enough clues across many places that the missing pieces can always be worked out from the clues that remain.
It is natural because of a counting argument. If k numbers are unknown, k independent equations about them are enough to find them, and any k of the equations will do, as long as no equation is redundant with the others. Storing the data itself gives k equations. Adding m more equations (the parities) that are independent of the first k, and of each other, means that any k of the k + m still carry the full information. The code stores the minimum extra, m equations, to tolerate m losses. Replication, by comparison, is a wasteful way to get redundancy because each copy repeats information that other copies already have.
mistakes: People often think erasure coding is a kind of compression. In fact it adds data (the parity), so the stored size is larger than the original. The saving is relative to replication, which adds much more.
People often think it is strictly better than replication. In fact it trades storage for repair traffic, CPU, small-write cost and read fan-out. Replication reads from one node and repairs from one node, so hot, small, frequently updated data stays replicated in most systems.
People often think the code needs to know which data is lost or damaged. In fact it handles erasures, where the system knows which fragments are missing (a device is down). Detecting corrupt fragments uses checksums stored with each fragment; once a corrupt fragment is detected, it becomes an erasure. Detecting errors without knowing their position costs twice the parity.
People often think more parity is always safer. In fact each extra parity raises write and repair costs, and failures are often correlated, so a wider code on the same rack gives less protection than a narrower code spread over more failure domains. The safe design is the one whose fragments sit in independent domains.
friction: Repair is the first cost. A Reed–Solomon repair reads k times as much data as it rebuilds, and in a large cluster with many failures per day the repair traffic competes with user traffic and can take a significant share of the network between racks. Systems limit repair bandwidth, which lengthens the window during which more failures can accumulate, so durability depends on repair speed as much as on the code. LRC and regenerating codes reduce the traffic at the price of extra storage or complexity.
Small objects and small updates are the second. A stripe has k fragments, so an object smaller than k times the fragment size wastes space or becomes many tiny I/Os. Updating one data fragment requires updating all m parities: read the old data and old parities, compute the change, and write the new data and parities, a read-modify-write of m + 1 fragments that costs several times the I/O of overwriting one replica. For this reason systems erasure-code large, immutable, cold or warm data, and keep small and hot data replicated.
Third, read latency. A normal read fans out to k servers, and the slowest of the k sets the latency, which is the tail problem of speculative execution. Systems read k + 1 or more fragments and use the first k to arrive (a form of hedging that is natural for these codes). Degraded reads, when a needed fragment is missing, have to fetch k survivors and decode, which is slower, and a whole node failure makes many reads degraded until repair completes.
Fourth, correlated failures and placement. The independence assumption breaks for correlated events, so fragment placement must follow failure domains, and a code that spans three racks may be less safe than the number suggests. Cross-datacenter coding saves space but makes every repair cross a wide-area link. Fifth, complexity: encoding libraries, placement, repair schedulers, version management and background re-encoding add real operational burden, which is why most teams use a storage service rather than building it.
practice: Use an object store or distributed file system that already does it, and learn the parameters it exposes. In HDFS 3, set erasure coding policies per directory (hdfs ec -setPolicy), use RS-6-3-1024k (1.5× overhead) for cold data and large files, and keep hot, small-file or heavily updated directories on 3× replication. Avoid erasure coding for directories full of small files: the policy stripes data in 1 MB cells across 9 nodes, so a file of a few MB becomes many tiny blocks and loses most of the benefit. In Ceph, create erasure-coded pools for bulk object data (for example k = 8, m = 3 across hosts or racks) and replicated pools for metadata and for hot data.
Pick k and m from the failure model and the cluster shape. The number of failure domains must be at least k + m (otherwise two fragments share a domain). Wider codes (k = 17, m = 3, as Backblaze uses, about 1.18× overhead) save more space but need a larger cluster, make repair read more fragments, and fan out reads wider. Narrower codes (6+3) cost more space but are simpler to repair. To decide, estimate: raw capacity cost saved per PB, repair traffic per drive failure times failure rate, and the durability from a model with realistic repair time.
Monitor the repair backlog, the number of stripes with fewer than n fragments, and time to full redundancy; a growing backlog is a durability alarm. Test failure drills: take a rack out and measure repair time and the effect on user latency. Keep checksums on every fragment and scrub periodically so that silent corruption is found before it eats into the redundancy. When you build your own, use ISA-L or another SIMD library, and test decoding for every failure pattern for small codes.
In a lakehouse context, remember that you usually do not choose the code; the cloud provider does. What you choose is the storage class (standard, infrequent access, archive), replication across regions, and versioning, which build on the provider's durability. Use cross-region replication for disaster recovery, since the provider's erasure coding protects against device and rack failures but not against deleting data or losing an account.
tradeoffs: Choose erasure coding when data is large, written once and read many times, and capacity cost dominates: data lakes, backups, media, logs, warm object storage. Choose replication when data is small, hot, frequently updated, or latency-critical, when you have fewer than about 6 failure domains, or when repair traffic cannot be afforded. You give up capacity with replication (3× instead of 1.2–1.5×) and give up update speed, simplicity and repair bandwidth with erasure coding.
Choose a wide code such as 10+4 or 17+3 for cold data in a large cluster, with 15 or more racks and fast repair, where the saving is largest (1.2–1.4×). Choose a narrow code such as 6+3 or 4+2 for warm data or smaller clusters, where repair cost and read fan-out matter (1.5×). The deciding numbers are the number of failure domains, the repair bandwidth and the read latency target.
Choose an LRC (like Azure's 12, 2, 2 or Xorbas) when single-device failures are by far the most common event and repair traffic is the main cost: you accept slightly more storage than the MDS minimum (1.33× against 1.17×) for repair that reads half as much. Choose Reed–Solomon when you want the smallest storage for a given fault tolerance and repair is less frequent. Choose regenerating codes (Clay codes) when repair bandwidth is the main cost and you can accept a more complex implementation and more sub-fragments per fragment.
Choose to keep the first copy hot as a replica and encode later (the hybrid approach used by Azure and HDFS tiers) when you need low write latency and cheap long-term storage; you pay a background conversion job and temporary extra space.
interview: Explain how a 10+4 Reed–Solomon code can recover from any 4 failures. Why is the overhead 1.4×? || The 4 parity fragments are independent linear combinations of the 10 data fragments over GF(2⁸), so any 10 of the 14 fragments form an invertible 10 × 10 system and give back the data. Storage is 14/10 = 1.4× the original. The MDS property means no code with 4 parities can tolerate more failures.
Why is repair the main cost of erasure coding and how do LRC codes reduce it? || To rebuild one fragment of an RS(10,4) stripe you must read 10 other fragments, a 10× amplification of network and disk traffic. An LRC adds local parity over small groups, so a single failure is repaired from the group (for example 6 reads instead of 12 in Azure's design). It costs a little more storage and loses the MDS property.
You are designing storage for 50 PB of logs with 12 racks. Replication or erasure coding, and which parameters? || Erasure coding, since the data is write-once, large and cold. With 12 racks I would use something like 6+3 (9 domains) with each fragment on a different rack, 1.5× overhead, or 8+3 over 11 racks. A 10+4 needs 14 domains, so I would not use it with 12 racks. I would check repair time and traffic against the rack uplinks.
Why are small random writes expensive on an erasure-coded system? || Changing a single data fragment changes all m parities, so the system must read the old data and old parities, recompute, and write m + 1 fragments: a read-modify-write across several nodes. Systems avoid it by buffering writes, writing full stripes, or keeping small and hot data replicated.
How would you estimate the durability of an erasure-coded cluster, and what would make the estimate wrong? || Model fragment failure probability per repair window, then compute the probability that more than m of n fragments are down at once (a binomial or Markov model that includes repair rate). It is wrong when failures are correlated (same rack, same firmware, same batch of disks), when repair is slower than assumed because of throttling, and when silent corruption is not scrubbed.
How does erasure coding interact with read tail latency, and what can you do? || A read touches k servers, and its latency is the maximum of k response times, so stragglers dominate. Request k + 1 or more fragments and decode from the first k to arrive, which is a hedge that comes naturally from the code, or use a cache for hot objects. The cost is some extra network traffic.
quiz: What does it mean that a (k, m) Reed–Solomon code is MDS? || Any k of the k + m fragments are enough to rebuild the data, so it tolerates the loss of any m fragments, which is the best possible for that amount of added storage.
What is the storage overhead of 6+3 and of triple replication, and how many failures does each tolerate? || 6+3: 9/6 = 1.5× and 3 failures. Triple replication: 3× and 2 failures.
Why does repairing one fragment of a 10+4 stripe read 10 fragments? || The lost fragment is a combination of the other data, and the only way to recompute it is to gather any 10 surviving fragments and solve the linear system. Replication has a full copy to read.
In the mod-7 example with fragments [3, 5, 1, 6], the survivors are d₀ = 3 and p₂ = 6. What is d₁? || From 3 + 2·d₁ = 6 (mod 7), 2·d₁ = 3, and multiplying by the inverse of 2 (which is 4) gives d₁ = 12 mod 7 = 5.
A 20 TB drive in a 17+3 cluster fails. Roughly how much data must be read to rebuild it, and what does this imply? || About 17 × 20 TB = 340 TB, read across the cluster. The rebuild must be spread over many servers and takes network bandwidth, so wide codes need fast, well-parallelised repair; an LRC or regenerating code reduces the read volume.

@@ reconcile
value: A reconciliation loop is a program that keeps a system in a wanted state by repeating one cycle forever: look at the desired state, look at the actual state, find the difference, take a step to reduce it, and look again. You do not tell the system to "start two more servers". You declare that you want five servers, and the loop makes it so, today, after a crash tomorrow, and after somebody deletes a server by hand next week. It is the control pattern behind Kubernetes (every controller is one), infrastructure-as-code tools, GitOps systems such as Argo CD and Flux, and the operators that manage databases and Spark applications on a cluster.
The reason this pattern replaced scripts is that real systems fail in the middle. A script that runs "create 3 pods" can fail after creating 1, and then nobody knows what to repeat. Messages get lost, machines restart, people make manual changes, and two automation tools overlap. A loop that compares states needs no memory of what it did before: whatever has happened, it looks at the current situation and moves towards the goal. This makes the system self-healing and makes every step safe to repeat, which is the property engineers call idempotence.
One lower layer supplies the idea. Control theory (L4) is the study of feedback: a controller measures the output of a system, compares it with a setpoint, and applies a correction proportional to the error, as a thermostat does. The reconciliation loop is a negative-feedback controller in which the setpoint is the declared spec, the measurement is the observed status, and the actuator is an API call. Control theory also names the ways such loops go wrong, and they appear in software too: delay in measurement leads to overshoot, aggressive gain leads to oscillation, and two controllers on the same variable fight. The layers above are container orchestration (Kubernetes is a collection of controllers) and infrastructure as code (tools that plan the difference between declared and real infrastructure).
terms: Desired state (spec) — what the user wants, stored as data: for example "3 replicas of this container image".
Observed state (status) — what actually exists now, as measured by the controller or reported by the system.
Controller — a process that runs one reconciliation loop for one kind of object.
Level-triggered — reacting to the current state ("there are 2 pods, 3 are wanted") instead of reacting to the event that changed it.
Edge-triggered — reacting to an event ("a pod was deleted"); it breaks if an event is lost, duplicated or reordered.
Idempotent — an operation that has the same effect whether it is run once or many times.
Informer — a client component that lists objects, then watches for changes, and keeps a local cache and a stream of change notifications.
Work queue — a queue of object keys that deduplicates: if a key is added ten times before it is processed, it is processed once.
Finalizer — a marker on an object that blocks its deletion until a controller has cleaned up the external resources it owns.
Drift — a difference between declared and actual state that arises without a change to the declaration, such as a manual edit or a crash.
history: Feedback control is older than computers. James Watt's centrifugal governor (1788) kept a steam engine's speed near a set value, and James Clerk Maxwell's paper "On governors" (1868) began the mathematical study of why such loops are stable or oscillate. The idea of using feedback to manage computer configuration came much later. Mark Burgess created CFEngine in 1993 and described it in "A site configuration engine" (Computing Systems, 1995). Its model was that each machine repeatedly checks its configuration against a policy and fixes the differences, a property Burgess called convergence. Puppet (2005) and Chef (2009) followed the same idea.
Google's cluster manager Borg (described in Verma and others, EuroSys 2015) was built on state kept in a central store and on components that watch it and drive the cluster towards the declared state. Kubernetes, released as open source in 2014 with version 1.0 in July 2015, made this the central design. In "Borg, Omega, and Kubernetes" (ACM Queue, 2016), Burns, Grant, Oppenheimer, Brewer and Wilkes explain that Kubernetes is built from small, independent reconciliation loops that communicate only through objects in the shared API, rather than through direct calls. The Kubernetes community also borrowed the terms edge-triggered and level-triggered from electronics, where a circuit can respond to a signal change or to the signal's level, and its design guidance prefers level-triggered logic because it survives lost and repeated events.
The pattern spread beyond container scheduling. Terraform (HashiCorp, 2014) introduced the plan and apply cycle, a reconciliation that runs when you invoke it. CoreOS (Brandon Philips, 2016) named the operator pattern: a custom controller that encodes the knowledge of a human operator for a database or other application, using custom resource definitions. Weaveworks coined GitOps (2017), with Flux, followed by Argo CD (from Intuit, 2018), which continuously reconcile a cluster with manifests in a Git repository. The controller-runtime library and Kubebuilder (2018) turned writing a controller into a standard task with a single Reconcile function.
how: The loop has five steps. (1) Watch: learn that something may need attention. (2) Read the desired state, the spec of an object. (3) Read the actual state, from the cluster or from an external system. (4) Compute the difference and act: take the smallest idempotent steps that reduce it. (5) Record the outcome in the object's status, and finish. Then the controller waits for the next trigger, and the next trigger can also be time (a periodic resync). A loop that finds no difference does nothing.
A Kubernetes controller makes this concrete. Objects live in the API server, backed by etcd, with a spec field (desired) and a status field (observed). A controller starts an informer for the types it cares about: the informer lists all objects once, then opens a watch from the list's resource version, so that it receives every later change as a stream. It stores the objects in a local cache and, on each change, calls a handler. The handler does not carry the change forward; it only puts the key of the affected object (namespace and name) on a work queue. Worker goroutines take a key, call Reconcile(key), which reads the current object and related objects from the cache, and decide what to do.
The work queue is where level-triggering happens. If an object changes ten times quickly, the key is queued once, and Reconcile sees only the latest state. Intermediate values are lost on purpose, because they do not matter: the goal is defined by the current spec. If Reconcile returns an error, the key is put back with a delay that grows with consecutive failures (the default rate limiter in client-go starts at 5 milliseconds and doubles up to 1,000 seconds, combined with an overall limit), so a broken object does not overload the system. Because the loop reads the world fresh each time, a controller can crash, restart, list all objects again and continue, with no recovery code for missed events.
A Reconcile function should follow a few rules. It must be idempotent: "ensure there are 3 pods" rather than "create a pod". It must read the actual state and not trust memory of earlier actions. It should make one decisive change, or a few, and let the next run handle the rest, since every change triggers another reconcile. It updates status, with conditions, to record progress and errors. Sub-objects that it creates carry an owner reference to the parent, so when the parent is deleted, the garbage collector deletes the children. A finalizer on the parent delays deletion until the controller has removed things the garbage collector does not know about, such as a cloud load balancer.
Several concerns come from the delay between acting and observing. After the ReplicaSet controller creates a pod, the pod may not appear in the informer's cache for a few milliseconds or more. If another reconcile runs in that gap, it would count too few pods and create a duplicate. Kubernetes avoids this with expectations: the controller records "I have created 1 pod, I expect to see 1 add event" and skips acting until the expectation is met or expires. In control language, this handles the dead time of the measurement. Writes are guarded by optimistic concurrency: every object has a resource version, and an update with a stale version fails with a conflict, in which case the controller simply requeues and tries on fresh data. Only one replica of a controller acts at a time, enforced by leader election on a lease object.
The loop acts as a proportional controller in some systems. The Horizontal Pod Autoscaler computes desiredReplicas = ceil(currentReplicas × currentMetric / targetMetric). With 4 replicas at 90% average CPU against a target of 60%, the answer is ceil(4 × 1.5) = 6. It adds a dead band (a tolerance of 10% by default, so a ratio of 1.05 changes nothing) and a stabilisation window for scale-down (5 minutes by default) so measurement noise does not cause oscillation. These are exactly the tools of control theory: error, gain, dead band and delay.
Controllers compose without calling each other. A Deployment controller creates and updates ReplicaSet objects; the ReplicaSet controller creates Pod objects; the scheduler is another loop that fills in the node name of unbound pods; the kubelet on each node is a loop that starts containers for pods bound to it and reports status. Each loop reads one kind of object and writes another, so the whole system is a chain of small feedback loops with the API server as the shared memory. A failure anywhere stops only that link, and when the link recovers, the chain catches up, because every link recomputes from the state it finds.

example: Take a ReplicaSet named web with spec.replicas = 3 and pods p1, p2, p3 running. At t0 the controller reconciles: it lists the pods owned by web, counts 3 active, desired 3, difference zero. It does nothing except make sure status.replicas says 3.
At t1, p2 crashes and is removed. The informer sees a delete event and enqueues the key default/web. A worker calls Reconcile. The cache shows 2 active pods (p1, p3). Desired 3, so the difference is +1. The controller creates one pod, p4, with an owner reference to web, and records an expectation: one create is pending. A moment later p4's add event arrives. Reconcile runs again: the cache now shows 3 pods (p1, p3, p4), the difference is 0, the expectation is met, and the controller updates status. If some other event had triggered Reconcile before p4 showed up in the cache, the expectation would make the controller wait instead of creating a second replacement, which would give 4 pods.
Now the coalescing property. A user runs scale commands quickly: replicas 6, then 4, then 5. Each edit queues the key default/web, but the queue holds a key only once, so the worker may run only once, after all three edits, and read replicas = 5. It sees 3 active pods and creates 2. The intermediate values 6 and 4 were never acted on, and nothing is lost. An edge-triggered design would have to handle "scale to 6" and "scale to 4" as separate events in order.
Now a crash. The controller is down for ten minutes, during which p1 and p3 die and nobody sees an event. On restart, the informer lists all objects: one active pod, p4. Desired is 5, so the difference is 4 and the controller creates 4 pods. The same code that handled a single death handles a mass failure. The loop never needed to know how the difference came about, only how large it is.
Failure and backoff. Suppose the API server rejects pod creation because a quota is exhausted. Reconcile returns an error and the key is requeued with delays of 5 ms, 10 ms, 20 ms and so on. After 10 consecutive failures the delay is 5 ms × 2¹⁰ ≈ 5.1 seconds, and it can grow to a cap of 1,000 seconds. When an administrator raises the quota, the next retry succeeds without anyone restarting the controller. Compare the autoscaler: with 4 replicas at 90% against 60% it asks for 6. After the scale-up the load per replica falls to 60% and the ratio is 1.0, so it stops; it does not oscillate because of the dead band and stabilisation window.
Scale. Kubernetes documents support for clusters up to 5,000 nodes and 150,000 pods. At that size a controller keeps tens of thousands of objects in its cache, and a restart relists all of them. Reconcile is expected to be cheap (read from the cache, compare, maybe one API call), because a restart or a resync makes the controller reconcile every object once.
intuition: A thermostat: it does not care how the room got cold, only that it is colder than the setting, and it keeps pushing until the difference is gone.
The design is natural because it separates the question "what do I want?" from "what do I do?". Wanting is data, stored once and checked by everybody. Doing is a cheap function of the data and the world, so it can be run any time, by anyone, as often as you like. Events are only hints that it may be time to run. This removes the need to remember history, to deliver messages reliably, or to coordinate who did what. Anything that goes wrong between two runs is repaired by the next run, the way a thermostat repairs an open window.
mistakes: People often think a controller reacts to events such as "pod deleted". In fact good controllers use events only as a reason to look, then compute everything from the current state. A design that depends on seeing each event breaks when an event is lost, duplicated or reordered, which happens at every restart and every network partition.
People often think declarative means the system runs your plan instantly. In fact it means the system keeps working towards your state, which takes time and can stall. The status field tells you whether it has converged. "Applied" does not mean "done": check the conditions and observed generation.
People often think Terraform is a reconciliation loop in the same sense as a Kubernetes controller. In fact Terraform compares declared and real infrastructure only when you run plan or apply, so drift persists until the next run. Continuous reconcilers (Kubernetes controllers, Argo CD, Crossplane) keep comparing without being asked. Both are useful, with different risks.
People often think more controllers watching the same thing give more safety. In fact two loops with different goals for the same field will fight forever: one sets replicas to 3, the autoscaler sets it to 6, and each undoes the other. Every field needs exactly one owner.
friction: The main failure is conflict and oscillation. Two controllers (or a controller and a human) that manage the same field overwrite each other in a loop, creating constant API load and flapping resources. Server-side apply with field ownership in Kubernetes makes ownership explicit and reports conflicts, but the problem remains in practice with tools that have not adopted it. Feedback with delay can overshoot or hunt, as with autoscalers that scale up on a spike, then down, then up again; dead bands and stabilisation windows help but need tuning.
Convergence can be slow or never finish. A large difference may be corrected at a limited rate (to avoid overloading the API or the cloud provider), and a loop whose action fails every time keeps retrying and backing off while the system stays out of the desired state. Status that says nothing helps nobody, so controllers must report why they are stuck. Hot loops are a classic bug: a controller writes its status, the write creates an event, the event queues the key, and the loop never stops; filters on spec changes (generation) fix this.
Debugging is hard because the cause of an observed state is spread across many independent loops and the history is not stored in the object. The question "why is this pod here?" requires reading events, owner chains and logs of several controllers. Reads come from caches, so a controller can act on stale data; most of the time this is harmless because of retries, but it is the source of subtle bugs, such as the duplicate-creation problem above.
Finally, a reconciler faithfully applies mistakes. If the declared state is wrong (a bad manifest merged into the Git repository), the loop will propagate it to every cluster and revert any manual fix. Safe operation needs review, staged rollout, and a way to pause reconciliation. Deletion is risky too: a declaration that disappears can mean "delete everything it owned", so pruning and finalizers need careful policy.
practice: Write each controller around one object type and one question: given this object and the world, what is the smallest next step? Make the function idempotent and free of hidden state. Use the cache for reads and the API for writes; handle NotFound, conflict and already-exists as normal outcomes; return an error (so that the queue retries with backoff) or an explicit requeue-after, never sleep inside Reconcile. Put results in status with conditions (type, status, reason, message) and set observedGeneration so users can tell whether status describes the latest spec.
Use owner references for children and finalizers only where external cleanup is needed, and remove the finalizer in all paths, including when the external resource is already gone. Filter events so that status updates do not retrigger your own loop (compare generation, or use predicates). Limit concurrency per controller to what the downstream API tolerates. Track metrics: queue depth, reconcile duration, error rate and retries per controller (controller-runtime exports reconcile totals and workqueue metrics). Alert on a queue that stays non-empty and on a growing retry count.
For infrastructure and data platforms, prefer continuous reconcilers for state that must hold (Argo CD or Flux for cluster configuration, a Spark operator for SparkApplication resources) and on-demand tools for changes that need human review (Terraform plan in a pull request, then apply). Turn on drift detection for Terraform with a scheduled plan that only alerts. Start with automatic sync for non-production, and manual sync or sync windows for production. Always have a documented way to pause reconciliation of a resource when you must change it by hand.
tradeoffs: Choose a continuously running reconciler (a Kubernetes controller, Argo CD, Crossplane) when state must hold without human action, drift is common, and an automatic revert is what you want. Choose an on-demand plan and apply (Terraform in CI) when changes are risky or irreversible (databases, networks, IAM), you want a human to review the exact diff first, and surprise corrections are worse than a bit of drift. You give up auto-healing with the second and some control with the first.
Choose level-triggered logic for almost everything: it needs no event history and survives restarts. Choose event-driven handling only for one-off actions that must happen exactly once per event (sending a notification), and then make them idempotent with a record of what was already done. The cost of level-triggered logic is some repeated checking; the cost of event logic is a design for lost, duplicated and reordered events.
Choose watch-based triggering with a periodic resync (every 10 hours is a common default for informers) when objects are many and changes are rare; the watch gives low latency and the resync repairs rare missed cases. Choose polling alone when the external system has no change notifications, and then choose the interval from the cost of a check against the acceptable drift time (Argo CD checks Git every 3 minutes by default, which you can reduce with webhooks).
Choose many small controllers, each owning one resource, over one large controller that does everything: they are easier to reason about and to scale, and they fail independently. You pay with more objects and indirect cause-and-effect chains. Choose a longer stabilisation window and a wider dead band for controllers with expensive actions (adding a node takes minutes) and a short one for cheap actions (adding a pod takes seconds).
interview: Why does Kubernetes use level-triggered controllers rather than event handlers? || Events can be lost (restart, partition), duplicated or reordered, and a handler that depends on them diverges permanently. A level-triggered controller recomputes from current spec and status each time, so any missed event is repaired by the next run. Events only decide when to look; the work queue also collapses many events into one reconcile.
Walk through what happens between kubectl scale and a new pod running. || The API server stores the new spec. The ReplicaSet (or Deployment) controller's informer sees the change and queues the key. Reconcile counts owned pods, finds a shortfall and creates pod objects. The scheduler sees unbound pods and binds each to a node. The kubelet on that node sees the binding and starts the containers, then reports status back. Each step is a separate loop reading and writing objects.
Two controllers keep fighting over the same field. How do you diagnose and fix it? || I look at the object's managed fields and recent update events to see who writes the field, and at API request rates for the object. The fix is single ownership: server-side apply with distinct field managers, remove the field from one tool's manifest, or let the autoscaler own replicas and drop it from Git. Controllers must never overwrite fields they do not own.
What is wrong with a Reconcile function that creates a pod each time it sees the count is below desired? What protects against it? || Between creating the pod and seeing it in the cache, another reconcile may still see the old count and create a duplicate. Solutions: expectations (record pending creates until their events arrive), deterministic names so a duplicate create fails as already exists, or reading with a consistent API read when it matters.
How would you build a reconciler for an external resource, such as a cloud database, and what are the pitfalls? || A custom resource holds the spec; Reconcile reads the actual database through the cloud API, diffs, and calls update APIs idempotently, and writes status. Pitfalls: API rate limits and eventual consistency (a create not yet visible), long-running operations (store the operation ID in status), partial failures, finalizers for deletion and drift from manual changes. I would add backoff, conditions and metrics.
What goes wrong if reconciliation also reverts changes you made on purpose during an incident? || The loop restores the declared state within seconds, undoing a manual mitigation such as scaling down a bad service. During incidents I would pause reconciliation (disable auto-sync or annotate the resource), make the change, and then update the source of truth so that the declaration matches before resuming.
quiz: What are the two states a reconciliation loop compares, and what does it do with the difference? || The desired state (the spec) and the observed state (the status or the real system). It takes idempotent steps to reduce the difference and then records the result.
Why is level-triggered logic more robust than edge-triggered logic? || It computes what to do from the current state, so it does not matter if events were lost, duplicated or reordered; the next run corrects any mistake. Edge-triggered logic depends on seeing every event once, in order.
A work queue receives the same key 10 times before a worker is free. How many times does Reconcile run for it, and why is that safe? || Once. The queue deduplicates keys, and Reconcile reads the latest state each time, so the result is the same as having processed every event.
An HPA has 4 replicas at 90% CPU with a target of 60%. How many replicas does it request, and what would happen at 63%? || ceil(4 × 90/60) = 6 replicas. At 63% the ratio is 1.05, inside the default 10% tolerance, so it makes no change.
What happens if a controller returns an error 10 times in a row, with a backoff that starts at 5 ms and doubles? || The delay before the tenth retry is about 5 ms × 2¹⁰ ≈ 5.1 seconds, growing toward the cap (1,000 seconds by default). This keeps a broken object from overloading the system while still retrying after the cause is fixed.
