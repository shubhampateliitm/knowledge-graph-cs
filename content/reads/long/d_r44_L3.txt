@@ usb
value: USB is the standard way to connect a keyboard, a flash drive, a camera, a microphone or a phone charger to a computer. Before 1996 each kind of device had its own connector and its own rules: a serial port for modems, a parallel port for printers, PS/2 for keyboards, SCSI for disks, a game port for joysticks. Each needed a driver, and many needed switches or jumpers set by hand so that two cards did not use the same interrupt. You turned the computer off before you plugged anything in. USB replaced all of this with one cable, one set of rules, and one idea: the device describes itself, and the host decides what to do with it.
The design makes the host the only master. The host controller (a chip in the computer, today almost always an xHCI controller) starts every transfer. A device never speaks unless it is asked. That keeps devices cheap: a keyboard needs no bus arbitration, no collision handling and no clever addressing, only a small state machine that answers "send me what you have". It also lets the host plan bandwidth, so a webcam or a microphone can be promised a fixed share of the bus.
For the layers above, USB gives a stable contract. The Linux kernel's USB core enumerates devices and binds drivers by class code, so a new keyboard works with no new driver. Hardware security keys use USB, and NFC, to prove who you are (identity). Phones, laptops and monitors share one connector that carries data, video and up to 240 W of power.
The layers below make this work. Digital logic gives the serial engine that turns bytes into a differential signal. Hardware clocks let a device with a cheap crystal lock to the host's bit rate and agree on a 1 ms frame. DMA lets the host controller move packet data into RAM without the CPU, and interrupts let it tell the CPU once, when a batch of transfers is complete.
terms: Host controller — the chip in the computer that runs the bus; on modern machines it is an xHCI controller that reads work from queues in RAM by DMA.
Hub — a device that turns one port into several; USB is a tiered star, so hubs form a tree with the host controller's root hub at the top.
Descriptor — a small data structure in the device that describes it: its vendor and product IDs, its class, how much power it needs and what endpoints it has.
Endpoint — a numbered one-way data channel inside a device, with a direction (IN to the host or OUT from it), a transfer type and a maximum packet size.
Transfer type — one of four contracts for moving data: control, bulk, interrupt or isochronous.
Enumeration — the sequence after plug-in in which the host resets the device, gives it an address and reads its descriptors.
Device class — a published agreement for a family of devices (HID for keyboards and mice, mass storage, audio, video), so one generic driver serves all of them.
USB Power Delivery — a protocol on the USB-C cable's CC wire that lets the two ends agree on a voltage and current, from 5 V up to 48 V and 5 A (240 W).
history: The idea came from engineers at Intel in the early 1990s (Ajay Bhatt is the name most often connected with it), to make the PC easy to extend without opening the case. In 1995 seven companies (Compaq, Digital Equipment, IBM, Intel, Microsoft, NEC and Nortel) agreed to write one specification. USB 1.0 appeared on 15 January 1996 with two speeds: low speed at 1.5 Mbit/s for keyboards and mice, and full speed at 12 Mbit/s. Hardly anything used it until Apple's iMac of 1998 removed all legacy ports and had only USB; USB 1.1 (1998) fixed early problems.
USB 2.0 (April 2000) added high speed, 480 Mbit/s, and a new host controller interface, EHCI. USB 3.0 (November 2008) added SuperSpeed, 5 Gbit/s, over extra wires, and the xHCI controller interface, which replaced the earlier OHCI, UHCI and EHCI with one design. USB 3.1 (2013) doubled the speed to 10 Gbit/s with a more efficient line code, and USB 3.2 (2017) allowed two lanes for 20 Gbit/s.
The connector changed too. The USB Type-C specification (August 2014) defined a small reversible plug with 24 contacts, two configuration pins (CC) for orientation and role, and room for four high-speed pairs. USB Power Delivery grew to 240 W (version 3.1, 2021). USB4 (2019) took Intel's Thunderbolt 3 protocol as the base of a 40 Gbit/s link that tunnels PCI Express, DisplayPort and USB 3; version 2.0 (September 2022) reached 80 Gbit/s with active cables. In the EU, USB-C became mandatory for phones, tablets and cameras on 28 December 2024, and for laptops on 28 April 2026.
how: Start with the topology. The host controller has a root hub with several ports. Every device plugs into a port or into a hub, which forms a tree with at most five hubs in a chain below the root (seven tiers counting the root and the device). The bus holds 127 devices (7-bit addresses; address 0 is for a device just reset). All communication is host-initiated. Even when a keyboard has something to say, it must wait until the host asks.
The physical layer of USB 2.0 is four wires: power (VBUS), ground and two data wires, D+ and D−. The data wires carry one differential signal: the receiver looks at the difference between them, which cancels most noise picked up by both. Bits are sent with NRZI coding, and bit stuffing guarantees an edge at least every seven bit times. Every packet starts with a SYNC pattern (a run of alternating bits), and the device uses its edges to align its clock with the host's. This is where hardware clocks matter: full speed needs a bit clock within 0.25% of nominal and high speed within 500 parts per million, which a cheap quartz crystal can meet. The host also sends a start-of-frame packet every 1 ms (every 125 µs at high speed), a shared heartbeat that devices use to count time.
Everything on the bus is a packet of three kinds. A token packet (IN, OUT or SETUP) names a device address and an endpoint number, with a 5-bit CRC. A data packet carries up to the endpoint's maximum size, 8 to 64 bytes at full speed and 512 bytes for bulk at high speed, with a 16-bit CRC. A handshake packet (ACK, NAK or STALL) is only a PID byte. A transaction is a token, then data, then a handshake. Data packets alternate between DATA0 and DATA1, so a receiver that already took a packet (its ACK was lost) can drop the repeat. A NAK from a device means "nothing yet, ask later"; it is not an error.
Four transfer types give four contracts. Control transfers carry commands and descriptors, using a SETUP packet of 8 bytes; the bus keeps a share of each frame for them so a device can always be configured. Bulk transfers carry large data, for example a disk copy: they have error checks and retries, but no promise about timing, and they use whatever bandwidth is left. Interrupt transfers are for small, regular data such as keystrokes. The name is misleading, because the device cannot interrupt: the host polls the endpoint at the interval the device asked for, so the delay is bounded. Isochronous transfers carry audio and video: the host reserves a fixed number of bytes per frame, nothing is retried (a late sample is useless) and a bad CRC just loses the data. The host can reserve at most about 90% of a full-speed frame and 80% of a high-speed microframe for interrupt and isochronous traffic together.
Enumeration is how the host learns what it has. A pull-up resistor on D+ (full speed) or D− (low speed) tells the hub that something is attached. The host waits for the connection to settle, drives a reset for at least 10 ms and the device now answers at address 0. The host sends a GET_DESCRIPTOR request for the first 8 bytes of the device descriptor, only to learn the maximum packet size of endpoint 0. It sends SET_ADDRESS with a free address (1 to 127). Then it reads the full 18-byte device descriptor, then the configuration descriptor, which contains the interface and endpoint descriptors in one block. At last it sends SET_CONFIGURATION. Before that the device may draw only 100 mA from the 5 V line; afterwards, up to what it declared, typically 500 mA for USB 2.0. The host then matches the device to a driver, using the class, subclass and protocol codes or the vendor and product IDs.
The host controller's own work uses the lower layers heavily. In xHCI the driver builds transfer request blocks (TRBs, 16 bytes each) in rings in RAM, then writes to a doorbell register. The controller reads the TRBs by DMA, plans the frame, runs the transactions, writes incoming data straight into the buffers by DMA, and posts completion events to an event ring. It then raises one message-signalled interrupt, possibly moderated so that several completions share a single interrupt. The CPU sees a finished transfer, not 64 separate packets.
USB 3 and later keep the same logical model (descriptors, endpoints, the four transfer types) over a new physical layer: separate transmit and receive pairs, so the link is full duplex, and a different line code (8b/10b at 5 Gbit/s, which loses 20% to coding, and 128b/132b from 10 Gbit/s). Devices need not wait to be polled: a device sends an ERDY message when it is ready and the host then schedules the transfer, which saves bus time and power. USB-C adds a new layer on top: the host and device find each other's orientation and role through resistors on the CC pins, advertise current (default, 1.5 A or 3 A at 5 V), and Power Delivery messages on CC raise the voltage when both sides agree.

example: Plug in a USB keyboard and follow the first steps. The hub sees the pull-up on D− (low speed) and reports a connect event. The host resets the port for 10 ms or more. The keyboard now listens at address 0. The host sends a SETUP packet with 8 bytes: 80 06 00 01 00 00 08 00. Read them as bmRequestType 0x80 (device to host, standard request), bRequest 6 (GET_DESCRIPTOR), wValue 0x0100 (the device descriptor, index 0), wIndex 0, wLength 8. The keyboard returns 8 bytes; the last is the maximum packet size of endpoint 0, here 8. The host sends SET_ADDRESS (00 05 07 00 00 00 00 00) to give address 7, then reads the 18-byte device descriptor (vendor ID, product ID, USB version) and the configuration descriptor: one interface of class 3 (HID), subclass 1 (boot), protocol 1 (keyboard), and one interrupt IN endpoint of 8 bytes with a 10 ms interval. SET_CONFIGURATION 1 follows, the kernel's HID driver binds, and about 100 to 200 ms have passed.
Now cost one keystroke. At low speed, 1.5 Mbit/s, one bit lasts 667 ns. An IN token is 32 bits (about 21 µs), the data packet is 8 + 8 + 64 + 16 = 96 bits (about 64 µs), and the ACK is 16 bits (about 11 µs). With gaps, one report takes around 100 µs. Polling every 10 ms means 100 polls a second, so the keyboard uses about 1% of a low-speed bus, and most polls end in a shorter NAK. Polling is not a performance problem for such devices. The cost is latency: a key pressed at a random moment waits on average 5 ms before the host asks. A gaming keyboard with a 1 ms interval cuts that to 0.5 ms.
Now a bulk example. A high-speed microframe (125 µs) carries at most 13 bulk transactions of 512 bytes, so USB 2.0 has a ceiling of 13 × 512 × 8,000 = 53 MB/s, and real drives reach 35 to 45 MB/s: 1 GB takes 23 to 30 s. USB 3.0 at 5 Gbit/s leaves 4 Gbit/s = 500 MB/s after 8b/10b coding, about 400 MB/s after protocol overhead; then the flash chips are the limit.
intuition: A teacher who asks each pupil in turn whether they have something to say, and who also hands out the rooms, the time slots and the electric power.
The design is natural once you accept the cost of the opposite choice. If every device could talk when it wanted, the bus would need arbitration and collision recovery, and every cheap device would need a complex chip. With a single master, USB moves that complexity into the computer, where chips are plentiful. The poll is cheap because the host controller does it in hardware, and self-describing devices let the host use a generic driver for each class.
mistakes: People often think a USB device interrupts the CPU when you press a key. In fact a USB 2.0 device cannot signal anything by itself, except to wake a suspended bus. The host controller polls the keyboard's interrupt endpoint on a timer, and the CPU is interrupted by the controller, not by the keyboard. "Interrupt transfer" means a transfer with bounded latency.
People often think USB-C means fast. In fact USB-C is only a connector. A USB-C port may speak USB 2.0 at 480 Mbit/s, USB 3.2, USB4 or only charge. Cables are just as mixed: a charge-only cable has no high-speed wires, and a 5 A cable has a small chip (an e-marker) that announces its limits. The cable, the port and the device must each support a speed for the link to use it.
People often think a USB device can only be what its shape says. In fact the device chooses its class in its descriptor, and the host trusts it. A "flash drive" can enumerate as a keyboard and type commands, so plugging in an unknown device is a security decision.
friction: The first limit is shared bandwidth and latency from the polling model. Every device under a root port shares the same link, and a full-speed device behind a high-speed hub shares one translator. Audio devices have their own clock, so a stream needs feedback from the device or resampling to avoid drift.
The second is power and cables. A host offers 100 mA before configuration and 500 mA (USB 2.0) or 900 mA (USB 3) after, and a bus-powered disk that needs more can reset on spin-up. USB-C cables look the same but differ in speed (480 Mbit/s to 80 Gbit/s) and power (3 A or 5 A), and a poor cable can negotiate wrongly. USB 3 signals also radiate noise that can disturb 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi and Bluetooth when shielding is poor.
The third is trust. Any device can claim to be anything, and the host runs drivers for it with kernel privilege. Attacks include HID injection (a device that types), a fake network adapter that becomes the default route, and descriptor parsing bugs; the BadUSB research of 2014 showed that the firmware of common controllers could be reprogrammed to do this. The defence is a data blocker for public chargers, and allow-lists for ports.
practice: On Linux, `lsusb -t` shows the tree, the bound drivers and the negotiated speed (480M, 5000M); `lsusb -v` prints the descriptors; `dmesg` shows enumeration errors such as "device descriptor read/64, error -71", which usually means a bad cable, a bad port or too little power. For protocol problems, capture with usbmon and open the trace in Wireshark. Treat the cable as part of the system, test a link by reading the negotiated speed, and on kiosks restrict ports with USBGuard or udev rules.
When designing a device, use a standard class if one fits (HID, mass storage, CDC, audio), because the operating system already has a driver. Choose the transfer type by the contract you need, keep the polling interval as long as the application allows (1 ms makes the host work 8 times more than 8 ms), and declare the real power draw.
tradeoffs: Choose a standard device class when you can, and a vendor-specific class only when you must. A class driver means zero installation and works on every operating system, but it limits you to what the class defines (a HID report has a few bytes, a mass-storage device looks like a disk). A vendor-specific interface with libusb or WinUSB can use any protocol and any packet size, but you ship and maintain drivers, and signing requirements on some operating systems add cost.
Choose interrupt endpoints for control-like data and bulk for throughput, and isochronous only for real-time streams. Isochronous gives a guaranteed share of the bus, but no retries and a hard reservation that can make the host refuse your device when the bus is full. Bulk wastes nothing and never loses data, but if the bus is busy its latency is unbounded. If your data is a stream and you can buffer 100 ms, bulk with a ring buffer is often easier than isochronous.
Choose USB for external storage up to roughly 1 GB/s (10 Gbit/s links) or 2 to 4 GB/s (20 to 40 Gbit/s), and internal NVMe or a Thunderbolt/USB4 PCIe tunnel when you need PCIe-class speed (about 7 GB/s on PCIe 4.0 x4). Pay for the right cable and charger for power: 15 W for a phone, 65 to 100 W for a laptop, 240 W (e-marked cable) for a workstation.
interview: How does a host find out what a device is? || The host resets the port; the device answers at address 0. It reads 8 bytes of the device descriptor to learn the packet size of endpoint 0, sends SET_ADDRESS, then reads the full device and configuration descriptors (interfaces and endpoints) and sends SET_CONFIGURATION. Class codes or vendor and product IDs choose the driver. Before configuration the device may draw only 100 mA.
Why is USB host-polled, and what does it cost? || A single master removes arbitration and collisions and lets the host reserve bandwidth, so devices stay cheap. The cost is latency (half the polling interval on average) and bus time spent on NAKed polls. USB 3 reduces the second cost: the device sends ERDY when it has data.
You design a USB microphone. Which transfer type and what problems? || Isochronous IN, because late audio is useless: the host reserves bytes per frame (48 kHz × 2 bytes = 96 bytes per ms). Problems: device and host clocks differ, so the stream drifts unless there is a feedback endpoint or adaptive resampling, and the reservation can fail on a full bus.
A USB 3 SSD in a dock copies at 30 MB/s instead of 400. How do you debug? || Check the negotiated speed with `lsusb -t` (it may have fallen back to 480M because of a USB 2.0 cable or port), whether the dock shares one upstream link, and whether the drive uses UASP or bulk-only mode. Read dmesg for resets, capture with usbmon, and try another cable.
quiz: Who starts every transfer on a USB 2.0 bus, and what does a NAK from a device mean? || The host controller starts every transfer. A NAK means "I have no data yet, ask again later"; it is a normal answer to a poll, not an error.
Why does bit stuffing exist in USB 2.0? || NRZI encodes a 1 as no change, so a long run of 1s makes no edges and the receiver's clock could drift. After six 1s the sender inserts a 0, which forces an edge so the receiver can stay locked.
A low-speed keyboard report costs about 100 µs on the bus and is polled every 10 ms. What fraction of the bus does it use, and what is the average input delay due to polling? || 100 µs per 10 ms is 1% of the bus. A key pressed at a random time waits on average half the interval, 5 ms.
Why can a 480 Mbit/s USB 2.0 drive never reach 60 MB/s, even with a perfect disk? || Bulk transfers use at most 13 transactions of 512 bytes per 125 µs microframe, so the ceiling is 13 × 512 × 8,000 ≈ 53 MB/s, and token, handshake and gap time take the rest.
When would you choose an isochronous endpoint over a bulk one? || When data must arrive on time and a late packet is useless, as in live audio or video. Isochronous reserves bandwidth and never retries; bulk retries and keeps every byte but has no timing promise.

@@ nfc
value: NFC (near-field communication) lets a phone or a card talk to a reader that is a few centimetres away. You meet it when you tap a bank card or phone to pay, touch in at a metro gate, open a hotel door, read a passport, or touch a hardware security key to a phone. The problem it solves is hard to see until you list the requirements: a card in a wallet has no battery and no radio transmitter, it must work in about a tenth of a second, it must be cheap, and it must prove it is genuine to a machine that does not trust it. NFC does this with a trick from transformers: the reader's coil makes a magnetic field, the card's coil picks up energy from it, and the card answers by changing how much energy it takes.
This makes the system passive and short-range by physics, not by policy. The field is useful only within about 4 cm, so a tap is itself a sign of intent. Nothing needs pairing, and nobody charges the card. For the layer above, NFC moves a few kilobytes between two touching devices and runs a challenge-response conversation with a tamper-resistant chip. Identity systems use it to bind a person to a physical key or document; payment systems build tokens and signed transaction codes on top.
The layers below explain each part. Electromagnetism (Maxwell) gives induction: a changing magnetic field makes a voltage in a nearby coil, which is both the power supply and the data link. Noise explains why the card's reply is hard to detect and why the signal uses a subcarrier, narrow filters and CRC checks. Digital logic gives the small state machine and secure processor on the chip, which even takes its clock from the carrier. Digital signatures and PKI let the chip prove it is genuine.
terms: Reader (PCD) — the active device that makes the 13.56 MHz field and starts the conversation; a payment terminal, a gate, or a phone in reader mode.
Tag or card (PICC) — the passive device that is powered by the field; it can be a bare memory chip or a full secure microcontroller.
Load modulation — the way a passive tag sends data: it switches an electrical load on its coil, and the reader sees the change in its own coil voltage.
Subcarrier — a second, lower frequency (847.5 kHz) that the tag switches at while sending, so the reply sits to the side of the strong carrier instead of on top of it.
APDU — application protocol data unit; the command and response format of smart cards (ISO 7816), with a status word such as 90 00 for success.
Secure element — a tamper-resistant chip that stores keys and runs code that cannot be read out; it is in a bank card, in a phone, or inside a SIM.
Host card emulation (HCE) — a phone feature where an ordinary app, not a secure element, answers the reader's APDUs.
Relay attack — an attack that does not break the cryptography; it forwards the messages between a real card and a real reader over a long distance.
history: Passive radio tags are old. In 1948 Harry Stockman described communication by reflected power, and in the 1970s inventors such as Mario Cardullo patented passive tags. By the 1980s, 125 kHz tags were used for animals and door locks. In 1994 the first MIFARE chip brought contactless cards at 13.56 MHz to transit, and ISO/IEC 14443 for "proximity cards" followed in the early 2000s. Sony's FeliCa ran Hong Kong's Octopus card from 1997 and Japan's Suica from 2001.
NFC was defined by Philips and Sony around 2002 as a way to make a phone behave both like a reader and like a card, compatible with these cards (ECMA-340 in 2002, ISO/IEC 18092 in 2004). Nokia, Philips and Sony founded the NFC Forum in 2004 to define tag formats and test rules. The first NFC phones arrived in 2006, but the business stalled because banks, phone makers and operators did not agree who would hold the card keys.
Two events changed that. Google Wallet (2011) put a payment applet on a secure element, and Android 4.4 (2013) added host card emulation, which let apps answer a reader and moved secrets to the cloud as tokens. Apple Pay (October 2014) paired NFC with a secure element and tokenisation, and made tapping with a phone normal. Security history matters too: MIFARE Classic's cipher (Crypto1) was broken in 2008, pushing access control towards AES-based chips such as DESFire, and Gerhard Hancke published the relay attack on contactless cards in 2005. It remains the main structural weakness, because distance is not part of the cryptography.
how: Begin with power. The reader's antenna is a flat loop driven with a 13.56 MHz alternating current. The wavelength at this frequency is 22 m, so a 5 cm antenna cannot radiate effectively; it only makes a magnetic field in its near zone, which is what "near-field" means. A card has its own loop of 4 to 6 turns, usually the size of the card, tuned with a small capacitor so that it resonates at 13.56 MHz. By Faraday's law, the changing flux through that loop induces a voltage of ω × N × A × B. A rectifier and a regulator turn that into a steady supply of a few volts and a few milliwatts, enough to start a microcontroller in a few milliseconds. The chip also divides the 13.56 MHz it sees on its coil by 128 to get its bit clock, so it needs no crystal; digital logic here is an oscillator-free state machine locked to the reader's carrier.
The reader talks to the card by changing its field. In ISO 14443 Type A, the reader uses 100% ASK with Modified Miller coding at 106 kbit/s (13.56 MHz / 128): to send a bit it switches the field off for a short pause of about 2 to 3 µs, and the position of the pause inside the bit time tells 1 from 0. The card's envelope detector sees the pauses; the field comes back at once, so the card keeps power. Type B uses 10% ASK with a different coding, which keeps more power in the field during data. Faster rates (212, 424 and 848 kbit/s) exist but are less used.
The card answers by load modulation, and this is the clever part. The reader and card coils are weakly coupled, like the two windings of a transformer with a large air gap. If the card changes the load across its coil, for example by switching a resistor, that change is reflected into the reader's coil as a small change in impedance, and so in the voltage across the reader's antenna. No transmitter is needed on the card. The card does not switch at the bit rate; it switches a load at the subcarrier of 847.5 kHz (the carrier divided by 16), and in Type A the subcarrier is switched on and off in a Manchester pattern. In frequency terms, the reply appears as two sidebands at 13.56 MHz ± 847.5 kHz, away from the carrier itself. This is where noise matters. The reflected signal is tiny, and the strong carrier has phase and amplitude noise right next to it. Moving the reply 847.5 kHz away lets the reader filter out the carrier and use a narrow band, which improves the signal-to-noise ratio. The reflection grows with the square of the coupling factor, and the coupling falls with the cube of distance, so the reply drops with about the sixth power of distance beyond the antenna radius. That is why the data range is shorter than the power range.
The next layer is a link protocol. ISO 14443-3 handles startup and collisions. The reader sends a 7-bit REQA command; every Type A card in the field answers with ATQA. The cards have unique identifiers of 4, 7 or 10 bytes, and the reader runs a bitwise anticollision loop: it asks for bits of the UID, a collision shows up as a bit that is both 0 and 1 at the same time, and the reader fixes that bit and goes on until one card is selected. ISO 14443-4 then defines the "ISO-DEP" transport, which carries APDUs in blocks with sequence numbers, a frame size of up to 256 bytes, a waiting-time extension if the card needs longer to compute, and CRC on every frame.
On top of this link sit applications, all using the same tap. A tag with an NDEF message holds a URL or text, readable by any phone. A payment card runs the EMV contactless kernel: the reader selects an application by its AID, and the card answers with a MAC-protected transaction code computed in its secure element (the payment journey describes this exchange). An electronic passport (ICAO 9303) admits a reader only after the PACE key agreement, using data from the printed page, and then proves the data are genuine through the issuing state's digital signature, stored on the chip with a certificate chain behind it. A FIDO2 security key speaks CTAP2 over NFC. The role of signatures is always the same: the chip holds a private key that never leaves it, and the verifier trusts the public key through certificates up to a root it already has.
A phone differs from a card in two ways. Its antenna is small and surrounded by metal, so the NFC controller in card-emulation mode uses active load modulation: instead of passively switching a load, it generates the sidebands itself with a small transmitter, synchronised to the reader's carrier. And the controller routes each incoming APDU by the application ID: some AIDs go to the embedded secure element, others to the operating system and an app (host card emulation). The routing decision happens before any app code runs.

example: Estimate what a card receives. Take a reader coil of radius R = 3 cm. On its axis, the magnetic field of a loop at distance x is proportional to \( R^2 / (R^2 + x^2)^{1.5} \). Compared with the value at the coil surface, the field is 0.35 at x = 3 cm, 0.089 at 6 cm and 0.014 at 12 cm. So a card at 6 cm gets about one eleventh of the field it gets at touch, and at 12 cm only about 1.4%. Now the standard asks readers to produce at least 1.5 A/m at the card's position. That is \( B = \mu_0 H \) = 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 1.5 ≈ 1.9 µT. A card antenna with N = 5 turns and an area of 30 cm² (0.003 m²) sees an open-circuit voltage of about \( \omega N A B \) = 8.5 × 10⁷ × 5 × 0.003 × 1.9 × 10⁻⁶ ≈ 2.4 V peak. That is enough for the chip, and resonance raises it further. At the upper limit of 7.5 A/m it would be five times higher, so a shunt regulator burns the surplus as heat.
Now the timing of one tap, at 106 kbit/s. One bit lasts 128 / 13.56 MHz = 9.44 µs; a byte plus its parity bit takes 9 bits = 85 µs. The terminal turns the field on. About 5 ms later the card is awake. REQA, ATQA, anticollision and SELECT take roughly 5 to 10 ms for a single card. Then the payment dialogue: SELECT of the payment directory (about 20 bytes down, 60 bytes back), SELECT of the application, GET PROCESSING OPTIONS, and a couple of READ RECORD commands. Say 8 exchanges with 40 bytes each way on average. Each way costs 40 × 85 µs = 3.4 ms, so the radio time is 8 × 2 × 3.4 ≈ 54 ms. Add card processing (the cryptogram takes 10 to 30 ms on a small chip) and waiting times, and the tap finishes in about 100 to 200 ms. If you pull the card away at 80 ms, the dialogue stops in the middle and the terminal says "try again". Raising the rate to 424 kbit/s would cut the radio time to about 14 ms, but the card side has to support it and the sidebands must remain readable.
intuition: A torch that both lights a mirror and reads the flicker the mirror sends back.
The design is natural once you see that a reader is already a transformer with one winding missing. Power must flow from reader to card anyway; the only extra step is to notice that the card's load can be felt at the reader. So the card needs no transmitter, and the energy for the reply comes from the reader itself. The price is short range and low rate, both of which are a free security feature: a tap is deliberate. All the remaining difficulty is moved into cryptography, because the radio link itself proves nothing about who is on the other end.
mistakes: People often think NFC is a short-range Bluetooth. In fact it is not a radio link in the usual sense. The 13.56 MHz wave is 22 m long, and the two devices are coupled by a magnetic field in the near zone, like a transformer. That is why a passive card can work with no power source, and why the signal falls off so quickly with distance.
People often think a card's number is read and then charged, as with a magnetic stripe. In fact the card number (or a token) is only an identifier. The proof is a one-time cryptogram, computed by the chip over the amount, a terminal random number and a transaction counter with a key that never leaves the card. Skimming the number from a tap does not let a thief make a new payment.
People often think a phone sends the real card number when you tap. In fact a wallet stores a token (a device account number) that is valid for that device, and the payment network maps it back to the card. A leaked token is of little use outside the device and the merchant types it was provisioned for.
friction: The first limit is physical. Metal near the coil absorbs energy and detunes the antenna, so phones and cards put a ferrite sheet behind it. Two cards in the field detune each other, so a tap with a thick wallet can fail. Antenna position differs between phones. At 106 to 424 kbit/s the link is slow, so NFC starts a faster link (Bluetooth or Wi-Fi) when much data must move.
The second is security. A relay attack has no cryptographic defence on a basic EMV card, because the cryptogram is valid. The defences are procedural: spending limits that force a PIN, issuer checks on the transaction counter, timing checks (a relay adds delay), and relay-resistance protocols in newer EMV kernels that measure the round trip of a challenge. Cheap tags are another weak point: cards that send only a fixed ID can be cloned in seconds.
The third is ecosystem friction. Tag types (1 to 5), card types (A, B, F, V) and phone support differ. Payment access involves a secure element whose owner (phone maker, bank or operator) decides who can load a card, which is a business problem as much as a technical one. On Android, host card emulation is open but relies on software protection and tokens, not tamper-resistant hardware.
practice: To use NFC tags, write NDEF records with a normal phone app. An NTAG213 holds 144 bytes, an NTAG215 504 and an NTAG216 888, so a short HTTPS URL fits everywhere. Lock a tag in a public place, because anyone can otherwise overwrite it, and treat a URL read from a tag as untrusted input.
For payments, never implement the protocol yourself: use the wallet and processor SDKs, which handle tokens, EMVCo certification and scheme rules. For access control, choose chips with real cryptography (DESFire EV2/EV3 or similar, AES mutual authentication), avoid fixed-ID cards and MIFARE Classic, and diversify keys per card from a master key. For hardware design, the antenna matters more than the chip: tune it to 13.56 MHz with the real enclosure in place, measure with a network analyser, and add ferrite if there is metal behind. In a security review, test a relay scenario, not only the cryptography.
tradeoffs: Choose NFC over Bluetooth Low Energy when the action must be deliberate and need no setup, for example payment, ticket gates and sharing a Wi-Fi password: a tap is instant, needs no pairing and works with a passive tag costing a few cents. Choose BLE or ultra-wideband when you need range, a data stream or hands-free entry (a car key that unlocks as you approach). UWB measures distance by time of flight, which also defeats relay attacks, but it needs power and costs more.
Choose a secure element when the key protects money or a legal identity, and host card emulation when you need to ship quickly on many phones. A secure element gives tamper resistance and certification, but you depend on its owner for access and installation, and each load of a card is a business process. HCE lets any app answer the reader and keeps the secrets as short-lived tokens in the cloud, but the keys live in software and the model needs a network or limited-use keys. Payment wallets on Android use tokens and limits to compensate.
Choose a crypto chip (DESFire, a JavaCard secure element) over a plain memory tag (NTAG) whenever someone could gain by copying the tag. A memory tag costs cents and is fine for public content such as a poster URL; a crypto chip costs roughly ten times more and needs integration work, but cloning it requires its key.
interview: How can a passive card send data to a reader if it has no transmitter and no battery? || The reader's 13.56 MHz field powers the chip through the card's coil. To transmit, the chip switches a load across its coil at an 847.5 kHz subcarrier. Through the mutual inductance, that changes the impedance seen by the reader's coil, so the reader's antenna voltage shows sidebands at ±847.5 kHz, which a filter and demodulator turn into bits.
Why does NFC use a subcarrier, and what has noise to do with it? || The card's modulation is a tiny change on top of a strong carrier, and the carrier has phase and amplitude noise close to its own frequency. A subcarrier moves the information to a frequency offset, so the reader can use a narrow bandpass filter away from the carrier's noise and get a better signal-to-noise ratio, and the many cycles per bit allow reliable detection.
Explain how a contactless payment resists replay and card cloning. || The card computes an application cryptogram, a MAC over the amount, currency, date, a random number from the terminal and its own transaction counter, with a session key derived from a secret that never leaves the chip. A recorded response cannot be reused for another amount or terminal. Cards with public-key support also sign dynamic data, which proves the chip is genuine. The issuer rechecks the cryptogram and the counter online.
What is a relay attack, why does the cryptography not stop it, and what mitigations exist? || An attacker forwards the messages between a real card and a real reader using two devices and a network link. The cryptogram is genuine and fresh, so the issuer accepts it. Mitigations: PIN or device unlock above a limit, issuer checks on counters and location, timing limits on the exchange, and relay-resistance protocols that measure the round-trip time of a challenge, or UWB distance bounding.
quiz: At what frequency does NFC work, and what is its approximate range? || 13.56 MHz, with a working range of a few centimetres (up to about 10 cm by the standard, 1 to 4 cm in practice).
Why does the card's reply fall off much faster with distance than the reader's power does? || The field falls as \( 1/r^3 \) in the near zone. The reply is a reflection that depends on the square of the coupling, so it falls with about the sixth power of distance. Power only needs to reach the card; the weak reflection must still be detected back at the reader.
How long does one bit take at 106 kbit/s, and how many bytes can a 255-byte APDU response carry in what time? || One bit lasts 128 / 13.56 MHz ≈ 9.44 µs. A byte with its parity bit takes 9 bits ≈ 85 µs, so a 255-byte response takes about 22 ms.
Why does the payment cryptogram include a terminal random number and a transaction counter? || The random number makes each answer specific to this terminal and this attempt, and the counter makes each answer new. An attacker who records a cryptogram cannot replay it, and the issuer can detect a cloned card whose counter is out of step.
Why might a company choose DESFire cards for office access instead of the cheaper MIFARE Classic or fixed-ID badges? || MIFARE Classic's Crypto1 cipher was broken in 2008, and fixed-ID badges can be cloned by reading and replaying the number. DESFire uses AES-based mutual authentication with per-card keys, so cloning needs the key and a breach of one card does not expose others, at a higher unit price.

@@ display
value: A display turns numbers in memory into light. The program and the GPU only write bytes into an array in RAM, the framebuffer. A separate hardware block, the display controller, reads that array from start to end at a fixed rate, often 60 to 240 times a second, and sends it to the panel. This is called scanout. A 4K screen has 8.3 million pixels, each with three subpixels, and every one must get a fresh value in every refresh, in strict order, on a precise clock. No software loop could do that, so dedicated hardware does it and never stops.
The design gives the layer above a very simple contract: draw into a buffer, and what you drew appears on the next refresh. It also creates the problem that every graphics system must solve: the scanout does not wait. If a program changes the buffer while the controller is reading it, the screen shows part of the old image and part of the new one (tearing). If a frame is not ready in time, the old one is shown again (stutter). Double buffering, vertical sync (vsync), compositors and variable refresh rate are all answers to this single timing problem.
Video streaming ends here: decoded frames wait in memory until a refresh picks them up, and the video frame rate (24, 30, 60) must be fitted to the screen's refresh rate. Automatic content recognition (ACR) on smart TVs also lives here, because it samples the frames that the display pipeline is about to show.
The layers below make it possible. Digital logic provides the counters and state machines that generate row and column timing. Hardware clocks provide the pixel clock, a PLL-generated frequency (148.5 MHz for 1080p at 60 Hz) from which the refresh rate follows. DMA lets the scanout engine fetch pixels from RAM without the CPU. Photonics and the p–n junction explain the panel: backlight LEDs and OLED pixels are diodes that turn current into photons, and liquid crystals with polarizers act as light valves.
terms: Framebuffer — an array in memory that holds the colour of every pixel, for example 4 bytes per pixel for 8 bits each of red, green, blue and an unused byte.
Scanout — the continuous reading of the framebuffer by the display controller, line by line, to feed the panel.
Pixel clock — the frequency at which pixels are sent out; refresh rate is the pixel clock divided by the number of pixels per frame including blanking.
Blanking interval — time in each line and each frame when no visible pixels are sent; the horizontal blank sits between lines and the vertical blank between frames. It comes from the days of cathode-ray tubes, which needed time to move the beam back.
VSync — the signal (and the moment) that marks the start of a new frame; in software, "vsync on" means that the frame buffer swap waits for it.
Tearing — a visible seam where the upper part of the screen shows one frame and the lower part another, because the buffer changed during scanout.
Variable refresh rate (VRR) — a mode in which the display waits for the next frame, within a range such as 48 to 144 Hz, instead of refreshing on a fixed schedule.
EDID — a small data block in the monitor, 128 bytes per block, that lists the resolutions, timings and features it supports; the computer reads it over a side channel of the cable.
history: Scanning comes from television. In the 1930s Philo Farnsworth and Vladimir Zworykin built electronic cameras and picture tubes in which an electron beam swept the screen in lines, left to right, top to bottom. The picture was never stored; the beam painted it, again and again, in step with a signal. North American television (NTSC) used 525 lines at roughly 60 interlaced fields per second, tied to the 60 Hz mains frequency. The "blanking" time exists because the beam needed a short moment to fly back.
Computers reused this machinery. In the early 1970s Richard Shoup built SuperPaint at Xerox PARC, an early frame buffer with 8 bits per pixel, and the Xerox Alto (1973) had a bitmapped display held in main memory. IBM's VGA (1987) set 640 × 480 at 60 Hz with a 25.175 MHz pixel clock. VESA, formed in 1989, standardised timings; its Coordinated Video Timings (CVT, 2002) added "reduced blanking" for LCDs, which do not need a beam flyback.
The panel changed. Twisted-nematic liquid crystals (1971) and active-matrix thin-film transistors (Peter Brody at Westinghouse, 1970s) led to LCD monitors, which replaced CRTs in the 2000s. Organic LEDs (Tang and VanSlyke at Kodak, 1987) reached phones around 2010. The cable went digital: DVI (1999), HDMI (2002), DisplayPort (2006). NVIDIA's G-Sync (2013) brought variable refresh; VESA made it open as Adaptive-Sync (2014), and FreeSync (2015) and HDMI 2.1 (2017) use it. Linux added kernel mode setting in 2009 and atomic mode setting in 3.19 (2015), which lets a compositor change several things in one frame-exact commit.
how: The display pipeline starts with the framebuffer. A 2560 × 1440 screen in the common XRGB8888 format needs 2560 × 1440 × 4 = 14.7 MB per frame. The display controller has one or more planes, each with its own DMA reader: a primary plane for the desktop, an overlay plane (often used for video, which may use another pixel format such as YUV), and a small cursor plane. For each plane the DMA engine fetches pixels in raster order into a small line buffer. Because the pixel clock never waits, the DMA must keep the buffer filled; if memory is slow at that moment (the memory clock is changing, or another device hogs the bus), the buffer empties and the screen shows an underrun as a flicker or garbage line. This is why the scanout engine gets high-priority access to memory.
Next the planes are scaled, blended and passed through a colour pipeline of fixed-function hardware: a lookup table to undo the gamma encoding, a 3 × 3 matrix for colour space conversion, and a lookup table to apply the output gamma. Doing this in hardware, at the last moment, lets the colour management and the brightness slider apply to everything with no CPU cost.
The timing generator is the heart. It is a pair of counters driven by the pixel clock: one counts pixels along a line, the other counts lines. At fixed counts it raises horizontal sync, vertical sync and a "data enable" signal that marks visible pixels. For 1080p60 (the CTA-861 timing) each line has 2200 pixel slots, of which 1920 are visible, and each frame has 1125 lines, of which 1080 are visible. The pixel clock is 148.5 MHz, and 148.5 MHz ÷ (2200 × 1125) = 60.00 Hz. The refresh rate is an arithmetic result of a clock frequency and two counter limits. The clock comes from a phase-locked loop on a crystal reference; video modes often run at 59.94 Hz because the pixel clock is divided by 1.001.
Then the encoder serialises the pixels onto the cable. HDMI sends three data channels and a clock, with 10 bits per channel per pixel clock (8b/10b TMDS) until HDMI 2.0, and fixed-rate-link packets up to 48 Gbit/s in HDMI 2.1. DisplayPort uses one to four lanes at 1.62 to 8.1 Gbit/s each (HBR3: 32.4 Gbit/s raw, 25.92 usable), and DisplayPort 2.x uses 128b/132b coding at 10, 13.5 or 20 Gbit/s per lane (up to 77.37 Gbit/s usable). Display Stream Compression (DSC), a VESA scheme, shrinks the stream roughly 3:1 with a visually lossless result. When you plug in a monitor, the computer reads its EDID over a slow I²C channel (DDC), picks a mode, and the driver programs the timing generator and the link: this is a "mode set".
Inside the monitor, a timing controller (TCON) receives the stream, may rescale it and apply overdrive, and drives the panel, a matrix of rows and columns. Gate drivers switch on one row of TFTs at a time; the source drivers put an analogue voltage on every column (7,680 for a 2560-wide RGB panel). On a 1440p panel at 60 Hz a row stays on for about 16.7 ms ÷ 1481 lines = 11 µs, long enough to charge each pixel's capacitor. Then the row is switched off and the capacitor holds the voltage until the next frame. This "sample and hold" avoids flicker but causes motion blur, because the eye tracks a moving object while the image stands still. In an LCD the voltage twists liquid crystals that turn the polarisation of light from an LED backlight (a p–n junction diode with a phosphor), so more or less light passes the second polariser and the colour filter. In an OLED the voltage sets the current in each pixel's own diode, and light comes from electrons and holes recombining in an organic layer.
Finally, software must hand frames to this machine without tearing. The kernel (KMS) exposes the controller as planes, CRTCs (timing generators), encoders and connectors. A compositor draws a frame into a new buffer and submits an atomic commit: "show this buffer on this plane at the next vblank". The address goes into shadow registers that the hardware copies to the active registers only during the vertical blank, so scanout never sees a half-updated pointer. A GPU fence ensures the GPU finished drawing. After the flip the kernel sends a page-flip event with a timestamp, which the compositor uses to schedule the next frame. With two buffers, one shown and one drawn, this is double buffering, and the swap at vblank is vsync.

example: Take 1080p at 60 Hz with a 4-byte pixel. The framebuffer holds 1920 × 1080 × 4 = 8.3 MB. The controller reads it 60 times a second, so scanout alone uses 8.3 MB × 60 ≈ 500 MB/s of memory bandwidth; for 4K at 60 Hz it is 33 MB × 60 ≈ 2 GB/s, and at 120 Hz about 4 GB/s. On a phone with DRAM bandwidth of 30 to 60 GB/s that is a noticeable share, which is why phone chips compress framebuffers and why laptop panels have a self-refresh mode in which the panel keeps its own copy and the memory can sleep.
The refresh arithmetic: 2200 × 1125 = 2,475,000 pixel slots per frame; at 148.5 MHz that is 16.67 ms. The visible part takes 1080 lines × 2200 slots ÷ 148.5 MHz = 16.0 ms, and the vertical blank 45 lines = 0.67 ms. A line lasts 14.8 µs. Visible data rate on the cable (TMDS, 3 channels × 10 bits × 148.5 MHz) is 4.455 Gbit/s, though only 2.99 Gbit/s are actual pixel bits (1920 × 1080 × 24 × 60).
Now tearing. Suppose the GPU finishes a new frame and swaps the buffer pointer immediately, without waiting for vblank, 5.3 ms after the scan started. The scan has covered 5.3 ÷ 16.0 of the visible lines, about line 358. Lines 1 to 358 came from the old frame and the rest come from the new one. If the camera pans sideways, there is a visible jump at line 358. With vsync, the swap waits until the vertical blank, and no seam appears.
The price of waiting: suppose the app needs 18 ms per frame on a 60 Hz screen with double buffering. The first frame finishes just after the vblank at 16.7 ms, so it waits for the next one at 33.3 ms, and the app is blocked meanwhile because both buffers are busy. The result is 30 frames per second, not 55. A third buffer (or a "mailbox" present mode) restores about 55 fps at the cost of one more frame of latency. Variable refresh does better: the display waits for the frame, giving 55.6 Hz with no tear and almost no added delay.
intuition: A printer that redraws the whole page, top to bottom, 60 to 240 times a second, whether or not anything changed.
The idea is natural because a screen has a huge number of cells and only one cable. The cheapest way to send millions of values over a few wires is a fixed order on a fixed clock, so no addresses are needed: the position of a value in time is its address. The display controller is a pair of counters that turn a memory array into such a stream, and the panel is a pair of counters that turn it back into rows and columns. Tearing, vsync and frame pacing all come from one fact: the scan is a hard real-time process that never waits, and the programs that fill the buffer are not real-time.
mistakes: People often think the refresh rate and the frame rate are the same thing. In fact the refresh rate belongs to the display (how often it reads the buffer), and the frame rate belongs to the program (how often it makes a new image). A 60 fps video on a 144 Hz screen shows each frame 2 or 3 times in an uneven pattern (2.4 on average), which is a small judder; a game at 100 fps on a 60 Hz screen without VRR shows some frames only partly (tearing) or drops them.
People often think an LCD at 60 Hz flickers like a CRT. In fact the LCD backlight is on constantly and the pixel holds its value for the whole frame; the "refresh" only rewrites the stored voltages. There is no flicker from the refresh, but the hold causes motion blur. OLED phones can flicker for another reason: they dim by pulsing the pixel current (PWM) at a few hundred to a few thousand hertz.
People often think "1 ms response time" means a sharp image in motion. In fact the number is a best-case grey-to-grey transition of the liquid crystals. What the eye sees in motion depends mostly on the hold time: at 60 Hz each image is shown for 16.7 ms, and a moving object tracked by the eye smears by about its speed times that time (at 960 pixels per second, 16 pixels). Faster pixels do not help until the refresh rate is higher or the backlight is strobed.
friction: The first cost is latency. Between drawing and a changed pixel there are the compositor's frame, the wait for the next vblank (0 to 16.7 ms at 60 Hz), the scanout itself (the bottom line is shown a whole frame after the top line), and the panel's response time. Typical input-to-photon delay on a desktop is 20 to 50 ms, and each extra buffer adds a frame. Higher refresh rates cut all of these terms in proportion.
The second is bandwidth and power. Pixels per second grow with resolution × refresh × colour depth, and cables, memory and chips must grow with them: 8K at 120 Hz with HDR needs well over 100 Gbit/s uncompressed, so DSC or chroma subsampling becomes standard. The display is also a top power consumer in a laptop or phone, hence variable-rate panels (1 to 120 Hz) and panel self-refresh.
The third is content that does not fit the clock. Film at 24 frames per second on a 60 Hz screen needs 3:2 pulldown (frames held for 3 and 2 refreshes alternately), which causes judder; 120 Hz or a 24 Hz mode fixes it. Panels have their own faults: OLED burn-in from static logos, LCD backlight bleed and blooming. Link negotiation is fragile: a wrong EDID or poor cable can silently give a lower mode or a black screen after hot-plug.
practice: Know the numbers of your system. Check the active mode and the refresh rate: on Linux `modetest -c` or `drm_info` lists connectors and modes, `xrandr --verbose` shows the timings, and `edid-decode` prints the monitor's capabilities from `/sys/class/drm/*/edid`. The `cvt` tool gives the timing line for any resolution. If a monitor is stuck at a low mode, suspect the cable, the port or the EDID before the driver.
For applications, draw when the display is ready, not on a sleep timer: requestAnimationFrame in a browser, frame callbacks and the presentation-time protocol on Wayland. Keep work per frame well below the budget (16.7 ms at 60 Hz, 8.3 ms at 120 Hz) and animate with properties the compositor handles on the GPU (transform and opacity).
Choose the present mode by the goal. For video, match the refresh rate to the content (23.976 or 24 Hz for film). For games, use VRR if available and cap the frame rate a little below the maximum refresh. Measure latency with a high-speed camera or photodiode, not software timers.
In TV and ad-tech work, treat the pipeline as stages with different clocks: decode, on-screen display, scaler and panel. ACR samples frames from the video pipeline before the panel, so it sees what the viewer is about to see, but its sample times follow the decoder's clock, not the moment the light leaves the glass.
tradeoffs: Choose vsync on with double buffering when you want clean frames and your renderer reliably finishes within one refresh. You lose some latency, and if a frame is late the frame rate drops to half. Choose a mailbox or triple-buffered mode when frame times vary and you want a smooth rate without tearing; the price is one more frame of latency and more memory. Choose vsync off only where latency matters more than clean frames, such as a competitive game.
Choose variable refresh rate for content with varying frame times (games, interactive tools). It removes tearing and the half-rate cliff and has near-zero added delay, but it works only within the panel's range (for example 48 to 144 Hz), needs a compatible monitor, cable and driver, and some panels flicker in brightness as the rate changes. Choose a fixed rate for video and multi-monitor setups.
Choose OLED for contrast, near-instant pixel response and wide viewing angle; choose LCD with local dimming (mini-LED) for sustained high brightness and static content with no burn-in. OLED can burn in and dims with PWM; LCD has limited contrast, blooming and slower transitions.
interview: Trace what happens from a program drawing a pixel to light leaving the screen. || The program renders into a buffer; the compositor combines windows into a new framebuffer and submits an atomic commit. A fence waits for the GPU; the flip is latched at the next vblank. The display controller DMAs the framebuffer line by line, scales and colour-converts it, serialises it over HDMI or DisplayPort, and the panel's TCON drives rows and columns so the backlight and liquid crystals (or OLED pixels) emit light.
What causes tearing, and why does vsync with double buffering fix it? What does it cost? || Tearing occurs when the buffer being scanned out changes mid-frame, so lines above the swap come from the old frame and below from the new one. Vsync delays the swap to the vertical blank. The cost is waiting: a frame that misses the deadline waits a full refresh, so an 18 ms renderer on a 60 Hz screen drops to 30 fps.
How does variable refresh rate work technically? || The display's refresh is controlled by the pixel clock and the vertical blank length. With VRR, the source keeps the active lines at the normal speed but stretches the vertical blanking (front porch) until the next frame is ready, within a minimum and maximum. The panel refreshes when the frame arrives. Below the minimum rate the source repeats frames (low framerate compensation) to stay in range.
Compute the uncompressed bandwidth of 4K at 144 Hz with 10-bit RGB, and say which link can carry it. || 3840 × 2160 × 144 × 30 bits ≈ 35.8 Gbit/s plus about 5% blanking, so about 37.5 Gbit/s. DisplayPort 1.4 (25.92 usable) cannot without DSC. HDMI 2.1 (about 42.6 usable) and DisplayPort 2.1 (up to 77.37) can.
quiz: What is the refresh rate of a mode with a 148.5 MHz pixel clock, 2200 pixels per line and 1125 lines per frame? || 148.5 MHz ÷ (2200 × 1125) = 60 Hz.
Why does a display still have blanking intervals when LCDs need no beam flyback? || The timing format was inherited from CRTs and the hardware and standards kept it. The time is still used for sync, audio and control data and for latching a new frame pointer; reduced-blanking timings shorten it.
Why does a swap that is not synchronised with vblank cause tearing? || The controller keeps reading the buffer line by line during the visible period. If the pointer changes in the middle, the lines read before the swap show the old frame and the lines after it show the new frame, giving a visible seam.
A game takes 18 ms per frame on a 60 Hz display with vsync and double buffering. What frame rate do you see, and how can you improve it? || 30 fps, because each frame misses one vblank and waits for the next. Use triple buffering or a mailbox mode (about 55 fps, one more frame of latency), VRR (about 55.6 Hz without extra waiting), or make the frame cheaper than 16.7 ms.
Why is an LCD that refreshes at 60 Hz free of flicker, yet still blurry in motion? || The backlight stays on and each pixel holds its voltage for the whole frame, so there is no flicker. The hold means an image stays constant for 16.7 ms while the eye is moving, which smears a moving object across the retina.
