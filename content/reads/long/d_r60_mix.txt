@@ roadrouting
value: Road-network routing answers "what is the fastest way from A to B?" for a map with tens of millions of intersections, in well under a millisecond, while traffic changes every minute. The textbook answer is Dijkstra's algorithm. On the road network of Western Europe (18 million intersections), a single Dijkstra query reads about 9.3 million nodes and takes roughly 2.2 seconds on one core. A navigation service that answers 10,000 queries per second would need more than 20,000 cores for this alone, and a dispatch system that must compare 1,000 drivers with 1,000 riders would wait for minutes.
Routing engines solve this by splitting the work in two. In an offline preprocessing phase they study the graph once and store extra information, mainly shortcut edges. In the online query phase they use that information so that the search touches a few hundred nodes, not millions. With contraction hierarchies (CH), the same Europe query scans about 280 nodes and takes about 110 microseconds, around 20,000 times faster than Dijkstra, after about 5 minutes of preprocessing (Bast et al., 2016 survey, one core of a 2010-era server).
For the layer above, this is the engine behind map apps, delivery and ride-hailing dispatch, ETA estimates, isochrone maps ("everywhere I can reach in 20 minutes") and map matching of GPS traces. It combines with spatial indexes, which find the nearest road to a point, while routing works on the road graph itself. The layers below supply the machinery: graph algorithms (L3) give Dijkstra and bidirectional search, graph theory (L4) explains why road graphs have a hierarchy that can be exploited, and trees, heaps and tries (L3) give the priority queue that decides which node to explore next.
terms: Edge weight — the cost of driving one road segment, usually travel time (length divided by speed, plus turn penalties).
Settling a node — fixing its final shortest distance from the source in Dijkstra's algorithm; the number of settled nodes is the search cost.
Contraction — removing a node from the graph and adding shortcut edges so that distances between the other nodes do not change.
Shortcut — an edge that stands for a longer path through contracted nodes; its weight is the length of that path.
Witness search — a small local search that checks whether another path, avoiding the node being removed, is as short as the path through it; if so, no shortcut is needed.
Node order (rank) — the order in which nodes are contracted; important nodes (highway junctions) come last and get the highest rank.
Upward edge — an edge that goes from a node to a node of higher rank; CH queries only follow upward edges, from both ends.
Customization — a fast second preprocessing step that recomputes shortcut weights after edge weights change, without repeating the whole preprocessing.
history: Edsger Dijkstra published his shortest-path algorithm in 1959, and the A* algorithm (Hart, Nilsson and Raphael, 1968) added a heuristic that guides the search toward the target. Pohl (1971) proposed searching from both ends at once. For decades these were enough for small maps, but not for national road networks, which became digital in the 1990s and 2000s.
Around 2005 the research community made routing a competition. The 9th DIMACS Implementation Challenge (2005 to 2006) published large road graphs, including the United States and later Europe, and many groups raced to speed up queries. Goldberg and Harrelson (2005) introduced ALT, which uses distances to a few "landmark" nodes and the triangle inequality to build a strong A* heuristic. Sanders and Schultes (2005) introduced highway hierarchies, which stop exploring small roads once the search is far from the endpoints. Bast, Funke, Sanders and Schultes (2007) showed that long trips pass through a small set of "transit nodes", so a query can be a table lookup.
Geisberger, Sanders, Schultes and Delling published contraction hierarchies in 2008. It was simpler than highway hierarchies, quick to preprocess and fast to query, and so it spread widely: open-source engines such as OSRM and GraphHopper implement it. Hub labeling (Abraham, Delling, Goldberg and Werneck, 2011) goes further, with queries below one microsecond but tens of gigabytes of memory. Real traffic was the next problem, because CH preprocessing depends on the weights. Customizable Route Planning (CRP; Delling, Goldberg, Pajor and Werneck, 2011), used in Bing Maps, and Customizable Contraction Hierarchies (Dibbelt, Strasser and Wagner, 2014) split preprocessing into a slow, weight-independent part and a fast part that is repeated whenever weights change.
how: The system has four parts: a graph model, a baseline search, the preprocessed hierarchy, and a way to handle changing weights.
Step 1: Model the roads as a graph (graph theory, L4). Each intersection is a node. Each road segment is a directed edge, with weight equal to travel time. Turn restrictions and turn penalties ("no left turn", "20 seconds to cross traffic") are handled by turning each road segment into a node and each allowed turn into an edge, which about doubles the size. Road graphs are sparse (about 2.3 edges per node on average) and nearly planar. These two properties matter: planar graphs have small separators (a set of about \( \sqrt{n} \) nodes splits the graph in two), and roads have a hierarchy, since a long trip leaves the local streets, joins a few main roads, and returns to local streets. Both properties make shortcuts effective.
Step 2: The baseline is Dijkstra (graph algorithms and heaps, L3). Dijkstra keeps a priority queue of nodes by tentative distance, repeatedly removes the closest one, settles it, and relaxes its outgoing edges. With a binary heap the cost is \( O((n + m)\log n) \). The search spreads like a circle around the source, so for a long trip it settles about half of the graph before it reaches the target (9.3 million of 18 million nodes in the Europe benchmark). Bidirectional search runs a second search from the target backward and stops when they meet, which settles two smaller circles and saves about half of the work, a modest gain. A* with straight-line distance helps little on travel time, because a highway makes the straight-line bound far too optimistic.
Step 3: Build the hierarchy by contraction. Choose an order of nodes. Take the first node \( v \) and remove it. For every pair of neighbours \( u \to v \to w \), compute the length \( d = w(u,v) + w(v,w) \) and run a witness search from \( u \) in the remaining graph, limited to length \( d \) and ignoring \( v \). If it finds a path to \( w \) of length at most \( d \), the path through \( v \) is not needed. If not, add a shortcut \( u \to w \) with weight \( d \) and remember \( v \) as its middle node. Then contract the next node, and so on. The order is chosen greedily by an importance score; the main term is the edge difference (shortcuts that would be added minus edges removed), plus a count of already contracted neighbours so that contraction spreads evenly. Scores are updated lazily with a priority queue. Unimportant nodes (dead ends, residential streets) go first and need few shortcuts; highway junctions go last. The result is the original graph plus shortcuts, with every node having a rank. On road networks the number of shortcuts is of the same order as the number of edges, so memory stays small (0.4 GiB for Europe in the survey).
Step 4: Query with upward searches only. To find a route from \( s \) to \( t \), run Dijkstra forward from \( s \) and backward from \( t \), but each search follows only edges that go to higher-ranked nodes. The key fact is that for any pair of nodes there is a shortest path in the augmented graph that first goes only up in rank and then only down. The highest-ranked node on the path is the meeting point, and contraction guarantees that a shortcut replaces every dip below it. So the answer is the minimum over nodes \( v \) settled by both searches of \( d_f(v) + d_b(v) \). Each upward search climbs toward the few important nodes and ends after about a hundred nodes. A cheap extra rule called stall-on-demand drops nodes that can be reached more cheaply through a higher node. To output the route, each shortcut is expanded recursively through its stored middle node.
Step 5: Live traffic. The shortcut weights depend on the edge weights, so when a road slows down, some shortcuts become wrong. Rebuilding everything takes minutes (5 minutes for Europe in the survey), which is too slow if speeds update every minute. Three approaches are used. (a) Customizable methods: CRP cuts the graph into cells with small boundaries (using the separator property), and stores for each cell the distances between its boundary nodes; when weights change, only these small tables are recomputed, in seconds with several cores, and queries (about 1.65 ms for Europe in the survey) run on the cell overlay. OSRM offers this as its "multi-level Dijkstra" mode. CCH does the same with a weight-independent node order, so the shortcut structure stays and only the weights are recomputed. (b) Time-dependent routing: edge weights are functions of the departure time (a road that is slow from 8 to 9 a.m.), and the speed forecasts come from historical profiles and live probes. The search must keep the property that leaving later never arrives earlier. (c) Smaller rebuilds: update only the affected region or accept some staleness and re-check candidate routes.
Step 6: Many queries at once. Dispatch needs distances between many sources and many targets. With CH, compute the upward search from each target once and store its results in buckets at the nodes it reaches, then run an upward search from each source and combine with the buckets on the way. For 1,000 × 1,000 that is 2,000 small searches, not 1,000,000 queries.

example: Contract the node \( v \) in the diagram. It has three neighbours: \( u \) (edge weight 2), \( w \) (3) and \( x \) (4). There is also a direct edge \( u \)–\( w \) of weight 4. Pairs: (u, w) through v costs 2 + 3 = 5. The witness search finds the direct edge with cost 4, which is shorter, so no shortcut. (u, x) through v costs 2 + 4 = 6, and the only path from u to x without v does not exist, so add a shortcut u–x of weight 6. (w, x) costs 3 + 4 = 7; the path w–u–x does not exist without v (u–x does not exist yet), so add a shortcut w–x of weight 7. Contracting v removed 3 edges and added 2 shortcuts: edge difference −1, so v is a good node to contract early. A query from \( u \) to \( x \) now finds the weight 6 directly, and the route is expanded back through the middle node \( v \).
Now the size of the savings. In the Europe benchmark, Dijkstra settles 9,326,696 nodes and takes 2.195 s. CH settles 280 nodes and takes 110 µs. That is a reduction of 33,000 times in nodes and 20,000 times in time. If your service handles 5,000 queries per second, Dijkstra would need about 11,000 cores (5,000 × 2.2 s); CH needs 5,000 × 110 µs = 0.55 core-seconds per second, so one core is more than enough.
Next, a dispatch matrix. A ride-hailing system wants travel times from 1,000 drivers to 1,000 waiting riders, which are 1,000,000 pairs. At 110 µs each, plain CH queries take 110 seconds. With the bucket method it needs 2,000 upward searches of about a hundred nodes each, with a scan over the buckets at each node; this takes a fraction of a second. The same search, on a stale graph, would return wrong ETAs; so after each traffic update, the customization step must finish quickly. With CRP-style customization in a few seconds, a one-minute traffic refresh is affordable, while a full CH rebuild of 5 minutes is not.
intuition: To drive across a continent you do not look at every street; you find the nearest highway, ride it, and leave near the destination, and contraction hierarchies write that habit into the map ahead of time.
The idea is natural because road networks already have levels. Dead ends and residential lanes matter only near the ends of a trip, while highway junctions matter for many trips. Contraction makes this explicit: it removes the unimportant nodes first and records, as a shortcut, what they were used for. Then a search from the start only needs to climb toward the important nodes, and a search from the target does the same; where the two climbs meet is the best route. The work moves from the thousands of queries to the single preprocessing step, which is the standard trade of caches and indexes.
mistakes: People often think a faster routing engine uses a better version of Dijkstra. In fact the fastest engines do not run Dijkstra on the original graph at all; they run a very small search on a graph with extra shortcuts that were computed before any query.
People often think A* with straight-line distance is enough. In fact travel time makes the heuristic weak: the bound must assume the fastest road speed everywhere, so it is far below the true time, and A* still scans millions of nodes. Landmark-based A* (ALT) is much better but still far slower than CH.
People often think precomputed routes cannot handle traffic. In fact weight-independent structures (CRP, CCH) separate the layout from the weights, so a traffic update only recomputes small tables or shortcut weights, in seconds. Plain CH is the one that needs a full rebuild.
People often think the hierarchy stores all paths. In fact it stores shortcuts of the order of the number of original edges. Route lengths are not stored; only the up-down structure is, and paths are rebuilt by expanding shortcuts.
People often think the shortest path is the same as the best route. In fact real products optimise for time with turn costs, tolls, ferries, road classes, user preferences and reliability, which changes the weights and sometimes needs several criteria; it also needs alternative routes, which a single shortest path does not give.
friction: Preprocessing and freshness pull in opposite directions. The more a method precomputes (hub labels with 18.8 GiB for Europe), the faster the query and the longer the update. A system with per-vehicle preferences (avoid tolls, trucks with height limits) or per-minute traffic cannot keep one precomputed structure for every profile, so each profile needs its own CH or customization, which multiplies memory.
Time-dependent and real-world details are harder than the clean model. Weight functions must be FIFO; predicted speeds are wrong when an accident happens; ETAs for 30-minute trips depend on traffic at the time of arrival on each road, not at the start. Turn costs, one-way roads, roundabouts, tolls and map errors make the graph messier, and bad map data (a missing one-way sign) gives a confident wrong route.
The method also depends on road-like structure. Dense urban grids with many equally good routes give more shortcuts, and graphs without a hierarchy (some public transport networks, with timetables) do not work well with plain CH; they need dedicated algorithms. Contraction can create a few high-degree nodes that make later contraction slow. Multi-criteria queries (time, cost, comfort) have many incomparable results, so the result set can grow large.
practice: Use an existing engine unless routing is your product: OSRM (C++, supports CH and multi-level Dijkstra), GraphHopper (Java, supports CH and flexible mode), Valhalla (tiled, hierarchical A* with dynamic costing) or a commercial API. Start from OpenStreetMap extracts, and check how the engine handles the profile you need (car, truck, bike) and whether it can reload traffic without a restart.
Choose by your update rate. If weights are fixed (planning, simulation), use plain CH and rebuild nightly. If traffic changes every few minutes, use a customizable method (OSRM's multi-level mode or CCH) and time the customization step. If you need many-to-many matrices (dispatch, delivery batching), use an engine with a table or matrix API that uses buckets, and batch requests.
Snap points to the road graph carefully: a GPS point near a parallel road or a service lane gives a different route, so snap with heading and use map matching for traces. Cache only what is stable, such as matrices for fixed depots. Validate against real driving times: compare predicted ETAs to measured trip times by road class and hour, and correct the speed profiles rather than the algorithm. If you build your own, test the up-down search against plain Dijkstra on random pairs, because bugs in witness searches silently return slightly too long routes.
tradeoffs: Choose plain CH when weights are fixed or change rarely (a nightly rebuild is fine) and the profile count is small; you get queries near 100 microseconds with small memory. You give up fast reweighting: a traffic update means a rebuild of minutes for a continent.
Choose CRP or CCH (customizable methods) when weights change every minute or per request, as with live traffic, closures or per-customer penalties. Queries are slower (about 1 to 2 ms for Europe in the survey, 15 times slower than CH) and preprocessing is a bigger design, but updates take seconds.
Choose hub labeling or transit-node routing when you need sub-10-microsecond queries and have memory to spare (18.8 GiB for Europe in the survey for HL), for example matrices with billions of pairs on a static graph. You give up flexibility and fast updates.
Choose A* or ALT, or plain Dijkstra on a hierarchical tile graph (Valhalla style), when the cost function is complex or changes with every request and you cannot precompute; you give up speed (tens of milliseconds to seconds) for full flexibility.
Choose time-dependent routing when predicted congestion changes the best route during the trip, such as a 90-minute drive across a city at rush hour; choose static current-speed weights for short trips where conditions barely change. Time dependence needs profiles and bigger preprocessing, and it is only as good as the traffic forecast.
interview: Why is plain Dijkstra too slow for a global map, and what does the offline/online split buy? || Dijkstra settles about half the graph for a long trip (about 9 million nodes and 2 seconds for Western Europe), and cost per query is multiplied by the request rate. Preprocessing builds a structure once, so each query touches only hundreds of nodes. The price is preprocessing time and memory, and a structure that depends on the weights.
Explain how contraction hierarchies work and why the query is correct. || Nodes are contracted in order of importance. Removing a node adds a shortcut for each neighbour pair whose shortest path used it, unless a witness path is as short. For any pair, a shortest path exists in the augmented graph that goes up in rank and then down, with its highest node as the meeting point. So two upward Dijkstra searches that meet at the minimum of forward plus backward distance are exact.
Live traffic changes edge weights every minute. How do you keep CH-style speed? || Plain CH needs a rebuild of minutes. Use a weight-independent structure: CRP partitions the graph into cells and keeps boundary-to-boundary distance tables, and CCH keeps a separator-based order with fixed shortcuts. A metric update only recomputes the table or shortcut weights in seconds, at the cost of queries about 10 to 15 times slower than CH.
How would you compute travel times between 5,000 drivers and 20,000 orders? || A matrix of 100 million pairs is too big for pairwise queries at 100 µs (about 3 hours). Use the bucket-based many-to-many with CH: run upward searches from the 20,000 targets and store them in buckets, then search from the 5,000 sources and scan the buckets. Also restrict the matrix by a spatial prefilter (only nearby pairs) and parallelise.
Your routes are 5% longer than the optimum for some queries. What do you check? || Check witness searches (a limited search that misses a witness only adds unneeded shortcuts, but one that wrongly finds a witness drops a needed one), the stall-on-demand rule, rounding of weights, turn-cost modelling, and the stop condition of the bidirectional search. Compare against plain Dijkstra on random pairs to find a failing case.
Why does contraction work on road networks but not on arbitrary graphs? || Roads have a hierarchy and small separators (low highway dimension): a few important nodes cover most long shortest paths. In random or dense graphs, contracting a node creates many shortcuts, so the graph becomes dense and the query space grows.
quiz: What do the forward and backward searches in a CH query follow? || Only edges that lead to nodes of higher rank. They meet at a node, and the answer is the smallest sum of forward and backward distance over nodes reached by both.
Why must a witness search be run before adding a shortcut? || The shortcut is only needed if the path through the removed node is the unique shortest path between its neighbours. If another path of equal or smaller length exists, a shortcut would just add edges and slow the queries.
Western Europe: Dijkstra settles 9.3 million nodes, CH settles 280. By roughly what factor are the node counts reduced? || 9,326,696 / 280 ≈ 33,000 times fewer nodes.
A service gets 2,000 route requests per second. How many cores for Dijkstra at 2.2 s per query, and for CH at 110 µs? || Dijkstra: 2,000 × 2.2 = 4,400 core-seconds per second, so about 4,400 cores. CH: 2,000 × 0.00011 = 0.22, so a single core is enough.
When would you choose CRP-style customization over plain CH? || When edge weights change often, for example live traffic every minute or per-request penalties. CRP queries are slower (about 1.65 ms vs 110 µs for Europe) but weights can be refreshed in seconds without a full rebuild.

@@ cellular
value: A cellular network lets millions of phones share a limited amount of radio spectrum while moving at highway speed. Radio waves are a shared medium: if two nearby transmitters use the same frequency at the same time, both are lost. The cellular idea is to cut a city into many small cells, each served by its own base station (a tower with antennas), to reuse the same frequencies in cells that are far enough apart, and to hand a phone over from one cell to the next as it moves. More cells means more total capacity from the same spectrum, which is why networks keep adding small sites.
Each cell is a scheduling problem. In 4G LTE a base station divides its bandwidth (for example 20 MHz) into 1,200 narrow subcarriers and 1-millisecond time slots. Every millisecond it decides which phone gets which pieces of this grid and which modulation to use, based on the signal quality each phone reports. A 20 MHz LTE carrier with good signal and four antennas can reach about 300 to 400 Mbit/s in the best case; a typical user in the middle of a busy cell gets a few Mbit/s to tens of Mbit/s. 5G NR keeps the same design, with wider carriers (up to 100 MHz below 7 GHz and 400 MHz in millimetre-wave bands), shorter slots and many more antennas.
For the layer above, this is the network that carries mobile apps, video and ads. Mobile bandwidth changes from second to second as users move and as other users join the cell, which is why video players use adaptive bitrate streaming. The layers below make it work: Maxwell's electromagnetism (L5) says how radio waves travel and fade, thermal noise (L5) sets the weakest usable signal, information theory (L4) gives the capacity limit and the coding methods that approach it, the FFT (L3) makes OFDM cheap to compute, and IP routing (L3) is how the core network carries the user's packets to the Internet while the phone moves.
terms: Cell and base station — a cell is the area served by one base station; the base station (eNodeB in 4G, gNB in 5G) is the radio unit that talks to phones.
Spectrum — the range of radio frequencies a network may use, licensed from the regulator; low bands (700 MHz) travel far, high bands (3.5 GHz, 28 GHz) carry more data over shorter distances.
OFDM — Orthogonal Frequency Division Multiplexing; the data is sent in parallel on many narrow subcarriers (15 kHz apart in LTE) so that each is easy to decode.
Resource block — the unit the scheduler hands out: in LTE, 12 subcarriers (180 kHz) for one 0.5 ms slot.
SNR — signal-to-noise ratio; the received signal power divided by noise plus interference power, usually in decibels (dB).
Link adaptation — choosing, for each phone and each millisecond, the modulation (QPSK to 256-QAM) and coding rate that the current SNR can support.
Handover — moving an active connection from one cell to another without dropping it.
Core network — the part behind the radio network that authenticates phones, tracks their location and tunnels their IP packets to the Internet (EPC in 4G, 5G Core in 5G).
history: Mobile radio telephones existed from the 1940s but used one big transmitter per city and could serve only a few dozen calls at once. Bell Labs engineers described the cellular concept in the late 1940s and 1960s. Martin Cooper at Motorola made the first handheld call in 1973. The first commercial cellular systems followed: NTT in Japan in 1979, and AMPS in the United States in 1983 (analogue, voice only).
Second generation (2G) went digital. GSM, standardised in Europe, started commercial service in 1991 and brought encrypted calls, SIM cards and text messages. Third generation (3G, around 2001) added packet data, and 4G LTE, specified in 3GPP Release 8 (2008), was the first design built for data from the start, with IP everywhere and OFDM. The first commercial LTE networks started in Stockholm and Oslo in December 2009. 3GPP, the standards body that groups the telecom standards organisations, has defined each generation in numbered releases since 1998.
The 5G New Radio standard was frozen in Release 15 (the stand-alone version in mid 2018), with the first commercial networks in 2019. It added flexible slot lengths, millimetre-wave bands, massive MIMO (many antennas), LDPC and polar error-correcting codes, and a new service-based core that supports network slicing. Releases 16 to 18 added industrial, low-latency and satellite features, and work on 6G studies began within 3GPP in the mid 2020s. The key science did not change: Shannon's capacity formula (1948) and the OFDM and coding techniques developed since the 1960s and 1990s are the base.
how: A phone call or a video stream passes through four parts: the radio link, the scheduler, the mobility machinery, and the core network.
Step 1: The radio channel (electromagnetism and noise, L5). Radio waves from the tower spread out, so the received power falls with the square of distance and also with the square of the frequency, in free space. In decibels, the free-space loss is \( 20\log_{10} d_{\text{km}} + 20\log_{10} f_{\text{MHz}} + 32.4 \). Buildings add 20 to 40 dB more. Waves also bounce, so a phone receives several delayed copies of the signal (multipath), and it fades as it moves. At the receiver, thermal noise of \( kTB \) (about −174 dBm per Hz) sets the floor: a 20 MHz channel has about −101 dBm of noise, and a phone receiver adds 7 dB or so. Everything the radio does is a fight to keep the signal above this floor, and to keep other cells' signals (interference) from adding to it.
Step 2: What the channel can carry (information theory, L4). Shannon's formula gives the most a channel can carry:
\[ C = B \log_2 (1 + \text{SNR}) \]
where \( B \) is the bandwidth in hertz. Doubling bandwidth doubles capacity, while doubling the signal power adds only about 1 bit per second per hertz at high SNR. This is why operators buy spectrum and add cells: the second is cheap compared with the first. Engineering moves the system toward this limit with strong error-correcting codes (turbo codes in LTE, LDPC and polar codes in 5G) and with hybrid ARQ, in which a phone that fails to decode a packet keeps the soft information and combines it with a retransmission; in LTE the retransmission cycle takes 8 ms.
Step 3: OFDM and the FFT (FFT, L3). A single fast stream suffers from multipath, because delayed echoes of one symbol smear over the next. OFDM cuts the channel into many narrow subcarriers, each carrying a slow stream whose symbol lasts \( 1/\Delta f = 66.7\ \mu s \) for 15 kHz spacing, much longer than the echo delays. A short copy of the end of each symbol (the cyclic prefix, 4.7 µs) is placed in front, so echoes fall inside it and do not mix symbols. The subcarriers are orthogonal, so they can overlap in frequency without interfering. The transmitter creates the sum of 1,200 subcarriers with one inverse FFT of size 2048 (sample rate 30.72 MHz for 20 MHz), and the receiver uses an FFT to separate them. A direct computation would need about \( N^2 \approx 4\) million operations per symbol; the FFT needs about \( N \log_2 N \approx 22{,}000 \). Without the FFT, a phone could not afford OFDM.
Step 4: The scheduler. Every 1 ms (a subframe in LTE) the base station chooses which resource blocks go to which phone. Phones report channel quality; the base station picks the modulation and coding (QPSK at 2 bits per symbol up to 256-QAM at 8 bits) that gives a block error rate of about 10%, and the HARQ retries fix the rest. A common rule is proportional fair: favour a phone whose current rate is high relative to its own long-term average. A phone with a good signal gets more bits per resource block, and a phone at the cell edge gets few. Multiple antennas help: MIMO sends several independent streams at once when the channel allows it, and beamforming points energy at a phone. 5G mid-band base stations use 32 to 64 antenna elements.
Step 5: Mobility. An idle phone listens to the network and updates the core when it enters a new tracking area (a group of cells), so the network knows where to page it. A connected phone measures the neighbouring cells and sends a report when a neighbour is better than the serving cell by some margin for a set time. The serving base station then prepares the target cell, forwards the packets in flight, and tells the phone to switch; the core network switches the data path. The break is tens of milliseconds, and the phone keeps its IP address.
Step 6: The core network (IP and routing, L3). The core is an IP network with a mobility layer on top. The phone gets an IP address from a gateway (the P-GW in 4G, the UPF in 5G). Between the base station and this gateway the user's packets travel inside GTP tunnels, which are IP packets wrapped in IP packets. When the phone moves, only the tunnel's radio-side end changes; the gateway, and the address that outside servers see, stay fixed. This is why an ongoing TCP or QUIC connection survives a handover, and why a device's public address often belongs to the operator's carrier-grade NAT, not to the device. The 5G core splits these functions into network functions that talk over HTTP APIs (AMF for access, SMF for sessions, UPF for the user plane).

example: A phone is 1 km from a base station using 3.5 GHz and 20 MHz. Free-space loss is \( 20\log_{10}(1) + 20\log_{10}(3500) + 32.4 = 0 + 70.9 + 32.4 = 103.3 \) dB. At 700 MHz it would be 89.3 dB, which is 14 dB better, a factor of 25 in power; this is why low bands cover rural areas and 3.5 GHz needs more sites. Assume a 46 dBm transmitter (40 W) with 17 dBi antenna gain and an urban non-line-of-sight extra loss of 40 dB. The received power is 46 + 17 − 103.3 − 40 = −80.3 dBm. The noise is −174 + 73 (for 20 MHz) + 7 (receiver) = −94 dBm. So SNR is about 14 dB, a factor of 25.
Shannon gives \( C = 20\,\text{MHz} \times \log_2(26) = 20 \times 4.7 \approx 94 \) Mbit/s for one spatial stream. A practical LTE link at this SNR uses about 64-QAM with a code rate near 0.75 and reaches roughly 50 Mbit/s per stream after overhead. At the cell edge, with SNR near 0 dB, the limit is \( 20 \times \log_2(2) = 20 \) Mbit/s, and at −10 dB it is \( 20 \times \log_2(1.1) \approx 2.7 \) Mbit/s. So the same carrier gives a factor of 30 between the centre and the edge, and the scheduler must handle this.
Now the best-case peak. 20 MHz holds 100 resource blocks, which is 1,200 subcarriers. There are 14 OFDM symbols per 1 ms subframe. With 256-QAM (8 bits per symbol) the raw rate is \( 1200 \times 14 \times 8 = 134{,}400 \) bits per millisecond, or 134 Mbit/s per spatial layer. After about 25% overhead (reference signals and control) and a code rate near 0.9, one layer gives about 90 Mbit/s, and four layers give roughly 360 Mbit/s, consistent with the 300 to 400 Mbit/s quoted for LTE peak rates.
Finally sharing: if 50 phones are active in the cell at a mid-cell average rate of 50 Mbit/s each time they are scheduled, each gets about 1 Mbit/s over time (50 / 50), while the cell as a whole carries 50 Mbit/s. This is why a stadium full of phones feels slow even with good signal: the cell is divided between users, and the fix is more cells, more spectrum or more antennas.
intuition: A cellular network is a huge shared whiteboard in the air where a controller hands out small squares of space and time a thousand times a second to whoever can use them best.
The design follows from two facts. Radio is a shared medium that fades with distance, so the only way to serve many users is to reuse the same frequencies in many small cells and to give every user a fair, quality-aware share. And users move, so the network must keep a stable identity (an IP address) while the radio attachment changes underneath. The cell cuts the radio problem into small pieces; the tunnel hides the moving pieces from the Internet. Shannon's formula says where the limit lies, OFDM and the FFT let a cheap chip approach it, and the scheduler spends each millisecond where it gives the most bits.
mistakes: People often think a higher G number is a faster phone connection for everyone. In fact the speed you get depends on the spectrum band, the distance, the number of users in your cell and your signal quality. 5G in a low band can be little faster than good 4G; 5G in a millimetre-wave band is very fast but covers only a few hundred metres.
People often think a signal with more bars means higher speed. In fact bars show received signal strength, but speed depends on SNR and on how busy the cell is. A phone with full bars in a crowded stadium can have a worse connection than a phone with two bars in an empty street.
People often think the phone connects to the Internet through the tower like Wi-Fi to a router. In fact the tower only carries the radio link; the phone's traffic goes through the operator's core network and a gateway, inside tunnels. The mobile operator, not the tower, assigns the IP address and often shares one public address among many phones through NAT.
People often think more transmit power solves poor coverage. In fact neighbouring cells reuse the frequency, so more power in one cell adds interference to the others, and the phone's own transmitter is limited to about 0.2 W by battery and health rules. Capacity grows by splitting cells and adding spectrum, not by shouting louder.
People often think 5G latency is always 1 ms. In fact 1 ms is the target of the special ultra-reliable low-latency mode in the radio. End-to-end latency to a server on the Internet is typically 10 to 30 ms on a good 5G stand-alone network, longer on a loaded 4G network, and it includes the distance to the server.
friction: Spectrum is scarce and costly: operators pay billions for licences, and the regulator decides who may use each band. Higher frequencies give wide bandwidth but weak penetration; millimetre waves are blocked by walls, leaves and even a hand, so they need dense small cells and line of sight. Coverage and capacity trade against each other, and indoor coverage is hardest, because most traffic is indoors while the towers are outdoors.
Interference and mobility limit the model. At a cell edge, a neighbour's signal can be as strong as your own, which pushes SNR toward 0 dB. Motion causes Doppler shift (at 3.5 GHz and 120 km/h it is about 390 Hz, which is small compared with the 15 kHz subcarrier spacing but grows in higher bands and at higher speeds), and handovers in dense small-cell networks happen often. Mobile bandwidth is therefore very variable, and applications see throughput changes of ten times within seconds, which is why adaptive bitrate and congestion control matter.
Costs show up in energy and in deployment: a base station uses hundreds of watts to several kilowatts, and 5G sites with many antennas use more than 4G sites. Backhaul fibre to every small cell is expensive. The core is also complex: network slicing, edge computing and private 5G need operators to expose features that vary between vendors. Security has known weaknesses too, for example fake base stations that capture phone identifiers, which later releases reduce by encrypting the permanent identifier.
practice: As an engineer who builds on top of mobile networks, treat the network as a variable, lossy path. Design apps for round-trip times from 30 to 100 ms and throughput that can drop by an order of magnitude within seconds. Use adaptive bitrate for video, retry with exponential back-off, send small requests and avoid chatty protocols, and prefer QUIC or HTTP/2 connection reuse because a new TCP and TLS connection costs several round trips on a mobile link.
Expect the radio state to affect battery and latency. A phone in idle state needs tens to hundreds of milliseconds to wake the radio for the first packet, and keeping the radio awake with frequent small transmissions drains the battery; batch background work. Do not assume the address of a phone is stable or unique: a handover keeps the IP address, but switching between Wi-Fi and cellular changes it, and carrier NAT hides many users behind one address, which matters for rate limiting and for ad and fraud systems that use IP as an identifier. Test with a network emulator that has bandwidth, delay, loss and handover profiles, and with real devices on a few carriers.
For learning, read Shannon's 1948 paper, then Tse and Viswanath's "Fundamentals of Wireless Communication", and the free 3GPP overview documents. A software-defined radio (a cheap RTL-SDR dongle) lets you see an OFDM signal in a spectrogram. Apps like Network Cell Info show the band, cell ID and measured RSRP and SINR on your own phone, which makes the theory visible.
tradeoffs: Choose a low band (600 to 900 MHz) when coverage and indoor reach matter, as in rural areas and for IoT; choose mid-band (2.5 to 3.7 GHz) for the best mix of capacity and coverage in cities; choose millimetre wave (24 to 40 GHz) only for dense, mostly line-of-sight places such as stadiums and fixed wireless access. You give up range for each step in bandwidth.
Choose more cells (densification) when a hot spot is saturated and spectrum cannot be added; choose more spectrum or carrier aggregation when you can buy or share it; choose more antennas (massive MIMO) when you have spectrum but poor SNR. Each costs money differently: sites need power, fibre and permission; spectrum needs auctions; antennas need new radios.
Choose 5G non-stand-alone (5G radio on a 4G core) for fast rollout and good speed; choose stand-alone 5G (5G radio and 5G core) when you need slicing, lower latency or private networks. Stand-alone is cleaner but needs a new core and new roaming agreements.
Choose Wi-Fi for dense indoor spots where you control the site and the spectrum is unlicensed and free; choose cellular when you need wide-area mobility, guaranteed quality and managed security. Wi-Fi is cheaper per bit but has no seamless handover across operators and no service guarantees; cellular is more expensive but works everywhere you move.
Choose to tune the scheduler for fairness when many users matter, and for throughput when peak rates matter. Proportional fair serves the strong users more often and the weak ones less often; max-rate scheduling gives the highest total but starves cell-edge users.
interview: Why does a cellular network use small cells and frequency reuse, and what limits how small cells can be? || Spectrum is fixed, so capacity per area grows only by reusing it in more places. Splitting a cell into smaller ones multiplies capacity, but interference between neighbours rises (SNR at the edge falls toward 0 dB), handovers become more frequent and each site needs power, backhaul fibre and planning permission. The limit is cost and interference, not radio physics.
Explain OFDM and why the FFT matters. || OFDM sends data on many narrow orthogonal subcarriers, so each symbol is long compared with the multipath delay spread, and a cyclic prefix removes inter-symbol interference. The transmitter builds the signal with an inverse FFT and the receiver separates it with an FFT, which costs about N log N operations (22,000 for N = 2048) instead of N² (4 million).
Using Shannon's formula, why do operators care more about spectrum than about transmit power? || Capacity grows linearly with bandwidth B but only logarithmically with SNR, so doubling the power gains about one bit per second per hertz at high SNR, while doubling bandwidth doubles capacity. Extra power also raises interference in neighbouring cells.
How does a call survive a handover, and what happens to the IP address? || The phone measures neighbours and reports a better one; the serving station prepares the target and forwards in-flight packets; the phone switches and the core network moves the tunnel end to the new station. The gateway, and so the phone's IP address, stays fixed, so TCP or QUIC connections continue, with a gap of tens of milliseconds.
A user complains that video buffers in a stadium though they have full signal bars. What do you check? || Bars show signal strength, not capacity. Check cell load (resource-block utilisation, number of active users), SNR/SINR and interference from neighbouring cells, backhaul congestion, and the band in use. The cell's capacity is shared, so with thousands of users each gets a small share; the fix is more cells, spectrum, antennas or a lower video bitrate.
What are the main differences between 4G LTE and 5G NR at the radio level? || NR supports wider carriers (up to 100 MHz below 7 GHz and 400 MHz in millimetre wave), flexible subcarrier spacing (15 to 240 kHz) and shorter slots for lower latency, massive MIMO with beamforming, LDPC and polar codes instead of turbo codes, and a service-based core with slicing. The basic OFDM and scheduling approach is the same.
quiz: What does a base station's scheduler decide every millisecond? || Which phones get which resource blocks (frequency and time) and which modulation and coding rate each will use, based on the channel quality the phones report.
Why does a cyclic prefix help OFDM? || Multipath makes delayed copies of a symbol arrive late. A copy of the symbol's end placed in front absorbs the echoes, so they do not spill into the next symbol and the subcarriers stay orthogonal.

value: Computational geometry is the study of algorithms for questions about shapes: which points form the outer boundary of a cloud, which road segments cross each other, whether a GPS point lies inside a delivery zone, which shop is nearest to each house, how to cut a polygon into triangles. These questions are easy to see on paper and surprisingly easy to get wrong or slow in code. A naive program compares every pair of objects, which for a million objects means about \( 5 \times 10^{11} \) comparisons. Geometric algorithms reach answers in \( O(n \log n) \) steps, a few tens of millions for the same input, by sorting and by sweeping a line across the plane so that each object is compared only with its neighbours.
Two ideas make this possible. First, almost every geometric question can be reduced to one tiny test, the orientation test: does the point c lie left of, right of or on the line from a to b? This is the sign of a small determinant. Second, geometric structure is ordered in some direction, so after sorting, the interesting pairs are adjacent. Together they give convex hulls, segment intersection, closest pairs, Voronoi diagrams and Delaunay triangulations, all in \( O(n \log n) \) or close to it.
For the layer above, spatial indexes (bounding boxes, point-in-polygon, polygon overlays) use these routines for exact tests after the index has found candidates; rasterization uses them for triangle coverage and clipping; motion planning uses them for collision checks; and map services, GIS (PostGIS, GEOS, Shapely), CAD kernels and game engines run them all day. The layers below provide the parts: sorting (L3) gives the order that a sweep needs, divide and conquer (L3) gives the second method for hulls, closest pairs and Voronoi diagrams, linear algebra (L4) provides determinants and cross products for the tests, and metric spaces (L4) define what "nearest" means and give Voronoi diagrams their meaning.
terms: Convex hull — the smallest convex polygon that contains all the given points; imagine a rubber band stretched around nails.
Orientation test — the sign of \( (b_x - a_x)(c_y - a_y) - (b_y - a_y)(c_x - a_x) \): positive if a, b, c turn left (counter-clockwise), negative if right, zero if collinear.
Sweep line — an imaginary vertical line moved from left to right; the algorithm keeps a "status" structure of the objects it currently crosses and handles "events" in sorted order.
Voronoi diagram — a division of the plane into cells, one per site (a given point), where each cell holds all places that are closer to its site than to any other.
Delaunay triangulation — the triangulation of a point set in which no point lies inside the circumcircle of any triangle; it is the dual of the Voronoi diagram and avoids thin triangles.
Output-sensitive — an algorithm whose running time depends on the size of the answer as well as of the input, such as \( O(n \log h) \) for a hull with h vertices.
Predicate — a yes/no or sign test on coordinates (orientation, in-circle); all robust geometry code is built from a few predicates.
Degeneracy — an input that breaks general-position assumptions, such as three collinear points or two points with the same x coordinate.
history: Geometry has algorithms since Euclid, but the field of computational geometry began in the 1970s. In 1975 and 1976 Michael Shamos and Dan Hoey showed that closest-pair and segment-intersection questions could be answered in \( O(n \log n) \) time instead of \( O(n^2) \). Shamos gave the field its name in his 1978 Yale thesis, and the first textbook, by Preparata and Shamos, appeared in 1985.
The convex hull is the field's first model problem. Ronald Graham published his scan in 1972 (sort by angle, then keep only left turns), Jarvis gave the gift-wrapping method in 1973, with cost \( O(nh) \), and Preparata and Hong (1977) made a divide-and-conquer hull in \( O(n \log n) \). A matching lower bound shows no hull algorithm can beat \( n \log n \) in general, because computing the hull of points on a parabola sorts the numbers. Kirkpatrick and Seidel (1986) and Chan (1996) reached the output-sensitive \( O(n \log h) \). Bentley and Ottmann (1979) gave the sweep-line algorithm that finds all k intersections of n segments in \( O((n + k) \log n) \). Fortune (1987) computed Voronoi diagrams with a sweep, and Guibas and Stolfi (1985) with divide and conquer and a clean data structure for triangulations. Chazelle (1991) showed that a simple polygon can be triangulated in linear time, though the algorithm is too complex to use; practical code uses ear clipping or monotone-polygon methods.
A second era began in the 1990s, when programmers found that textbook algorithms crash on real data, because they assume exact real numbers. Shewchuk's "Adaptive Precision Floating-Point Arithmetic and Fast Robust Geometric Predicates" (1997) showed how to evaluate the orientation and in-circle tests exactly and fast. The CGAL library (from the late 1990s) and the JTS/GEOS libraries behind PostGIS and Shapely turned these ideas into software used widely in GIS and CAD.
how: Almost every algorithm here has three pieces: a predicate that answers a local question exactly, an ordering that makes the global problem one-dimensional, and a data structure that keeps the current state. Here is each, and then three standard algorithms.
Step 1: The orientation predicate (linear algebra, L4). For points a, b, c, the cross product of the vectors \( b - a \) and \( c - a \) is the determinant
\[ \operatorname{orient}(a,b,c) = (b_x - a_x)(c_y - a_y) - (b_y - a_y)(c_x - a_x) \]
Its sign tells the turn direction, and half its absolute value is the area of the triangle abc. From this one test come the convex hull (keep only left turns), segment intersection (two segments cross if the endpoints of each lie on opposite sides of the other), point in polygon, polygon area (the shoelace sum of cross products) and triangulation. The in-circle test, a 3×3 determinant, plays the same role for Delaunay triangulations. These determinants are why linear algebra is the lowest layer here.
Step 2: Sorting and sweeping (sorting, L3). A sweep algorithm sorts the interesting x coordinates (segment endpoints, and intersection points found along the way) into an event queue, then moves from event to event. A balanced search tree keeps the segments that currently cross the sweep line, ordered by their y position. When a segment starts, it is inserted and compared only with its two neighbours; when it ends, it is deleted and its two former neighbours become adjacent and are compared; when two neighbours cross, they swap places in the order. Two segments can only intersect after they have become neighbours, so each event needs \( O(\log n) \) work, and the total is \( O((n+k)\log n) \) for k intersections. The sort supplies the order; without it the sweep has no sense of "next".
Step 3: Convex hull by monotone chain (sorting and divide and conquer, L3). Sort the points by x, then by y: \( O(n \log n) \). Build the lower hull by scanning left to right and keeping a stack: while the last two points on the stack and the new point do not make a left turn, pop the top; then push the new point. Build the upper hull the same way going right to left, and join them. Every point is pushed once and popped at most once, so the scan is \( O(n) \), and the total is dominated by the sort. Graham's scan is the same idea with an angular sort. The divide-and-conquer version splits the points in half by x, computes the two hulls recursively, and merges them by finding the two common tangent lines in linear time, which gives \( T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n) = O(n \log n) \). This is the master theorem at work, and the same recurrence appears for closest pair and Voronoi diagrams.
Step 4: Closest pair and Voronoi diagrams (divide and conquer and metric spaces, L3/L4). To find the closest pair, split the points by x, solve both halves recursively, let d be the smaller of the two answers, and then check only points within a strip of width 2d around the dividing line. In the strip each point needs comparing with at most 7 others when the strip is sorted by y, so the merge is linear. The Voronoi diagram of a point set partitions the plane by the nearest site under the chosen distance (Euclidean, in the usual case). Its edges are pieces of perpendicular bisectors, and it has at most \( 2n - 5 \) vertices and \( 3n - 6 \) edges, so its size is linear. Its dual, the Delaunay triangulation, is built by incremental insertion with edge flips (flip an edge when the in-circle test fails), by divide and conquer, or by Fortune's sweep. A nearest-site query then becomes point location in the diagram.
Step 5: Robustness. Computers use floating-point numbers, in which a sign can be wrong when the true value is near zero. An orientation test that gives different answers for the same three points in different orders can make a hull algorithm loop forever or drop points. The standard fixes are: use exact integer coordinates (scaled latitude and longitude), use a filtered predicate (compute in floating point with an error bound, and fall back to exact arithmetic only if the result is within the bound), or use Shewchuk's adaptive method. Almost all inputs pass the fast filter, so the average cost is close to plain floating point.

Add (2,1): orient((0,0),(1,3),(2,1)) = 1·1 − 3·2 = −5, a right turn, so pop (1,3); the stack is (0,0), (2,1). Add (2,2): orient((0,0),(2,1),(2,2)) = 2·2 − 1·2 = 2, a left turn, so push. Add (4,0): orient((2,1),(2,2),(4,0)) = 0·(−1) − 1·2 = −2, right turn, pop (2,2); orient((0,0),(2,1),(4,0)) = 2·0 − 1·4 = −4, right turn, pop (2,1); push (4,0). Add (4,4): orient((0,0),(4,0),(4,4)) = 16, left, push. Lower hull: (0,0), (4,0), (4,4). The upper hull, scanning right to left, ends with (4,4), (1,3), (0,0). The hull is (0,0), (4,0), (4,4), (1,3); P2 and P3 lie inside. The shoelace sum gives the area: (0·0 − 4·0) + (4·4 − 4·0) + (4·3 − 1·4) + (1·0 − 0·3) = 0 + 16 + 8 + 0 = 24, so the area is 12.
Now a point-in-polygon test by ray casting for this polygon. For the query q = (3,2), shoot a ray to the right (increasing x). Edge (0,0)–(4,0) is horizontal at y = 0, so it is skipped. Edge (4,0)–(4,4) spans y = 2 and is at x = 4 > 3, so it crosses the ray: count 1. Edge (4,4)–(1,3) spans y from 3 to 4 and does not include 2. Edge (1,3)–(0,0) spans y = 2 at x ≈ 0.67, which is left of q, so no crossing. The count is 1, an odd number, so q is inside. For q = (5,2), the edge at x = 4 is left of q and no edge is to the right, so the count is 0 and q is outside.
Now the effect of the sweep. Suppose a road layer has n = 100,000 segments and we want all crossings, of which there are k = 10,000. The brute-force method tests \( n(n-1)/2 \approx 5 \times 10^9 \) pairs. The sweep does about \( (n + k)\log_2 n \approx 110{,}000 \times 17 \approx 1.9 \) million steps, a factor of 2,600 fewer. In practice a grid or R-tree filter is often even simpler, but the sweep gives the guaranteed bound.
intuition: A rubber band around nails, a ruler sliding across a table and a bisector between two towns: computational geometry is the habit of replacing "look at everything" by "keep the boundary and look only at the neighbours".
The ideas are natural because geometry is local. Whether a point is inside a shape depends on what is near it, not on the whole plane, and the only global fact needed is an order (left to right, or by angle). Sort once, and the next relevant object is always adjacent. The orientation test is a single arithmetic expression, which is why geometric programs can be fast; the difficulty is not speed but exactness, because a wrong sign anywhere breaks the structure built on it.
mistakes: People often think geometry code is easy because the formulas are simple. In fact the formulas are simple and the edge cases are not: collinear points, duplicate points, vertical segments, touching polygons and nearly parallel lines. A large share of bugs in GIS and CAD come from these degeneracies and from floating-point rounding.
People often think a convex hull needs \( O(n^2) \) work. In fact \( O(n \log n) \) is enough and is optimal in the general case, and \( O(n \log h) \) when the hull is small.
People often think checking a point against a polygon needs a library only for complex shapes. In fact ray casting is simple, but the details matter: points exactly on an edge or vertex, rays that touch a vertex, and polygons with holes or crossing the date line all need explicit rules, and a spherical earth is not a plane at large scale.
People often think latitude and longitude can be used as x and y. In fact for small areas near the equator the error is small, but degrees of longitude get shorter toward the poles, polygons crossing the antimeridian wrap around, and edges between two points are great-circle arcs on a sphere (as in the S2 library), not straight lines in the degree plane.
People often think more precision, such as using higher-precision floats, solves robustness. In fact it only moves the failure point. The reliable solutions are exact predicates, filtered arithmetic or integer coordinates, together with consistent handling of ties.
friction: Robustness is the central cost. Exact arithmetic is slow, and floating-point shortcuts are unreliable; filtered exact predicates cost little on average but are hard to implement, which is why most teams use CGAL, GEOS or Shewchuk's code. Geometry libraries still disagree on invalid input: a self-intersecting polygon (a "bow-tie") can be valid in one system and rejected by another, and boolean operations (union, intersection) of large polygons can fail or become slow.
Dimension is another limit. Many algorithms are simple in 2D and hard in 3D: the 3D convex hull is \( O(n \log n) \) but needs more complex code, 3D Delaunay triangulations can have \( O(n^2) \) cells in the worst case, and in high dimensions the size of the hull or diagram grows exponentially with the dimension. This is why nearest-neighbour search in 50 dimensions uses approximate methods (LSH, graph indexes) and not Voronoi diagrams.
Practical performance depends on memory and parallelism more than on operation counts. Sweeps use balanced trees with poor cache behaviour; on big data engines prefer grids, R-trees or tile partitioning that parallelise easily. Output size can also dominate: overlaying two detailed coastlines can produce millions of tiny pieces. Finally, geometry on the sphere and the ellipsoid, with geodesic distances and wrap-around, is harder than the plane and many libraries only approximate it.
practice: Do not write your own geometry kernel. Use JTS (Java), GEOS (C++, behind PostGIS and Shapely in Python), or CGAL when you need guaranteed exactness. In Spark and Databricks, use a geospatial library (such as Sedona or the built-in spatial functions) so that the index, partitioning and exact predicates are done for you. For hulls, triangulations and Voronoi diagrams in Python, SciPy's `scipy.spatial` wraps Qhull and gives `ConvexHull`, `Delaunay` and `Voronoi` classes.
For point-in-polygon at scale, always use two stages: a coarse spatial index (bounding boxes, an R-tree, or a covering by H3 or S2 cells) finds candidate polygons, then an exact test decides. A geo-fence service with 100,000 advertiser polygons and thousands of device locations per second cannot test every polygon; it indexes the polygons once, and then each location needs only a few exact tests. Precompute: simplify complex boundaries to the accuracy you need, and prepare the polygon (JTS "prepared geometry") if it is tested many times, since this builds an index of its edges.
Treat coordinates carefully. Fix the coordinate system and precision at the boundary of your system, snap or round to a grid when inputs come from different sources, and validate polygons before use (`ST_IsValid` in PostGIS). Test with degenerate cases: duplicate points, collinear points, touching boundaries, tiny slivers, huge polygons, and polygons crossing the antimeridian. To learn the subject, work through "Computational Geometry: Algorithms and Applications" (de Berg et al.), implement the monotone chain hull and a line-segment intersection test, and then read Shewchuk's notes on predicates.
tradeoffs: Choose a sweep-line or divide-and-conquer algorithm when you need a guaranteed worst case on a whole data set, such as all intersections between two map layers; choose a grid or R-tree filter with exact tests on the candidates when objects are spread out and you want simple code and easy parallelism. The sweep gives the best bound but is sequential and complex; the grid is easy but can degrade when objects cluster.
Choose exact predicates (integer coordinates, CGAL-style exact arithmetic or adaptive filters) when correctness of topology matters, as in CAD, boolean overlays and meshing; choose plain floating point with tolerances when small errors are acceptable, as in rendering, games and approximate analytics. Exact methods cost 2 to 10 times more in the worst case and need care; tolerances are fast but can create invalid shapes.
Choose a convex hull or bounding volume as a cheap approximation when you need a quick yes/no test (for example collision pre-checks or "is the point near the cluster at all"); choose the exact shape when the boundary matters (legal zones, delivery areas). Hulls are fast but include empty space, so they raise false positives.
Choose Delaunay triangulation when you want well-shaped triangles for interpolation, meshing or terrain; choose a Voronoi diagram when the question is "which site is nearest", such as service areas of stores. Choose approximate nearest-neighbour indexes instead when the dimension is more than about 10, because exact diagrams grow too large.
Choose planar geometry (projected coordinates) for city-scale work, and spherical geometry (S2, H3, geography types) for continental scale or global data. Planar is faster and simpler; spherical is correct near the poles and across the antimeridian but slower and offers fewer operations.
interview: How would you compute the convex hull of a million points, and what is the best possible complexity? || Sort by x and run the monotone chain with the orientation test, keeping a stack and popping non-left turns; it costs \( O(n \log n) \). That is optimal for the general case, since the hull of points on a parabola gives a sorted order, so a faster hull would beat the sorting bound. If the hull is small, Chan's \( O(n \log h) \) algorithm is better.
Find all intersections among n line segments efficiently. || Use the Bentley–Ottmann sweep: sort endpoints into events, keep the active segments in a balanced tree ordered by y, and test only neighbours; when two cross, record it and swap them. It runs in \( O((n+k)\log n) \) for k crossings, against \( O(n^2) \) for all pairs. In practice a grid or R-tree filter is often used when k is small and code simplicity matters.
Why do geometric algorithms fail on real data, and how do you make them robust? || Floating-point rounding makes the orientation sign wrong for nearly collinear points, so the algorithm sees inconsistent answers and may loop or return invalid shapes. Use integer coordinates, exact or adaptive-precision predicates (Shewchuk), or filtered predicates that compute fast and fall back to exact arithmetic near zero, and define consistent tie-breaking for degenerate cases.
You must check 50,000 device locations per second against 200,000 advertiser polygons. How do you design it? || Use two stages. Index the polygons once (R-tree on bounding boxes, or cover each with H3/S2 cells and store a cell-to-polygon map), so a location finds a few candidate polygons by lookup. Then run an exact point-in-polygon test, on prepared geometries for large polygons. Handle boundary points and antimeridian polygons explicitly and keep the index in memory.
Why is the Delaunay triangulation preferred over an arbitrary triangulation, and how is it related to the Voronoi diagram? || Delaunay maximises the minimum angle among all triangulations, so it avoids thin triangles that cause errors in interpolation and meshing. Its edges join sites whose Voronoi cells share a boundary, so each is the dual of the other and one can be computed from the other in linear time.
When do these 2D techniques stop working well? || In high dimensions the size of hulls, Voronoi diagrams and Delaunay triangulations grows exponentially, and 3D triangulations can be quadratic. Also on a sphere, planar assumptions fail. Use approximate nearest neighbour methods, spherical libraries or sampling instead.

In a sweep for segment intersections, why only compare neighbours in the status structure? || Two segments can cross only if they are adjacent just before the crossing; between them there is no other segment. So every crossing is found when the pair becomes adjacent, at an insertion, deletion or swap event.

@@ splines
value: A computer needs a way to store a smooth curve with very few numbers. The outline of the letter "S", the path of a camera in an animation, the body of a car, and the speed-up curve of a button animation are all smooth shapes, but a computer can store only a finite list of numbers. One option is to store thousands of points and join them with straight lines; this is large, and it looks jagged when you zoom in. Splines store a few control points and a rule that turns them into a curve that you can evaluate at any resolution.
A Bézier curve is the simplest such rule. You give n + 1 control points; the curve starts at the first, ends at the last, and is pulled toward the ones in between without usually touching them. Moving one control point changes the curve smoothly and in a predictable way, which makes it a good tool for designers. For long or complex shapes, one curve of high degree behaves badly, so a spline joins many low-degree pieces (usually cubic) and requires the pieces to meet smoothly: same point, same direction, and sometimes the same curvature.
For the layer above, this is the maths behind vector graphics: fonts (every glyph in TrueType, OpenType and PostScript fonts is a chain of Bézier segments), SVG paths and the HTML canvas, the easing curves of CSS animations, CAD and 3D modelling surfaces, and the smooth trajectories of robots. The layers below make it work: calculus (L4) defines "smooth" as continuity of derivatives at the joins, linear algebra (L4) shows that a curve is a weighted sum of control points, which means the same code works in 2D, 3D or any dimension, and numerical analysis (L4) explains why evaluating by repeated interpolation is stable.
terms: Control point — a point that shapes the curve; the curve is a weighted average of the control points.
Bézier curve — the curve \( B(t) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} \binom{n}{i} t^i (1-t)^{n-i} P_i \) for \( t \in [0,1] \); degree n has n + 1 control points.
Bernstein polynomial — the weight \( \binom{n}{i} t^i (1-t)^{n-i} \) of control point \( i \); all weights are at least 0 and sum to 1.
de Casteljau's algorithm — evaluation by repeated linear interpolation between neighbouring control points.
Knot vector — in a B-spline, the non-decreasing list of parameter values where the polynomial pieces join.
C^k and G^k continuity — the curve and its first k derivatives agree at a join (C^k, parametric), or the curve has the same shape, such as the same tangent direction, even if speeds differ (G^k, geometric).
NURBS — Non-Uniform Rational B-Splines; B-splines with a weight for each control point, able to describe circles and ellipses exactly.
Flattening — approximating a curve by short straight segments for drawing, within a tolerance.
history: The idea of a spline is older than the computer. Shipbuilders and draftsmen bent a thin flexible strip of wood, held in place by weights called "ducks", to draw smooth curves. Isaac Schoenberg gave the mathematical name "spline" in 1946, and the theory of B-splines grew from his work, including a 1966 paper with Curry.
Car makers needed the same ideas on a computer. Paul de Casteljau at Citroën developed in 1959 the evaluation algorithm that now carries his name, as an internal report that stayed unpublished for years. Pierre Bézier at Renault published the curves in the 1960s as part of the UNISURF design system, and they became known by his name. The Bernstein polynomials behind them are from 1912. Carl de Boor and Maurice Cox found the stable recurrence for B-spline basis functions in 1972. Catmull and Rom proposed an interpolating spline in 1974, and Versprille's 1975 thesis introduced rational B-splines, now NURBS, which became the standard in CAD systems.
Typography spread them. Adobe's PostScript (1984) described glyph outlines with cubic Bézier curves. Apple and Microsoft's TrueType (around 1991) used quadratic curves, which are cheaper to render, and OpenType allows both. SVG (2001) put the same path commands into web graphics, and CSS adopted cubic Béziers for animation timing. A newer use is machine learning: Kolmogorov–Arnold networks (2024) replace fixed activations by learned B-splines.
how: A spline rests on three pieces: a basis (the weights that blend control points), an evaluation method, and continuity rules for joining pieces.
Step 1: The curve is a weighted sum of control points (linear algebra, L4). For degree n the Bézier curve is
\[ B(t) = \sum_{i=0}^{n} b_{i,n}(t)\, P_i, \qquad b_{i,n}(t) = \binom{n}{i} t^i (1-t)^{n-i} \]
The functions \( b_{i,n} \) are non-negative and sum to 1 for every \( t \), so \( B(t) \) is always a weighted average of the control points. Three properties follow directly. The curve stays inside the convex hull of its control points. It is affine invariant: moving, rotating or scaling the control points moves, rotates or scales the curve in the same way, so you can transform a whole glyph by transforming a few points. And it interpolates the first and last control points: \( B(0) = P_0 \) and \( B(1) = P_n \). Because the formula is linear in the \( P_i \), the same code works for 2D points, 3D points or colours.
Step 2: Smoothness from calculus (calculus, L4). Differentiating gives \( B'(t) = n \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} b_{i,n-1}(t)\,(P_{i+1} - P_i) \). At the ends, \( B'(0) = n(P_1 - P_0) \) and \( B'(1) = n(P_n - P_{n-1}) \). So the curve leaves the start point in the direction of the first control segment, with speed proportional to its length. To join two cubic Bézier pieces \( P_0 \ldots P_3 \) and \( Q_0 \ldots Q_3 \) with \( Q_0 = P_3 \), the join is C¹ (equal first derivative) if \( Q_1 - Q_0 = P_3 - P_2 \), and G¹ (same tangent direction, visually smooth) if \( Q_1 - Q_0 \) is any positive multiple of \( P_3 - P_2 \). Design tools keep the three points \( P_2, P_3, Q_1 \) on one line for this reason. C² continuity also needs the second derivatives to match, which is what B-splines give automatically.
Step 3: Evaluation by repeated interpolation (numerical analysis, L4). De Casteljau's algorithm computes \( B(t) \) without any powers or binomials. Set \( P_i^{(0)} = P_i \), then \( P_i^{(r)} = (1-t)\,P_i^{(r-1)} + t\,P_{i+1}^{(r-1)} \). After n rounds, \( P_0^{(n)} = B(t) \). Each step is a convex combination, a number between its two neighbours, so there is no subtraction of large numbers and rounding errors stay small. The expanded polynomial form \( a_0 + a_1 t + \dots \) subtracts large terms and loses digits at higher degrees; the Bernstein form is known to be among the most stable bases. The intermediate points also give a free bonus: they are the control points of the two halves of the curve, so one run splits the curve at \( t \) into two Bézier curves of the same degree (subdivision).
```python
def de_casteljau(points, t):
    pts = list(points)
    while len(pts) > 1:
        pts = [((1 - t) * a[0] + t * b[0],
                (1 - t) * a[1] + t * b[1])
               for a, b in zip(pts, pts[1:])]
    return pts[0]
```
Step 4: Splines join low-degree pieces. Why not use one polynomial of high degree through many points? Polynomial interpolation through many equally spaced points oscillates wildly near the ends (the Runge phenomenon), and a single control point changes the whole curve. A spline instead uses many low-degree pieces with local control. The B-spline is the standard form. It is defined by a degree p, control points \( P_i \), and a knot vector \( u_0 \le u_1 \le \dots \). The basis functions come from the Cox–de Boor recurrence:
\[ N_{i,0}(u) = \begin{cases} 1 & u_i \le u < u_{i+1} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}, \quad N_{i,p}(u) = \frac{u - u_i}{u_{i+p} - u_i} N_{i,p-1}(u) + \frac{u_{i+p+1} - u}{u_{i+p+1} - u_{i+1}} N_{i+1,p-1}(u) \]
and the curve is \( C(u) = \sum_i N_{i,p}(u)\,P_i \). Each basis function is non-zero over only p + 1 knot spans, so moving one control point changes at most p + 1 pieces: local control. At a simple knot the pieces join with C^{p−1} continuity, so a cubic B-spline (p = 3) is automatically C². Repeating a knot lowers the continuity at that point by one per repeat, which lets a designer make a corner. A single Bézier curve is the special case where the knots are 0 repeated p + 1 times and 1 repeated p + 1 times.
Step 5: Interpolating splines and rational curves. A B-spline does not pass through its control points. To pass exactly through given data points, choose a spline whose control points are solved from the data. A natural cubic spline joins cubic pieces with C² continuity at the data points; the unknown second derivatives satisfy a tridiagonal linear system, solved in \( O(n) \) time, and the result has the least total bending energy \( \int f''(x)^2\,dx \) among smooth interpolants. Catmull–Rom splines use a simple local rule: the tangent at point \( P_i \) is \( (P_{i+1} - P_{i-1})/2 \), and each piece is a cubic Hermite curve, which is the same as a Bézier with inner control points \( P_i + m_i/3 \) and \( P_{i+1} - m_{i+1}/3 \). NURBS attach a weight \( w_i \) to each control point and divide: \( C(u) = \sum N_i w_i P_i / \sum N_i w_i \). A polynomial can never draw a circle exactly, but a rational quadratic can (see the example).
Step 6: Drawing. A GPU or a rasterizer draws lines and triangles, so a curve is flattened first: split it until each piece is within a tolerance of a straight segment. For a quadratic the largest distance between the curve and its chord is \( |P_0 - 2P_1 + P_2|/4 \), and dividing the curve into \( m \) equal pieces reduces this by a factor \( m^2 \). Font renderers do this per glyph at the requested size, or fill the curve directly on the GPU (as in Loop and Blinn's method).

example: Take the cubic Bézier with control points \( P_0 = (0,0),\ P_1 = (1,2),\ P_2 = (3,2),\ P_3 = (4,0) \). First, by the formula at \( t = 0.5 \): the weights are \( (1, 3, 3, 1)/8 \). So \( x = (0 + 3\cdot1 + 3\cdot3 + 4)/8 = 16/8 = 2 \) and \( y = (0 + 3\cdot2 + 3\cdot2 + 0)/8 = 12/8 = 1.5 \). Now by de Casteljau: level 1 gives the midpoints (0.5, 1), (2, 2), (3.5, 1); level 2 gives (1.25, 1.5), (2.75, 1.5); level 3 gives (2, 1.5). The same answer, with only additions and halving. The tangent at the start is \( B'(0) = 3(P_1 - P_0) = (3, 6) \). The points found on the way also split the curve: the left half has control points (0,0), (0.5,1), (1.25,1.5), (2,1.5) and the right half has (2,1.5), (2.75,1.5), (3.5,1), (4,0).
Next, a C¹ join. To continue this curve smoothly from \( P_3 = (4,0) \), the next piece must start with \( Q_0 = (4,0) \) and have \( Q_1 = Q_0 + (P_3 - P_2) = (4,0) + (1,-2) = (5,-2) \). Any other \( Q_1 \) on the same line but at a different distance is still G¹ (the same direction, visually smooth), but not C¹; for an animation that uses the curve as a position over time, that gives a jump in speed.
Now flattening. A quadratic with \( P_0 = (0,0),\ P_1 = (50,100),\ P_2 = (100,0) \) pixels has \( P_0 - 2P_1 + P_2 = (0,-200) \), of length 200, so one chord misses the curve by up to \( 200/4 = 50 \) px. With m pieces the error is \( 50/m^2 \). For a tolerance of 0.25 px we need \( m^2 \ge 200 \), so \( m = 15 \) segments.
Finally a circle. A quarter of the unit circle is exactly a rational quadratic Bézier with control points (1,0), (1,1), (0,1) and weights \( 1, \sqrt{2}/2, 1 \). At \( t = 0.5 \) the Bernstein weights are 1/4, 1/2, 1/4. The numerator is \( \tfrac14(1,0) + \tfrac12\cdot0.7071\,(1,1) + \tfrac14(0,1) = (0.6036, 0.6036) \), and the denominator is \( 0.25 + 0.3536 + 0.25 = 0.8536 \), so the point is (0.7071, 0.7071), exactly at distance 1 from the origin. A plain cubic Bézier can only approximate a circle: the usual choice, with the inner control points at a distance 0.5523 times the radius, has a maximum radius error of about 0.03% per quarter.
intuition: A spline is a flexible ruler held by a few pins: each pin pulls the strip, and the strip's stiffness decides how smooth the curve is.
The idea is natural because averages are smooth. A weighted average of a few points, with weights that change gently with t, cannot jump or kink. Repeated interpolation (take the point 30% of the way along each segment, then do it again on the new points) builds a curve step by step, like folding a rope into smaller and smaller pieces, and the result is a curve that follows the polygon loosely but never leaves its hull. Splines use low-degree pieces because a low-degree polynomial has few ways to wiggle, and they overlap their basis functions so that each piece fades into the next. The continuity conditions are then plain calculus: equal values give C⁰, equal slopes C¹, and equal curvature C².
mistakes: People often think a Bézier curve passes through all its control points. In fact it passes only through the first and last. The others act like magnets that pull the curve toward them. If you need a curve through given points, use an interpolating spline, such as a natural cubic or Catmull–Rom spline.
People often think a higher degree gives a better, smoother curve. In fact high-degree curves are harder to control (each control point affects the whole curve), and high-degree interpolation oscillates. Practical systems use cubic or quadratic pieces and join them.
People often think that stepping t in equal steps moves along the curve at constant speed. In fact the speed \( |B'(t)| \) varies along a Bézier curve, and arc length has no simple closed form. For constant-speed motion or evenly spaced dashes, you must reparameterise by arc length, computed numerically.
People often think "spline" means "Bézier". In fact Bézier curves are one kind. B-splines, NURBS, natural cubic splines and Catmull–Rom splines have different control and continuity properties; a "smooth" curve in the visual sense (G¹) is not necessarily smooth in the parametric sense (C¹).
People often think a circle can be drawn exactly with a cubic Bézier. In fact no polynomial curve can be an exact circle. A cubic approximates a quarter circle to within about 0.03% of the radius, which is invisible on screen but not exact for CAD; NURBS give a true circle.
friction: Bézier and B-spline curves are easy to draw and hard to compute with exactly. Finding the intersection of two curves, the distance from a point to a curve, offset curves (outlines of a stroke) and the arc length all lack closed forms. Software solves them numerically by subdivision, Newton iteration or polynomial root finding, and outline stroking and offsetting are a long-lasting source of bugs in graphics libraries.
Design also has costs. The control points are not on the curve, so editing by dragging the curve needs more work (fitting). Quadratic TrueType outlines and cubic PostScript outlines are not interchangeable without conversion, and converting a cubic to quadratics needs several segments. NURBS bring a large standard with many special cases (weights, multiple knots, trimmed surfaces), and the same surface can have many control point sets, which makes exchanging models between systems fragile.
Smoothness limits what splines can show. Splines are smooth by construction, so sharp features need repeated knots or separate pieces. In data fitting, splines can overshoot between points: a cubic through monotone data may go up and down, so monotone-preserving variants (PCHIP) exist. And in higher dimensions, tensor-product surfaces (grids of control points) do not handle arbitrary topology well, which is why subdivision surfaces and meshes took over in animation.
practice: Where you meet them. In the browser, SVG path commands `M`, `L`, `Q` and `C` are lines and Béziers; the Canvas API has `bezierCurveTo`; CSS animation timing uses `cubic-bezier(x1, y1, x2, y2)`, where the curve is from (0,0) to (1,1) with the two given control points, and x must stay in [0, 1] so time is monotone. The standard `ease` is `cubic-bezier(0.25, 0.1, 0.25, 1.0)`. In Python, `scipy.interpolate` has `CubicSpline` (natural or clamped interpolation), `PchipInterpolator` (shape-preserving), `make_interp_spline`, `BSpline` and `UnivariateSpline` (smoothing). In statistics, splines are the base of generalised additive models and smooth regression, so a trend fitted with a cubic spline is a spline with a handful of knots.
Choose by the job. For design tools and fonts, edit with cubic Béziers and keep the handles collinear at smooth points. For data that must pass through points, use a natural cubic spline, or PCHIP when overshoot is harmful (for example for non-negative quantities). For smoothing noisy data, use a smoothing spline or a regression spline with few knots and check the fit on held-out data. For trajectories in robots and animation, use cubic or quintic polynomials in time, with the velocity (and acceleration) continuous at the joins, because a jump in acceleration is a jump in force.
To study, evaluate a cubic Bézier with de Casteljau by hand, then write the subdivision and flattening code, then implement the Cox–de Boor recurrence for a uniform cubic B-spline. Farin's "Curves and Surfaces for CAGD" and the free "A Primer on Bézier Curves" by Pomax cover the whole subject from different sides.
tradeoffs: Choose a Bézier curve (a single piece) for a short, simple shape that a human edits: glyph strokes, icon paths and animation easing. You get direct, predictable handles; you give up local control and smooth joins across many pieces unless you manage the joins yourself.
Choose a B-spline when you need a long smooth curve with local control and automatic C² continuity, as in CAD and camera paths. You give up interpolation (the curve does not pass through its control points) and a simple mental model of knots.
Choose an interpolating spline (natural cubic, Catmull–Rom) when the curve must pass through measured or given points. Natural cubic splines are globally smooth (C²) but one change affects the whole curve; Catmull–Rom is local but only C¹. Both can overshoot; PCHIP stops overshoot at the cost of lower smoothness (C¹).
Choose NURBS when you need exact conics and a CAD-compatible exchange format; choose plain non-rational B-splines when you do not, since the weights add complexity and make derivatives more complicated.
Choose quadratic pieces when rendering speed and simplicity matter (TrueType, GPU curve filling); choose cubic pieces when design quality matters, because a cubic can express an inflection (an S-shape) in one piece, which a quadratic cannot.
Choose a spline fit over a high-degree polynomial or a table of points nearly always: it is smaller than the table and more stable than the polynomial. Choose a table or a learned model only if the shape has no smooth structure.
quiz: What are the three main properties that follow from the Bernstein weights being non-negative and summing to 1? || The Bézier curve stays inside the convex hull of its control points, it is affine invariant (transforming the points transforms the curve), and it can be evaluated by repeated interpolation without numerical cancellation.

value: Working memory is the small mental workspace where a person holds the things they are thinking about right now: the three variable names in a function they are reading, the digits of a phone number they are about to dial, the steps of a runbook they are following. It is not the same as long-term memory, which stores facts and skills for years and has no practical size limit. Working memory is tiny, fragile and fast. Experiments find that people can hold only about four separate items (called chunks) in it at once, and that anything held there fades in a few seconds unless it is refreshed or something else is not displacing it.
This one small limit shapes almost every human-facing system. A form with 12 unrelated fields, an API with 8 positional parameters, a dashboard with 30 charts, a function with 6 nested conditions, an incident that involves 7 services: each demands more than four things at once, so the reader makes mistakes, forgets context, or gives up. Good engineering practice often works by lowering this load: names that stand for a whole idea, modules with narrow interfaces, checklists that hold the steps so that the mind does not have to.
Working memory has no lower layer in this map, because it comes from biology and psychology. It feeds the layers above it. Human-computer interaction (L1) designs interfaces to fit within a few chunks. Accessibility (L2) treats cognitive load as a barrier that clear, predictable design reduces. Software engineering principles (L3) such as modularity, abstraction and naming exist largely because programmers cannot hold a whole program in mind: they can hold only a few pieces and must trust the rest to be hidden.
terms: Working memory — the limited system that holds and manipulates a few pieces of information for seconds while a task is carried out.
Chunk — a unit that is meaningful to the person; "FBI" is one chunk for someone who knows it and three for someone who does not.
Span — the longest list of items a person can repeat back correctly in order; digit span is about 7 for most adults.
Rehearsal — silently repeating information to keep it active; it stops the memory from fading but uses attention.
Cognitive load — the amount of working-memory capacity a task demands, split into intrinsic (the problem itself), extraneous (poor presentation) and germane (building understanding).
Interference — one memory item disturbing another, which is the main cause of forgetting in working memory, together with fading over time.
Context switch cost — the extra time and errors after changing tasks, caused by having to rebuild the contents of working memory.
Long-term memory — the large, durable store; knowledge in it lets one chunk stand for many items.
history: In 1956 the psychologist George Miller published "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two". He noticed that people could repeat back about seven digits, seven letters or seven words, whatever the kind of item, and argued that the limit is about the number of chunks, not bits of information. The paper's cheerful title made "7 ± 2" famous, and it is often quoted wrongly as a rule for menus and phone numbers. Miller himself treated it as a curious pattern, not a design law.
Alan Baddeley and Graham Hitch (1974) replaced the idea of a single short-term store with a model of working memory that has parts: a phonological loop for sounds, a visuospatial sketchpad for images and positions, and a central executive that directs attention (an episodic buffer was added in 2000). Experiments of the 1960s to 1970s showed that lists are forgotten within about 20 seconds when rehearsal is blocked (Peterson and Peterson, 1959) and that people scan their memory item by item (Sternberg, 1966). Chase and Simon (1973) showed that chess masters remember real game positions far better than novices do, but not random positions, which supports the idea that skill is made of large chunks stored in long-term memory.
In 2001 Nelson Cowan reviewed many experiments in which rehearsal and grouping were controlled, and concluded that the true capacity is about four chunks, with Miller's seven reflecting grouping and rehearsal. John Sweller's cognitive load theory (1988) applied the limit to teaching and design. Today there is still a debate on whether the limit is a fixed number of "slots" or a flexible pool of resource that is shared more thinly as items are added; both fit the main result that the load matters and that four is about the point where accuracy drops.
how: Working memory is produced by the brain keeping some neural activity going after the stimulus is gone. Then three limits act on that activity: how many items it can hold, how long they last, and how much attention can be applied.
Step 1: Holding information. When you hear a phone number, neurons in the prefrontal cortex and in the sensory and parietal areas keep firing in a pattern that stands for the number, even after the sound has stopped. This activity needs energy and attention, and it is replaced when new input arrives. In Baddeley's model the sounds are kept in a phonological loop that lasts about two seconds of speech and is refreshed by silent rehearsal, which is why it is easier to remember a list of short words than a list of long words, and why talking while memorising a list hurts. Positions and shapes are held in a separate visuospatial store, which is why you can do a visual task and a verbal task together better than two visual tasks.
Step 2: Why the capacity is small. Two main explanations are studied. In the slot view, there are a few discrete places, and extra items are simply lost. In the resource view, a fixed amount of neural resource is shared: with two items each gets half, so each is stored more noisily, and with more items the noise grows until recall fails. One physiological model (Lisman and colleagues) links the number of items to rhythms of the brain: fast gamma cycles, one per item, are nested inside a slower theta cycle, and only about four to seven gamma cycles fit in one theta cycle. It remains a hypothesis. Whatever the mechanism, the data are consistent: accuracy is high up to about three items and falls steeply after four, and the number does not rise with practice on a raw span test.
Step 3: How people exceed four items in practice: chunking. A chunk is whatever long-term memory lets you treat as one unit. A well-known study by Ericsson, Chase and Faloon (1980) trained a student, S.F., who started with a normal digit span of about 7, to recall 79 digits after about 230 hours of practice. He did not expand working memory. He learned to recode groups of 3 or 4 digits as running times (for example 3492 as "3 minutes 49.2 seconds, a near world-record mile"), and then organised these groups into a retrieval structure in long-term memory. The same mechanism makes an experienced programmer see `for (i = 0; i < n; i++)` as one idea, and makes a chess master see a position as five familiar patterns instead of 25 pieces. Expertise is, to a large extent, a library of chunks.
Step 4: Attention is a second limit. Working memory is not just storage; its contents are used by an attention system that can do only one demanding thing at a time. When two tasks both need the central executive (for example reading code and following a conversation), the second must wait: this is the psychological refractory period, of order 100 to 300 ms per switch for simple tasks and much more for complex ones. Switching also leaves traces: the previous task keeps interfering for a while, and rebuilding the state of a complex task after an interruption takes minutes, not seconds, because the goals, the partial results and the next step all have to be recalled (Altmann and Trafton's memory-for-goals model describes this). Multitasking is, in practice, rapid switching with a cost each time.
Step 5: What this means for computing. For interface design (HCI, L1), every choice that asks the user to remember something across screens, steps or time spends the budget. Showing information at the place where it is needed (recognition) is cheaper than asking people to recall it. For accessibility (L2), people with cognitive disabilities, anxiety, stress, fatigue or a second language have even smaller effective capacity, so plain wording, consistent layout and short flows help many people at once. For software engineering (L3), the limit explains why code should be written for the reader: small functions that do one thing, descriptive names that compress a concept into one chunk, shallow nesting, few parameters, and abstractions that let you ignore details. Modularity does not make the machine faster; it makes the human reader able to hold the part they are changing.

If you see them as "15 August 1947", the date of India's independence, it is 3 chunks (day, month, year) or 1 chunk, and recall is easy. The raw information is the same; the chunks differ because of long-term knowledge. This is also why a phone number is shown as 3-3-4 digits.

intuition: Working memory is a desk with room for four papers; skill is learning to make each paper stand for a whole folder.
The limit is natural once you see what the system does: it keeps the current state of a thought active and available at once, and holding something active costs energy and attention. A mind that kept every fact active at once would be unable to focus, so the system keeps a few and lets the rest rest in long-term memory. Chunking is the mind's compression: it uses stored knowledge to replace many items with one pointer. This is exactly what naming, abstraction and modularity do in software, and why they are not matters of taste but responses to a hard human limit.
mistakes: People often think Miller's "seven plus or minus two" is a rule that menus and lists must have at most seven items. In fact the number applies to items held in memory over a short time, not to items shown on a screen. A menu of 10 clear items is fine because people read it instead of remembering it. Also, the better estimate for the true capacity is about four chunks (Cowan, 2001).
People often think working memory can be trained to be much larger. In fact practice mainly builds better chunks and retrieval skills in a specific domain, as with the runner S.F. who learned to recall 79 digits, while his span for letters stayed close to normal. Brain-training games show little transfer to other tasks.
People often think multitasking is a skill some people have. In fact for tasks that both need attention, people switch rapidly and pay a cost each time, in speed and errors. Only a task that is automatic (for example walking) can run alongside a demanding one without much cost.
People often think a good design is one that shows everything at once so that the user needs to remember nothing. In fact showing too much raises the amount to scan and decide on (cognitive load), and some information is better hidden until needed. The goal is to show what the current step needs and to keep the rest reachable.
People often think experts handle more complex material because their working memory is bigger. In fact experts have the same limit but larger chunks. This is why the same code looks simple to its author and impossible to a newcomer, and why documentation for experts and novices must differ.
tradeoffs: Choose to externalise memory (checklists, notes, visible state, autosave drafts, runbooks) when a task is long, rare or high-stakes, such as a deployment or an incident. You give up some speed and some flexibility, because following a list is slower than acting from skill, but you remove a whole class of omissions.
Choose to reduce the number of items (fewer options, defaults, progressive disclosure) when the user is a novice or under pressure; choose to expose many controls at once when users are experts who have built chunks and need speed, as in an IDE or a trading terminal. Defaults hide complexity but can hide important choices; many visible controls help experts but can overwhelm new users.
Choose consistency and conventions (same names, same layout, same error format) over local optimisation when a person must work across many parts of a system. A familiar pattern becomes one chunk; a clever new one costs extra capacity every time it appears, for every reader.
Choose small, focused changes and short documents when review quality matters; choose a larger batch only when the parts are tightly coupled and reviewing them apart would hide the connection. Reviewers who must hold more than a few related changes at once miss defects.
Choose protected focus time (fewer meetings, batched messages) when the work needs deep context, such as design or debugging; choose frequent short syncs when decisions are fast and the work is made of small independent tasks. Interrupting deep work costs many minutes to rebuild context; but delay in communication also has a cost.
friction: The numbers vary. The capacity of four applies to simple, unrelated items under controlled conditions; real tasks mix chunks of different sizes, use long-term memory heavily and differ across people, age, stress, sleep and language. Capacity falls with fatigue, anxiety, noise and time pressure, and with working in a second language. A single number is useful to remind designers of the limit, but it is not a precise budget.
The theory is not settled. Researchers still disagree about slots versus resources, about whether chunking is free, and how working memory relates to attention and to long-term memory. Measuring cognitive load in the field is hard: questionnaires such as NASA-TLX are subjective, and physiological measures such as pupil size and eye tracking are noisy. Claims such as "interruptions cost 23 minutes" are often repeated without a clear source and depend on the task.
Application has costs too. Designs that reduce load for novices (wizards, hidden options) can slow experts, and designs for experts can lose new users. Externalisation adds maintenance: runbooks and checklists decay if they are not updated, and a checklist that is ignored is worse than none. Finally, the same limit applies to the people who build the systems: complex architectures, rarely understood tools and large pull requests are reasons that teams make errors, and the cost is hard to see in any metric until an incident.
practice: Design for recognition over recall: show options, previous values and current state instead of asking users to remember them; keep a form's rules next to the field; do not make a user carry a value from one screen to the next. Limit the number of decisions in a step, put the main action first, group related items (4 to 5 groups are easy to scan), and use progressive disclosure for the rest. Let people save drafts and go back; an interruption must not destroy the state.
Write code for the reader. Use names that carry the meaning, so one name is one chunk; extract functions when a block needs a comment to explain it; prefer early returns over deep nesting; keep functions to roughly one screen; keep parameter lists short (about four or fewer; use a named object for more); and avoid hidden coupling, such as a function that depends on a global changed elsewhere. Cognitive-complexity metrics (for example SonarSource's) count nesting and branching to give a rough measure of the load. When you review, ask "how many things must I keep in mind to follow this?" If the answer is more than four, restructure or add a diagram.
For operations, design dashboards and alerts with a clear top layer of a few signals, link each alert to a runbook, and remove alerts that no one acts on, because each noisy alert uses attention. Use checklists for deployments and incident roles (one person to lead, one to communicate, one to investigate), which splits the load among people. For your own work, write down the current plan and the next step before you stop, so that you can return to it later; keep one task in focus at a time and handle messages in batches. To learn more, read Cowan's 2001 review for the science, Sweller's work on cognitive load for teaching and design, and "The Design of Everyday Things" by Don Norman for interface practice.

@@ fitts
value: Two simple formulas from the 1950s let a designer predict how long a person needs to do two basic things on a screen: reach a target with a hand, and choose one item from a set. Fitts's law says that the time to move a pointer to a target grows with the logarithm of the ratio between the distance to the target and its size. Hick's law says that the time to choose among n equally likely options grows with the logarithm of n + 1. In both cases the time grows slowly (with a logarithm), and in both cases the formula gives a number in bits of difficulty that can be compared across designs.
These two laws turn arguments about user interfaces into estimates. A large button close to the cursor is fast; a tiny button in the far corner is slow. A menu with 8 items is only a little slower to choose from than a menu with 4. A button on the screen edge, where the cursor stops, behaves as if it were infinitely large in one direction. Designers use these rules for touch-target sizes, the position of the primary action, context menus that open under the pointer, and the choice between wide and deep menus. Because they predict time, they can be tested with a small experiment on real users.
Fitts's and Hick's laws sit at the base of the map because they come from human biology and psychology: from the way the motor system aims a movement and the way the brain selects a response. They feed human-computer interaction (L1), where they are used to reason about pointing, choosing and shortcuts. They are not exact physical laws like Newton's. They are well-supported statistical regularities that hold for a wide range of simple tasks, and they say little about complex ones.
terms: Index of difficulty (ID) — the number of bits that measures how hard a pointing task is; in the common form it is \( \log_2(D/W + 1) \).
Movement time (MT) — the time from the start of the movement until the target is selected.
Target width (W) — the size of the target along the line of movement; larger is easier.
Distance (D) — the distance from the starting point to the centre of the target.
Throughput — the number of bits per second a person achieves with a device, \( ID / MT \); used to compare devices.
Reaction time (RT) — the time from the appearance of a stimulus (here, a set of options) to the start of the response.
Speed–accuracy trade-off — the fact that moving faster makes the endpoint more variable, so people choose a balance between speed and misses.
Keystroke-level model (KLM) — a method that predicts expert task time by adding standard times for keystrokes, pointing, mental preparation and moving the hands.
history: Paul Fitts, an American psychologist who studied pilots and controls, published his law in 1954 in a paper on "the information capacity of the human motor system in controlling the amplitude of movement". He had a simple experiment: people tapped back and forth between two metal plates of width W placed a distance D apart, as fast as possible. He saw that the time depended on the ratio of D to W, not on either alone. Claude Shannon's information theory (1948) was new, and Fitts used its language: the movement is a communication channel, the distance is a signal and the tolerance is noise, and the difficulty is measured in bits. His original index was \( \log_2(2D/W) \). Scott MacKenzie (1992) proposed the form \( \log_2(D/W + 1) \), named after the Shannon–Hartley theorem, which cannot give negative values and fits data better; it is now the standard form in HCI and in ISO 9241-9 (published 2002), which defines how to test pointing devices.
William Hick (1952) and Ray Hyman (1953) studied choice reaction time. They showed that when there are n equally likely stimuli, each with its own key, reaction time rises with \( \log_2(n+1) \): the extra "+1" stands for the uncertainty about whether any signal has come. They also read this as information processing: each doubling of the options adds a fixed delay.
The laws entered computing when Card, English and Burr (1978) used Fitts's law to compare the mouse with other pointing devices at Xerox PARC and showed that the mouse had the highest throughput. Card, Moran and Newell (1980, 1983) put the numbers into the keystroke-level model and the book "The Psychology of Human–Computer Interaction". Later work includes the steering law (Accot and Zhai, 1997) for moving through a tunnel, such as a nested menu, and models of touch input, in which the finger itself hides the target.
how: Both laws are fits to data, but each rests on a mechanism in the body that explains why the relation is logarithmic.
Step 1: The pointing law. Movement time is a straight-line function of the index of difficulty:
\[ MT = a + b \cdot ID, \qquad ID = \log_2\!\left(\frac{D}{W} + 1\right) \]
The constants \( a \) and \( b \) are found by regression for a device and a person group. The constant \( a \) is the fixed cost to start and stop (including the time to see the target), and \( b \) is the time per bit (roughly 0.1 to 0.25 s per bit for mouse tasks). The reciprocal \( 1/b \) is the throughput in bits per second, which for a mouse is roughly 4 to 5 in laboratory studies, and lower for a trackpad or a finger on a touch screen. Notice what the formula says: doubling the distance and doubling the width together leave the difficulty unchanged, since only the ratio matters (this is also the law's known weakness at extreme scales). Making the target twice as wide saves about one bit, which is about 0.1 to 0.2 s per selection.
Step 2: Why a logarithm: aimed movement as a series of corrections (motor biology). A movement starts with a fast, ballistic push toward the target. Muscle commands are noisy, and the noise grows with the force: the faster the push, the larger the spread of the endpoint (signal-dependent noise, studied by Harris and Wolpert in 1998). If the first push lands inside the target, the movement is over. If not, the eye and hand see the error and a smaller corrective sub-movement follows, and so on. In the stochastic optimised-submovement model (Meyer and colleagues, 1988), the best strategy to minimise time for a given accuracy requires a number of sub-movements that grows with the ratio D/W, and each costs a fixed time. Summing the times gives a law that is logarithmic in D/W for the common case. This also explains the speed–accuracy trade-off: people can go faster only by accepting more misses.
Step 3: The choice law. For n equally likely options the reaction time is
\[ RT = a + b \cdot \log_2(n + 1) \]
and for unequal probabilities \( \log_2(n+1) \) becomes the entropy-like sum \( \sum_i p_i \log_2(1/p_i + 1) \). Each added bit of uncertainty adds a roughly fixed time (often of the order of 0.1 to 0.2 s). The brain behaves as if it narrows the options by roughly half at every step. The law holds best when the choice is simple and practised, and when each option has its own learned response (for example, pressing one of 8 keys for 8 lights). It does not apply well when people must search an unfamiliar, unsorted list item by item, in which case the time grows about linearly with the length of the list.
Step 4: Implications for screen design (HCI, L1). Because only \( D/W \) matters, a designer has three levers. Reduce the distance: put the next likely action close to where the pointer is, as with context menus that open at the cursor, and pie menus, where all items are at the same short distance. Increase the size in the direction of motion: make buttons bigger, give them padding that is clickable, and use the screen edges and corners, which stop the cursor and so behave as targets of unlimited depth (the reason the macOS menu bar at the top and the Windows taskbar at the bottom are fast to hit). Reduce the number of steps: cut the number of choices on each screen, but remember that depth also adds time. For touch, target sizes must also cover finger width and the hand's inaccuracy: Apple's guidelines recommend at least 44 by 44 points, Material Design 48 by 48 dp (about 9 mm), and WCAG 2.2 requires targets of at least 24 by 24 CSS pixels at level AA, with 44 by 44 at level AAA.
Step 5: Combining the laws. A menu task has a choice part and a pointing part, and the two laws combine: the time is the time to decide plus the time to point. The keystroke-level model gives typical times for the other components (about 0.2 to 0.3 s per keystroke, 1.1 s for pointing to a target with a mouse, 0.4 s to move a hand between keyboard and mouse, and 1.35 s for mental preparation), so an expert's task can be estimated by listing the operators. Use these as order-of-magnitude values, not as exact constants.

example: Use illustrative constants \( a = 0.1 \) s and \( b = 0.15 \) s per bit (real values depend on the device and the users). A 40-pixel-wide button is 400 pixels from the cursor: \( ID = \log_2(400/40 + 1) = \log_2 11 = 3.46 \) bits, so \( MT = 0.1 + 0.15 \times 3.46 = 0.62 \) s. Shrink the button to 20 pixels: \( ID = \log_2 21 = 4.39 \), \( MT = 0.76 \) s, so 0.14 s slower per click. Move the 40-pixel button to 100 pixels away: \( ID = \log_2 3.5 = 1.81 \), \( MT = 0.37 \) s, 0.25 s faster than at 400 pixels. For a button on the screen edge (effective width, say, 400 pixels in the direction of travel) at distance 400, \( ID = \log_2 2 = 1 \) bit and \( MT = 0.25 \) s. If a task needs 20 clicks per minute, a design that cuts each click by 0.2 s saves 4 seconds a minute, which is about 7% of the time, with no change in the number of features.
Now the choice law. A menu with 8 equally likely items: \( \log_2 9 = 3.17 \) bits, so \( RT = 0.1 + 0.15 \times 3.17 = 0.58 \) s. With 4 items: \( \log_2 5 = 2.32 \) bits, 0.45 s. So halving the options saves 0.13 s, not half the time. Now compare structures for 64 commands. One flat list: \( \log_2 65 = 6.02 \) bits. Two levels of 8: \( 2 \times \log_2 9 = 6.34 \) bits. Three levels of 4: \( 3 \times \log_2 5 = 6.97 \) bits. The decision cost is almost the same, so Hick's law alone says that depth is not a big gain. But each extra level adds a pointing move and a screen change (the constant \( a \) again, a few tenths of a second), so the flat or broad structure usually wins; studies since the 1980s found broad, shallow menus better than deep ones. The limit is the space on the screen and the time to scan a long list, so about 8 to 12 well-grouped items per level are typical.
Last, the keystroke-level model. Saving a file with the shortcut: mental preparation 1.35 s + two keystrokes at 0.2 s = 1.75 s. With the mouse from the keyboard: mental preparation 1.35 + hand move 0.4 + point to "File" 1.1 + click 0.1 + point to "Save" 1.1 + click 0.1 = 4.15 s. The shortcut is 2.4 s faster, which is why expert users in IDEs and editors learn shortcuts: the saving is repeated hundreds of times a day.
intuition: Reaching for a light switch in the dark: the smaller the switch and the farther away it is, the more you slow down and correct, and the more choices you have, the longer you pause.
Both laws say that doing something precise or deciding among many things costs a small, steady amount of time per doubling. A hand does not move in a perfect line: it throws itself toward the target and then fixes errors, and each fix is cheaper than the first throw. A brain does not compare all options at once: it halves the possibilities in a few steps. In both cases each doubling of difficulty adds a fixed delay, which is the signature of a logarithm. That is why the formulas look like information theory: aiming and choosing both reduce uncertainty, and the time is the price of the bits.
mistakes: People often think Fitts's law says "bigger is always better". In fact it says that only the ratio D/W matters, so the gain from enlarging a target shrinks once it is large (the logarithm flattens), and a big target in a bad position, or one that crowds other targets and causes mis-clicks, is not a good design.
People often think Hick's law means that fewer menu items are always faster. In fact the time grows only with the logarithm of the number of options, so removing half the items saves only about one bit of time. Hiding choices behind extra clicks or levels often costs more, since every level adds a pointing move and a screen change. The law also needs practised, known positions; for unfamiliar lists people scan item by item, and the cost is linear.
People often think the numbers are physical constants. In fact \( a \) and \( b \) change with the device (mouse, trackpad, finger, joystick), the user group (age, expertise), the task and the way the experiment is set up. Use the laws to compare designs and find trends, and measure your own users for absolute times.
People often think the laws apply to every interaction. In fact they describe rapid aimed movements and simple choices. They do not cover reading, decisions that need thought, searching in unknown layouts, hidden targets, or long tasks where the cost is understanding, not moving.
People often think a faster click means a better interface. In fact speed is one part of usability. A design can be fast to hit and still cause errors, or be hard to learn. Error rate, learnability and the cost of mistakes (such as a delete button placed in a fast, easy corner) matter as much as time.
tradeoffs: Choose bigger, closer targets for the primary action, especially on touch screens and for users who are in a hurry, have motor impairments, or are on the move. You give up screen space, and a crowd of large targets pushes other content away.
Choose edge and corner placement for frequent controls when the cursor can stop on the edge (desktop apps, full-screen); on touch screens edges are not special, and thumb reach from the lower part of the screen matters more. Edge placement gets the speed bonus but can lead to accidental activation near the edge and does not work well in windows that are not at the screen edge.
Choose a flat or broad menu (about 7 to 12 items per level, grouped) over deep nesting when users know roughly what they want; choose a hierarchy or search when the list is long (hundreds of commands) and users do not know the names. A command palette with search turns a long choice into typing, which is best for experts but hides discoverability.
Choose keyboard shortcuts and gestures for expert, repeated actions, and visible menus and buttons for discovery. Shortcuts are faster by seconds per use (the model above) but must be learned and are invisible; make sure each important shortcut also has a visible route.
Choose to guard dangerous actions with distance, size and confirmation (a small, separated "Delete" with an undo) when mistakes are costly, even though this deliberately violates the laws' advice for speed. Placing a destructive button in a fast, large position is a common cause of accidents.
friction: The laws are descriptive fits with known limits. Fitts's law was derived for one-dimensional movement to a known target. In two dimensions the "width" depends on the direction of approach and the shape, and different formulas exist; for very short or very long movements, or when both D and W vary over a wide range, the fit gets worse. Touch input adds the finger's own occlusion and imprecision, and so the effective width must be bigger than the visible target. Studies often disagree on how to fit and report \( a \) and \( b \), and the choice of method can change results more than a real difference between devices.
Hick's law is the weaker of the two. It holds for simple, practised, one-to-one choices, but its parameters depend strongly on stimulus–response compatibility, and in many interface tasks (reading labels, searching a page) people do not follow it. Misusing it to justify minimalism, such as "never show more than five choices", is a typical error.
The deeper limit is that these laws measure low-level, expert-free motor and perceptual time. Real interface cost is dominated by thinking, reading, waiting for the system, recovering from errors and learning, and none of these is in the formulas. The keystroke-level model also assumes error-free expert use. So the laws help to compare and fix details of a design that is already sound; they cannot tell you whether a feature is wanted or understood.
practice: Use the laws as a checklist for details. Make click targets large and give them padding that is part of the target (a link with padding is easier to hit than text alone). Keep the size of touch targets at 44 pt or 48 dp or more, and leave space between them so that a miss does not hit a neighbour. Place the next likely action near the last one; open context menus under the pointer; keep related controls together to cut travel; use full-width bars and screen edges for frequent desktop controls. Avoid putting a tiny control next to a large, frequent one. In forms, make the whole label clickable and keep the tab order logical.
Reduce choices honestly. Group items by meaning and put the most frequent first, since people scan from the top; use defaults; offer search or a command palette for large sets; do not hide frequent actions in a submenu to make a screen look clean. For menus with submenus, allow the pointer a diagonal path without the submenu closing (a "safe triangle"), since steering through a narrow tunnel is slow and error-prone.
Measure. To compare two designs, run a short study with 10 to 20 users, record the time and errors, and compute throughput with the effective-width method described in ISO 9241-9. Use click logs to find slow or often-missed targets (high miss rate, repeated clicks, long times from first movement). Estimate expert flows with the keystroke-level model before building, to see which of two flows needs fewer operators. For further reading: MacKenzie's "Fitts' Law as a Research and Design Tool in Human-Computer Interaction" (1992), Card, Moran and Newell's book, and Jef Raskin's "The Humane Interface".

@@ rigidbody
value: Newtonian mechanics explains how things move when forces act on them: a ball in flight, a car braking, a robot arm swinging under its own weight. Rigid-body dynamics is the part that treats an object as a solid that never bends. That one assumption is very useful, because a rigid body's whole motion is described by just 6 degrees of freedom: 3 for where its centre is and 3 for which way it points. Its state is those 6 numbers plus their rates of change, 12 numbers in all, however many atoms the object has.
The laws are short. Force equals mass times acceleration, \( F = ma \), for the motion of the centre. Torque equals the rate of change of angular momentum, \( \tau = dL/dt \), for the rotation. Together they let a computer predict motion from forces: given the state now and the forces, compute the acceleration, and step forward in time. Newton published this framework in the Principia in 1687, and Euler turned it into the equations for spinning solids in the 1750s. Nothing since then has changed the engineering use of it; relativity and quantum physics matter only at speeds near light and at atomic scales.
For the layer above, this is the physics that simulation and control software must obey. Physics engines (L2) step rigid bodies through time to animate games and train robots. Robotics (L1) uses the same equations in the form of the robot "manipulator equation" to compute the torques its motors need. Control theory (L4) takes these equations as the model of the plant it wants to stabilise, for example a drone or a balancing robot. There is no lower layer in this map for it; it is a first principle, and the rest of the stack takes it as given.
terms: Rigid body — an idealised object whose points keep fixed distances from each other, so it moves as one piece.
Centre of mass — the average position of the mass; the point whose motion follows \( F = ma \) for the total external force.
Torque — the turning effect of a force, \( \tau = r \times F \), where \( r \) is the vector from the pivot or centre of mass to the point where the force acts.
Angular velocity (ω) — a vector whose direction is the axis of rotation and whose length is the rotation rate in radians per second.
Moment of inertia (inertia tensor I) — how hard the body is to spin; a 3×3 matrix that depends on how mass is spread around the centre.
Angular momentum (L) — \( L = I\omega \); it changes only when a torque acts.
Impulse — force times the time it acts, in N·s; it changes momentum by the same amount.
Degrees of freedom (DOF) — the number of independent numbers needed to place the system; 6 for a free rigid body, \( n \) for a robot arm with \( n \) joints.
history: Galileo (early 1600s) found that falling bodies speed up at the same rate and that motion continues without a push. Isaac Newton collected these ideas into three laws and the law of gravitation in the Principia Mathematica (1687) and showed that the same rules explain a falling apple and the orbit of the Moon. For a single point mass, that was enough.
Extended bodies needed more. Leonhard Euler (1750s) wrote the laws for a rigid solid, introduced the inertia tensor, and derived the equations of rotation that bear his name. Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1788) and William Rowan Hamilton (1833) recast mechanics in terms of energy and generalised coordinates, which made it easy to write the equations for linked bodies such as a robot arm and showed that conservation laws come from symmetries (later made precise by Emmy Noether in 1918). Hamilton also invented quaternions in 1843, which are now the usual way to store 3D orientation.
Computers made the equations practical for complex machines. In the 1980s, robotics researchers (Walker and Orin, 1982; Featherstone, 1983) found algorithms that compute the dynamics of an arm with \( n \) joints in time proportional to \( n \), instead of \( n^3 \) or worse, which made real-time control possible. In graphics, Baraff and Witkin's work from the late 1980s onward added contact and constraints. Featherstone's book "Rigid Body Dynamics Algorithms" (2008) is still the reference. Today the same equations run in game engines, robot simulators such as MuJoCo, and the flight controllers of drones.
how: The mechanism is a small set of laws, applied to the centre of mass and about the centre of mass, plus a rule for what happens when bodies touch.
Step 1: Translation. For a body of mass \( m \) with total external force \( F \), the centre of mass has acceleration \( a = F/m \). Equivalently, the linear momentum \( p = mv \) changes at the rate \( dp/dt = F \). Internal forces between the atoms of the body cancel in pairs (Newton's third law), so only external forces count. This is why a thrown wrench can be tracked as a point (its centre of mass follows a parabola) while it spins around it.
Step 2: Rotation. A force applied away from the centre of mass also produces a torque \( \tau = r \times F \). Rotation obeys the same pattern as translation, with mass replaced by the inertia tensor: angular momentum is \( L = I\omega \) and \( dL/dt = \tau \). The inertia tensor depends on how the mass is spread: for a solid sphere of radius \( r \), \( I = \tfrac25 m r^2 \) about any axis; for a solid cylinder about its axis, \( \tfrac12 m r^2 \); for a box of sides \( w, h, d \) about the axis along \( d \), \( \tfrac{m}{12}(w^2 + h^2) \). The parallel-axis theorem gives the inertia about another axis at distance \( s \) from the centre: \( I = I_{cm} + m s^2 \). A body has three principal axes along which \( I \) is a simple number; in a frame fixed to the body, Euler's equations are
\[ I\,\dot\omega + \omega \times (I\omega) = \tau \]
The second term, the gyroscopic term, is why rotation is not like translation: even with zero torque, \( \omega \) can change direction while \( L \) stays constant. A book thrown in the air spins stably about its shortest and longest axes but tumbles about the middle axis (the intermediate-axis theorem, or tennis-racket effect). A point mass has no such behaviour.
Step 3: State and integration. A free rigid body has the state \( (x, q, p, L) \): position (3 numbers), orientation as a unit quaternion (4 numbers, with 3 degrees of freedom because of the unit length), linear momentum (3) and angular momentum (3). The rates are \( \dot x = p/m \), \( \dot q = \tfrac12\,(0,\omega)\,q \), \( \dot p = F \), \( \dot L = \tau \), where \( \omega = I_{world}^{-1} L \) and \( I_{world} = R\,I_{body}\,R^T \) is the inertia rotated into the world frame by the orientation \( R \). This is an ordinary differential equation (differential equations, L4), which a program advances in small steps with a numerical integrator; the choice of method decides whether energy drifts (see physics simulation, L2). Quaternions are renormalised after each step so they stay unit length.
Step 4: Conservation laws. When no external force acts, momentum \( p \) is constant; when no external torque acts, angular momentum \( L \) is constant; and if only conservative forces act, the total energy \( \tfrac12 m v^2 + \tfrac12\,\omega\!\cdot\! I\omega + U \) is constant. These are not extra rules but consequences of symmetry (Noether): the laws look the same when you shift in space, rotate in space or wait in time. Simulators use them as checks: if energy rises, the integrator is wrong. A figure skater pulling her arms in lowers \( I \), so \( \omega \) rises to keep \( L = I\omega \) constant.
Step 5: Contact. Bodies touching each other exert forces only along the contact normal (and friction along the surface). Because these forces are large and short, they are modelled as impulses. For a collision at contact point with normal \( n \), the relative velocity along the normal is \( v_{rel}\cdot n \), and restitution \( e \) (0 for a lump of clay, near 1 for a hard ball) sets how much of it bounces back. The impulse magnitude is
\[ j = \frac{-(1+e)\,v_{rel}\cdot n}{\tfrac1{m_A} + \tfrac1{m_B} + n\cdot\big((I_A^{-1}(r_A \times n))\times r_A + (I_B^{-1}(r_B \times n))\times r_B\big)} \]
where \( r_A \) and \( r_B \) are the vectors from each centre of mass to the contact point. It adds \( j/m \) to each velocity and changes each angular velocity by \( I^{-1}(r \times jn) \). The bracket in the denominator is how much the rotation resists the push: a hit far from the centre of mass moves the body less, because some of the impulse goes into spinning it.
Step 6: Linked bodies. A robot arm is a chain of rigid bodies joined at motors. Writing the energy of all links in terms of the joint angles \( q \) and applying Lagrange's method gives the manipulator equation
\[ M(q)\,\ddot q + C(q,\dot q)\,\dot q + g(q) = \tau \]
where \( M(q) \) is the joint-space inertia matrix, the \( C \) term collects centrifugal and Coriolis effects, \( g(q) \) is gravity and \( \tau \) is the vector of motor torques. Control software uses it two ways: forward dynamics (given torques, find accelerations, for simulation) and inverse dynamics (given a desired motion, find the torques, for control). The recursive algorithms of Featherstone and of Walker and Orin do both in \( O(n) \) time.

example: First, a rolling race. Three solid objects of the same mass and radius start from rest and roll without slipping down a 30° slope: a solid sphere, a solid cylinder and a thin ring. Gravity pulls with \( mg\sin\theta \) along the slope. Friction at the contact point makes the body roll, and the energy goes into both moving and spinning. With \( v = \omega r \), the acceleration is \( a = g\sin\theta / (1 + I/(mr^2)) \). With \( g\sin 30° = 4.905 \) m/s²: sphere (\( I/mr^2 = 0.4 \)): \( a = 4.905/1.4 = 3.50 \) m/s²; cylinder (0.5): \( 4.905/1.5 = 3.27 \); ring (1.0): \( 4.905/2 = 2.45 \). Over a 2 m slope, \( t = \sqrt{2L/a} \): sphere 1.07 s, cylinder 1.11 s, ring 1.28 s. The result does not depend on mass or radius, only on how the mass is spread: the object with its mass nearer the centre spins with less energy for the same speed, so more energy goes into moving forward. (A frictionless block would slide with \( a = 4.905 \).)
Next, a robot arm. A uniform rod of length \( l = 0.5 \) m and mass \( m = 2 \) kg is hinged at one end to a motor and held horizontal. Its centre is at 0.25 m, so gravity gives a torque of \( mg \cdot l/2 = 2 \times 9.81 \times 0.25 = 4.9 \) N·m about the hinge. The motor must supply 4.9 N·m just to hold it. Its moment of inertia about the hinge is \( \tfrac13 m l^2 = \tfrac13 \times 2 \times 0.25 = 0.167 \) kg·m². If the motor lets go, the initial angular acceleration is \( \alpha = \tau / I = 4.9 / 0.167 = 29.4 \) rad/s² (equal to \( 3g/2l \)); so the tip, 0.5 m from the hinge, starts at 14.7 m/s², about 1.5 g, faster than free fall. Motor sizing, gravity compensation and the idea that torque demand is highest when the arm is stretched out all follow from this one line.
Finally, an impulse. A 1 kg ball moving at 5 m/s hits a fixed floor head-on at the contact point directly below its centre (so \( r \times n = 0 \), no spin), with \( e = 0.6 \). The floor has infinite mass, so \( 1/m_B = 0 \), and the denominator is \( 1/m_A = 1 \). With \( v_{rel}\cdot n = -5 \): \( j = (1 + 0.6)\times 5 / 1 = 8 \) N·s. The ball leaves at \( -5 + 8/1 = +3 \) m/s, which is \( e \) times 5. If the ball is hit off-centre, a part of the impulse spins it and it bounces less high.
intuition: A rigid body is a coin that you can both push and twist: force moves it, torque turns it, and the only difference between the two is how the mass is spread.
These laws are natural because they say that nothing changes without a cause. A body keeps its momentum unless pushed, and keeps its angular momentum unless twisted. Rigidity removes the many internal motions and leaves only 6 numbers, so a solid with trillions of atoms becomes a small, computable object. The inertia tensor is just the bookkeeping of how mass far from an axis counts more (like a longer lever) than mass near it. Because the laws come from symmetry, they are the same everywhere and always, and this is why one engine can simulate a robot, a car and a falling book.
mistakes: People often think heavier things fall faster. In fact in a vacuum all bodies have the same acceleration \( g \), because the force of gravity is proportional to mass and \( a = F/m \) cancels it. Air drag, not mass, causes differences in practice.
People often think rotation is just like translation with new letters. In fact the inertia depends on the axis and on orientation, \( L \) and \( \omega \) need not point in the same direction, and a spinning body can tumble even with no torque (the gyroscopic term). This is why rotation simulation is harder than position simulation.
People often think a force applied to a body only changes its position. In fact a force whose line does not pass through the centre of mass also spins it. The same push moves a box differently when applied at the centre or at a corner.
People often think energy is lost when objects collide, so conservation of energy fails. In fact the energy is converted into heat, sound and deformation inside the objects, which a rigid model leaves out. Momentum is still conserved in the collision; that is why the impulse equation works. The restitution coefficient is a shortcut that summarises the lost energy.
People often think a rolling object slows down because friction opposes the motion. In fact for rolling without slipping, the contact point is momentarily at rest and static friction does no work; it only transfers energy between moving and spinning. Friction acts as a brake on a rolling object mainly through rolling resistance, which comes from deformation.
tradeoffs: Choose the rigid-body model when objects deform very little compared with the motion you care about (machines, vehicles, boxes, bones as a first model); choose a deformable model (finite elements, mass–spring, position-based dynamics) when bending, squashing or vibration is the point, such as cloth, soft grippers or a car crash. The rigid model has 6 numbers per body and is cheap; the deformable one has thousands and costs far more.
Choose the energy-based (Lagrangian) form when you work with linked bodies, constraints and generalised coordinates, as in robot arms, because it gives compact equations in joint angles; choose the Newton–Euler form (forces and torques on each body) when you need the forces inside joints or you want a recursive \( O(n) \) algorithm. Both give the same physics; they differ in convenience and cost.
Choose quaternions (4 numbers) for orientation in simulation and sensor fusion because they avoid the singularity of Euler angles (gimbal lock) and interpolate smoothly; choose rotation matrices when you transform many vectors; use Euler angles only for human input and display. Quaternions need re-normalising after each step.
Choose impulse-based, instantaneous collision handling for hard, brief hits (balls, bricks) in games and robotics; choose penalty (spring-like) contact forces when contact is soft or long-lasting, or when you need smooth force signals for control. Impulses are stable but ignore contact time; penalty forces need a small time step because they are stiff.
Choose the simple point-mass or planar model for a first design (a car's braking distance, a pendulum), and move to the full 3D model only for effects that need it, such as roll, spin and precession. The simpler model is easier to understand and to identify from data, but misses coupled effects.
friction: The rigid-body idealisation fails when parts flex or move relative to each other: a fast arm bends, a drone's propellers flex, gears have backlash, a tyre deforms, and a liquid in a tank sloshes. Real robots need extra terms (joint friction, motor dynamics, flexibility), and the model parameters are hard to know: the inertia tensor of a real part needs CAD data or measurement, and an error of a few percent in mass or centre of mass is common.
Contact and friction are where the model is weakest. Coulomb friction is a rule of thumb, not a law: the coefficient depends on the surfaces, speed, temperature and dirt. Impact is not instantaneous in reality, and a rigid model with multiple simultaneous contacts (a pile of boxes, a billiards break) can have no unique answer. This is the root of the sim-to-real gap in robotics.
Numerics add their own errors. Energy drifts with the integrator and step size, quaternions drift from unit length, and explicit handling of the gyroscopic term can blow up spinning bodies; engines use implicit gyroscopic terms or small steps. At very large scales, relativity, and at very small scales, quantum mechanics, replace the Newtonian picture; for engineering, the practical limit is usually modelling and measurement error, not the laws.
practice: Where an engineer meets this. Physics engines such as Box2D, Bullet, PhysX and MuJoCo implement exactly these equations; reading the state of one body (position, quaternion, linear and angular velocity, mass, inertia) shows the 12-number state. Drone and phone sensor code integrates a gyroscope (angular velocity) into a quaternion and fuses it with an accelerometer; robot control uses inverse dynamics to compute feed-forward torques; vehicle models use the planar rigid-body equations with tyre forces. Differentiable simulators let a learning algorithm compute gradients through these equations.
To use them well, get the units and frames right (a large share of bugs come from mixing body and world frames, or radians and degrees), keep masses and inertias realistic (an inertia far too small makes a body spin wildly from a small force), and keep mass ratios moderate. Check energy and momentum in a test: a free body should keep its momentum and (with no damping) its energy to within the integrator's error. Compare against a known case such as the rolling race, a pendulum period, or a ball bounce height after a collision with coefficient \( e \) (it returns to \( e^2 \) of the height).
To study it, work the examples by hand (inclined plane, pendulum, collision), then read the rigid-body chapters of Lynch and Park, "Modern Robotics" (free online), and Ruina and Pratap, "Introduction to Statics and Dynamics" (free online). For simulation, read Baraff and Witkin's course notes, then Featherstone's book if you work on robots. A practical exercise is to write a 2D rigid-body integrator with a box and a floor, and watch what changes when you use explicit and semi-implicit Euler.
quiz: What is the state of a free rigid body? || Position (3 numbers), orientation (3 degrees of freedom, usually stored as a 4-number unit quaternion), and the rates of change: linear and angular velocity (or momentum), 3 numbers each. That is 6 degrees of freedom and 6 rates.

A rigid model gives a single point of contact and an infinite stiffness, and so cannot predict the grip force or the slip.

@@ phonetics
value: Phonetics is the science of speech sounds: how the body makes them, what they look like as sound waves, and how the ear hears them. For an engineer, its most useful result is a simple physical picture of the voice. The vocal cords in the throat produce a buzz with a certain pitch. The tube above them (throat, mouth, nose, lips) acts as a filter that strengthens some frequencies and weakens others. The strengthened bands are called formants, and their positions are what make an "ee" sound different from an "ah". This is the source–filter model, developed by Gunnar Fant in 1960.
The picture explains why computers analyse speech as a spectrogram: a plot of how much energy there is at each frequency, over time. In a spectrogram, vowels appear as bands of energy at the formants, consonants appear as short bursts, noise or silence, and the whole utterance becomes an image that a person can learn to read and a neural network can learn to recognise. A second consequence is compression. The voice has only a handful of slowly changing controls (pitch, the shape of the tract, whether the cords vibrate), so a speech codec can send a model of the tract, not the waveform: phone codecs use roughly 5 to 24 kbit/s, against 256 kbit/s for raw 16 kHz audio.
For the layer above, speech recognition and synthesis (L1) are built on this: acoustic features such as log-mel spectrograms are chosen to match what the vocal tract and the ear do, and text-to-speech must reproduce the right formants, timing and pitch. Speech codecs (L0), from the GSM phone network to modern video-call codecs, model the vocal tract with linear prediction. There is no lower layer in this map; the knowledge comes from acoustics and anatomy, and it uses the Fourier ideas that the signal-processing layers provide.
terms: Fundamental frequency (F0) — the rate at which the vocal cords open and close, heard as pitch; roughly 100 Hz for an adult man and 200 Hz for an adult woman on average.
Harmonics — the buzz from the cords has energy at F0 and at whole-number multiples of it (2·F0, 3·F0, ...), like the overtones of a string.
Formant — a resonance of the vocal tract: a frequency band where speech has strong energy; the first two or three (F1, F2, F3) identify vowels.
Phoneme — the smallest sound unit that can change a word's meaning (for example /p/ and /b/ in "pat" and "bat"); English has about 40 to 44.
Voicing — whether the vocal cords vibrate during a sound: /z/ is voiced, /s/ is not.
Spectrogram — an image with time on the horizontal axis, frequency on the vertical axis and the energy at each point shown by brightness.
Voice onset time (VOT) — the delay between the release of a stop consonant and the start of voicing; the main cue to tell /p t k/ from /b d g/.
Coarticulation — the overlap of neighbouring sounds, since the mouth prepares for the next sound while making the current one.
history: People have studied speech for thousands of years, but its physics began in the 19th century. Hermann von Helmholtz (1863) explained vowels as resonances of the mouth and built mechanical resonators to imitate them. Alexander Graham Bell's father Melville Bell created "Visible Speech" (1867), a written system of symbols for mouth positions. The International Phonetic Alphabet was first published in 1888 to write the sounds of all languages.
Electronics made speech visible. The Bell Labs sound spectrograph (1946, described by Potter, Kopp and Green in "Visible Speech", 1947) drew spectrograms on paper and showed that vowels have stable formant patterns. At Haskins Laboratories in the early 1950s researchers painted spectrograms by hand and played them back (the Pattern Playback) to find which pieces of the picture a listener needs to hear a given sound. Peterson and Barney (1952) measured the first two formants of vowels from 76 speakers and showed that the vowels form clusters with some overlap. Gunnar Fant's "Acoustic Theory of Speech Production" (1960) formalised the source–filter model with a mathematical treatment of tubes.
Digital methods followed. Atal and Hanauer (1971) and Itakura and Saito used linear prediction to estimate the vocal-tract filter from a short segment of speech, and Schroeder and Atal's CELP coder (1985) became the base of phone codecs such as GSM AMR and later speech codecs. Davis and Mermelstein (1980) introduced MFCC features that mimic the ear's frequency scale, which dominated speech recognition for 30 years. Klatt's formant synthesiser (1980) and the neural vocoder WaveNet (2016) mark the progress of speech generation, and in the 2010s deep networks learned directly from spectrogram-like features, as in OpenAI's Whisper (2022), which takes an 80-channel log-mel spectrogram with 25 ms windows every 10 ms.
how: Speech is produced in three stages: air from the lungs, a vibrating source, and a shaped tube. Each stage has a clear acoustic signature.
Step 1: The source. Air pushed from the lungs passes between the vocal folds in the larynx. For voiced sounds (all vowels, and consonants such as /m/, /z/, /b/) the folds vibrate: they close, pressure builds, they blow open, and close again, 100 to 250 times per second. The result is a train of pulses, whose spectrum has energy at F0 and its multiples, falling off at about 12 dB per octave. For unvoiced sounds (/s/, /f/, /p/) the folds stay open and the sound is noise made by air rushing through a narrow gap or released from a closure. Whispered speech uses noise as the source for all sounds, yet is still understood, which shows that the filter carries most of the message.
Step 2: The filter. The vocal tract is a tube about 17 to 18 cm long in an adult man, closed at the vocal folds and open at the lips. Such a tube resonates at frequencies where a quarter wavelength, three quarters, five quarters and so on fit its length: \( F_n = (2n-1)\,c / (4L) \), where \( c \approx 350 \) m/s is the speed of sound in warm, moist air. For \( L = 17.5 \) cm, this gives \( F_1 = 500 \) Hz, \( F_2 = 1500 \) Hz and \( F_3 = 2500 \) Hz, which are the formants of the neutral vowel (the "uh" in "about"). Any resonance of the tube boosts the nearby harmonics of the source, so the output spectrum is the source spectrum multiplied by the tube's response. The speaker changes the shape of the tube to change the formants: that is how vowels are made.
Step 3: What the formants mean. Two numbers separate most vowels. The first formant, F1, goes up when the jaw opens and the tongue is low (as in "ah", F1 about 700 to 800 Hz) and goes down when the tongue is high (as in "ee" and "oo", about 250 to 300 Hz). The second, F2, goes up when the tongue is pushed forward (as in "ee", about 2,200 to 2,400 Hz) and goes down when the tongue is back and the lips are rounded (as in "oo", about 600 to 900 Hz). Plotting (F2, F1) for many speakers gives the familiar vowel chart: "ee" at top front, "oo" at top back, "ah" at the bottom. Women and children have shorter tracts, so all their formants are higher, by about 15% and 20 to 30% on average, and a listener's brain normalises for this. Consonants add other cues: stops (/p t k b d g/) are a brief closure followed by a burst; nasals (/m n/) route sound through the nose and add a low resonance at about 250 Hz with dips (antiformants); fricatives (/s sh f/) are noise filtered by the small cavity in front of the constriction, so /s/ has energy above 4 kHz and /sh/ has it lower. English /b/ and /p/ differ mainly in VOT: about 0 to 25 ms for /b/ and 40 to 100 ms for /p/ in initial position.
Step 4: Seeing it: the spectrogram. To analyse the signal, cut it into short overlapping frames (typically 25 ms long, shifted by 10 ms), apply a window, compute an FFT for each frame and take the magnitude. A window of about 5 ms gives a "wideband" picture, with poor frequency detail but sharp time detail, where each vocal-fold pulse appears as a vertical stripe and the formants as dark horizontal bands. A window of 30 ms or more gives a "narrowband" picture, where individual harmonics are visible as thin horizontal lines. This is the time–frequency trade-off: a short window sees when things happen, a long one sees which frequencies are present, and you cannot have both sharply.
Step 5: Matching the ear and the codec. The ear does not hear all frequencies equally: the cochlea resolves low frequencies finely and high frequencies coarsely, as described by the mel scale \( m = 2595\log_{10}(1 + f/700) \). Speech features therefore sum the FFT energy into about 40 to 80 mel-spaced bands, take logarithms, and optionally apply a cosine transform to get MFCCs. A pre-emphasis filter lifts the high frequencies to compensate for the downward tilt of the source. For compression, linear prediction models each frame as an all-pole filter with about 10 coefficients (the formants) driven by a pulse train or noise, so the codec sends the filter and the pitch, not the waveform. This works because the tract moves slowly: the parameters change on the scale of 10 to 20 ms.
Step 6: Why recognition is hard. The same word looks different in each speaker's spectrogram because of tract length, pitch, accent, speaking rate and noise. Sounds also run together: coarticulation changes formants near a neighbouring consonant (the F2 "transition" is itself a cue for the place of the consonant). Conversational English has roughly 10 to 15 sounds per second, with no gaps between words. Modern recognisers learn these variations from data, using the spectrogram as input, and the phonetic knowledge shows up in design choices: the frame size, the mel scale, the sampling rate of 16 kHz (capturing up to 8 kHz, where fricatives live), and the need for a language model to resolve ambiguity.

example: First, the tube. A man's vocal tract is \( L = 17.5 \) cm and the speed of sound is \( c = 350 \) m/s = 35,000 cm/s. Then \( F_1 = 35{,}000 / (4 \times 17.5) = 500 \) Hz, \( F_2 = 3 \times 500 = 1{,}500 \) Hz and \( F_3 = 5 \times 500 = 2{,}500 \) Hz. A child with a 12 cm tract gets \( F_1 = 35{,}000/48 = 729 \) Hz, so every formant is about 46% higher. This shows why the same vowel gives different numbers for different speakers, and why recognisers must normalise.
Next, the source and the filter together. A man speaks at F0 = 120 Hz. Harmonics are at 120, 240, 360, 480, 600 Hz. The neutral vowel's F1 = 500 Hz boosts the harmonics at 480 and 600 Hz, so they appear strong; the filter picks the harmonic amplitudes, and the ear and the recogniser read the envelope. Now a soprano sings at 880 Hz. Her harmonics are 880 Hz apart: 880, 1760, 2640. Whatever the vowel, only a few harmonics exist below 3 kHz and the envelope of formants is poorly sampled, so vowels become hard to tell apart. Trained singers tune a formant to a harmonic to be loud, giving up vowel clarity. This is a good example of why pitch and formants must be treated as two separate things.
Next, the numbers of a spectrogram. One second of speech sampled at 16 kHz has 16,000 samples. A 25 ms window is 400 samples; a 10 ms hop is 160 samples. The number of frames is \( 1 + \lfloor (16000 - 400)/160 \rfloor = 98 \). With a 512-point FFT, each frame gives 257 frequency bins spaced \( 16000/512 = 31.25 \) Hz apart. Compressed to 80 mel bands, the second becomes an 80 × 98 table of about 7,800 numbers, which is a picture that a convolutional or transformer model reads. The raw audio is 16,000 samples of 16 bits, 256 kbit/s. A narrowband phone call uses 8 kHz sampling and keeps only 300–3,400 Hz, which removes the high-frequency energy that separates /s/ from /f/ and makes them hard to tell apart on the phone (hence "F as in Frank").
Finally, information. Research on many languages (Coupé and colleagues, 2019) found that spoken language carries roughly 39 bits of linguistic information per second, whatever the language. A codec such as AMR at 12.2 kbit/s sends over 300 times more than that, because it must also carry the voice quality, the pitch and the timing that make the speaker recognisable, not just the words.
intuition: A voice is a buzzing reed (the vocal cords) played through a shape-shifting horn (the mouth), and a spectrogram is the sheet music of the horn's changes.
The model is natural because it separates two things that vary independently. The pitch of the buzz tells whether it is a high or low voice, a question or a statement. The shape of the horn decides which sound it is. A speaker can sing the same vowel at any pitch, and say all vowels at the same pitch; since the two are independent, the system can be described with few parameters and compressed. This is also why the ear and the machine both do better when they look at the spectrum envelope than at the raw waveform.
mistakes: People often think each letter is a sound. In fact spelling and sounds differ: English has about 26 letters and 40 to 44 phonemes, "through", "though" and "tough" share letters with different sounds, and "ph" is /f/. Speech systems model sounds, which is why they use phonetic or learned units, not letters, as a first step.
People often think the vocal cords make the sounds of speech. In fact the cords only make a buzz with pitch. The vowels and most consonants are made by the shape of the tract above the cords. A whisper has no cord vibration and is still easy to understand.
People often think a speech waveform shows the words. In fact the raw waveform is nearly unreadable; the information is in how the energy is distributed across frequencies and how this changes over time. This is why the spectrogram, not the waveform, is the standard input.
People often think there are clear gaps between spoken words. In fact in continuous speech there is often no silence between words, and there are often silences inside words (during a stop closure, for example). Word boundaries are inferred from knowledge of the language.
People often think that 16 kHz or 8 kHz sampling is an arbitrary choice. In fact the sampling rate must be at least twice the highest frequency that matters (the Nyquist rate). Most vowel information is below 4 kHz, but fricatives have energy at 4 to 8 kHz, so 16 kHz is used for recognition and 8 kHz for old telephone lines, at the cost of confusing /s/ and /f/.
tradeoffs: Choose a short analysis window (5 to 10 ms) when you need timing, such as finding a stop burst or the pitch period; choose a long one (30 ms or more) when you need frequency detail, such as separating harmonics or measuring pitch. The standard 25 ms window with a 10 ms step is a compromise that works for recognition. Neither is right for everything.
Choose a model-based representation (linear prediction, formants, the source–filter model) when bit rate or interpretability matters, as in phone codecs and low-power devices; choose waveform-based or neural representations when you need top quality and naturalness. The model-based approach saves bits and fails when the signal does not fit the voice model (music, noise, singing, several speakers).
Choose 8 kHz (narrowband) for telephony where legacy networks or bandwidth are limited, 16 kHz (wideband) for recognition and most voice apps, and 24 to 48 kHz for music or high-quality voice, where fricatives and breath are audible. Every step up in sampling rate costs bits and gives gains in intelligibility that shrink after 8 kHz.
Choose mel-scale features (log-mel spectrograms) as input to a neural model of speech because they are close to the ear and are compact; choose raw waveform input when you have a lot of data and can afford the compute, so that the network learns its own filters. Mel features throw away phase and fine detail that matter for generating speech, so synthesis needs a separate vocoder.
Choose phoneme-based pipelines when you have little data, many rare words, or need pronunciation control (names, medical terms); choose end-to-end models when you have much data and the domain is open. Phoneme pipelines are interpretable and need a pronunciation dictionary; end-to-end systems are simpler but harder to correct.
friction: The source–filter model is an approximation. The source and tract interact, nasal sounds add side branches with antiresonances, fricatives are not produced at the glottis, and the real tract is not a straight tube but has a bend and varying width. The model fits vowels well, and consonants less well.
Variation is the main practical problem. Speakers differ in tract length, accent, speaking rate and style; the same speaker changes with emotion and noise (the Lombard effect makes people speak louder and change formants in noise). Children, tonal languages (where F0 contours carry meaning), whispered or disordered speech, and code-switching between languages all stretch models that were developed on adult speech in one language. Recognition errors are therefore uneven across groups, which is a fairness issue for products.
The science has gaps. Linking the acoustic signal to the units a listener perceives is not fully solved: phonemes are a useful abstraction, but the signal has no clean boundaries and listeners use context heavily. Measuring formants automatically is unreliable for high-pitched voices, because the harmonics sample the envelope too sparsely. And while neural systems now match humans on clean read speech, they still fall behind on overlapping speakers, strong noise and rare accents.
practice: Where an engineer meets this. Any speech pipeline: recording at 16 kHz, applying pre-emphasis, framing at 25 ms / 10 ms, computing log-mel features (for example with torchaudio or librosa), and passing them to a recogniser such as Whisper or a streaming model. Voice assistants, call-centre analytics, subtitles for video, voice search and ad-supported audio all depend on it. On the output side, text-to-speech systems model prosody (pitch, duration, loudness) and the spectrum, and then a vocoder makes the waveform. In telephony and conferencing, codecs such as AMR-WB and Opus use linear prediction at their core.
To study it, open a recording in Praat (free software for phonetics) or Audacity's spectrogram view and say the vowels "ee, ah, oo" at one pitch, then the same at a different pitch. You will see the formant bands stay put while the harmonic spacing changes. Measure F1 and F2 for each vowel and plot them to make your own vowel chart. Then compute a spectrogram in Python with a 25 ms and a 5 ms window, and compare the two pictures. Peter Ladefoged's "A Course in Phonetics" and Kenneth Stevens' "Acoustic Phonetics" are standard texts; for engineering, Rabiner and Schafer's "Theory and Applications of Digital Speech Processing" and Jurafsky and Martin's "Speech and Language Processing" cover the signal and the recognition side.
When you build a product, test with the speakers you will serve: different ages, accents, microphones and noise levels. Log word-error rate by group. Choose a sampling rate and microphone path that keep the 4 to 8 kHz range if your task needs consonants (such as names and spelled-out letters). Watch for the effect of codecs on the audio: a voice call passed through AMR-NB has lost the high band before your model sees it, and a model trained on clean data may need to be trained on telephone audio for call-centre use.
quiz: What are the two parts of the source–filter model, and which carries the pitch and which the vowel? || The source (the vibrating vocal folds) gives the buzz and its pitch, F0. The filter (the vocal tract) shapes the spectrum with formants, which identify the vowel. Pitch and vowel are largely independent.
Why are F1 and F2 enough to separate most vowels? || F1 mostly follows the height of the tongue and the jaw opening, and F2 follows how far forward the tongue is and the lip rounding. Together they locate the tongue's position, and each vowel has its own region on this chart.
For a 16 kHz signal, 25 ms windows and a 10 ms hop, how many frames are in 2 seconds? || 32,000 samples; window 400, hop 160: \( 1 + \lfloor (32000 - 400)/160 \rfloor = 1 + 197 = 198 \) frames.
A tube 20 cm long, closed at one end and open at the other, has its first resonance at what frequency (c = 350 m/s)? || \( F_1 = c/(4L) = 35{,}000/(4 \times 20) = 437.5 \) Hz. The next ones are 3 and 5 times this value.
When would you use a long analysis window and when a short one? || Use a long window (30 ms or more) to resolve individual harmonics and measure pitch; use a short window (about 5 ms) to see fast events such as stop bursts and each glottal pulse. 25 ms is a compromise for recognition.
