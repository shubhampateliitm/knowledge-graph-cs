@@+ kafka
tradeoffs: Choose Kafka over RabbitMQ when many independent readers need the same events, when readers must replay history, or when you need per-key order at more than about 50,000 messages per second. Choose RabbitMQ when the job is a work queue with per-message routing, priorities, delayed retries and request–reply, at under roughly 10,000 messages per second. You give up replay and cheap fan-out with a queue, and you give up rich routing and per-message acknowledgement with a log. Kafka 4 share groups narrow the gap for the queue case, but they are new and lack the routing features.
Choose the partition count from throughput: divide peak MB/s by what one partition sustains (often 5–10 MB/s write, less for consumers that do real work) and add 2x headroom. Choose fewer, larger partitions when order and low overhead matter (a few thousand per broker is a practical ceiling); choose more when consumer parallelism is the bottleneck. Either way you give something up: too few caps your consumer parallelism forever, because adding partitions later remaps keys and breaks per-key order, while too many costs file handles, memory, and longer leader elections after a broker failure.
Choose acks=all with replication factor 3 and min.insync.replicas=2 for anything you cannot regenerate (billing, audit, impressions that are paid for). Choose acks=1 only for telemetry where losing a few seconds of data on a leader crash is acceptable. The price of the safe setting is latency (one extra follower round trip, about 1–5 ms within a region) and availability (writes stop if two of three replicas are down). On AWS, replication across three zones is also a bill: at $0.01 per GB each way between zones, copying to two followers costs about $0.04 per GB ingested before any consumer reads.
Choose Kafka on its own brokers when you want the lowest latency (single-digit milliseconds) and your team can run it; choose Redpanda when you want the Kafka API with fewer moving parts and better tail latency on the same hardware, accepting a different license and smaller ecosystem; choose Pulsar when you need millions of topics or hard multi-tenancy and can run BookKeeper beside the brokers; choose Kinesis (about 1 MB/s in and 2 MB/s out per shard) when you are all-in on AWS and prefer paying more per GB to run nothing. Diskless and object-storage designs (tiered storage, WarpStream-style) cut cost several times but add hundreds of milliseconds of latency.
Do not use Kafka as your database. It has no secondary indexes and no point updates; a compacted topic is a changelog for rebuilding state, not something you query by field. Do not use it for request–reply either. Use it as the durable, replayable spine, and let a database, Pinot or a stream processor build the queryable views.
interview: Why can a Kafka consumer group not scale past the partition count, and what do you do when you hit it? || Each partition is read by exactly one member per group, so extra members sit idle. Options are to add partitions (which remaps keys and breaks per-key order, so plan a migration to a new topic), to fan work out inside one consumer with a thread pool while committing offsets carefully, or to use share groups when per-key order is not needed.
You see consumer lag growing on one partition only. What do you check? || A hot key (skewed partitioning), a slow or stuck consumer on that partition (poison message, long processing, rebalance loop), or a broker with a slow disk or network leading that partition. Compare the partition's produce rate with others, look at per-partition processing time, and check the leader's request queue time.
A team wants exactly-once from Kafka into Postgres. What do you tell them? || Kafka transactions give exactly-once only for Kafka-to-Kafka. For an external database, make the write idempotent (upsert by a unique event ID, or store the offset in the same database transaction as the result) and accept at-least-once delivery from Kafka.
Why not just give every topic 1,000 partitions? || Each partition costs file handles, memory, controller metadata and failover time, and smaller per-partition batches compress and replicate worse. You also cannot reduce the count later. Size for peak consumer parallelism plus headroom.
Cross-region active–active with Kafka: what breaks? || Offsets differ between clusters, so consumers cannot simply fail over; MirrorMaker 2 translates offsets with lag. Ordering across regions is not guaranteed, and the same key written in two regions needs a conflict rule. Many teams make each region own its topics and treat the other region's data as a read-only mirror.
Why does acks=all not guarantee no data loss by itself? || It waits for the current in-sync replicas, which can shrink to the leader alone. Without min.insync.replicas=2 the leader accepts writes that exist on one machine. Also, no fsync per write means a simultaneous power loss on all replicas can lose recent data.
@@+ flink
tradeoffs: Choose Flink over Spark Structured Streaming when you need latency under about a second at scale, large keyed state (hundreds of GB to TB), complex event-time logic or timers, or pattern matching (CEP). Choose Spark Structured Streaming when your team and data already live in Spark or Databricks, freshness of 5 seconds to minutes is enough, and you want one engine and one codebase for batch and streaming. You give up operational simplicity by choosing Flink (a second cluster type, savepoints, state compatibility), and you give up latency and state scale by choosing micro-batches; Spark's Real-Time Mode narrows the latency gap but is newer.
Choose Kafka Streams instead of Flink when the job is a service that reads Kafka and writes Kafka, with state up to a few hundred GB spread across instances, and you want to deploy it like any other application with no cluster. Choose Flink when the sources or sinks are not Kafka, when jobs need to scale and be rescheduled independently of the app, or when several teams share one managed runtime. You give up Flink's SQL, connectors and resource isolation with the library, and you gain not having a cluster to run.
Choose the heap state backend only when state is small (under a few GB per task) and you want the fastest access; choose RocksDB when state exceeds memory or is large enough that garbage collection pauses hurt. RocksDB makes each state access go through serialization and a disk-backed store, several times slower per lookup than heap, but it gives incremental checkpoints, so a 1 TB state does not copy 1 TB every time. Set the checkpoint interval between 10 seconds and a few minutes: shorter means less replay after failure and lower sink latency, longer means less overhead. With two-phase-commit sinks, results only become visible at checkpoint completion, so the interval is a floor on end-to-end latency for exactly-once output.
Choose event time with watermarks when results must be correct despite late or out-of-order data (mobile clients, cross-region events); choose processing time when you only need approximate, low-latency monitoring. A bounded-out-of-orderness watermark of 30 seconds means windows close 30 seconds late and hold state for that long; a late event after that goes to a side output or is dropped. You choose between completeness and latency, and no setting gives both.
Set max parallelism before the first production deploy (the default is derived from parallelism, often 128 or more but rounded from the initial value). It fixes the number of key groups, so it is the ceiling for rescaling from a savepoint, and changing it later means discarding state. Also assign stable uid values to stateful operators, or a job change can make old state unrecoverable.
interview: How does Flink get exactly-once state, and where does it stop? || Barriers flow with the data; each operator snapshots its state when the barrier passes, and a completed checkpoint is a consistent cut of all operators plus source offsets. On failure the job restores the checkpoint and replays from the offsets. External effects are only exactly-once if the sink is transactional (two-phase commit) or idempotent.
A Flink job's checkpoints keep growing slower. What do you look at? || Backpressure (a slow operator delays barriers), state size and RocksDB compaction, skewed keys, and alignment time. Unaligned checkpoints or buffer debloating help with backpressure; incremental checkpoints and a state TTL help with size; fix skew with a two-stage aggregation.
Flink or Spark Structured Streaming for a fraud-scoring pipeline with 200 ms latency? || Flink: record-at-a-time processing with low per-record overhead and timers fits it. Spark micro-batches have a floor of a few hundred milliseconds to seconds, though Real-Time Mode targets this. I would also ask about state size and who operates it before deciding.
What happens to a keyed stream when you scale from 40 to 80 tasks? || State is stored in key groups (max parallelism of them); rescaling from a savepoint redistributes key groups among the new tasks, as long as the new parallelism does not exceed max parallelism. The job stops, restores and resumes, so scaling is not free of downtime.
How do you handle a hot key in a keyed window? || Pre-aggregate locally (combiner) or split the key with a salt, aggregate in two stages, then merge. Keyed state for one key lives on one task, so parallelism cannot help without changing the key.
@@+ spark
tradeoffs: Choose Spark for multi-hour ETL, wide shuffles (joins and aggregations over terabytes), and jobs that must survive node loss; choose Trino when people wait at a keyboard and queries should finish in seconds to a few minutes; choose DuckDB or Polars when the data fits on one machine. A useful rule: if the input is under about 200–500 GB of Parquet and one 64-core, 512 GB machine is available, scaling up is usually faster and cheaper than a cluster, because Spark pays for job startup (tens of seconds to minutes), serialization and network shuffles. Past a few TB, or when many people share the compute, Spark wins. You give up interactive latency and pay overhead to get scale and fault tolerance.
Choose Spark over Ray when the work is tables, SQL and shuffles; choose Ray when it is arbitrary Python tasks, GPUs, long-lived actors holding a model, or reinforcement learning. Spark's scheduling cost per task is tens of milliseconds and its model is stage-by-stage; Ray's is around a millisecond and it supports dynamic task graphs. Choose Spark over Dask when you need SQL, a mature optimizer, and multi-tenant operations; choose Dask when the code is already pandas or NumPy and the team lives in Python. You give up ecosystem and governance with the Python-native engines, and you give up flexibility with Spark.
Choose the DataFrame and SQL APIs over RDDs and Python UDFs. Catalyst and Tungsten can only optimize what they can see, so a built-in function runs in the JVM at full speed, while a plain Python UDF ships rows to a Python process and is often 5–10x slower. If you must use Python, use pandas UDFs (Arrow batches) or move the logic into SQL expressions. Choose RDDs only for truly unstructured, custom-partitioned work.
Tune partitions to the data, not the default. The default spark.sql.shuffle.partitions of 200 is wrong for both 20 GB and 20 TB jobs; aim for shuffle partitions of roughly 100–300 MB. Adaptive Query Execution (on by default) coalesces small partitions and splits skewed joins at runtime, so start with it before hand-tuning; salting a key is the fallback when one key holds a large share of rows. Too many partitions waste scheduler time and create small files; too few spill to disk or run out of memory.
Choose where to run it by who operates it. YARN fits an existing Hadoop estate, Kubernetes fits a platform team that already runs it, and a managed service (Databricks, EMR) fits teams that do not want to tune clusters. Spot instances cut compute cost 60–80% but lose shuffle files when a node is reclaimed, so use them for workers, not the driver, and enable decommission or external shuffle services. On Databricks, Photon often gives 2x or more on scan, join and aggregation heavy SQL for a higher price per hour, so check cost per job, not speed alone.
interview: A Spark job has 199 tasks finishing in 1 minute and one task running for 40 minutes. What is happening and what do you do? || Data skew: one key or partition holds far more rows. Confirm in the Spark UI (task duration and shuffle read size), enable AQE skew join, or salt the hot key and aggregate in two steps; for joins, broadcast the small side if it fits.
When would you not use Spark? || When data fits on one machine (DuckDB or Polars is faster), for interactive sub-second queries (Trino, a warehouse or ClickHouse), for low-latency record-at-a-time streaming (Flink), and for GPU-heavy or actor-style Python workloads (Ray).
Why is a Python UDF slow, and what are the alternatives? || Each batch of rows is serialized to a Python worker and back, and Catalyst cannot optimize through it. Use built-in functions, SQL expressions, pandas UDFs with Arrow, or write the logic in Scala/Java if it must be custom.
How does Spark recover from a lost executor halfway through a job? || It reruns the lost tasks, and if shuffle files on that node are needed, it recomputes the parent stage partitions from lineage. With long lineages, a checkpoint or writing to Delta cuts recomputation. External shuffle storage avoids losing map output.
Explain a broadcast join versus a sort-merge join and how Spark picks. || Broadcast sends the small table to every executor and avoids shuffling the large side; it is used when the small side is under spark.sql.autoBroadcastJoinThreshold (10 MB by default, and AQE can switch at runtime using real sizes). Sort-merge shuffles and sorts both sides and scales to any size. Raising the threshold risks driver and executor out-of-memory errors.
@@+ spark-sss
tradeoffs: Choose the default micro-batch trigger when you want freshness of a few seconds and simple operations; choose trigger availableNow (incremental batch) when freshness of 15 minutes to hours is enough. With availableNow the cluster starts, processes everything new, and stops, so you pay only for processing time, often 50–90% less than a cluster that runs around the clock, and you keep checkpointed, exactly-once progress. You give up latency and the benefit of warm state in memory. Choose Real-Time Mode (Spark 4.1) for sub-second needs on simple stateless or lightly stateful queries; choose Flink when you need millisecond latency with large state and complex event-time logic.
Each micro-batch has a fixed cost (planning, scheduling, committing offsets and state), usually 100 ms to a second, so setting the trigger to 1 second on a small stream makes the cluster spend most of its time on overhead. Set the trigger interval to the freshness you really need, not the lowest the engine allows. Writing to Delta every few seconds also creates many small files; you then need auto compaction or periodic OPTIMIZE, which trades freshness against file count and read speed.
Choose the default in-memory (HDFS-backed) state store for small state; choose the RocksDB state store once state reaches millions of keys per executor, because the default keeps state on the JVM heap and long garbage-collection pauses stall batches. RocksDB costs some serialization and local disk, and with changelog checkpointing it avoids copying the full state at each batch. State only shrinks if you set a watermark (for aggregations and stream–stream joins) or a time-to-live (for arbitrary state); without one, state grows forever and the job eventually fails.
Choose foreachBatch with a MERGE into Delta when you need upserts or writes to several sinks; choose a plain streaming sink when appends are enough. Exactly-once is achieved by replayable sources plus idempotent sinks keyed by batch ID; if your foreachBatch code writes to an external system without using the batch ID, a retried batch writes twice. The checkpoint ties to the query plan, so changing stateful operators or the number of shuffle partitions often needs a new checkpoint and a backfill. Plan for that before the first deploy.
interview: How does Structured Streaming get exactly-once semantics? || The source is replayable (offsets are stored in the checkpoint's write-ahead log before the batch runs), the engine commits each batch once, and the sink must be idempotent or transactional using the batch ID. After a failure the same batch is re-planned with the same offsets and rewritten safely.
Your stream job lags 10 minutes behind Kafka after a deploy. What do you check? || Whether batch duration exceeds the trigger interval, input rows per second versus processed rows per second, state store size and commit time, shuffle partition count, skew, and maxOffsetsPerTrigger to cap batch size. Scale executors or lower the batch size, then confirm it catches up.
When is availableNow better than a continuous stream? || When freshness requirements are minutes to hours, since you save the idle cluster cost and get retry and checkpoint semantics. It is also the way to run backfills in controlled-size batches.
Why must you set a watermark on a stream–stream join? || Without it the engine cannot know when no more matching rows can arrive, so it keeps all state forever. The watermark plus a time-range join condition bounds the state and lets it emit outer-join results.
Spark Structured Streaming versus Flink: how do you decide? || Latency target (sub-second with large state points to Flink), team and platform (Databricks and Spark skills point to SSS), state size, and the operational cost of a second system. If 5-second to minute latency is fine, the single-engine choice usually wins.
@@+ mapreduce
tradeoffs: Choose MapReduce today only to keep an existing job alive: a stable nightly job that works, is cheap to run, and would cost engineer-weeks to rewrite. Choose Spark (or Spark SQL) for any new work. The numbers explain why: in 2014 Spark sorted 100 TB in 23 minutes on 206 machines, where Hadoop MapReduce had taken 72 minutes on 2,100 machines, and for iterative jobs that reread the same data the gap was often 10x or more, because MapReduce writes every stage's output to HDFS. You give up a very simple, very robust model (every task is restartable and nothing is held in memory) and gain speed and a richer API.
For a single-pass job (read once, one shuffle, write once) the gap is much smaller, often 2–3x, because both engines then spend most of their time on disk and network. If such a job runs in a window you never miss, the migration case is mostly operational: you want one engine, one set of skills, and one security model, not speed. Quantify the rewrite cost before deciding, and leave jobs alone when the total saving is a few node-hours a night.
Choose Hive on Tez or Spark SQL over Hive on MapReduce for SQL; they plan a whole query as one DAG and avoid the HDFS round trip between stages. If you are migrating, convert the logic to SQL or DataFrames rather than translating mapper and reducer classes line by line, which carries over their inefficiencies (no combiner, too few reducers, identity stages).
Inside MapReduce, the decisions that matter are the number of reducers (aim for tasks of about one block of output each, 0.95–1.75 times the cluster's reduce slots), using a combiner when the reduce function is associative (it can cut shuffle volume by 10x or more on counting jobs), and enabling speculative execution only when stragglers are a hardware problem, not data skew. Skew gives one reducer far more data; duplicating that task does not help.
MapReduce is still worth knowing as a model. The shuffle (partition, sort, merge), the reliance on re-running deterministic tasks, and the idea of moving computation to data all survive in Spark, Trino's fault-tolerant mode and Flink's batch mode. When you reason about a Spark shuffle, you are reasoning about the MapReduce one with a different file layout.
interview: Why did Spark beat MapReduce, and where does it not? || Spark keeps intermediate data in memory across stages, plans a whole DAG, and avoids JVM start-up per task; iterative and multi-stage jobs gain most. Single-pass, disk-bound jobs gain little, and Spark can run out of memory where MapReduce would just spill.
What does a combiner do and when is it safe? || It runs the reduce function on each mapper's output before the shuffle, cutting network volume. It is safe only when the function is associative and commutative (sums, counts, max), and the framework may run it zero, one or many times, so correctness must not depend on it.
How does MapReduce handle failure? || A failed task is rerun on another node; map outputs live on local disk, so if a node dies after its map finished, those maps are rerun too. The ApplicationMaster retries tasks up to a limit, and the job output is only committed at the end, so partial output is discarded.
A reducer takes 10x longer than the others. Why and what do you do? || Key skew: one key has most of the values. Add a combiner, use a custom partitioner or a salted key with a second job to merge, or sample keys for a range partitioner. Speculative execution will not fix it.
Would you ever write a new MapReduce job in 2026? || Almost never. Possible exceptions are a locked-down Hadoop cluster with no Spark, or a tiny existing pipeline. Otherwise Spark SQL or DataFrames give faster code that is much shorter.
@@+ hdfs
tradeoffs: Choose HDFS when you run on-premises hardware, when compute can sit next to the disks, and when you want cheap sequential throughput with atomic directory renames and appends. Choose object storage (S3, GCS, ADLS) when you are in the cloud, want compute and storage scaled separately, and do not want to run a NameNode. S3 stores each object with very high durability and you pay about $0.023 per GB-month at list price, while HDFS on cloud VM disks costs the disk three times (replication) plus the idle compute that keeps the data alive. You give up data locality and atomic rename with S3, and you give up elasticity and operations-free storage with HDFS.
Choose triple replication for hot data that many jobs read (locality and read parallelism across three copies); choose erasure coding (Reed–Solomon 6+3, available since Hadoop 3) for cold data older than weeks. Erasure coding stores about 1.5x the raw data instead of 3x, saving half the disks, but reads and recovery must fetch from several nodes, there is no locality, and small files do not suit it. A common policy: replicate for the first 30 days, then convert.
The NameNode is the scaling limit. It keeps every file, directory and block as an object in heap, roughly 150 bytes each, so 100 million files with their blocks need tens of GB of heap and long garbage-collection and restart times. A million 1 MB files is a far worse deal than one 1 TB file. Fix small files by compaction (merge into 128–512 MB files), by using columnar formats such as Parquet, or by splitting the namespace with federation. The choice is: tolerate the pain, or move to a table format on object storage where metadata scales differently.
Choose a block size of 128 MB (the default) to 256 MB for large scans; bigger blocks lower NameNode metadata and task overhead but reduce parallelism for medium files. Choose HA with a quorum journal (two NameNodes, three or more journal nodes) for any real cluster; the single NameNode of the early days was a famous outage source. Expect failover in tens of seconds, and test it.
If starting fresh in 2026, you will almost never choose to build HDFS on cloud VMs. Use object storage plus a table format (Delta, Iceberg) for the lake, and keep HDFS for estates where the hardware is already paid for and the NameNode is well run. Migrating is the real work: rename-heavy committers, permissions (POSIX versus IAM) and listing behavior all differ.
interview: What does the NameNode hold, and what happens when it fails? || The namespace (directories, files, block lists) in memory, backed by an edit log and a periodic image; block locations come from DataNode reports. With HA, a standby NameNode reads the shared edit log through journal nodes and takes over after fencing the old one.
Why is HDFS bad with small files and what do you do about it? || Every file costs NameNode memory and a map task per file, so millions of small files exhaust the heap and make jobs slow. Merge into large files, use Parquet or ORC, or use a table format with compaction.
How does HDFS decide where to put the three replicas? || By default: first on the writer's node, second on a different rack, third on another node in that second rack. This survives a rack failure while limiting cross-rack write traffic to one copy.
Why did the industry move from HDFS to S3 for data lakes? || Elastic, separately scaled storage with no cluster to run, strong durability, and fast enough networks that locality matters less. The cost was losing atomic rename and cheap listing, which Delta, Iceberg and committers had to solve.
How would you cut HDFS storage cost by 40% without deleting data? || Move cold data to erasure coding (1.5x instead of 3x), compact small files, drop replication to 2 only for easily regenerable data, apply retention policies, and compress with a better codec and format.
@@+ databricks
tradeoffs: Choose Databricks when you want Spark, Delta tables, SQL warehouses, governance and ML on one managed platform and your team is too small to run clusters well; choose open-source Spark on EMR, Kubernetes or YARN when you have a platform team and large, steady workloads where the platform fee dominates. Databricks bills Databricks units (DBUs) on top of the cloud VM bill, so the real price is VMs plus DBUs. You give up some control and pay a premium (often 20–50% over raw VM cost on busy clusters, less on idle-prone ones), and in return you avoid building autoscaling, upgrades, access control, a catalog and notebooks yourself.
Choose job clusters for scheduled pipelines: at list price they cost roughly half or less per DBU than all-purpose clusters, and they start fresh and stop when the job ends. Choose all-purpose clusters only for interactive work, with auto-termination at 10–30 minutes, because an idle all-purpose cluster is the most common waste. Choose serverless (jobs, notebooks, SQL warehouses) when workloads are bursty or short and startup time matters; it starts in seconds and has no idle cost, but unit prices are higher, so for a cluster that is busy 80% of the day, classic compute is cheaper.
Choose Photon on SQL and DataFrame heavy jobs over Delta or Parquet: it often gives 2x or more speedup on scans, joins and aggregations. On classic clusters it roughly doubles the DBU rate, so it saves money only when the speedup is more than that factor; measure cost per run, not run time. Skip it for Python UDF heavy or tiny jobs.
Choose Unity Catalog from the start. Its three-level namespace (catalog, schema, table), lineage and fine-grained permissions are hard to add after hundreds of tables are in the old workspace-level Hive metastore, and migrating means rewriting table paths, grants and jobs. The trade-off is lock-in to Databricks' governance model: Delta and the Unity Catalog open-source project reduce it, but features such as Photon, serverless and Lakeflow are proprietary.
Choose spot instances for workers (a 60–80% VM discount) with an on-demand driver, and accept task retries when instances are reclaimed. Avoid spot for long stateful streaming jobs with strict freshness requirements unless you tolerate a restart. Use cluster policies and tags so cost attribution works from day one.
interview: When is Databricks the wrong choice? || When the workload is small enough for one machine (DuckDB, Polars), when you need sub-second serving (use ClickHouse, Pinot or a database), when one team runs a huge steady Spark estate and can run it cheaper itself, or when cloud independence is a hard requirement.
How would you cut a Databricks bill by 30% without changing business logic? || Move scheduled work to job clusters or serverless where idle time is high, auto-terminate interactive clusters, use spot workers, right-size and cap autoscaling, enable Photon only where cost per run falls, compact small files and use liquid clustering to reduce scanned data, and tag every cluster for attribution.
Explain the lakehouse in terms of what Databricks adds over raw Parquet on S3. || Delta adds a transaction log (ACID, schema enforcement, time travel, upserts), Unity Catalog adds governance and lineage, and the engine adds optimizers and indexing hints such as data skipping and clustering. Without them you have files; with them you have tables.
How do you organize bronze, silver and gold layers, and what goes wrong? || Bronze keeps raw data append-only, silver cleans and conforms it, and gold serves business aggregates. Common failures are rewriting bronze (losing replay), skipping data contracts at the silver boundary, and creating dozens of gold tables nobody owns.
All-purpose versus SQL warehouse versus job cluster: how do you pick? || Job cluster for scheduled ETL (cheapest), SQL warehouse (serverless or pro) for BI and ad hoc SQL with concurrency scaling, all-purpose for notebooks and development. Choose by workload shape and idle time, not by habit.
@@+ photon
tradeoffs: Choose Photon for SQL and DataFrame workloads that scan, filter, join and aggregate large Parquet or Delta tables: speedups of 2–4x are common, and more on scan-heavy queries. Choose standard Spark execution when the work is dominated by Python or Scala UDFs, RDD code, tiny data, or operators Photon does not support. Photon is not a different Spark; it replaces operators it supports and falls back to the JVM for the rest, so a plan can mix both, with a cost for converting between row and columnar forms at each boundary.
The money question is whether the speedup beats the price. On classic compute, Photon runs at a higher DBU rate (roughly twice per hour at list price), so a query that gets 1.5x faster costs more, and one that gets 3x faster costs about a third less. Test on your real jobs with cost per run as the metric; do not trust average benchmark numbers. On serverless SQL warehouses, Photon is always on and priced into the unit, so the decision is made for you.
Compare with other designs for fast execution. Spark's own Tungsten whole-stage code generation compiles JVM code per query and is hard to debug and limited by JVM memory behavior; Photon is a vectorized interpreter over column batches in native C++, using SIMD and off-heap memory, which is easier to extend and profile. Open alternatives that put native engines under Spark (Velox through Apache Gluten, or DataFusion-based Comet) exist if you need to leave the vendor; Trino and DuckDB reach similar vectorized speed but are different engines with different fault-tolerance and SQL dialects. You give up portability by choosing Photon: it is closed, and a job that depends on its speed will run slower anywhere else.
Operational limits matter. Photon needs memory outside the JVM heap, so cluster sizing differs; you check the Spark UI or query profile to see how much of the plan ran in Photon, and a low fraction means the speedup will be small. Fix fallbacks by replacing UDFs with built-in functions or SQL, not by tuning clusters.
interview: Why is a vectorized C++ engine faster than JVM code generation for SQL? || It processes batches of column values in tight loops (good for CPU caches and SIMD), avoids per-row object allocation and garbage collection, and manages memory off-heap. Interpreting a batch amortizes the dispatch cost, so it needs no per-query compilation.
A query is not faster with Photon on. How do you investigate? || Open the query profile and see which operators ran in Photon; look for UDFs, unsupported expressions or data sources causing fallback. Also check whether time goes to I/O, small files or skew, which Photon does not fix.
How would you decide whether Photon pays for itself? || Run the same job with and without it a few times on identical data, compare cost (DBU rate times hours plus VM cost), not time. If the speedup is greater than the DBU multiplier, enable it.
What does Photon not do? || It does not speed up Python UDFs, RDD code or driver-side work, and it does not make bad layout (many small files, skew, no pruning) go away. Data layout work still comes first.
Why are columnar formats and Photon a natural pair? || Parquet stores values by column with encodings, so the engine can read only needed columns, apply filters on encoded data and process whole vectors at a time. Row-oriented inputs lose most of the benefit.
@@+ ray
tradeoffs: Choose Ray when the workload is Python functions and stateful objects spread over a cluster: batch inference on GPUs, hyperparameter search, simulation, reinforcement learning, model serving, or data preprocessing feeding a GPU. Choose Spark when the workload is tables, SQL, joins and large shuffles. The reason is the scheduling model: Ray schedules individual tasks with around a millisecond of overhead and lets tasks create more tasks and call long-lived actors, but it has no SQL optimizer and its shuffle is less mature; Spark plans a whole stage graph and optimizes it, but its tasks cost tens of milliseconds and it is awkward for GPU pipelines. You give up query optimization with Ray and give up flexibility with Spark.
Choose Ray Data for GPU batch inference over Spark when you need to overlap CPU preprocessing, GPU inference and writes in a streaming pipeline, keeping the GPU busy (a GPU costing $2–4 an hour idle during decode is the real waste). Choose Spark with a pandas UDF when the model is small, runs on CPU and the surrounding job is already Spark. Ray's streaming executor can use heterogeneous resources in one pipeline; Spark needs stage-level scheduling to approximate it.
Choose Ray over Dask when the work is a dynamic task graph or needs actors, and when you want a single runtime for data, training and serving; choose Dask when the code is pandas or NumPy shaped and you want it to look the same at scale. Both are Python-native with a small community compared to Spark. Choose Ray Train or Tune to orchestrate PyTorch jobs when you need elasticity and fault tolerance around training; for a single fixed job on one node group, plain PyTorch distributed or DeepSpeed has less machinery.
Know the failure modes. Task size matters: tasks under about 100 ms spend more time on overhead than on work, so batch them. The object store (shared memory per node, spilling to disk when full) gives zero-copy reads of NumPy arrays on one node, but large objects cross the network and can fill the store. Objects are owned by the worker that created them, so if that worker dies, its objects are lost (they can be recomputed from lineage in many cases). The head node holds cluster metadata; production clusters should use GCS fault tolerance or a managed service.
Choose KubeRay when you already run Kubernetes and want autoscaling Ray clusters per job; choose a managed service (Anyscale or a cloud vendor offering) when you do not want to own cluster operations. The trade-off is cost and lock-in against operational effort, similar to the Spark-versus-Databricks choice.
interview: When would you pick Ray over Spark for a batch pipeline? || When the pipeline has GPU stages, heterogeneous resources, models that must be loaded once and reused (actors), or an ML-heavy workflow that mixes training, tuning and inference. Not for large SQL-style joins and aggregations.
What is the difference between a Ray task and an actor, and when do you need an actor? || A task is a stateless function call; an actor is a long-lived process holding state, with method calls executed in order. Use an actor to hold a loaded model, a counter, or a connection; use tasks for independent, retriable work.
A Ray job slows down and workers hit out-of-memory errors. What do you check? || Object store usage and spilling, number of in-flight large objects (backpressure with limited concurrency), task output size, and whether results are being gathered on the driver. Reduce block size, limit concurrency, or stream results to storage.
How does Ray handle node failure? || Tasks are retried on other nodes, and lost objects can be reconstructed from lineage; actors can be restarted with a retry limit but their in-memory state must be rebuilt by your code. Head node failure needs GCS fault tolerance.
Why is a very fine-grained task graph a problem? || Each task has scheduling and serialization overhead, so tiny tasks waste cluster time and flood the scheduler and object store. Batch inputs so tasks run for 100 ms to a few seconds.
@@+ dask
tradeoffs: Choose Dask when the code is already pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn or xarray and you need to scale beyond one machine's memory without rewriting; choose Spark when you need SQL, a cost-based optimizer, multi-team operations or shuffles over many terabytes; choose Polars or DuckDB when the data fits on one large machine, because they are often 3–10x faster than Dask on a laptop-to-single-server scale and have no scheduler overhead. Pandas needs about 2–5x the data size in RAM, so a 10 GB CSV can need 50 GB; that is where Dask first helps. You give up speed against the single-node Rust and C++ engines and give up SQL maturity against Spark.
Choose partition (chunk) sizes of about 100 MB to 1 GB. Each task costs roughly a millisecond of scheduler time, so a graph of ten million tiny tasks keeps the single-threaded Python scheduler busy before any work starts; chunks that are too large cause memory spikes and spilling. For arrays, chunk along the dimension your computation reduces over so tasks need little communication.
Choose threads for code that releases the GIL (NumPy, pandas, Arrow) and processes for pure-Python code. The default mix is usually fine for dataframes, but a pure-Python function in map_partitions will be limited by the GIL unless workers are separate processes. Shuffles (joins on non-aligned keys, groupby with many groups) are the expensive operation: the peer-to-peer shuffle in recent versions is much more stable than the old task-based one, but still behind Spark on very large shuffles.
Choose the deployment by environment: LocalCluster on one machine for development, Kubernetes or a managed service (Coiled) for cloud, and the HPC integrations with Slurm or PBS for research clusters. Dask is strongest in scientific computing (xarray, geoscience, imaging), where the data is multidimensional arrays and Spark is a poor fit. Choose Spark for governed, scheduled enterprise tables. The community is smaller than Spark's, so expect to debug more yourself.
interview: Why can Dask be slower than Spark or Polars on the same data? || Its scheduler is a Python process that handles each task individually, with overhead per task, and its data moves through Python serialization. Spark plans stages in bulk, and Polars avoids a scheduler altogether on one machine.
What does lazy evaluation give Dask? || Operations build a task graph that is optimized (fusing steps, pushing filters and column selection into reads) and run only on compute() or persist(). The risk is calling compute() repeatedly, which recomputes the graph each time; persist() intermediate results that you reuse.
A Dask job runs out of memory on one worker. What are the likely causes? || Chunks too big, a skewed groupby or join putting too much data on one partition, large results gathered to the client, or many persisted intermediates. Reduce chunk size, repartition after filtering, and avoid collecting large data to the client.
When is Dask a better choice than Spark? || When the team writes pandas, NumPy or xarray and needs the same API at scale, when the workload is custom Python task graphs (dask.delayed), array or scientific data, or when integration with the Python ML stack outweighs SQL features.
How would you process a 300 GB Parquet dataset on one 64 GB machine? || Try Polars lazy scanning (streaming) or DuckDB first, which read only needed columns and spill; Dask on a single machine with proper partitions also works. Only add a cluster if these still fail.
@@+ trino
tradeoffs: Choose Trino for interactive SQL (seconds to a few minutes) over data in several places: Iceberg, Delta or Hive tables on object storage, plus PostgreSQL, MySQL and others joined in one query. Choose Spark for long ETL, large writes, UDF-heavy or ML-adjacent jobs, and anything that must survive a worker dying at hour three. Trino pipelines data between stages in memory with no checkpoints, so by default one lost worker fails the whole query; that is the price of low latency. Its fault-tolerant execution mode (with an external exchange on object storage) retries tasks, but it is slower, so it is for long queries rather than dashboards.
Choose Trino over a warehouse engine that has its own storage (ClickHouse, Pinot, a Databricks SQL warehouse) by the latency and concurrency you need. Trino reads lake files, so a scan of 500 GB takes seconds to minutes depending on workers, which is fine for analysts and ad hoc work; sub-second dashboards with hundreds of concurrent users need pre-aggregated or indexed storage. Choose Trino when you want to keep data in open formats and avoid copying it; choose a warehouse when you want the fastest repeated queries and accept loading data into it.
Know the memory model. Hash joins hold the build side in memory on every worker, and per-query limits (query.max-memory, per-node limits) kill queries that exceed them; spilling to disk exists but is off by default and slower. Choose broadcast joins when one side is small (hundreds of MB) and partitioned joins when both are large; the cost-based optimizer needs table statistics (ANALYZE) to choose well. Resource groups protect shared clusters from a single huge query.
File layout decides performance more than cluster size. Partition pruning and Parquet min/max statistics can skip most data; many small files slow split planning on the coordinator and waste task slots. Compact files to 128–512 MB. Choose Iceberg or Delta tables over plain Hive directories for correct concurrent writes and metadata-based planning.
Choose Trino over PrestoDB when you want the more active community and features (the project split in 2020 and Trino is the continuation of the original creators' work); Athena is a managed service based on Trino, and Starburst is a commercial one. You give up control over upgrades with managed services and gain no cluster operations. Autoscaling workers on spot instances is only safe with fault-tolerant execution.
interview: Why does a Trino query fail when a worker dies, and how would you handle it? || Intermediate data is streamed between workers in memory and not persisted, so there is nothing to resume. Retry at the client, use fault-tolerant execution with an external exchange for long queries, or run long ETL in Spark.
A Trino query that joined two big tables runs out of memory. What are your options? || Check the join order and distribution (broadcast versus partitioned), refresh statistics so the optimizer picks the smaller build side, filter and project earlier, raise memory limits if the cluster has room, enable spilling, or split the query into stages written to a table.
How does Trino differ from Spark SQL architecturally? || Trino is an MPP engine with long-running workers and pipelined execution, optimized for low-latency queries; Spark launches executors per application, writes shuffle data to disk, and recovers by recomputing tasks. Trino has connectors for federation and no built-in ETL scheduler or ML stack.
How do you make a federated join across Postgres and Iceberg safe? || Push down filters to Postgres, limit the rows pulled from it, make sure the join runs with the small side broadcast, and avoid scanning large tables from an operational database during peak hours. Federation is convenient but each connector has its own load and consistency behavior.
Why can many small files make a query slow even with a large cluster? || Planning enumerates every file as a split on the coordinator, and each tiny split costs scheduling and I/O latency, so the engine spends time on overhead. Compact to larger files.
@@+ duckdb
tradeoffs: Choose DuckDB when the data fits on one machine (a laptop for tens of GB, a single 64-core, 512 GB server for a few TB of Parquet), the workload is analytical SQL, and you want no cluster, no server and no admin. It runs inside your Python, R, Java or CLI process, reads Parquet, CSV, JSON and Iceberg or Delta tables directly from local disk or S3, and spills joins and sorts to disk when memory is short. Choose Spark or Trino when the data is far beyond one machine's disks, when many users query at once, or when you need high availability. You give up horizontal scale and multi-user serving; you gain startup in milliseconds and zero operations.
Choose DuckDB over Polars when the team thinks in SQL, needs a persistent database file with transactions, wants to query many file formats in place, or wants extensions (spatial, full-text search, httpfs). Choose Polars when the work is a Python pipeline built from expressions, tight code integration and Arrow interchange matter, and you do not want SQL strings in code. Both are fast and columnar and often close on benchmarks; DuckDB tends to be stronger on complex joins and larger-than-memory sorts, Polars on expression-heavy transformations. They interoperate through Arrow at almost no cost, so mixing is normal.
Choose DuckDB over ClickHouse for embedded analytics, notebooks, CI tests, per-tenant or per-user data, and local development. Choose ClickHouse (or Pinot) for a shared service with many concurrent users, continuous ingestion and terabytes of hot data with sub-second dashboards. DuckDB has historically been one writer process at a time with no network protocol; the 2.0 client-server mode (Quack) addresses part of this but is new, so treat it as something to test before relying on it for production serving.
Do not use DuckDB for OLTP. It is column-oriented and optimized for scans, so frequent single-row inserts and updates are slow and concurrent transactional writers are not the design point. Use PostgreSQL or SQLite for that, and use DuckDB to analyze their data (it can attach and scan both).
Know the performance limits. Reading Parquet from S3 is limited by network bandwidth (a 25 Gbps instance gets roughly 3 GB/s at best), so a 500 GB scan takes minutes even if the engine is fast; local NVMe is several times faster. Set memory_limit and a temp directory with spare space, give it threads equal to cores, and store data as sorted, well-partitioned Parquet so row groups can be skipped. Many teams find that scaling up one machine replaces a Spark cluster for jobs under about 1 TB, a point to check before building distributed infrastructure.
interview: When would you use DuckDB instead of Spark? || When data fits on one machine (up to a few TB with a big instance), jobs are batch SQL, and the cluster startup and operations cost more than the work. Not when I need many parallel writers, high availability, or data too large for one node's storage and network.
How does DuckDB process data faster than a row-based database? || It stores and processes columns in vectors (about 2,048 values), which fits CPU caches, allows SIMD, avoids per-row interpretation, and reads only the columns needed. It pushes filters into Parquet scans and skips row groups using statistics.
What happens when a DuckDB query needs more memory than available? || Operators such as hash join, aggregation and sort spill intermediate data to a temp directory, within memory_limit; performance falls but the query usually finishes. Some operations still need to fit in memory, and a missing temp directory or full disk makes it fail.
Two processes need to write to the same DuckDB file. What do you do? || A database file allows one writing process at a time, so serialize writers through one process or queue, write separate Parquet files and query them together, or use the new client-server mode if tested. For concurrent transactional writes use Postgres.
How would you build a data application where each customer has up to 20 GB of data? || One DuckDB or Parquet set per customer, loaded on demand into a pool of workers, giving isolation and cheap scaling. Watch memory per query, file placement for fast storage, and caching.
@@+ polars
tradeoffs: Choose Polars over pandas for new pipelines on data from hundreds of MB to a few hundred GB: it is commonly 5–30x faster, uses far less memory than pandas, uses all cores, and has a lazy optimizer that pushes filters and column selection into the file scan. Choose pandas when you depend on libraries that expect it (scikit-learn, many plotting and finance packages), when the data is tiny, or when the code is legacy; conversion with to_pandas is cheap through Arrow when you only need pandas at the edge. Pandas 3 (Arrow-backed strings, copy-on-write) closed part of the gap, but not the multicore and lazy-optimizer advantages.
Choose lazy mode (scan_parquet, then collect) for pipelines and anything large; choose eager mode (read_parquet) for exploring a small dataset in a notebook. Lazy mode can drop unused columns and filter rows before reading, which often cuts memory by 10x on wide files. Since 2.0 the streaming engine is the default for out-of-core work, so a query over data larger than RAM can run in batches; check the explain plan, because not every operation can stream and some fall back to full in-memory execution.
Choose Polars over DuckDB when your logic is a chain of column expressions inside Python, you want compile-checked-ish typed code instead of SQL strings, or you need rolling, window and list operations in a DataFrame style. Choose DuckDB when SQL is the team language, the data lives in many files and tables you want to query in place, or you need transactions on a database file. Both read Parquet fast and exchange data via Arrow; neither is a cluster.
Choose Polars over Spark when the data fits on one big machine, which covers many "big data" jobs below roughly 1 TB. Choose Spark when the data or concurrency exceeds one machine, or the surrounding platform (governance, scheduling, Delta tables) is built on it. Polars Cloud and its distributed engine exist, but they are young compared to Spark, so test them with your workload. You give up multi-user governance and a mature ecosystem; you gain speed per dollar and no cluster.
Keep expressions native. A Python function applied row by row (map_elements) runs in the interpreter and loses most of Polars' speed; express logic with built-in expressions, or write a Rust plugin for custom code. Polars is strict about types and distinguishes null from NaN, so code ported from pandas needs care: casts that pandas coerced silently now raise errors, which is good for pipelines but a migration cost.
interview: Why is Polars faster than pandas? || Rust code, columnar Arrow memory, multithreaded execution across cores, vectorized kernels, and a lazy query optimizer that reorders work and prunes columns and rows before reading. Pandas runs mostly single-threaded, and eager step by step.
What is the difference between scan_parquet and read_parquet? || scan_parquet returns a lazy frame, so filters and column selections are pushed into the file read and the plan is optimized before running; read_parquet loads the whole file eagerly. Use scan for pipelines and large data.
You have a 200 GB dataset and a 64 GB machine. How do you proceed with Polars? || Use the lazy API and the streaming engine, select only needed columns, filter early, and write results to Parquet with sink_parquet instead of collecting. Check explain to see whether operations stream; if a step cannot, restructure it or use DuckDB.
Where does Polars stop being the right tool? || When data or concurrency needs more than one machine, when you need shared governance and transactional tables (use Spark/Databricks), or when the key operations are not supported in streaming mode and the data exceeds memory.
How do you migrate a pandas pipeline to Polars safely? || Port stage by stage, compare outputs on sample data including nulls, dtypes and ordering, replace apply calls with expressions, and measure memory and time. Keep to_pandas at the boundaries that need pandas libraries.
