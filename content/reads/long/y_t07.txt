@@+ consensus
tradeoffs: Paxos family or Raft. Choose Raft when you are building a new system and want a protocol your team can read, test and debug: one strong leader, a log with no holes, and mature libraries (etcd/raft, hashicorp/raft, openraft). Choose Multi-Paxos, EPaxos or Flexible Paxos variants only when you need something Raft makes awkward: out-of-order commit for commutative commands, leaderless writes from several regions, or an asymmetric quorum such as 4 of 6 for elections and 3 of 6 for replication. You give up, with Raft, a bit of flexibility (the leader is a throughput and latency bottleneck, and a client far from the leader pays an extra wide-area hop); you give up, with the Paxos variants, simplicity, and you take on a much larger space of subtle bugs. Almost every production system that says "Paxos" today is a Raft-like leader-based log in practice.
Crash-fault (CFT) or Byzantine-fault (BFT) tolerance. Choose CFT with 2f + 1 nodes (3 nodes for f = 1, 5 for f = 2) when all replicas belong to one organisation and fail by crashing, which is the case for databases, Kubernetes and coordination services. Choose BFT (PBFT, HotStuff, Tendermint) with 3f + 1 nodes (4 for f = 1) only when replicas belong to parties that do not trust each other, as in public or consortium blockchains. You pay for BFT with an extra communication round or signature checks, throughput of hundreds to a few thousand commands per second per group instead of tens of thousands, and 33 percent more machines for the same f. For an internal service, BFT is almost always the wrong answer; a checksum and a good disk are cheaper protection against the failures you really see.
One group, or many groups. Choose a single group of 3 or 5 for metadata that is small (megabytes to a few gigabytes) and written at hundreds to low thousands per second: etcd, ZooKeeper, a Kafka KRaft controller quorum. Choose many groups (one per range or shard, as in CockroachDB, TiKV, YugabyteDB) when the data is terabytes and the write rate is beyond one leader's disk and CPU, at the cost of heartbeat overhead (thousands of groups can mean tens of thousands of messages per second unless batched or coalesced), per-group memory, and cross-shard transactions that need two-phase commit on top. Never put bulk data through a consensus log if an object store or a plain replicated file can hold it, and keep only the pointer and the checksum in the log.
Cluster size and placement. Choose 3 nodes in one region, across 3 zones, when you want one-failure tolerance and commit latency of 1 to 3 ms. Choose 5 nodes when you must survive one failure while another node is down for maintenance, which is the real reason most clusters are 5 and not 3. Choose multi-region (3 regions 30 to 100 ms apart) only when the business demands surviving a region loss, and accept that every write pays at least one inter-region round trip, 30 to 100 ms; if reads dominate, add non-voting learners or follower reads near users instead of more voters. You give up, with more voters, latency (the commit waits for the k-th fastest of the others) and with fewer, availability headroom.
When consensus is the wrong tool. Choose asynchronous replication or a single primary with backups when you can accept losing the last seconds of writes on failover and you want the lowest latency; choose CRDTs or last-writer-wins when you need availability under partition and operations commute or conflicts are rare; choose a single-writer design with a lease from an existing coordination service when you only need to elect one owner, not order every command. The deciding question is simple: does losing or reordering one acknowledged write cost more than a round trip to a majority? If not, consensus is expensive insurance.
interview: Why do production systems pick Raft over Paxos, and when would you not? || Raft fixes a strong leader, an ordered log without gaps, and explicit rules for elections and membership change, so it is far easier to implement and test; Paxos only specifies a single decision, and every team re-invents the multi-decree parts. I would look at Paxos variants for leaderless or multi-region writes (EPaxos), or when flexible quorums reduce the common-path cost (Flexible Paxos). Otherwise the extra complexity is not repaid.
Your 5-node cluster spans three regions and writes take 120 ms. How do you improve it without losing durability? || Latency is set by the (majority-th) fastest acknowledgement, so look at which nodes are in the quorum path. Move or pin the leader near the writers, change the placement to 2+2+1 so a majority can be reached from two nearby regions, or use Flexible Paxos with smaller replication quorums. If readers are the issue, add follower or learner reads with read-index. What I will not do is acknowledge before a majority has fsynced, because that changes the guarantee.
Leader election keeps flapping in production. What are the likely causes? || Slow disks (fsync stalls delay heartbeats and appends), an election timeout shorter than the worst-case network or GC pause, CPU starvation of the leader, a noisy-neighbour VM, or an asymmetric partition where one node can reach some peers but not others. Fixes: measure fsync p99 and heartbeat round trip, raise the timeout to a multiple of both, use pre-vote so a partitioned node does not bump the term, and use a check-quorum rule so a leader that cannot reach a majority steps down.
Can you add a sixth node to a 5-node cluster to be safer? || No: 6 nodes need a majority of 4, so the cluster still tolerates only 2 failures, the same as 5, and every write now waits for one more acknowledgement. Even sizes only add cost. To add safety, add replicas in a different failure domain, or move to 7 voters if you really need to survive 3 failures; otherwise add non-voting learners for read scale.
How do you serve a linearizable read? || Either send it through the log (one round trip to a majority, simplest), use a read-index (the leader records its commit index, confirms leadership with a heartbeat round, then serves after applying up to that index), or use a leader lease (no round trip, but correct only if clock drift is bounded). Stale reads from followers are fine only when the product accepts bounded staleness.
@@+ raft
tradeoffs: Raft or a managed coordination service. Choose to embed a Raft library (etcd/raft, hashicorp/raft, openraft, Dragonboat) when consensus is part of your product, such as a database that replicates ranges, and you need control over snapshots, membership and storage. Choose a ready service (etcd, ZooKeeper, Consul) when you need leader election, locks, config or service discovery for other systems; building your own Raft for that is a year of correctness work (Jepsen has found bugs in many first implementations). You give up, with the service, tight coupling to your data path and its limits (etcd's default 2 GB, about 8 GB recommended maximum database size, and writes in the low thousands per second); you give up, when embedding, engineering time and a pager that rings at 3 a.m.
Election timeout and heartbeat settings. The rule of thumb is broadcast time much smaller than election timeout, which is much smaller than the time between failures. Choose a heartbeat of 50 to 150 ms and an election timeout of 10 times that (for example 1 to 1.5 s) on a LAN or a single region; raise both (heartbeat 500 ms, timeout 5 s or more) across continents or on noisy shared VMs. Short timeouts detect a dead leader in under a second but cause spurious elections on any GC pause or disk stall; long timeouts mean an outage of the same length after each real leader failure. Randomise the timeout per node (for example 1.0 to 2.0 s) so split votes are rare, and turn on pre-vote and check-quorum.
Batching and pipelining, or lowest latency per request. Choose batching (collect entries for 0.5 to 2 ms or until a size limit, one fsync and one network message per batch) when throughput matters: it can raise a leader from about 1,000 to 50,000+ small writes per second on a normal SSD, because fsync is the bottleneck. Choose no batching, with group commit disabled, only when there is a single client and the p50 matters more than the throughput. You give up the extra milliseconds of waiting per request, and a larger loss window of in-flight requests if the leader fails; neither affects safety.
Snapshot and log compaction policy. Choose a snapshot every 10,000 to 100,000 entries, or when the log is a few hundred MB, so restart replay stays short and the disk does not fill. Choose rarer snapshots when the state is huge (gigabytes) and a snapshot stalls the apply thread, but then a follower that falls too far behind must receive a full snapshot rather than log entries, which can saturate the network. Use incremental or copy-on-write snapshots (RocksDB checkpoints) to avoid pauses.
Membership changes. Choose single-server changes (add or remove one voter at a time, the Raft dissertation approach) in nearly every case: simple and proven. Choose joint consensus only for replacing several nodes at once, such as moving a cluster to new hardware. Always add a new node first as a non-voting learner, let it catch up, and only then promote it; adding a voter that is 10 GB behind stalls commits, because it counts toward the majority before it can acknowledge.
interview: Walk through what happens when the leader crashes after replicating an entry to one follower of five. || The entry exists on 2 of 5 nodes, so it is not committed and the client has not been told success. A follower times out and becomes a candidate with a higher term. It wins only if its log is at least as up to date as a majority's (compare last term, then index). If the winner holds the entry, it will replicate and commit it later; if not, the entry is overwritten. Both outcomes are safe, because the client never saw an acknowledgement.
Why can Raft only commit entries from the current term by counting replicas? || An old-term entry replicated to a majority can still be overwritten by a later leader whose log lacks it (figure 8 in the paper). The leader commits an old entry only indirectly, when a newer entry of its own term is replicated to a majority. Practically, new leaders append a no-op at start.
What does pre-vote fix? || A node cut off from the network keeps timing out and raising its term. When it returns, its higher term forces the healthy leader to step down, causing an outage. With pre-vote, a candidate first asks whether peers would vote for it without changing terms; peers that hear from a live leader refuse, so the term does not inflate.
A follower is 30 minutes behind and the leader has already truncated the log. What happens, and what do you watch? || The leader sends an InstallSnapshot, then the remaining entries. I watch network and disk load during the transfer, whether it slows foreground commits, and snapshot size relative to the compaction interval. A cluster that does this often needs rate limits or a larger retained log.
Why do you need fsync before acknowledging an AppendEntries message? || Raft's safety argument assumes a node that acknowledged an entry still has it after a crash. Without fsync a power loss can erase an acknowledged entry from two nodes, and then a committed entry can be lost. Disabling fsync trades correctness for speed, and some systems do it knowingly only when a power-loss of a majority at once is accepted.
@@+ exactlyonce
tradeoffs: Transactions inside the stream system, or idempotent writes in the sink. Choose Kafka transactions (or Flink two-phase-commit sinks) when input, state and output are all in Kafka or in a sink that supports transactional commit: the consume-process-produce loop commits output records and input offsets atomically, and costs roughly 3 to 10 percent throughput plus a commit interval of latency (Flink checkpoint interval of 1 to 10 s; read_committed consumers see data only after the commit). Choose idempotent writes with a deterministic key (upsert by event ID, MERGE INTO, INSERT ... ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING) when the sink is a database or a lakehouse table: it works with plain at-least-once delivery, has no commit latency, and survives replays from any point. You give up, with transactions, end-to-end latency and operational coupling (a hung transaction blocks consumers at the last stable offset); you give up, with idempotent sinks, the ability to write append-only data with no natural key.
At-least-once with deduplication, or true transactional processing. Choose at-least-once plus a dedup window (state store or Redis set of event IDs for 24 hours, a Bloom filter in front of an exact check) when duplicates are rare and cheap to remove, and when the sink is read-heavy and can filter at query time. Choose transactions when the side effect cannot be repeated or filtered later, such as charging a card, sending a message, or incrementing a counter. A dedup window of N hours also means a duplicate arriving after N hours slips through, and its state costs memory: 100 million IDs per day at 16 bytes is about 1.6 GB per day just for keys.
At-most-once, at-least-once, or exactly-once. Choose at-most-once (commit offset before processing) only for metrics, logs or sampling where losing 0.01 percent is fine. Choose at-least-once as the default for pipelines whose consumers are idempotent: it is simple, fast and recovers by replay. Choose exactly-once only for the paths where a duplicate or loss is a business error (billing, ledger, inventory). You give up, with exactly-once, throughput and simplicity; you give up, with at-least-once, the freedom to have non-idempotent consumers; you give up, with at-most-once, completeness.
Where exactly-once stops. Exactly-once is a property of a closed system: Kafka to Kafka, or Flink checkpoint to a transactional sink. The moment you call an HTTP API, send an email or write to a store without transactions, you are back to at-least-once, and the fix is an idempotency key sent with the call (Stripe-style) and a table of seen keys. Choose to make external calls last in the pipeline and keyed by a stable ID (event ID, not a timestamp or retry counter), and expect the remote side to dedupe within its own retention (24 hours for many payment APIs).
Checkpoint interval and transaction timeout. Choose a short checkpoint interval (1 to 5 s) when downstream readers need fresh data and state is small, and a long one (30 to 60 s or more) when state is large (gigabytes per task) and checkpoints are expensive. A shorter interval also means more small files in a lakehouse sink; a longer one means more reprocessing after failure and a bigger burst of output at each commit. Keep the transaction timeout well above the checkpoint interval and the longest expected restart, or the broker aborts a transaction that the job still thinks it can commit, and data is lost or duplicated.
interview: Is exactly-once delivery possible? || Not as a pure network property: with an unreliable network a sender cannot know whether a message arrived (Two Generals). What systems provide is exactly-once effect: at-least-once delivery plus idempotent or atomic processing, so each message changes the state once. Kafka does it by idempotent producers (producer ID and sequence numbers), transactions that write output and offsets together, and consumers that read only committed data.
Your Flink job writes to a Postgres table and restarts after a crash. How do you avoid duplicates? || Make the write idempotent: INSERT ... ON CONFLICT (event_id) DO UPDATE, with the key derived from the event, not from processing time. Alternatively use a two-phase-commit sink that prepares a transaction at each checkpoint and commits on checkpoint success, and commits again idempotently on recovery. The upsert is simpler and recovers from any replay.
A Kafka consumer group shows growing lag after you enabled transactions. Why? || Consumers in read_committed mode stop at the last stable offset, so one open or hung transaction blocks everything behind it in that partition. Check for producers with long transaction timeouts, stuck commits, or very large transactions. Reduce the transaction size and timeout and monitor transaction duration.
How would you deduplicate 5 billion events per day with a 7-day window? || Exact: key by event ID in a state store with TTL; 35 billion IDs at 16 bytes is about 560 GB, so partition the state and compact it. Approximate: a Bloom filter per day cuts memory about 10 times, with false positives of 0.1 to 1 percent that cause real events to be dropped, which is usually unacceptable; use the filter as a fast negative check and confirm hits against the exact store. Or avoid state by making the sink idempotent.
What goes wrong with exactly-once when a consumer rebalances mid-batch? || A zombie consumer may still write output after its partitions moved. Kafka handles it by fencing: the transactional ID and epoch let the broker reject the old producer. Without fencing (plain consumers writing to a database), the old instance and the new one both write, so you need idempotent writes or a version check.
@@+ wal
tradeoffs: fsync on every commit, group commit, or relaxed durability. Choose fsync per commit (PostgreSQL synchronous_commit=on, MySQL innodb_flush_log_at_trx_commit=1) for financial or user-visible data: a commit costs one device flush, about 0.05 to 0.2 ms on a good NVMe SSD with power-loss protection, 1 to 10 ms on a cloud network disk. Choose group commit (several transactions share one flush, the default in every mature engine) when throughput matters: it can turn 1,000 commits per second into 50,000 at the cost of a sub-millisecond wait. Choose relaxed durability (innodb_flush_log_at_trx_commit=2, synchronous_commit=off, Redis appendfsync everysec) for caches, session stores and analytics ingest, accepting to lose the last 0.2 to 1 second of commits on a crash, but never corruption. You give up durability window in return for 2 to 10 times more commits per second.
Physical, logical, or physiological logging. Choose physical (page images or byte diffs, as in PostgreSQL full-page writes) when you need simple, idempotent redo, at the cost of log volume. Choose logical (SQL statements or row changes, as in MySQL binlog, Debezium streams) when the log must feed replicas of another version, a warehouse, or CDC, and accept that replay is slower and must be deterministic. Choose physiological (InnoDB, ARIES style: page ID plus a logical operation within the page) for the local crash-recovery log because it is compact and replays quickly. Many systems keep two logs for this reason, one for recovery and one for replication.
Log placement and size. Choose the log on a separate fast device when the data disk is busy with random writes: sequential appends then do not compete with page flushes, and commit latency stays flat. Choose a larger log (several GB, max_wal_size or redo log capacity) to space out checkpoints and cut write amplification, because each checkpoint forces dirty pages to disk; the cost is longer crash recovery (replay speed of roughly 50 to 200 MB per second, so 10 GB can take a minute or more) and more disk used. A small log forces frequent checkpoints and I/O spikes.
Local WAL or replicated log. Choose a local WAL with asynchronous replicas for lowest commit latency and accept data loss on primary failure of the unreplicated tail. Choose synchronous replication to one replica (PostgreSQL synchronous_standby_names) to survive the loss of a machine, paying a network round trip of 0.5 to 2 ms in a zone and about 10 ms or more across zones. Choose a consensus-replicated log (Raft, Kafka with acks=all and min.insync.replicas=2) when you also need automatic failover without losing acknowledged writes. Each step buys a stronger guarantee for more latency and more machines.
interview: Why is a commit durable only after the log is flushed, not when the write() call returns? || write() copies into the operating system page cache; a power failure or kernel crash loses it. Only fsync/fdatasync (or O_DSYNC, or a device with write-through) forces data and, on a good drive, the drive's cache to stable media. Consumer SSDs without power-loss protection can lie about flushes, which is how acknowledged commits disappear.
How does recovery work after a crash, and why is it correct? || Find the last checkpoint, then redo log records from there to restore pages to the state at the crash, then undo changes by transactions that never committed (ARIES: analysis, redo, undo). Each page stores the LSN of the last record applied, so redo is idempotent: if the page LSN is already at or beyond the record, skip it. Write-ahead means a log record always reaches disk before the page it describes.
Commit latency doubled after moving the database to cloud storage. What do you check? || Fsync latency of the network volume (p99, not average), whether the WAL shares a volume with data, IOPS or throughput throttling, and whether synchronous_commit is waiting on a replica in another zone. Fixes: provisioned IOPS or local NVMe for the WAL, group commit settings, or relaxed durability where the product accepts it.
What is write amplification in a WAL system and how do you reduce it? || Each change is written at least twice, to the log and later to the data page, and PostgreSQL also writes full-page images after a checkpoint to protect against torn pages. Reduce it with larger checkpoint intervals, compression of the WAL (wal_compression), and fewer dirty pages by a good buffer pool; or choose an LSM where the log is the main write.
How do you keep a log from growing without bound? || Truncate at checkpoint (once dirty pages are flushed, earlier records are not needed for crash recovery), but keep segments required by replicas and backups (replication slots, archive for point-in-time recovery). A stuck replica or an unused replication slot is the classic reason a WAL disk fills up and the database stops.
@@+ lsm
tradeoffs: LSM tree or B-tree. Choose an LSM (RocksDB, LevelDB, Cassandra, HBase, ScyllaDB) when the workload is write-heavy, with ingest of tens or hundreds of thousands of rows per second, keys that arrive in random order, and data that compresses well: sequential flushes give low write cost per row on SSDs and 2 to 10 times better space efficiency than a half-empty B-tree. Choose a B-tree (InnoDB, PostgreSQL, SQLite) when reads and range scans dominate, latency must be predictable at p99, and updates happen in place: one lookup is 3 to 4 page reads, with no background compaction competing for I/O. You give up, with an LSM, read cost (several files to check, hence Bloom filters) and tail latency during compaction; you give up, with a B-tree, write throughput and space efficiency.
Leveled or size-tiered (or universal) compaction. Choose leveled compaction (RocksDB default, fan-out 10) when reads and space matter: each key lives in at most one file per level, read amplification is low, and space amplification is about 1.1 times, but write amplification is high, often 10 to 30 times, which burns SSD endurance. Choose size-tiered or universal compaction (Cassandra STCS, RocksDB universal) when writes dominate and you can accept space amplification of 2 times (temporary up to 50 percent free space needed) and more files per read. Choose time-window compaction (Cassandra TWCS) for time series with TTL, where whole files expire at once and nothing needs rewriting. This is the RUM trade-off: you can lower any two of read, update and memory/space costs only by raising the third.
Bloom filters and block cache. Choose 10 bits per key in the Bloom filter (about 1 percent false positives) for point-lookup workloads; it costs about 1.2 GB per billion keys, and saves almost all wasted file reads. Choose prefix filters or no filters for range-scan-heavy workloads, where a filter does not help, and for the bottom level, where most data lives and which a miss must read anyway, so skipping filters there saves around 90 percent of filter memory. Size the block cache to hold index and filter blocks first, then hot data blocks; if they spill, every lookup pays extra I/O.
Write stalls and rate limits. Choose generous limits for L0 file count and pending compaction bytes, and a rate-limited compaction thread pool sized to the disk, because when compaction cannot keep up the engine slows or stops writes (RocksDB write stall) and tail latency jumps from milliseconds to seconds. Choose to over-provision disk throughput by about 2 times your ingest times write amplification. Provisioning to the average and ignoring burst is the usual cause of stalls.
Key-value separation. Choose to keep values in a separate log (WiscKey, BlobDB, Titan) when values are large (over 1 KB), so compaction moves only keys and write amplification drops several times. You pay with extra random reads for values, more complex garbage collection, and slower range scans. For small values (under 100 bytes), keep them inline.
interview: Explain write, read and space amplification for an LSM tree with fan-out 10 and 4 levels. || Leveled: each byte is rewritten about 10 times per level, so write amplification can reach 10 to 30; a read checks the memtable, each L0 file, then one file per deeper level, so about 4 plus the L0 count lookups, mostly avoided by Bloom filters; space amplification is about 1.1 since most data sits in the last level. Size-tiered flips this: write amplification near 4 to 6, read and space amplification higher.
Why does a read for a missing key cost so much more than for a present key in an LSM? || A hit can stop at the first level that has it, but a miss must be ruled out in every level and file. Bloom filters answer most of those checks in memory; without them a miss costs one disk read per level or per overlapping file.
Deletes seem to make reads slower in Cassandra or RocksDB. Why? || A delete writes a tombstone, which does not remove data until compaction reaches the bottom level and the grace period passes. Scans must read and skip tombstones, so a table with many deletes (queue patterns) can scan millions of dead entries. Use TTL with time-window compaction, avoid queue-in-a-table designs, or force compaction on affected ranges.
Your RocksDB service has p99 latency spikes every few minutes. How do you diagnose? || Correlate with compaction and flush events, L0 file count, pending compaction bytes and stall counters; check disk saturation and fsync latency. Typical fixes: more compaction threads and a rate limiter so background I/O does not starve the foreground, bigger memtables, subcompactions, or switching level style for a workload that is write-heavy.
When would you pick a B-tree storage engine over an LSM for a new service? || Mixed read-heavy workload with strict p99, many range scans, in-place updates of small rows, and no appetite to tune compaction (for example a user-profile store of 200 GB served from one MySQL or PostgreSQL). I would pick the LSM for ingest over 50,000 rows per second per node, time-series or log-like keys, or when storage cost drives the decision.
@@+ btree
tradeoffs: B+tree or LSM tree. Choose a B+tree when the workload is read-dominated or mixed, you need cheap range scans (leaves are linked and sorted), and p99 latency has to be steady: a lookup in a 1 TB table with 8 KB pages and fan-out of about 500 reads 3 to 4 pages, and the top two levels sit in memory, so it is often one disk read. Choose an LSM when writes dominate and the data arrives in random key order, because a B-tree turns each random insert into a random page write and a possible page split. You give up, with a B-tree, write throughput (random I/O, write amplification of 10 or more from rewriting full pages for small changes) and space (pages are about 70 percent full on average); you give up, with an LSM, read simplicity.
Page size. Choose 4 to 8 KB (PostgreSQL 8 KB, InnoDB 16 KB, SQLite 4 KB) for OLTP, where rows are small and each lookup should read little. Choose 64 KB to several MB for large scans and object-store-backed engines, where one request is costly in time and per-request price. Larger pages lower tree height and request count, but raise write amplification per update, since the whole page is rewritten, and raise lock contention. Match the page size to the device: an SSD page of 4 KB with 16 KB database pages needs atomic multi-page writes or a doublewrite buffer.
Key choice and insert pattern. Choose sequential or time-ordered keys (auto-increment, ULID, UUIDv7) when insert throughput matters: new rows append to the rightmost leaf, which stays in cache, and pages fill to near 100 percent. Choose random keys (UUIDv4) only when you need to avoid a hot right edge in a highly concurrent write workload, and accept page splits, 50 percent page fill, cache misses on every insert, and several times more WAL. With a clustered index (InnoDB) the primary key is also the row location, and a wide or random key is copied into every secondary index; keep it narrow.
Clustered or heap table with secondary indexes. Choose a clustered index (InnoDB, SQL Server) when most queries fetch by primary key or range on it: one traversal gets the row. Choose a heap with separate indexes (PostgreSQL) when rows are updated often and you have many indexes, since an update then does not rewrite the primary tree, although each non-HOT update touches every index. Each extra secondary index costs roughly one more random page write per insert and update, so five indexes on a hot table can cut insert rate by a factor of three or more; add only indexes that serve real queries.
Index types beyond the B-tree. Choose a hash index for pure equality lookups on a hot key where range scans never happen, a GIN or inverted index for full text and arrays, a BRIN (block range) index for huge append-only time-ordered tables where an index of a few MB can replace a B-tree of tens of GB, and a bitmap or columnar zone map for analytics. A B-tree is the default because it handles equality, range, ordering and prefix queries in one structure.
interview: Why are B-trees wide and shallow rather than binary? || Disk and SSD access cost is per page, not per comparison. A node sized to one page holds hundreds of keys, so fan-out is hundreds and height stays 3 to 4 for billions of keys, so lookup is 3 to 4 page reads (fewer with caching). A binary tree would need 30 or more reads for the same data.
Inserting random UUIDs makes your insert rate drop as the table grows. Why and what do you do? || Each insert goes to a random leaf, so once the index is larger than memory, each insert misses the cache and causes a random read, then a dirty page and sometimes a split. Switch to time-ordered IDs (UUIDv7, ULID, snowflake) or use a sequence plus a separate external ID; or partition so the active partition fits in memory.
What happens on a page split under concurrency, and how is the tree kept consistent after a crash? || The split allocates a new page, moves half the keys, and updates the parent; this must be atomic. Concurrency uses latch crabbing or B-link trees (a sideways pointer to the new page) so readers do not see a half-split. Crash safety comes from the WAL: the split is logged as a redo record, so replay reproduces it.
Why is a covering index fast, and what does it cost? || The query is answered from the index leaf alone, with no lookup of the row, saving one random read per row. It costs space (the extra columns are stored in the index), slower writes, and index-only scans in PostgreSQL still check the visibility map.
You have a 2 TB table and a query on a non-indexed column takes minutes. How do you decide whether to add an index? || Look at selectivity (an index helps when the predicate returns under about 1 to 5 percent of rows), how often the query runs, the write rate of the table, and the build time and lock behaviour (CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY). Often a partial index, a composite with the right column order (equality columns first, range last), or partitioning is better than a full index.
@@+ mvcc
tradeoffs: Snapshot isolation, read committed, or serializable. Choose read committed (PostgreSQL and Oracle default) for most OLTP: each statement sees data committed before it started, there are no read-write blocks, and anomalies like non-repeatable reads are tolerable for simple CRUD. Choose snapshot isolation or repeatable read when a transaction reads several rows and needs one consistent view, such as reports or balance checks, but know that it still allows write skew (two doctors both go off call). Choose serializable (PostgreSQL SSI, CockroachDB default) when invariants span rows and cannot be enforced by a constraint; it costs a few percent to 30 percent of throughput under contention and requires clients to retry on serialization failures, which can run at 1 to 10 percent of transactions in hot workloads. You give up concurrency anomalies or throughput, never both.
Where old versions live: in the table, in an undo log, or in a separate store. Choose in-place versions with vacuum (PostgreSQL heap) when updates are rare and you want cheap rollbacks and simple reads, accepting table bloat, a vacuum process that must keep up, and index entries for every version. Choose undo-log versions (InnoDB, Oracle) when updates are frequent and rows should stay compact: the newest row is in place and old ones are rebuilt from undo, so tables do not bloat, but a long reader must walk long undo chains and the undo tablespace can grow to tens of GB. Choose append-only versions with timestamps (Spanner, CockroachDB, Postgres-like LSM engines) when you need historical reads (AS OF SYSTEM TIME) and range-based garbage collection by a TTL such as 25 hours.
Long transactions and garbage collection. Choose to put hard limits on transaction age (idle_in_transaction_session_timeout of 1 to 5 minutes, statement_timeout, a read replica for reports) because one hour-long transaction pins the oldest snapshot, so no version newer than it can be removed. The result is bloat that can double table size, index scans slowed by dead entries, and in extreme cases PostgreSQL transaction ID wraparound at about 2 billion transactions, which forces a shutdown to protect data. You give up convenient long reports on the primary in return for a healthy database.
Pessimistic locking or MVCC with optimistic retry. Choose MVCC with optimistic conflict detection when conflicts are rare (under about 1 percent of transactions touch the same row): readers never block writers and throughput scales with cores. Choose explicit locks (SELECT ... FOR UPDATE, advisory locks, a queue table with SKIP LOCKED) for hot rows such as counters, inventory with one row per SKU, or job queues, where retries would waste work and abort storms are likely. A single hot row can only be updated at about 1,000 to 10,000 per second whatever the isolation level, so shard the counter or aggregate asynchronously.
Timestamps: logical, hybrid, or TrueTime. Choose a single logical counter (one-node database) when there is one writer authority; choose hybrid logical clocks (CockroachDB, YugabyteDB) when nodes have loosely synchronised clocks and you can handle an uncertainty window of a few hundred milliseconds that occasionally restarts a read; choose TrueTime-like bounded clocks (Spanner, 1 to 7 ms uncertainty, commit-wait) for external consistency across continents, at the cost of GPS/atomic clock hardware and added commit latency equal to the uncertainty.
interview: Explain how a snapshot read works without taking locks. || Each transaction gets a snapshot (a transaction ID or timestamp plus the list of transactions in progress). Every row version carries creator and deleter IDs; a read returns the newest version created by a transaction visible in the snapshot and not deleted by a visible one. Writers add new versions instead of overwriting, so readers never wait for writers.
What anomaly does snapshot isolation allow that serializable does not? Give an example. || Write skew: two transactions each read an overlapping set, then write different rows based on what they read, and both commit. Example: on-call rule requires at least one doctor; two doctors each check there are two on call and both go off. Fixes: serializable isolation, SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on the set, or a constraint or materialised row that makes both transactions write the same row.
Table size in PostgreSQL grows while row count is stable. What do you check? || Dead tuples: long-running or idle-in-transaction sessions, abandoned replication slots, or prepared transactions holding back the xmin horizon; autovacuum settings that cannot keep up with update rate; hot updates blocked by indexes. Then fix the root cause, tune autovacuum per table (scale factor 0.01, higher cost limit), and repack bloated tables with pg_repack if needed.
Why does InnoDB not bloat like PostgreSQL, and what is its drawback? || It updates in place and keeps old versions in the undo log, which is purged when no snapshot needs it. The drawback is that a long transaction makes undo grow and reads rebuild old versions by walking undo chains, which gets slower with age, and rollbacks of big transactions are slow.
How do you get a consistent read across a sharded database? || Use a single timestamp for the read across shards, which needs a global or bounded clock (TrueTime, HLC with uncertainty wait, or a timestamp oracle such as TiDB's PD), and every shard must be able to serve versions at that timestamp. Without it, you get fractured reads: some shards show the transaction, others do not.
@@+ columnar
tradeoffs: Row store or column store. Choose a row store (PostgreSQL, MySQL) when queries fetch whole records by key, rows are updated individually, and latency per query must be under 10 ms: one page read returns the full row. Choose a column store (Parquet, ClickHouse, Snowflake, Redshift, BigQuery, Delta) when queries scan millions to billions of rows but touch 5 of 100 columns, aggregate heavily, or filter on a few columns: reading only needed columns cuts I/O by 10 to 50 times, and compression of 3 to 10 times on top of that. You give up, with a column store, cheap point writes and updates (a single row update touches every column file) and fast single-row reads (one lookup touches one block per column). Hybrid designs (SingleStore, TiDB with TiFlash, PostgreSQL with a columnar extension) pay with two copies of the data.
Parquet or ORC or a proprietary format. Choose Parquet when data is shared between engines (Spark, Trino, DuckDB, Databricks, Athena) and lives in object storage; it has the widest tool support and good defaults (Snappy or Zstd, dictionary encoding, min/max statistics). Choose ORC when the stack is Hive-centred and you want built-in indexes and ACID features. Choose a proprietary or engine-native format (ClickHouse MergeTree, Snowflake micro-partitions) when one engine does almost all the queries and you want the best performance on updates, sorting and indexes. You give up portability for performance, or the reverse.
File and row-group size. Choose 128 MB to 1 GB files and 64 to 256 MB row groups for object storage: fewer, larger files reduce listing and request overhead (each S3 GET costs 10 to 50 ms before data flows) and give better compression and statistics. Choose smaller files only for streaming ingest where freshness matters, and run compaction afterwards. Millions of 1 MB files are one of the commonest reasons a query takes minutes instead of seconds. Large row groups raise memory use on write and reduce the benefit of skipping for selective filters.
Sort order, partitioning and clustering. Choose to partition by a low-cardinality field that nearly all queries filter on (date, often) with hundreds to thousands of partitions, not millions. Choose to sort or Z-order within files by the next most common filter columns, so min/max statistics skip 90 percent or more of the files. A wrong sort key leaves statistics useless, since every file then covers the full range. You give up flexibility, because a layout that is excellent for one query pattern is average for others, and re-clustering a table costs a full rewrite.
Encoding and compression. Choose dictionary and run-length encoding for low-cardinality columns (country, status), delta encoding for timestamps and IDs, and Zstd level 3 as the general codec; choose Snappy or LZ4 when CPU is the limit and the data is hot, since they decompress at several GB per second per core. Choose heavy compression (Zstd 9+) for cold data read rarely. Better compression saves storage and I/O but costs CPU on every scan, and vectorised engines can process dictionary-encoded data without decoding it.
interview: Why do column stores compress better than row stores? || Values in one column share a type and often a narrow range or few distinct values, so dictionary, run-length, delta and bit-packing encodings work well, and general codecs find more repetition in adjacent similar values. A row mixes strings, numbers and timestamps, which breaks patterns. Typical gain is 3 to 10 times against a row store's 1.5 to 3.
A dashboard query reads 40 GB from a 2 TB Parquet table even though it filters by customer_id. What do you look at? || Whether the data is sorted or clustered by customer_id (so min/max statistics prune files and row groups), whether the filter is pushed down to the Parquet reader, file sizes, bloom filters on the column, and partitioning. Likely fix: Z-order or cluster by customer_id, or use bloom filters or a pre-aggregated table.
How do column stores handle updates and deletes? || They avoid in-place edits: new data goes to a small write-optimised buffer or delta files, deletes are recorded as delete vectors or tombstone files, and background merges rewrite the files (Delta, Iceberg, ClickHouse merges). Readers combine base and delta at query time. Frequent single-row updates are therefore expensive and create small files.
What is vectorised execution and why does it fit columnar data? || The engine processes batches of 1,000 to 10,000 values at a time over a contiguous array with tight loops, SIMD instructions and few function calls, instead of one row at a time. Columnar layout provides contiguous typed arrays, so it makes good use of CPU caches and SIMD, giving several times the throughput of row-at-a-time interpretation.
When would you not use a columnar format for a service's main database? || OLTP with point reads and writes by key, many small transactions, strong consistency, and wide rows fetched whole. There the row store wins on latency and write cost. I would keep OLTP in a row store and replicate via CDC to a columnar store for analytics.
@@+ bloom
tradeoffs: Bloom filter, cuckoo or xor filter, or an exact set. Choose a Bloom filter when you need a simple, well-known filter that supports adding keys and merges by OR, at about 9.6 bits per key for 1 percent false positives (14.4 bits for 0.1 percent) against 1.44 times the ideal. Choose a cuckoo or quotient filter when you must also delete keys, or when the target false positive rate is low (under 3 percent), where cuckoo uses fewer bits and needs one or two cache lines per lookup. Choose a xor or ribbon filter (about 1.23 times the ideal size, 9 to 10 bits per key at 1 percent) for static sets built once, as in SSTables, with faster lookups and 20 to 30 percent less space. Choose an exact hash set or sorted array when the set is under a few million keys and an error is not acceptable; at 100 million keys a hash set takes gigabytes while a Bloom filter takes 120 MB.
False positive rate and size. The false positive rate is roughly 0.6185 raised to the power of bits per key, so every extra 4.8 bits per key divides errors by 10. Choose 10 bits per key and 7 hash functions when a false positive costs a disk read of 100 microseconds to 10 ms and the filter lives in RAM; choose 5 bits (about 10 percent) when memory is tight and a miss only costs a cheap cache check; choose 15 to 20 bits for very expensive checks such as a cross-region call. The deciding number is the cost of one false positive times its frequency against the cost of the memory.
Blocked Bloom filters. Choose a blocked or register-blocked Bloom filter (all k bits within one 64-byte cache line) when lookup speed matters: one cache miss per query instead of k, which can make the filter 2 to 4 times faster. You give up a slightly worse false positive rate (about 10 to 30 percent more errors at the same size) because load is uneven across blocks; add a few bits per key to compensate.
Sizing and growth. A plain Bloom filter must be sized in advance: if the real count is double the planned count, the false positive rate jumps from 1 percent to over 15 percent. Choose a scalable Bloom filter (a chain of filters with tightening rates) or a rebuild on schedule when the set grows unpredictably, and monitor the fill ratio (fraction of bits set; above 50 percent means over-full). Choose to rebuild periodically from the source of truth when deletes exist, since you cannot remove from a standard filter and counting variants cost 3 to 4 times the space.
Where it pays and where it does not. A Bloom filter pays when most queries are for absent keys and the check it guards is much more expensive than the filter lookup: LSM point reads, cache admission, the first-seen check before an exact deduplication, avoiding a database call for unknown usernames, and join pushdown of one side's keys into the other's scan. It does not pay when most keys are present (it saves nothing), when you need to enumerate the set, or when an error would be harmful (security block lists, where a false positive blocks a legitimate user).
interview: What guarantees does a Bloom filter give and what can go wrong? || No false negatives as long as nothing is deleted and the bits are intact; false positives at a predictable rate. It goes wrong if overloaded (more keys than planned), if the hash functions are weak or correlated, or if someone treats "maybe present" as "present".
Derive roughly how much memory you need for 1 billion keys at 1 percent false positives. || About 9.6 bits per key, so 9.6 billion bits, or 1.2 GB, with 7 hash functions. At 0.1 percent it is about 1.8 GB. Compare with an exact set of 8-byte hashes at 8 GB or more, plus overhead.
How does a Bloom filter speed up an LSM tree read, and where do you not use it? || It lets a point lookup skip files that cannot contain the key, so a miss costs a few in-memory probes rather than a disk read per file. Not useful for range scans (the filter works on exact keys; prefix filters help only for prefix queries), and less useful at the lowest level where most keys are present.
How would you use Bloom filters in a distributed join? || Build a filter from the keys of the smaller side, broadcast it (a few MB), and apply it while scanning the large side to drop rows that cannot match before shuffling them. A 1 percent false positive rate removes about 99 percent of non-matching rows from the network, and the join afterwards removes the rest exactly.
Can you merge or delete? || Two filters of the same size and hash functions merge with bitwise OR (the union). Deleting is impossible because a bit may be shared by many keys; use a counting Bloom filter or cuckoo filter, or rebuild.
@@+ hll
tradeoffs: HyperLogLog or an exact count. Choose HyperLogLog when the question is "how many distinct" over a large, high-cardinality stream, and a 1 to 2 percent error is acceptable: with precision p = 14 it uses 16 KB (12 KB in Redis's dense encoding) and gives a standard error of 0.81 percent, whether it counts a thousand or a trillion items. Choose an exact count (COUNT(DISTINCT), a bitmap, a hash set) when numbers are billed, audited or reconciled, when cardinality is under about 100,000 (a bitmap or set is then as small as the sketch), or when the result must be stable and explainable. You give up, with HLL, exact answers and the ability to list members or test membership; you give up, with exact counts, memory that grows linearly and distributed merges that move all values.
Precision parameter. The standard error is about 1.04 divided by the square root of the number of registers m = 2^p. Choose p = 12 (4,096 registers, 3 KB, 1.6 percent error) for many small sketches such as per-page or per-campaign counts; p = 14 (16 KB, 0.81 percent) as the default; p = 16 to 18 (64 to 256 KB, 0.4 to 0.2 percent) for a few important aggregates. Halving the error costs four times the memory. At large scale, 1 million sketches at p = 14 are 16 GB, so tiered sparse encoding for small counts matters.
HLL, HLL++ or theta sketches / KMV. Choose HyperLogLog++ (BigQuery, Spark approx_count_distinct, Redis PFCOUNT) for plain distinct counts and unions: sketches merge by taking the register-wise maximum, so partial results from shards combine exactly like the single sketch would. Choose a Theta or tuple sketch (Apache DataSketches) or a KMV sketch when you also need intersections and differences (users who saw both ad A and ad B) or counts of distinct with attached values; the price is 2 to 8 times the space for the same error, and intersection error can become large when the overlap is small. HLL cannot compute intersection directly except by inclusion-exclusion, which blows up the error.
Roll-up design. Choose to store one sketch per dimension combination per time bucket (for example per hour, per campaign) and merge at query time, which makes any later roll-up over days, weeks or arbitrary filter sets cheap and correct. Choose exact pre-aggregation only for dimension sets that are fixed. Merging many sketches loses nothing, but you cannot subtract: a rolling 7-day window needs seven daily sketches merged, not a running sketch with expiry.
Hash quality and bias. The estimate depends on well-distributed 64-bit hashes (MurmurHash3, xxHash, SipHash); a weak hash or a 32-bit hash that saturates near a few billion items makes the estimate wrong. Small cardinalities use linear counting and large ones need bias correction, which HLL++ includes and the original algorithm lacks. Choose a library that does, and do not write your own for production.
interview: How does HyperLogLog estimate distinct count from a stream, and why does it work? || Hash every item to a uniform bit string; use the first p bits to pick a register and keep, per register, the maximum number of leading zeros seen plus one. A maximum of k suggests about 2^k items went into that register, and a harmonic mean across registers removes outliers. A rare pattern of many leading zeros only appears if many distinct hashes were seen, and duplicates never change a register.
Why can you merge HLL sketches from different shards, and why not subtract them? || A register stores a maximum, and the maximum of maxima is the maximum of the union, so merging is lossless and order-independent. A maximum cannot be undone, so deletion and sliding-window removal need windowed sketches or recomputation.
Your daily active users figure from HLL differs 1.5 percent from the billing system. Is that a bug? || Not necessarily: p = 14 has 0.81 percent standard error, so 1.5 percent is within 2 sigma. Check the precision, hashing, and whether the exact system dedupes differently (ID resolution, time zones). Use HLL for dashboards and the exact one for billing, or raise precision.
How do you compute distinct users per day across 90 days with HLL? || You cannot add daily distinct counts, because users repeat. Store one sketch per day (16 KB each) and merge the 90 sketches by register max, then estimate. For any date range or any filter combination that was pre-bucketed, merging is cheap.
Name the cases where you would not use HLL. || Small cardinalities with exact needs, billing and compliance, when you need membership or listing, when intersections are the main question and overlap is small, and adversarial input where an attacker could craft hashes (use a keyed hash).
@@+ cms
tradeoffs: Count-min sketch or exact counting. Choose a count-min sketch when there are too many distinct keys to keep a counter each (hundreds of millions of URLs, IPs, user IDs) and you need approximate frequencies, mostly for the heavy ones: width w = e/epsilon and depth d = ln(1/delta); w = 2,000 and d = 5 (40 KB with 32-bit counters) gives an overcount of at most 0.14 percent of the total stream length with 99.3 percent probability. Choose exact counting (a hash map, a database GROUP BY) when the key set fits in memory (under about 10 to 100 million keys) or when small counts matter, because the error is additive and about the same for every key: a key seen 3 times may read as 5,000 in a busy stream. You give up exactness and the small tail; you gain fixed memory and mergeable counters.
Count-min, Count sketch or Misra-Gries/Space-Saving. Choose count-min when counts are non-negative and you can accept only overestimates (never an undercount): good for rate limiting and "is this above a threshold" checks. Choose Count sketch (random signs, median estimate) when you need unbiased estimates or deletions, and L2 error guarantees on skewed data; it uses roughly the same space but gives errors that go both ways. Choose Space-Saving or Misra-Gries when you want the actual top-k list with labelled keys: it stores keys explicitly with k counters and guarantees any item with frequency over N/k is present. A count-min sketch alone cannot list the heavy hitters, since it stores no keys; pair it with a small heap of candidates.
Conservative update. Choose conservative update (increment only the counters that equal the current minimum) when the stream is skewed and you only query, never delete: it reduces overestimation, often by 2 to 10 times on real traffic. You give up mergeability in its exact form, since merged conservative sketches lose some guarantees, and the ability to support deletions. Use the plain version when you must merge sketches across many machines and need predictable bounds.
Width, depth and sizing by error budget. Error is proportional to total count divided by width, so halving the error doubles the width, while depth only drives the failure probability (each extra row cuts it by about e times). Choose width from the heaviness threshold you care about (to see items above 0.1 percent of the stream, use width of at least 2,720 to 10,000) and depth of 4 to 7. Rolling counts need either one sketch per time window or exponential decay: periodically halve all counters, which keeps recent heavy items on top.
Where it fits. Choose it for hot-key detection (a cache or shard protection layer), per-IP rate limiting at line speed, trending items, TinyLFU admission in caches (4-bit counters, a few bytes per cached item), and query frequency estimates in database optimisers. Do not choose it for billing, for rare-event counts, for small streams, or when the stream length is small compared with width (then use a hash map).
interview: Why does a count-min sketch never undercount, and what is the error bound? || Each key increments one counter in each of d rows; other keys can only add to those counters, never subtract, so every counter is at least the true count. The estimate is the minimum across rows. With width e/epsilon and depth ln(1/delta), the overcount is at most epsilon times the stream length with probability at least 1 minus delta.
How would you find the top 100 heavy hitters in a stream of 10 billion events using a count-min sketch? || Keep the sketch plus a min-heap of 100 candidates. For each event update the sketch, take the estimate, and if it exceeds the smallest in the heap, insert or update the key. At the end the heap holds the heavy hitters with overestimated counts. For exactness, run a second pass over only the candidates. Space-Saving is the more direct alternative.
Why is a count-min sketch poor at estimating rare items? || The error is additive and set by the total volume divided by width, so for a stream of 1 billion events and width 10,000 the noise is up to 100,000 events per counter. A key that really occurred 10 times can be reported as thousands; counts below the noise floor are meaningless.
How do you use sketches in a distributed setting? || Each worker builds a local sketch with the same size and hash seeds; merging is element-wise addition, so partial sketches from shards or time buckets combine exactly into the sketch of the union. This allows map-side aggregation with small messages (kilobytes) instead of shipping every key.
How does a cache use a count-min sketch (TinyLFU)? || It estimates how often a key was requested recently with small 4-bit counters, and admits a new item to the cache only if its estimated frequency beats the item it would evict. Counters are periodically halved so history decays. This raises hit ratio over plain LRU on skewed traffic, at a few bits per entry.
@@+ eviction
tradeoffs: LRU, LFU, or something adaptive (ARC, TinyLFU, SIEVE, S3-FIFO). Choose LRU when access patterns shift with time (sessions, recent documents): simple, constant-time, and good enough in most cases. Choose LFU or TinyLFU when popularity is stable and skewed (Zipf: a small set of hot items gets most requests), since frequency protects the hot set from one-off scans. Choose ARC or Window-TinyLFU (Caffeine) when workloads mix scans and hot items and you cannot tune; Caffeine reaches hit ratios several points above LRU on many traces, and an extra 5 percent hit rate on a 90 percent base halves the miss traffic hitting the database. Choose S3-FIFO or SIEVE when you want near-state-of-the-art hit rates with queue-based, lock-friendly implementations at very high throughput. You give up, with adaptive policies, simplicity and some memory per entry; you give up, with plain LRU, scan resistance.
Cache size and the hit-ratio curve. The benefit of memory is not linear. Choose to measure a miss-ratio curve (simulate with a trace, or use tools like SHARDS sampling) before buying memory: on skewed traffic doubling the cache may raise the hit ratio from 80 to 88 percent, while on uniform traffic it raises it almost nothing. What matters is the cost of a miss: a miss to a 2 ms database at 10,000 requests per second with a 90 percent hit ratio is 1,000 database queries per second, and 95 percent halves that. Size the cache to the knee of the curve, not to the full data set.
Exact LRU or approximated. Choose approximated LRU (Redis samples 5 to 10 random keys and evicts the oldest; CLOCK in operating systems) when the cache has millions of keys and a global lock around a linked list would limit throughput: sampling comes within a few percent of exact LRU and has no list to update on each hit. Choose exact LRU with segmented locks or buffered updates (Caffeine records accesses in ring buffers and applies them in batches) when you need maximum hit ratio in a JVM or similar process. Per-hit list updates make cache hits writes, which cost cache-line contention across cores.
Eviction by cost, size and TTL. Choose size-aware eviction (GDSF, or weighing entries by bytes) when object sizes vary by 100 times or more, such as a CDN with 5 KB images and 500 MB videos, since count-based LRU lets a few large objects displace hundreds of small ones and lowers the byte hit ratio or request hit ratio, whichever matters to you. Choose TTL expiry on top of eviction for correctness (stale data, privacy), not capacity. Choose cost-aware policies when misses have unequal cost, for example a cached result that takes 2 seconds to compute versus one that takes 2 ms.
Admission, not only eviction. Choose an admission filter (TinyLFU, a Bloom filter requiring a second request before caching, "cache on second hit") when a large share of objects are requested once: on CDNs, about 50 to 70 percent of objects are one-hit wonders, and caching them wastes space and write endurance on flash caches. You give up first-hit latency for those items. Without admission, even a good eviction policy keeps churning the cache.
interview: Compare LRU and LFU. When does each fail? || LRU fails on scans: a one-time pass over a large data set pushes the hot set out. LFU fails when popularity changes: old popular items keep high counts and hold space; and a new item has a low count and gets evicted at once. Practical systems use decay (aging) and admission, as in TinyLFU, or mix recency and frequency as in ARC.
Implement an O(1) LRU cache. What are the concurrency problems? || A hash map to nodes of a doubly linked list; on get, move the node to the head; on put, insert at the head and evict the tail when full. Every read mutates the list, so a single lock serialises reads. Solutions: shard the cache by key, use sampled or CLOCK approximations, or buffer access records and replay them in batches.
Your cache hit ratio dropped from 95 to 80 percent after a deployment. How do you investigate? || Check key cardinality and key format (a new field or version in the key splits the cache), TTL changes, object size changes, new traffic patterns such as scans or a crawler, a smaller memory limit, and a restart that emptied the cache. Compare miss-ratio by key prefix, and look at eviction rate versus expiry rate.
What is a cache stampede and how do you prevent it? || Many requests miss on the same hot key at once (after expiry or eviction) and all hit the database. Prevent it with request coalescing (one loader, others wait), a lock or lease on the miss, serving stale data while refreshing, jittered TTLs, and probabilistic early refresh.
How does the operating system page cache or a database buffer pool evict pages? || Approximations of LRU: Linux uses active and inactive lists (a page must be touched twice to become active, which resists scans), PostgreSQL uses a clock-sweep with usage counts, InnoDB splits the LRU list into young and old sublists so a table scan loads pages into the old part only. All add scan resistance to plain LRU because scans are common in databases.
@@+ gd
tradeoffs: SGD with momentum, Adam/AdamW, or a second-order method. Choose SGD with momentum (0.9) when training convolutional vision models or when you have time to tune the learning rate schedule: it often generalises slightly better, has one state vector per parameter, and is cheap. Choose AdamW (beta1 0.9, beta2 0.95 to 0.999, weight decay 0.01 to 0.1) for transformers, language models and sparse or badly scaled problems: it adapts per parameter step sizes, tolerates a wider range of learning rates and needs less tuning. You give up, with Adam, memory (two extra states per weight: 12 bytes per parameter in FP32 on top of 4 for the weights and 4 for gradients, so a 7B model needs about 112 GB just for optimiser plus weights and gradients, before activations) and sometimes the last point of accuracy; you give up, with SGD, robustness to the learning rate. Choose L-BFGS or Newton-type methods only for small, smooth, full-batch problems (logistic regression, a few thousand parameters) where they converge in tens of iterations.
Batch size and learning rate. Choose a small to medium batch (32 to 512 for small models, 1 to 4 million tokens for LLMs) when you want noisy gradients that help generalisation and a good use of data; choose a large batch (thousands to millions of tokens) when you need to use many GPUs, and then scale the learning rate (linearly up to a critical batch size, square root for Adam) with a warmup of several hundred to thousands of steps. Past the critical batch size, doubling the batch gives no fewer steps: you are paying for GPUs with no faster learning. The gradient noise scale estimates this limit.
Learning rate schedule. Choose warmup plus cosine decay (to 10 percent of the peak) for fixed-budget training, since it gives the best final loss when you know the total number of steps. Choose warmup plus constant rate with a final decay (WSD, warmup-stable-decay) when you may continue training or want to branch checkpoints at any time. Choose one-cycle or a learning rate finder for quick experiments. Too high a rate at the start diverges, especially for Adam with large beta2; too low wastes compute. A typical peak for transformers is 1e-4 to 6e-4 for small models and 1e-5 to 3e-5 for fine-tuning.
Precision and stability. Choose BF16 for forward and backward passes with FP32 master weights and optimiser state as the default: same range as FP32 and no loss scaling. Choose FP16 with dynamic loss scaling only on older hardware; choose FP8 only when you have tested stability on your model. Choose gradient clipping (global norm 1.0) for transformers and RNNs to survive rare loss spikes. Loss spikes at scale are often fixed by lowering beta2 to 0.95, skipping the bad batch, or restarting from a checkpoint a few hundred steps earlier.
Where gradient descent breaks down. Choose a different method when the objective is not differentiable or the gradient is noisy (evolution strategies, Bayesian optimisation for under 20 hyperparameters, reinforcement learning with policy gradients and variance reduction), when the problem is convex and small (use a closed form or a convex solver, as the answer is exact), or when the loss landscape has many saddle points and narrow valleys (use momentum, normalisation layers, or better conditioning). In deep learning, local minima are less of a problem than saddle points, bad conditioning and sharp minima.
interview: Why does Adam work well, and why is AdamW preferred? || Adam keeps running averages of the gradient (first moment) and its square (second moment) and divides the step by the square root of the second, so each parameter gets its own step size, which suits sparse or badly scaled gradients. In classic Adam, L2 regularisation is added to the gradient and then rescaled by the adaptive term, so it does not decay weights uniformly. AdamW applies the weight decay directly to the weights, separate from the adaptive step, which regularises correctly.
Training loss suddenly becomes NaN or spikes at step 40,000. What do you do? || Check learning rate relative to the schedule, gradient norm history, a bad batch (data corruption, very long sequences), numerical range (FP16 overflow, softmax or log of zero), and Adam's epsilon and beta2. Restart from a checkpoint before the spike, skip or reshuffle the data, enable clipping, lower the peak learning rate or beta2, and use BF16 or higher precision in sensitive operations.
How does batch size affect training, and how do you choose it for a big cluster? || Below the critical batch size, bigger batches reduce the number of steps almost linearly, so more GPUs give faster training. Above it, extra samples reduce gradient noise that is already small, and returns drop. Estimate the gradient noise scale, scale the learning rate with the batch, use warmup, and prefer gradient accumulation over a bigger batch when memory is the limit.
Why can a model with perfect training loss still generalise badly, and how does gradient descent help or hurt? || The model can fit noise or memorise. Gradient descent from small initialisation with early stopping, weight decay and noise tends to find simpler or flatter solutions, an implicit regularisation, but not always. Check validation loss, use regularisation, data augmentation, and early stopping.
What memory does training with Adam need for a 1B-parameter model, and how do you cut it? || With FP32 master weights, FP32 gradients and two FP32 Adam states, about 16 bytes per parameter, so 16 GB plus activations (BF16 weights add 2 bytes). Cut it with sharding of optimiser state, gradients and parameters (ZeRO or FSDP) across GPUs, 8-bit optimisers, activation checkpointing, or Adafactor / Lion with fewer states.
