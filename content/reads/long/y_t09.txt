@@+ probability
tradeoffs: Choose a closed-form bound when you need a guarantee you can put in a design doc, and a simulation when the system has correlation, state or feedback. A union bound or Chernoff bound takes minutes and is safe, but it can be loose by 10x or more: it says "any of 1,000 machines fails" is at most 1%, which is fine when events are rare and useless when the sum passes 1. A Monte Carlo run of 10⁶ trials takes seconds and gives the answer to about 0.1% error for common events, but to see a 10⁻⁹ event you would need about 10¹¹ trials, so rare-event questions need analytic tail bounds or importance sampling instead. You give up tightness with the first and the proof with the second.
Choose the independence assumption only when you can name the mechanism that makes events independent (separate racks, separate power feeds, separate code paths); choose a correlated model (a common-cause factor, a beta-binomial, or scenario analysis such as "lose a whole zone") when replicas share a deploy, a rack, a kernel or a bug. Three replicas at 1% failure give 10⁻⁶ if independent, but if 10% of failures are shared-cause the real figure is nearer 10⁻³, a thousand times worse. The independent model is simple and optimistic; the correlated one needs data you rarely have, so a common compromise is to compute the independent number and then divide by a safety factor of 100 to 1,000 for shared-cause risk.
Choose frequentist reasoning (confidence intervals, p-values) for repeatable, high-volume experiments such as A/B tests with 10⁵ or more users per arm, where the rules are agreed in advance and the answer must not depend on an opinion. Choose Bayesian reasoning (priors, posteriors) when data is scarce (a new product with 50 conversions), when you must update continuously (bandits, anomaly detection), or when you want a statement like "a 92% chance B is better". You give up objectivity with a Bayesian prior and the direct meaning of the answer with a frequentist test; with plenty of data the two agree and the choice does not matter.
Choose averages and normal approximations (central limit theorem) for sums of many light-tailed items: with \( n \ge 30 \) independent samples of modest variance, a mean is close to normal. They break down for heavy tails: for a Pareto distribution with \( \alpha \le 2 \) the variance is infinite, the sample mean converges very slowly, and one extreme item can dominate a sum (file sizes, latency, key popularity). There, use percentiles, medians of means, or tail-specific models, and size capacity for p99 and p999 rather than the mean. The same rule applies to expected value as a decision rule: it is right for repeated bets, but a rare ruinous outcome (losing all replicas, a data-corrupting bug) needs a limit on the worst case, not on the average.
interview: Three replicas each have a 1% chance of being down. A teammate says the chance of losing all three is one in a million. When is that wrong, and what do you use instead? || It is right only if failures are independent. Replicas that share a rack, power feed, deploy or bug fail together, and if even 10% of the failure probability is shared the true figure is about 10⁻³. I would list the shared causes, place replicas across racks and zones to remove the largest ones, and model the rest as a correlated scenario (such as losing one zone) rather than multiplying probabilities.
Your fraud model catches 99% of fraud and flags 1% of honest users, and fraud is 0.1% of traffic. Should the team auto-block flagged users? || No. By Bayes' rule P(fraud | flagged) is about 9%, so about nine in ten blocks hit honest users. I would use the flag to route to review or a step-up check, or raise the threshold until precision is acceptable, and report precision and recall at the real base rate, not accuracy.
A bug corrupts one request in 10⁹ and the service handles 10¹⁰ requests a day. Is it worth fixing? || Yes: it fires about 10 times a day, and over a year about 3,650 times. A tiny per-event probability only matters after multiplying by volume, and the union bound gives the quick upper estimate of "at least once". Priority depends on what the corruption costs, but "one in a billion" is no argument for ignoring it.
Why does the p99 latency of a request that calls 100 backends look much worse than each backend's p99? || If each backend is slow (over its own p99) with probability 1%, then all 100 are fast with probability \( 0.99^{100} \) ≈ 37%, so 63% of user requests wait for at least one slow backend. Fan-out turns each backend's tail into the median experience. Fixes are hedged requests, tied requests, lowering the fan-out, and shrinking each backend's tail.
When would you not trust the central limit theorem when reporting a metric from a sample? || When the data is heavy-tailed (revenue per user, request sizes, latency), the sample is small, or observations are dependent (the same user many times, time series with autocorrelation). Then the normal interval is too narrow. I would use a bootstrap, cluster by user, report the median and percentiles, or use a method designed for heavy tails.
How does random hashing or sampling show up in a distributed system design, and what guarantee do you actually get? || Random placement (consistent hashing, random partitioning) gives balance only on average: with n keys on m nodes the heaviest node is about \( n/m + \sqrt{(n/m) \ln m} \), so small clusters need virtual nodes or the power of two choices. Sketches and Bloom filters give error bounds that hold with high probability (not certain), with parameters that come from Chernoff-style bounds, so I state the error rate and the failure probability together.
@@+ infotheory
tradeoffs: Choose Huffman coding when decode speed and simplicity matter and no symbol is very likely: table-driven Huffman decodes at 1 to 2 GB/s per core and loses less than 1 bit per symbol. Choose arithmetic coding or ANS when the distribution is skewed or the model adapts: ANS (Zstd uses a table-based ANS, FSE) gets within 0.1% of entropy at similar speed, while classic arithmetic coding is slower (a few hundred MB/s) but allows adaptive, context-mixed models, as in high-ratio archivers. You give up some code size and patent or complexity risk with ANS, and speed with arithmetic coding; for a symbol with probability 0.99, Huffman spends 1 bit where about 0.015 are needed.
Choose lossless encoding when you must reproduce exact bytes (logs, tables, model weights in storage) and a lossy or rate–distortion approach when a person or a model tolerates error (video, audio, embeddings, quantized weights). Lossless columnar compression typically gives 3x to 10x on low-entropy columns and almost nothing on hashes, IDs or encrypted data. Lossy gives 50x to 1,000x (H.264 and AV1 video, 4-bit weight quantization at 4x over 16-bit), but you give up exactness, and errors can add up across repeated re-encoding. The measure that decides is entropy under your model, not the file's apparent size: sorting, delta-coding or a better predictor lowers the entropy that matters.
Choose bits per token or perplexity to compare language models only when the tokenizer is the same; choose bits per byte (or per character) when comparing different tokenizers or datasets. A model with a 100K vocabulary and a 32K one assign different numbers of tokens to the same text, so their per-token losses cannot be compared. Also pick the divergence direction by the behaviour you want: forward KL(data ‖ model), which is cross-entropy training, covers all modes and can put mass between them; reverse KL(model ‖ target), used in some RLHF and variational methods, picks one mode and ignores the rest. You give up coverage with the second and the risk of blurry, over-wide outputs with the first.
Choose Shannon's limits as a design ceiling, not as a promise. The capacity formula \( B \log_2(1 + S/N) \) is a hard bound for a Gaussian channel, and modern LDPC and polar codes get within about 0.5 to 1 dB of it, so there is little left to win at the code level; the useful moves are more bandwidth, more signal power (3 dB buys only about 1 bit/s/Hz at high S/N), or more antennas (MIMO, which multiplies the number of parallel channels). The limit is asymptotic: it assumes long blocks and known noise statistics, and at the short blocks of control channels (100 to 1,000 bits) you pay a finite-length penalty and use different codes. Entropy estimates from data also break down when the alphabet is large relative to the sample, since plug-in estimates are biased low; with 10⁶ samples over 10⁶ possible values, the estimate is meaningless.
interview: You are choosing an encoding for a 10-billion-row column. How do you decide, and how do you know when to stop? || Measure first: distinct count, value frequencies and sort order, which give the empirical entropy. Low-entropy columns (country, status) use dictionary plus run-length or bit-packing and often reach 10x; sorted or slowly changing values use delta encoding; high-entropy columns (UUIDs, hashes) barely compress, so I leave them to a general-purpose codec such as Zstd and accept 1.0 to 1.1x. I stop when the remaining gain is under the cost in CPU or decode latency, usually when I am within about 10% of the entropy estimate.
Why do we compress before encrypting, and why is compressing encrypted data useless? || Good ciphertext looks uniform random, so its entropy is the maximum and no model can predict it. Compression needs predictable structure, which exists only in the plaintext. Compression before encryption can leak information through the output length (CRIME and BREACH attacks), so it is not free of risk, and for secrets mixed with attacker-controlled data it is often turned off.
Two language models report loss 2.1 and 2.3. Can you say the first is better? || Only if both use the same tokenizer and the same data, and in the same units (nats against bits differs by a factor of 1.443). Otherwise convert to bits per byte of the same text. I would also check that the evaluation set is not in the training data, since lower cross-entropy there reflects memorization.
Why does cross-entropy training make a model's probabilities match the data, and where does that go wrong? || Cross-entropy equals \( H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p \parallel q) \), and the second term is zero only when \( q = p \), so the minimum is reached by the true distribution. It goes wrong when the model class cannot represent p, when labels are noisy (the model learns the noise rate), or with finite data, where the model fits the sample and overfits. Probabilities can also be overconfident, so I calibrate them (temperature scaling) before using them for decisions.
A network engineer says a better modulation will get 400 Mbit/s over a 20 MHz link at 20 dB. What do you say? || Capacity is 20 MHz × \( \log_2(1 + 100) \) ≈ 133 Mbit/s, so 400 is impossible for a single stream whatever the modulation. Options are more bandwidth (40 or 80 MHz), better SNR (closer, directional antennas), or multiple spatial streams with MIMO, which uses several parallel channels.
Where does information theory show up when you design a data pipeline or logging system? || In the cost of bytes: schema choices, dictionary encoding, delta encoding and sampling decide storage and network use, and entropy tells how far you can go. Sampling or hashing to estimate distinct counts (HyperLogLog) trades exactness for memory; deduplication works only when there is redundancy. For features, mutual information shows which columns carry signal about the label, and constant or near-duplicate columns carry none.
@@+ queueing
tradeoffs: Choose a closed-form model (M/M/1, M/M/c, Pollaczek–Khinchine) when you need a capacity answer in five minutes and are within about a factor of two: the models say that at 70% utilization waits are about 3x the service time, and at 90% about 10x, which is enough to pick a target such as 60 to 70% peak utilization. Choose simulation or a replay of real traces when arrivals are bursty (a coefficient of variation of arrivals well above 1), service times are heavy-tailed, or the system has priorities, batching, retries or limits on queue length. The formulas are optimistic for bursty traffic (Kingman's approximation scales waiting with \( (C_a^2 + C_s^2)/2 \)), and replaying a day of production traffic through a model of 200 lines will show the effects you cannot write down.
Choose one shared queue with many workers (M/M/c) when jobs are interchangeable and the queue can be shared: at 87.5% utilization with 8 workers that cuts the time in system from 32 ms (eight M/M/1 queues) to about 6.5 ms. Choose separate queues, with sticky routing, when you need locality (cache affinity, per-key ordering in Kafka partitions, a shard that owns its data), because sharing is impossible or too costly. You give up some of the pooling gain; the usual middle path is the power of two choices (probe two random workers and send to the shorter queue), which captures most of the benefit with little coordination, or work stealing.
Choose a bounded queue with load shedding or back-pressure for a user-facing service, and an unbounded queue only for work where latency does not matter (batch ETL, offline indexing). A queue sized for 10 times the normal depth turns an overload into minutes of delay and a pile of requests that time out after being processed; a small queue (often 1 to 2 times the number of workers, or a latency budget such as 100 ms of work) fails fast and keeps accepted requests quick. You give up some successful requests during bursts, and you need clients that handle rejection with backoff. For batch pipelines, back-pressure from the consumer keeps memory bounded, and the cost is that producers stall.
Choose higher utilization for throughput-oriented, latency-tolerant work (batch jobs, GPU training, 85 to 95% is common) and lower utilization for latency-sensitive tiers (databases, brokers, payment paths, 50 to 70%). Autoscaling on utilization lags behind bursts by 1 to 5 minutes (instance start-up plus metrics delay), so the headroom must cover that gap; scaling on queue depth or request rate per replica reacts earlier. The cost of headroom is real money (running at 60% rather than 90% means about 50% more machines), so the decision should be written as latency target against cost, using the 1/(1 − ρ) curve to quantify it.
interview: Your service is at 50% CPU on average, yet p99 latency is high. What do you check? || I check utilization at the time scale of the latency target: a one-minute average hides bursts that run at 100% for a few seconds. Then queue wait against service time in traces (if wait dominates, it is queueing), variability of service times (a slow dependency, GC pauses), whether the load balancer creates separate queues, and fan-out. Fixes are splitting slow and fast work, pooling, lowering the burst, and hedging.
How would you size a connection pool for a service doing 2,000 requests per second with 150 ms in the database each? || Little's law gives 2,000 × 0.15 = 300 connections in use on average, so the pool needs about 400 for headroom, but the database must also accept that many. A bigger pool does not always help: past the database's own concurrency limit (about 2 to 4 times its cores) extra connections just queue inside the database, so I would shorten queries, add a read replica, or cap the pool and queue in front.
Why is my load test showing great latency while production is slow? || The test is likely a closed system: a fixed number of clients wait for each response before sending the next, so when the server slows, arrivals slow too and the queue never builds. Real users arrive independently (open system). I use an open-loop generator at a fixed arrival rate and measure latency from the intended send time, which avoids coordinated omission.
What is a metastable failure, and how does queueing explain it? || A trigger (a deploy, a cache flush) pushes load above capacity, queues grow, requests time out, and clients retry, adding load; the system stays overloaded after the trigger is gone because the retry load itself holds it above capacity. Remedies are retry budgets (for example, retries at most 10% of traffic), exponential backoff with jitter, load shedding with short queue limits, and circuit breakers.
Eight workers share a queue with a mix of 99% 4 ms jobs and 1% 200 ms jobs. What do you change? || The slow jobs raise the mean service time to about 6 ms and the variance sharply, so waits for fast jobs grow with \( (1 + C^2) \). I would route slow jobs to a separate pool or queue (or give them a deadline and cancel), so fast jobs do not wait behind them, and size each pool with Little's law.
How does Little's law help you debug a Kafka consumer that is falling behind? || Lag = arrival rate − processing rate integrated over time. If arrivals are 10,000/s and the group processes 9,000/s, lag grows by 1,000/s, so 600,000 messages of lag will take 600 s to build and, at a catch-up rate of 2,000/s extra, 300 s to drain. The fix is raising throughput (more consumers up to the partition count, faster processing) above the arrival rate with margin, not just restarting.
@@+ lightspeed
tradeoffs: Choose the simple model, RTT = 2 × route length ÷ 200,000 km/s, for design reviews: it gives the floor (about 1 ms per 100 km of fibre route) and is right to within a factor of 1.2 to 2 for real paths, because cables are longer than great-circle lines and routers add 0.1 to 1 ms per hop. Choose measured RTTs (ping, traceroute, your cloud's inter-region latency tables) when you need an actual number: Mumbai to Frankfurt has a floor near 65 to 80 ms but is measured at 110 to 130 ms. The model breaks down inside a data centre, where switch and software time (50 to 500 µs) is larger than propagation (under 5 µs), and so distance is not the cost you should be optimizing there.
Choose to pay the distance (synchronous cross-region replication, a consensus group spanning regions) only when losing a region without losing a committed write is worth 50 to 150 ms on every write. Choose asynchronous replication, a single-region leader with regional read replicas, or per-region data ownership (users pinned to a home region) when you can accept a few seconds of data loss or stale reads, and want writes at 1 to 5 ms. You give up zero-loss failover with the second option; you give up write latency, throughput per key (a serialized key in a 100 ms group can do about 10 commits a second) and money with the first.
Choose a CDN or edge cache when content is cacheable and read-heavy: a hit at 10 to 30 ms replaces an origin fetch of 150 ms or more, and the cost is staleness and invalidation. Choose moving the compute to the data, or to the user, when the request is a dynamic chain of calls (edge functions, regional deployments), so that many sequential round trips become one. Chatty protocols are the real enemy: ten sequential 100 ms round trips cost a second, and no bandwidth increase fixes it, whereas batching, pipelining, HTTP/3 with 0-RTT, and a TLS session reuse cut round trips directly.
Choose NTP or PTP and bounded clock uncertainty (as in Spanner's TrueTime with an error bound of a few milliseconds) when you need ordering by time; choose logical clocks (Lamport, hybrid logical clocks) when you cannot bound the error. Physical clocks assume symmetric delays, so a 10 ms outbound and 30 ms return path gives a 10 ms offset error. Choose microwave or hollow-core fibre (about 0.997c in air-core against 0.67c in glass) only where microseconds are worth millions, as in trading routes; their cost is bandwidth, weather, and tower or cable build-out, which makes them irrelevant for ordinary services.
interview: Your team wants synchronous replication across US-East, EU and Singapore for a payments ledger. What do you ask? || I compute the floor first: a majority needs the second-nearest region, so with US-East to EU at about 80 ms RTT every write costs at least 80 ms, and with Singapore as part of the majority 200 ms or more. I ask whether the business needs zero data loss across a region failure (RPO of 0), or whether a few seconds are fine. If zero is needed I accept the latency, batch writes, and keep a region-local fast path for reads; otherwise I use a regional leader with async replicas.
A page makes 12 sequential API calls from a browser in Mumbai to a server in Virginia. How do you speed it up? || At about 200 ms RTT the calls alone cost 2.4 s, and server speed is irrelevant. I would cut round trips: combine calls into one (a BFF or GraphQL), run independent calls in parallel, reuse connections (HTTP/2 or HTTP/3, TLS session resumption) and serve from a nearer region or edge. Bandwidth upgrades do nothing here.
Why can a 100 Gbit/s link between two regions give only a few Gbit/s for one TCP flow? || Throughput is limited by window ÷ RTT. With a 100 ms RTT, a 1 MB window gives 80 Mbit/s; filling 100 Gbit/s needs a 1.25 GB window (the bandwidth-delay product). Fixes: larger socket buffers and window scaling, a better congestion control (BBR), and several parallel streams.
Why can't two servers agree on 'now', and what does it mean for your design? || Messages take time, and delays are asymmetric and variable, so a clock offset can only be estimated with an error of about half the delay difference. Therefore I do not order events with raw timestamps unless I have a bounded uncertainty (TrueTime-like, wait out the error bound); otherwise I use logical or hybrid clocks, or a single sequencer.
How does the speed of light limit chips as well as networks? || At 4 GHz a cycle is 0.25 ns, in which a signal on a board travels about 4 cm and on a chip much less after RC delay, so designs pipeline and keep data near compute (caches, HBM stacked beside the GPU). That is why memory latency is the bottleneck, and why locality matters as much on one board as it does between continents.
@@+ transistor
tradeoffs: Choose the digital abstraction (a transistor is an ideal switch, gates have fixed delay) for software, architecture and capacity thinking, and the physical model (dynamic power \( \propto C V^2 f \), leakage, thermal limits) when you reason about energy, clock speed or why a chip stopped scaling. The switch model is right to within a factor of 2 for gate delay at a given node and tells you nothing about power. The physical model explains the facts that shaped software: clocks stalled near 3 to 5 GHz after about 2005 because power density hit roughly 100 W/cm², so performance moved to cores (multithreading), vectors and accelerators. You give up simplicity, but only the physical view tells you that halving voltage cuts dynamic power by 4x, and that a wide, slow chip beats a narrow, fast one.
Choose a newer process node (5 nm or 3 nm, with FinFET or gate-all-around) when performance per watt or density decides the product (phones, GPUs, large accelerators); choose a mature node (14 to 28 nm or older) when cost, analog performance and supply stability matter (microcontrollers, power management, many automotive and IoT chips). A leading-edge wafer costs on the order of $15,000 to $20,000 against $3,000 or less for a mature one, and mask sets reach tens of millions of dollars, so a design must ship in tens of millions of units or sell for a high price per chip. You give up cost and yield (large dies fail more often) with the first; you give up density and efficiency with the second.
Choose more transistors for parallelism (more cores, wider vector units, large caches, HBM) when the workload scales with parallel work, such as training or serving neural networks; choose fewer, faster, lower-latency transistors (big out-of-order cores, branch prediction) when the workload is serial, like a database's single-threaded commit path. Dark silicon makes this concrete: you cannot power all transistors at once at full frequency, so chips add special-purpose blocks (encoders, tensor cores) that are 10 to 100 times more energy-efficient than a general core at their task, at the cost of flexibility and silicon area.
Choose to treat Moore's law as a planning assumption only for density, and not for cost or speed: transistor counts still rise, but cost per transistor has stopped falling at the leading edge since about 28 nm, and frequency has been flat for fifteen years. So do not plan on a free 2x from the next generation; plan on gains from architecture, memory bandwidth (HBM, chiplets), lower precision (FP8, INT4) and software efficiency. The physical limits that matter near 1 to 2 nm are leakage, variability from a few atoms, wire resistance and heat, not the lithography alone.
interview: Why did CPU clock speeds stop rising around 2005, and how did that change the software you write? || Dennard scaling ended: voltage stopped falling because leakage and the thermal limit on threshold voltage (about 60 mV per decade of subthreshold swing at room temperature), so power density grew with frequency. Chips hit about 100 W/cm², so vendors added cores. Software had to become parallel: threads, async I/O, data-parallel and vectorized code, and attention to cache and memory bandwidth.
A GPU has 200 billion transistors but cannot run all of them at full speed. Why? || Power and heat: dynamic power is capacitance × \( V^2 \) × frequency, and a package can remove only around 700 to 1,000 W. So only a fraction of the chip can switch at once (dark silicon), and designers use clock gating, lower voltage, and specialised units. For me this means performance per watt, not transistor count, is the budget when I choose models, batch sizes and precision.
Why is moving data more expensive than computing on it, in terms of what the chip is made of? || An arithmetic operation switches a few thousand transistors locally (about 1 pJ), while a read from off-chip DRAM costs 100 to 1,000 times more energy because it drives long, high-capacitance wires. Hence caches, HBM next to the die, kernel fusion, quantization, and designs that reuse each loaded byte many times (arithmetic intensity).
When would you pick FPGAs or ASICs instead of a general CPU or GPU for a workload? || When volume and stability justify non-recurring cost: an ASIC costs tens of millions of dollars but gives the best performance per watt for a fixed workload (a TPU, a video encoder); an FPGA gives about a 2 to 10 times efficiency gain over a CPU for streaming or low-latency tasks with no mask cost, but is hard to program and slower than an ASIC. A GPU is the default when the workload is dense and parallel and still changing.
Why do soft errors and silent data corruption matter at fleet scale, and how does the transistor explain them? || Smaller transistors store less charge, so cosmic rays, voltage droop or ageing flip a bit more easily, and with millions of chips even a 10⁻⁵ per-chip-year rate becomes weekly events. I would use ECC memory, checksums end to end, redundancy for critical computation, and periodic fleet screening for chips that return wrong results without crashing.
@@+ mesos
tradeoffs: Choose Mesos-style offers (two-level scheduling) only if you must run several very different frameworks on one pool and each needs its own placement logic; this was true in 2010 to 2016, when Spark, Marathon, Cassandra and Hadoop each had a custom scheduler. Today choose Kubernetes (with operators) for new work: one declarative API, a large ecosystem, and managed offerings (EKS, GKE, AKS). With Mesos you give up the ecosystem and the hiring pool; Mesos is retired at Apache (Attic, 2025) and Spark 4.0 dropped Mesos support, so any new feature, security patch and driver will not come. Staying on it is a migration debt with a deadline, not a choice.
Choose a monolithic scheduler (Kubernetes, YARN, Borg) when one team can own the placement policy, you want global decisions such as preemption, bin-packing and gang scheduling, and you need up to about 5,000 nodes per cluster. Choose the offer model when each framework truly knows its own constraints better than a central scheduler (data locality for a storage system, say), and you can tolerate that a framework sees only what it is offered. The offer model scales to tens of thousands of agents because the master does little work, but locks resources while a framework decides, so one slow scheduler can starve others of 1 to 2 seconds of offers per round, and large jobs struggle to collect enough resources.
Choose to migrate off Mesos by moving the stateless services first (to Kubernetes Deployments), then the batch engines (Spark on Kubernetes, or Databricks and other managed services), and the stateful ones (Cassandra, Kafka) last, using their native replication to move a node at a time. A typical plan takes 6 to 18 months for a few hundred services. A lift-and-shift of Marathon apps to Kubernetes manifests is cheap but carries over bad habits (no health probes, host ports); a rewrite is slower but gives autoscaling and rolling updates. You give up some schedule time and double-running cost (running two clusters for the transition) for fewer outages at the end.
Choose to run Spark on managed services or Kubernetes instead of keeping a standing cluster manager when jobs are bursty: paying per use gives 30 to 60% savings against a 24-hour cluster sized for peak, but you give up the data locality and fast start-up of a warm shared cluster; for a steady 24-hour load, reserved or committed capacity is cheaper. This is the same question Mesos asked in 2009, answered now by the cloud's elastic nodes.
interview: What was two-level scheduling, and why did Kubernetes win despite Mesos scaling to more nodes? || The master only offered resources (agent, CPU, memory) to frameworks, which accepted or declined and chose their own tasks. It scaled well, but each framework had to build deployment, service discovery, autoscaling and health checks itself. Kubernetes gave one declarative API and a controller pattern that everyone could extend (CRDs, operators), so the ecosystem converged there. Scale beyond 5,000 nodes was rarely the real limit; operational uniformity was.
We still run a Mesos cluster with 300 services. How do you plan the exit? || Inventory services by type and state; stand up Kubernetes with equivalent networking, secrets and observability first; migrate stateless services in waves with traffic shifting (weighted DNS or mesh) and rollback; move batch engines to native Kubernetes or managed Spark; move stateful systems last using their own replication. Set a hard decommission date, since double running costs money, and measure success by incidents and cost per service.
How does Dominant Resource Fairness work and why does it matter? || Each framework's share is its largest fraction of any resource (CPU or memory); the allocator offers to the framework with the smallest dominant share. With 9 CPUs and 18 GB, a job needing 1 CPU and 4 GB has a dominant share of memory, another needing 3 CPUs and 1 GB has CPU. It equalizes them. It stops a CPU-heavy tenant from taking all CPUs while a memory-heavy one waits, and it is still the basis of fair sharing in YARN and Kubernetes quota discussions.
Why does the offer model have trouble with a job that needs 1,000 containers at once? || Offers arrive piecemeal, and a framework that holds them while it waits for the rest wastes capacity and may starve others, while declining them means it may never gather enough (hoarding against starvation). A central scheduler can reserve and gang-schedule. Mesos used reservations and quotas as a patch; Kubernetes uses Volcano or Kueue.
@@+ yarn
tradeoffs: Choose YARN when you already run HDFS and Hadoop-era engines (Hive on Tez, MapReduce, Spark, Flink) on dedicated machines and want data-local scheduling with queue-based sharing; choose Kubernetes when the rest of the company runs there, you want one platform for services and batch, or you want to use elastic cloud nodes. YARN is simpler for batch (the ResourceManager and its queues are well understood and a cluster of 5,000 to 10,000 nodes is proven), but you give up container images as a first-class idea (Docker on YARN works but is rarely used), a service ecosystem and autoscaling to zero. Kubernetes costs you gang scheduling, multi-tenant queue semantics and mature fair sharing, which you add with Volcano, Kueue or YuniKorn.
Choose the Capacity Scheduler (guaranteed percentages per queue, with elasticity up to a maximum) when you need predictable shares for named teams, such as etl 60%, adhoc 30%, ml 10%; choose the Fair Scheduler when many users submit ad hoc jobs and you want equal shares over time without hand-written queues. Choose preemption on only if your jobs tolerate losing containers: it returns guaranteed capacity to an under-served queue in seconds to minutes, but kills work that had run for a while, and a Spark job with a long lineage may then recompute for many minutes. Without preemption, a queue can wait an hour behind another queue's long tasks.
Choose container sizes against the node, not against the task: for 128 GB nodes with 120 GB offered to YARN, 22 GB containers waste about 10% of the node in rounding, and a vcore setting that is too high lets CPU-heavy tasks fight for cores because vcores are not enforced unless cgroups are on. Larger containers (16 to 32 GB) cut overhead and allow big joins but reduce parallelism and make scheduling harder to pack; smaller containers (4 to 8 GB) pack well but spill and fail on skewed data. You give up either memory efficiency or fairness; the simple default is to size by the memory of one task partition plus 10 to 20% overhead.
Choose to keep YARN for a stable on-prem Hadoop estate and not to rebuild it; choose to move to cloud-native Spark (Databricks, EMR on EKS, Spark on Kubernetes) when you want storage and compute separated so each scales alone. Moving off removes the cost of running HDFS and the NameNode (a single metadata server with roughly 150 bytes per block of memory, so a billion files needs about 150 GB of heap) and ties you to object-store consistency and listing cost instead. The cost of leaving is the data-locality advantage (remote reads at 1 to 3 GB/s per node instead of local disk) and a rewrite of queue policies and scheduling jobs.
interview: A Spark job on YARN fails with 'Container killed by YARN for exceeding memory limits'. What do you do? || The container's physical memory (heap plus off-heap, Python workers, shuffle buffers) went above what the NodeManager allows. interview: A Hive query that took 5 minutes now takes 40. How do you investigate? || Check the plan first: did a join change from broadcast to shuffle because table statistics were stale or missing (ANALYZE TABLE)? Use the SDK's workflow time, random and side-effect calls, and keep I/O in activities. Changing the order of activities in a new deploy also breaks old histories, so use patching or versioning.

interview: Why would a table with an auto-increment key limit write throughput in TiDB, and what do you do? || Rows with sequential keys land in the last Region, whose Raft leader sits on one node, so one TiKV node does all the writes while others sit idle (a write hotspot). The cost is losing key order, so range scans by insertion order need another index.

Throughput scales out, latency per transaction does not improve.
What is the PD, and what happens if it is slow or unavailable? || The Placement Driver stores cluster metadata, schedules Regions (balancing, splitting) and hands out the global timestamps (TSO). TiDB servers cache timestamps in batches, so short blips are absorbed, but I monitor TSO wait time as a key metric.

How does Scylla's shard-per-core design avoid locks, and what does it cost? || Each core owns a slice of the data, memory and its own I/O queue; cores communicate by explicit message passing rather than shared memory, so there are no locks and few cache misses. How do you replace a failed node, and what do you worry about? || Start a new node with the replace-address option, or with tablets let the cluster rebuild data from replicas; it streams its token ranges from the other replicas while serving traffic.
