value: Frequency capping limits how many times the same person sees the same ad (or campaign) in a given time window, for example "at most 3 times per day, at most 10 times per week". Advertisers ask for it because the 8th exposure of a car ad to the same person is mostly wasted money and often annoys the viewer. Publishers and platforms enforce it because they sell the promise "your budget will reach many different people", not "your budget will hit the same few people again and again".
The mechanism is a counter. For each pair (person, campaign) the system keeps the number of exposures inside the window, in a fast key-value store. Before it bids in an auction or picks an ad to serve, it reads the counter. If the counter has reached the cap, the campaign is removed from the candidate list. After an ad is really shown, the counter is increased. Everything else in the design is about doing this at a very large scale (hundreds of thousands of decisions per second, hundreds of millions of people), and about the fact that the counter is always slightly out of date.
Frequency capping sits between several layers. From below, hashing and hash tables (L3) give the O(1) key lookup that makes a counter per person and campaign affordable. Streaming and sketching algorithms (L3) give compact approximate counters when exact ones would cost too much memory. Rate limiting (L2) gives the counter-with-a-window pattern itself, because a frequency cap is a rate limit per person measured in hours or days instead of requests per second. Identity graphs (L2) tell the system which devices belong to the same person or household, which decides what "the same person" means. The layers above, programmatic advertising (RTB) and server-side ad insertion (SSAI), call the cap check on every bid and every ad break.
terms: Frequency cap — a rule of the form "at most N exposures per window", for example 3 per 24 hours.
Window — the time period over which exposures are counted: a fixed calendar window (today), a rolling window (the last 24 hours), or the whole campaign lifetime.
Exposure (impression) — one confirmed showing of an ad; this is what the counter should count, not the bid or the ad request.
Recency cap — a minimum gap between two exposures, for example no more than one ad from this brand in 30 minutes.
Over-delivery — showing an ad more times than the cap allows, usually because the counter was stale at decision time.
Under-delivery — failing to show ads that were allowed, because the system was too careful (for example it counted a bid that never won).
Pre-bid check — reading the counter before bidding, so the system does not spend money or time on an ineligible ad.
Identity key — the ID that the counter is stored under: cookie, mobile advertising ID, CTV device ID, hashed household ID or a person ID from an identity graph.
history: Frequency capping appeared with the first banner ad networks in the mid-1990s. Early ad servers such as DoubleClick (founded 1995) stored a small cookie in the browser with the number of times a user had seen an ad, so the cap needed no server-side database at all. This was cheap and simple, but it broke when the user deleted cookies, used another browser or changed device.
When real-time bidding (RTB) spread after about 2009, the picture changed. An exchange sends a bid request to many bidders, and each bidder has roughly 100 ms to answer. Now the cap check was done on the bidder side, in a server-side store keyed by cookie ID, and had to finish in a few milliseconds. In-memory key-value stores became the standard tool: Aerospike (built for this workload, with a strong ad-tech user base), Redis, Memcached and Couchbase. Large bidders began to describe their user-profile stores in terms of billions of keys and hundreds of thousands of reads per second.
Two later changes made the problem harder. First, people moved to many devices (phone, laptop, smart TV), so a cap per device is a weak cap per person. This pushed vendors toward identity graphs and household IDs. Connected TV is the extreme case: one TV serves a whole household, and the same ad can reach the same living room on the TV, the phone and the tablet. Second, privacy changes (Apple's App Tracking Transparency in 2021, the long decline of third-party cookies, and laws such as GDPR and CCPA) removed or restricted many stable IDs. Platforms now often cap on a first-party ID, a hashed household ID or a context-only signal, and accept coarser control. The core technique, a windowed counter in a fast store, did not change; what changed is the quality of the key.
how: The first design decision is what to count. The safest unit is a confirmed impression: the beacon or log event that says the ad was really rendered. A bid is not an exposure, because most bids lose the auction. A win notice is closer, but the ad may still fail to render. So the usual pipeline is: the decision service reads the counter, bids, and later the impression event arrives from a stream (for example Kafka) and a consumer increments the counter. This gives an honest count but a delay between the exposure and the counter update, usually a few hundred milliseconds to a few seconds, and in the worst case minutes.
The second decision is the window. A fixed window ("per calendar day") is the cheapest: one key per person, per campaign and per day, such as `fc:{person}:{campaign}:20261026`, with an expiry set at the end of the day. A single atomic increment updates it. Its weakness is the boundary: a user can see 3 ads at 23:59 and 3 more at 00:01. A rolling window ("last 24 hours") avoids this. Two ways to store it are common. A sorted list of exposure timestamps is exact, but it uses memory proportional to the cap, and it needs a trim of old entries on every write. Bucketed counters keep one small counter per hour (24 buckets for a day) and sum the buckets that fall inside the window. Bucketing is approximate at the edge by at most one bucket, and the memory is fixed. This is the same trade-off as in rate limiting (L2): fixed window counter, sliding window log and sliding window counter are the same three designs, with the window stretched from seconds to days. The token bucket from that concept is useful for recency caps: one token refilled every 30 minutes lets a brand through at most once per half hour.
The third decision is the data layout. A bid request for one person may have 20 to 100 candidate campaigns. Reading 100 separate keys costs 100 round trips. So all counters of one person are stored together under one key, a small map from campaign ID to a compact record (count, window start). One read returns everything needed to filter all candidates. The key is hashed (hash tables, L3) to pick a shard, so one person's counters are always on the same node, and the lookup is O(1). With a hash-partitioned store, a read costs well under 1 ms inside a data centre, and the bidder's total budget for the check is often 5 to 10 ms.
The fourth decision is the size of the store. Suppose 200 million active IDs, each with counters for an average of 40 live campaigns, 8 bytes per counter including the campaign ID and window information. That is about 200M × 40 × 8 = 64 GB, which fits in the RAM of a small cluster. Memory pressure comes from long tails: retargeting campaigns with lifetime caps, and IDs that appear once and never return. Expiry is therefore not optional; every key needs a time to live (TTL), set to the longest window of any cap on it. When even this is too large, streaming algorithms (L3) help. A count-min sketch stores approximate counts for many keys in fixed memory but only over-counts, which means the cap errs on the safe side (fewer ads than allowed). For a cap of exactly 1 ("show once"), a Bloom filter of seen IDs is enough and costs about 10 bits per user for a 1% error.
The fifth decision is consistency, and this is where frequency capping differs from a simple rate limiter. A bidder runs in several regions, and a person's counter is updated in the region that served the ad. Replication across regions takes tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Even inside one region, the impression event takes time to arrive. So at the moment of a decision the counter can be stale, and two simultaneous requests for the same person can both see "2 of 3 used" and both win. The system does not try to remove this error with distributed locks, because a lock on the hot path would cost more than the error. It accepts bounded over-delivery and reduces it in cheap ways: write the counter in the same region as the decision, count at bid time in a separate short-lived "pending" counter that expires after a few seconds if no impression arrives, and treat a cap of N as a cap of N − 1 for people who are in a burst. Advertisers usually accept 1% to 3% over-delivery; the contract language often says the cap is "best effort".
Last, the identity key. A counter is only as good as the ID it is stored under. If the same person has a phone ID, a TV ID and a hashed email, three counters exist, and each one sees only a part of the exposures. An identity graph (L2) links these IDs to one person or household node. The cap check first resolves the device ID to a node, then reads the counter under the node key. The cost is an extra lookup and a probabilistic link: a wrong link either merges two strangers (they share a cap) or misses a real link (the cap leaks). In practice platforms choose the key by channel: household for CTV, device for mobile app, person for logged-in publishers.

example: Take a cap of 3 exposures per rolling 24 hours for campaign C2, with hourly buckets. A person (node N42) has these confirmed impressions: 2 at 09:00, 1 at 14:00 today. The stored record is buckets {09h: 2, 14h: 1}. At 20:00 a bid request arrives. The service sums buckets inside the last 24 hours: 2 + 1 = 3. This equals the cap, so C2 is removed from the candidate list, and the bidder bids with other campaigns. No ad of C2 is shown.
At 09:30 the next day, the 09h bucket from yesterday is 24.5 hours old and falls outside the window, so the sum is only 1 (the 14h bucket). C2 is eligible again. A fixed daily window would have reset this person at midnight instead. With hourly buckets the error is at most one hour at the window edge: the bucket from 09:00 would still be counted at 09:30 if we included whole buckets that overlap the window. Many systems choose to count a bucket if any part of it is inside the window, which errs on the side of showing fewer ads.
Now the stale-counter case. Say the count is 2 of 3, and two bid requests for N42 arrive within 50 ms: one for a phone app, one for the TV. Both read 2, both pass the check, both bid, and both win. When the two impression events arrive, the counter reaches 4. One ad too many was shown: over-delivery of 1 out of 3, or 33% for this person. Is it a problem at scale? Suppose 1% of people are in a burst like this. Then total over-delivery is about 0.33 × 1% ≈ 0.3% of exposures, which is small. To reduce it further, the service can write a pending marker at bid time: `INCR` a key `pend:N42:C2` with a 5-second TTL, and count the sum (confirmed + pending) in the next decision. The second request now sees 3 and drops C2. The price is under-delivery: if the first bid loses the auction (typical win rates are 10% to 30%), the person is blocked for up to 5 seconds without having seen the ad. It is a small price for most campaigns.

intuition: A bouncer with a clipboard at a club door who ticks a mark next to your name each time you enter, and turns you away after the third mark.
The idea is natural because the rule is local and simple: "how many times has this person seen this ad lately?" is a question about one small piece of state, and the answer can be found with one key lookup. Nothing needs global agreement, since a wrong answer costs a little money and does not break correctness. This is why the design uses the cheapest possible tools (counters, hash tables, TTLs) and accepts that the counter is a little late. The hard part is not the counter; it is knowing who "this person" is, which is why identity sits beside the counter in the design.
mistakes: People often think frequency capping needs strong consistency, like a bank balance. In fact it needs the opposite: speed. A decision that takes 50 ms because of a lock misses the auction deadline, and an auction that is not answered earns nothing. A stale counter costs a few percent of over-delivery, which advertisers tolerate, so the design chooses availability and low latency over exact counts.
People often think the cap is checked after the ad is chosen. In fact it must be checked before the bid (a pre-bid filter), because bidding for an ad you may not show wastes the bid, and winning an auction for an ineligible ad wastes real money. A second check at serve time is useful for ad servers that choose among already-won ads, but it does not replace the first.
People often think counting bid requests or wins is good enough. In fact only rendered impressions match what the advertiser pays for and what the viewer saw. Counting wins over-counts when ads fail to render, which under-delivers the campaign. Counting bids is far worse: with a 10% win rate, a cap of 3 would be reached after about 30 bids, and the campaign would stop almost at once.
People often think a cap per device is a cap per person. In fact a person with a phone, a laptop and a TV has three device IDs, so a cap of 3 per device allows up to 9 exposures. The only fix is to key the counter on a person or household ID from an identity graph, and that link is itself uncertain.
tradeoffs: Choose counting at impression time when the advertiser is billed per impression and exact counts matter; the counter lags by seconds, so you accept some over-delivery. Choose counting at bid time (or pending counters) when the cap is strict, for example a brand-safety or legal limit; you accept under-delivery whenever bids are lost, which at a 15% win rate means most reservations are wasted for the few seconds they live.
Choose a fixed window when the product only needs "per day" and you want one atomic increment and the lowest memory; you give up smoothness at the boundary (up to twice the cap across midnight). Choose bucketed rolling windows when users notice bursts and the cap is part of a brand promise; you pay with more memory per person (24 buckets instead of 1) and a small read cost to sum them. Use an exact timestamp log only for tiny caps where the number of entries is at most the cap.
Choose exact counters in a key-value store when the number of keys fits in RAM (a few hundred GB across the cluster) and the cap is small. Choose a count-min sketch or Bloom filter when the key space is huge (billions of IDs) and the cap is 1 to 3; the sketch only over-counts, so you lose a little delivery and never break the cap, and you gain a fixed, small memory budget.
Choose household or person keys when the channel is shared or multi-device (CTV, logged-in publisher); you gain real person-level control but depend on the quality of the identity graph and its privacy rules. Choose device keys when the ID is the only stable one you have; you get a simple and fast system with a weaker cap.
friction: The first limit is the counter's age. Whatever the design, there is a gap between an exposure and the moment all regions know about it. During this gap, any number of parallel decisions can pass. Burst traffic is the worst case: a live sports stream starts an ad break on millions of TVs at the same second, and many requests for the same household can arrive together because several devices in the home are streaming.
The second limit is identity. A household may contain several people with different interests, and a TV cannot tell who is watching. Cross-device linking is probabilistic, and in many markets it is restricted by consent. When the ID is missing, which is now common on iOS and in the browser, the system cannot cap at all, or it caps only by session or by context. Advertisers then see higher effective frequency than they asked for. Industry reports often show that a small share of users receive a large share of impressions, which is the sign of caps that did not work.
The third limit is cross-system capping. The same advertiser can buy the same person through several platforms (a DSP, a CTV publisher, a social network). Each platform caps only its own exposures, so the person's total frequency is the sum. Closing this gap needs a shared ID or a clean room, and neither is complete today.
practice: Start from the product rule and write it down exactly: unit (impression), window (rolling 24 hours or calendar day), key (device or household), and the allowed error (for example up to 3% over-delivery). Then pick the cheapest design that meets it. For most systems this is one record per person with small hourly buckets, a TTL equal to the longest window, and a single batched read per decision.
Count from a deduplicated impression stream. Give every impression event an ID and make the updater idempotent. Keep the pre-bid read path separate from the write path: the decision service only reads, and a stream consumer writes. This keeps latency low and makes it easy to replay events after a bug.
Protect the hot path. Set a hard time budget on the counter read (for example 5 ms). If the store is slow or down, decide the failure mode in advance: "fail open" (bid anyway, accept over-delivery) is usually right for revenue, but "fail closed" is right for strict legal limits. Cache recent reads per request so one bid request that evaluates 50 campaigns does not read the store 50 times.
Watch the right metrics: the share of people whose exposures exceed the cap in the final logs (the real over-delivery), the age of the counter at decision time, the p99 read latency, store memory and eviction count, and the share of requests with no usable ID. Review the over-delivery number every week against the contract, and test with a replay: feed a day of logs into the counting logic offline and compare with the live result.
For CTV and SSAI, remember that ads are decided for a whole pod before the viewer sees any of them. Reserve the exposures for the pod together and reject the same creative twice in one pod. For ACR-based audience data and household-level graphs, document which ID is used for the cap and tell the advertiser plainly what level of person-level control they get.

value: A double-entry ledger is the way software keeps money correct. Every movement of money is written as a transaction with at least two entries: one debits an account, one credits another, and the total of debits always equals the total of credits. Money is never created or destroyed by a single line; it only moves from one account to another. A balance is not a number you overwrite. It is the sum of all entries on that account, and the entries are never edited or deleted. If something was wrong, you add a new entry that reverses it.
This design exists because the cost of a wrong balance is high and the causes of wrongness are many: a crash between two updates, a retried request that pays twice, a bank file that disagrees with your records, a bug that was found three months later. A table with one `balance` column and `UPDATE balance = balance - 100` cannot answer "why is this number what it is?" or "what was it last Tuesday?". A ledger answers both by construction, because the history is the data.
Reconciliation is the second half. Your ledger is your own opinion about where the money is. Banks, card networks and payment processors hold their own records, and the two sides must agree. Every day (or every hour) you compare the ledger with the external statements, find the differences (breaks), and explain each one. A ledger without reconciliation can be perfectly consistent and still wrong about the world.
It stands on four layers below. ACID transactions (L3) make the balanced entries land together or not at all. The relational model (L3) gives constraints, keys and joins to check them. Idempotence (L2) lets a retried payment request change the books once. The write-ahead log (L2) is the same append-only idea inside the database, and the ledger copies it at the business level. Above it, event sourcing (L1) generalises the pattern, and blockchains (L1) are ledgers replicated without a central owner.
terms: Account — a named container whose balance is the sum of entries posted to it, such as "customer wallet 42" or "cash at bank".
Entry (posting) — one line of a transaction: an account, a direction (debit or credit) and an amount.
Transaction (journal entry) — a group of entries written atomically whose debits equal its credits.
Debit and credit — the two directions of an entry. Which one increases a balance depends on the account type: debits increase assets and expenses, credits increase liabilities, equity and revenue.
Balance — the sum of an account's entries (credits minus debits for a liability account); it is derived, not edited.
Reversal — a new transaction that exactly cancels an earlier one; it is how mistakes are corrected without erasing history.
Reconciliation — comparing the ledger with an external record (bank statement, processor report) and explaining every difference.
Break — a difference found during reconciliation: a missing item, a duplicate, an amount mismatch or a timing difference.
Suspense (clearing) account — a temporary account that holds money whose final place is not yet known, so that every transaction still balances.
history: Double-entry bookkeeping is old. Merchants in northern Italy used it by the 13th and 14th centuries. The first printed description is by Luca Pacioli, a Franciscan friar and mathematician, in his "Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et proportionalita" (Venice, 1494). He calls it the Venetian method. It spread because a balanced book has a built-in check: if the two sides do not match, there is an error somewhere, and one can look for it.
Computers took the idea over in the 1960s and 1970s. Banks and ERP systems stored postings in relational tables, and the invariant "debits equal credits" became a database constraint or a nightly batch check. The relational model and ACID transactions (Codd 1970, Gray 1981) made this possible, because a transfer between two accounts is the standard example of a transaction.
The web changed the problem. Payment systems at companies such as Stripe, PayPal, Square and Uber needed to handle millions of small transfers across many partners (card networks, banks, wallets), and requests arrived over unreliable networks and were retried. This brought the idempotency key (for example the `Idempotency-Key` header in Stripe's API) into every payment API. Around 2020 a group of purpose-built products appeared: Modern Treasury and Formance (ledger services), and TigerBeetle (started around 2020), an open-source database written in Zig whose only job is debits and credits, designed for very high rates on a small hot set of accounts. Large companies described their own ledgers in engineering blogs (Stripe, Airbnb, Square, Uber, Lyft), and the common design is the same: immutable entries, a balance invariant, idempotent writes and daily reconciliation.
how: Start with the data model. Three tables are enough. `accounts` has an ID, a currency, a type (asset, liability, equity, revenue, expense) and metadata. `transactions` has an ID, a unique idempotency key, a timestamp and a description. `entries` has a transaction ID, an account ID, a direction (or a signed amount) and an amount in the smallest currency unit (cents, paise), stored as an integer. Floating point is never used for money, because 0.1 + 0.2 is not 0.3 in binary and errors accumulate over millions of rows. The currency is part of the account, and the invariant is checked per currency: a transaction that converts dollars to euros has four entries (two per currency, through a conversion account), so each currency still balances.
The central rule is an invariant: for every transaction, the sum of signed entry amounts is zero. It holds in three layers. The application builds the transaction through one function that refuses unbalanced input. The database enforces it with a deferred constraint or a trigger that checks the sum at commit. And a background audit recomputes the sum over all entries and alerts if it is not zero. Because the sum over the whole ledger is zero by construction, a single missing line shows up immediately as a non-zero total, which is exactly the property Pacioli's merchants relied on.
Atomicity comes from ACID transactions (L3). The transaction row and all its entries are inserted in one database transaction. A crash between the first and the second entry leaves nothing, because the write-ahead log (L2) rolls the partial transaction back on recovery. Isolation matters for balance checks: if a rule says "a wallet cannot go below zero", the check and the insert must happen under a lock on that account row (`SELECT ... FOR UPDATE`) or in a serializable transaction, otherwise two concurrent withdrawals both see enough money.
Idempotence (L2) protects against retries. The client sends a unique key with each payment request, and the `transactions` table has a unique index on it. The first request inserts the transaction. A retry (after a timeout, a duplicate webhook, a user double-click) hits the unique index, and the service returns the stored result instead of posting again. The key must be scoped (per client or per operation type) and kept as long as retries can happen, often 24 hours to many days. The unique index does the hard work, so the guarantee does not depend on application code being careful.
Balances can be computed two ways. A pure derived balance (`SELECT sum(amount) FROM entries WHERE account_id = ?`) is always correct but gets slower as entries grow. So systems keep a materialized balance, updated in the same transaction as the entries, plus periodic checkpoints (the balance as of a date) so the query only sums the entries since the last checkpoint. The materialized value is a cache. The entries stay the source of truth, and a job compares the two. This is the same idea as a snapshot over a write-ahead log: the log is the truth, and the snapshot is a speed-up.
Money often takes time to settle, so real ledgers model the pending state. A card authorization first creates a pending transaction that reserves the amount (the available balance falls, the posted balance does not). Later it is either posted (the money moves) or voided (the reservation is released). Two-phase transfers of this kind are built into TigerBeetle and ledger services as a primitive. The ledger records what you know at each moment, and does not guess the final result.
Reconciliation then compares your records with the outside world. For each external source (a bank statement file, a processor settlement report, a card network clearing file), a job loads the external lines and matches them to ledger entries using a shared reference (a processor transaction ID, a bank reference) and, when none exists, by amount, date window and counterparty. Matched items are marked. Unmatched items on either side are breaks. Typical causes are timing (the bank posts tomorrow), fees netted from a payout, duplicates, rounding in currency conversion, and real errors. Each break is explained by an adjusting transaction or investigated. Until it is understood, it sits in a suspense account, so the books still balance and the gap is visible. A three-way match (your ledger, the processor report, the bank statement) finds which party is wrong. Good teams track the number and age of open breaks as a health metric.

  merchant_payable = 100.00 - 3.20 - 96.80 = 0   <- settled

  daily reconciliation:
  ledger cash_at_bank  <--- compare --->  bank statement
  ledger receivable    <--- compare --->  processor report
  difference -> break -> suspense account -> investigate -> adjust
example: Follow one card payment of 100.00 for a merchant, with a fee of 2.9% plus 0.30. The fee is 2.90 + 0.30 = 3.20. Use integer cents: 10000, 320 and 9680.
Step 1: the charge succeeds. Post transaction T1: debit `receivable_from_processor` (an asset: the processor owes us the money) 10000, credit `merchant_payable` (a liability: we owe the merchant) 10000. Debits equal credits.
Step 2: apply the fee. T2: debit `merchant_payable` 320, credit `revenue_fees` 320. Now `merchant_payable` has a credit balance of 10000 − 320 = 9680.
Step 3: next day the processor settles the gross amount into our bank. T3: debit `cash_at_bank` 10000, credit `receivable_from_processor` 10000. The receivable is now zero.
Step 4: pay the merchant. T4: debit `merchant_payable` 9680, credit `cash_at_bank` 9680. The payable is zero and the bank account holds the 320 we earned.
Step 5: the retry. The client timed out at T1 and sends the same request again with the same idempotency key `pay_8f2`. The unique index rejects the second insert, and the service returns T1. The books show one charge, not two.
Step 6: reconciliation. The bank statement shows 10000 received from the processor. The ledger shows the same, so they match. Now suppose the statement shows 9990. That is a break of 10 cents. The job moves 10 into the suspense account so that the books stay balanced and the gap is visible. An analyst finds that the processor deducted a small fee that we did not model. The fix is an adjusting transaction: debit `processing_costs` 10, credit `suspense` 10, and a new rule so that the fee is booked automatically next time.
Step 7: a mistake. T2 used the wrong fee rate and charged 3.50. We do not edit T2. We post T5 reversing it (debit `revenue_fees` 350, credit `merchant_payable` 350) and T6 with the right fee. An auditor can see the error, the correction and the time of both. Every transaction sums to zero, so the whole ledger sums to zero.
intuition: A ledger is a diary of what happened, kept so carefully that the balance can always be re-derived from it, and checked each day against what other people wrote down about you.
The design is natural because money has a conservation law. Like energy in physics, it moves and never appears from nothing, and the two-sided entry is that law written in data. Once every change is a balanced pair, a whole class of bugs becomes visible as a non-zero sum, and an append-only history means no bug can hide the past. Immutability also fits how distributed systems fail: a retry, a replay or a crash can only add entries, never half-edit one, and the unique key makes the adding safe. Reconciliation completes the picture, because your records can be internally consistent and still disagree with the bank, and only comparing with an independent record catches that.
mistakes: People often think a ledger is just a transactions table. In fact the difference is the invariant. A transactions table with one amount per row can lose money silently. A double-entry ledger requires every transaction to balance, so a missing or duplicated half is detectable by a simple sum.
People often think you can fix a wrong entry with an UPDATE or DELETE. In fact entries are immutable. An edit destroys the evidence, breaks audit trails and the checkpoints built on the old data, and makes it impossible to explain a number that was reported to someone last week. The correct fix is a reversal plus a new entry.
People often think "debit" means money out and "credit" means money in. In fact the meaning depends on the account. A credit increases a liability (the money you hold for a customer) and a debit increases an asset (cash at the bank). Your bank "credits" your account when you deposit, because for the bank your balance is a liability. Read debit and credit as left and right sides, not as good and bad.
People often think a database transaction is enough to prevent double payments. In fact a transaction protects one attempt. A retry is a new attempt, and without an idempotency key it will post again. Both are needed: the transaction for atomicity, the unique key for exactly-once effect.
People sometimes think reconciliation is an accounting task done after the system is built. In fact it is part of the system's correctness. External systems fail, delay and change, and without a daily comparison your ledger drifts away from reality without any error being raised. Teams that skip it find the problem when a bank or an auditor does.
tradeoffs: Choose a relational database (PostgreSQL, MySQL) for the ledger when your volume is up to a few thousand transfers per second and you want SQL, constraints, joins and familiar operations. Choose a purpose-built ledger database (TigerBeetle) when you have extreme throughput on a few hot accounts (a platform fee account touched by every payment); you gain batching and a data model built for debits and credits, and you give up general queries, a mature ecosystem and flexibility.
Choose a derived balance (sum of entries) when the account has few entries or when correctness matters more than speed. Choose a materialized balance with checkpoints when reads are frequent or accounts have millions of entries; you gain O(1) reads and pay with a hot row. A single hot row can only be updated by one transaction at a time, so it limits throughput to a few thousand updates per second. If that is too low, split the account into N sub-accounts (shards) and sum them for reads, or post to a hot account in batches.
Choose strict checks at write time (no negative balance, enforced under a lock) for customer wallets, where overdraft is a real loss. Choose permissive posting with later detection for internal accounts, where blocking a legitimate transaction is worse than a temporary imbalance, and compensate afterwards. The first costs latency and contention; the second costs risk.
Choose synchronous reconciliation for high-value or high-risk flows (large transfers, payouts), where a break must be caught before money leaves. Choose daily batch reconciliation for the bulk of card and bank flows, because external files arrive once a day anyway. Real-time matching costs more engineering and still waits for the partner's data.
Choose explicit conversion accounts, with each currency balancing on its own, when you handle many currencies; you accept more accounts and more entries per transaction, and you gain the guarantee that each currency balances on its own and that foreign-exchange gains and losses are visible.
friction: Hot accounts are the first practical limit. Every payment touches the same fee, tax or settlement account, and row locks serialize them. Sharding the account or batching reduces the contention, but it complicates reads and the "no negative balance" rule. Another limit is long-running accounts: a merchant with 100 million entries needs checkpoints and archiving, or every balance query degrades.
Modelling is a real difficulty. A good chart of accounts needs thought: which events create a liability, how to represent holds, refunds, chargebacks, disputes, taxes and multi-party splits (a marketplace pays seller, courier and platform from one charge). Changing the model later means migrating history, and since history is immutable, you do it with new entries and mappings, not edits. Backdated entries (a bank tells you on the 10th about a payment made on the 3rd) raise the question of whether a closed period can change. Accounting periods are usually closed, and late items go into the current period with an adjustment note.
Reconciliation never reaches exactly zero effort. Partners send files in different formats, change them without notice, deliver them late, omit references and net items together. Matching rules that rely on amounts and dates produce wrong matches when many items have the same amount. Breaks must be assigned an owner and an age, or they pile up and become the normal state.
Finally, a ledger does not make bad business rules good. It records faithfully that you paid a fraudster. It guarantees conservation and traceability, not that each transfer should have happened. Controls such as limits, approvals and fraud checks sit around it. Distributed setups add one more issue: if the ledger is split across services or databases, you need sagas or two-phase commit to keep a transfer atomic, and each approach has its own failure cases.
practice: Model money as integer minor units and store the currency with the account. Write one library function that creates a transaction from a list of entries, checks that they sum to zero per currency, and inserts everything in one database transaction. All code paths use it. Add a database constraint as a second guard, and a periodic job that checks that the sum of all entries per currency is zero.
Put a unique idempotency key on the transaction and return the stored result on a repeat. Keep entries append-only: revoke UPDATE and DELETE permissions on the entries table for the application user. For corrections, write reversals. Store a reference to the cause on every transaction (order ID, partner reference, user ID), because this is what makes reconciliation and customer support possible.
Write queries that you will need on a bad day. For example, a trial balance and a check for unbalanced transactions:
```sql
-- every transaction must sum to zero per currency
SELECT e.transaction_id, a.currency, sum(e.signed_amount) AS diff
FROM entries e JOIN accounts a ON a.id = e.account_id
GROUP BY e.transaction_id, a.currency
HAVING sum(e.signed_amount) <> 0;
```
Build reconciliation as a pipeline: ingest the external file as-is (keep the raw file), normalize into a table, match by reference first and by rules second, write the unmatched items to a breaks table with an age and an owner, and alert on breaks older than your limit (often 2 to 3 business days). Keep clearing and suspense accounts for in-flight money, and make them go to zero over time; a suspense balance that grows is the main warning sign.
Use a purpose-built ledger or a managed ledger service if you do not want to design this yourself, but keep your own copy of the key facts. Whichever you choose, test the failure cases: crash in the middle of a transaction, duplicate request, partner file with a missing line, backdated entry.
interview: Design the ledger for a wallet product where users top up, pay merchants and withdraw to a bank. What are your tables and invariants? || Accounts (user wallets, merchant payable, cash at bank, fees, suspense), transactions with a unique idempotency key, and entries in integer minor units. The invariant is that each transaction sums to zero per currency, enforced in the library, by a deferred constraint and by a periodic audit. Wallet balances are materialized in the same database transaction, with a lock for the no-overdraft check.
A payment request times out and the client retries. How do you avoid charging twice? || A unique idempotency key on the transaction, with the unique index doing the check. The first request commits the transaction; the retry hits the index and the service returns the stored result. The key is scoped to the client and operation, kept for the retry window, and the request body is compared to detect key reuse with different content.
One account receives a payment in every transaction and the row lock limits throughput. What do you do? || Split the account into N shards chosen by hash or round robin, post each transaction to one shard, and compute the total by summing shards. Alternatively batch many postings into one update, or use a ledger engine built for hot accounts. The cost is a more complex read path and a harder no-negative-balance rule on that account.
Your daily reconciliation shows the bank statement is 5,000 higher than the ledger. How do you find the cause? || Put it in suspense and bisect. Match line by line using references first; list unmatched bank items and unmatched ledger items. Check timing (items posted on the bank's next day), duplicates, fees netted from payouts, and currency rounding. The three-way match with the processor report shows which side is wrong. Fix it with an adjusting entry and add a rule to prevent a repeat.
Why not use event sourcing with a normal table of balances and replay the events? || A ledger is event sourcing with a strict domain invariant: balanced entries and a conservation law. Plain event sourcing allows any event. The ledger adds the sum-to-zero check, account types and the reconciliation to external records, which make errors detectable instead of only replayable.
How would you correct a wrong entry that was already reported in last month's statement? || Do not edit. Post a reversal and a corrected entry dated in the current open period (or follow the rules for reopening a period if the business requires it), reference the original transaction, and record the reason. The original statement stays reproducible.
quiz: What invariant does a double-entry ledger enforce, and how does it help find bugs? || Each transaction's debits equal its credits, so the sum of all entries is zero. A lost or duplicated entry makes the sum non-zero, so the error shows up immediately.
Why are ledger entries never updated, and how do you correct a mistake? || Updates destroy the audit trail and the basis for earlier reports. You post a reversing transaction and a new correct one, so history shows both the error and its fix.
How does an idempotency key prevent a double charge when a request is retried? || The key is stored with a unique index on the transaction. The first request inserts it; the retry violates the unique index, so the service returns the stored result instead of posting again.
A payment of 250.00 has a fee of 2.9% + 0.30. What are the fee and merchant net in cents, and what entries record the fee? || Fee = 725 + 30 = 755 cents (7.55). Net = 25000 − 755 = 24245. Entry: debit merchant_payable 755, credit revenue_fees 755.
When would you choose a materialized balance over summing entries, and what is the risk? || When reads are frequent or entries are many, since it gives O(1) reads. The risk is a hot row that limits write throughput and a cache that could drift, so a job should compare it with the sum of entries.

@@ designpatterns
value: A design pattern is a named, tested way to arrange a few classes or functions so that one kind of change stays small. "Strategy" says: put the part that varies behind an interface, so you can swap it without editing the code that uses it. "Observer" says: let the owner of some state publish changes, and let anyone subscribe, so the owner does not need to know who is listening. The names matter as much as the shapes. When a reviewer writes "this should be a Strategy", a whole design discussion fits in three words.
SOLID is the companion set of five principles that explain why the patterns work: single responsibility, open/closed, Liskov substitution, interface segregation and dependency inversion. Together they say one thing. Code changes for a few reasons, so group together what changes together, separate what changes separately, and let parts depend on stable abstractions, not on concrete details. When you follow this, a new requirement ("support a new payment provider", "add one more pacing rule") means adding a class, not editing ten files.
This is useful in large, long-lived code bases, where the cost is not writing code but changing it safely. It is also a shared vocabulary for teams: Spring, Java's standard library, Kafka's client interfaces, React and most frameworks are built from these shapes, so knowing them lets you read unfamiliar code faster.
The layers below supply the parts. Object-oriented programming (L3) gives objects, encapsulation and polymorphism. Type systems (L3) give interfaces and generics, which are the "stable abstractions". Dynamic dispatch (L3) is the mechanism that actually calls the right implementation behind an interface. Closures (L3) show that many patterns, such as Strategy and Observer, shrink to passing a function. Software engineering principles (L3) supply the goal: low coupling, high cohesion, and cheap change. Above, microservices and architectural styles (hexagonal, layered) apply the same ideas at the scale of services.
terms: Pattern — a named solution to a problem that keeps coming back, with the situation, the structure and the trade-offs described.
Interface — a contract that lists operations without saying how they are done; callers depend on it, not on the implementation.
Coupling — how much one part must know about another; high coupling means a change in one forces changes in the other.
Cohesion — how closely the things inside one module belong together; high cohesion means one clear purpose.
Strategy — a family of interchangeable algorithms behind one interface, chosen at run time.
Observer — a one-to-many link in which a subject notifies its subscribers when its state changes.
Decorator — a wrapper that has the same interface as the object it wraps and adds behaviour before or after the call.
Dependency inversion — high-level code depends on an abstraction that it owns, and low-level details implement that abstraction.
history: The idea of patterns comes from the architect Christopher Alexander, whose book "A Pattern Language" (1977) described recurring solutions for towns and buildings. In 1987 Kent Beck and Ward Cunningham applied it to software at the OOPSLA conference, writing small patterns for Smalltalk user interfaces.
The turning point was the 1994 book "Design Patterns: Elements of Reusable Object-Oriented Software" by Erich Gamma, Richard Helm, Ralph Johnson and John Vlissides, known as the Gang of Four (GoF). It catalogued 23 patterns in three groups: creational (Factory Method, Abstract Factory, Builder, Singleton, Prototype), structural (Adapter, Bridge, Composite, Decorator, Facade, Flyweight, Proxy) and behavioural (Strategy, Observer, State, Command, Iterator, Template Method and others). The examples were in C++ and Smalltalk, and the main advice was "program to an interface, not an implementation" and "favour object composition over class inheritance". The book came when C++ and then Java were spreading and many teams were building large object-oriented systems for the first time.
SOLID has a separate history. Bertrand Meyer stated the open/closed principle in "Object-Oriented Software Construction" (1988). Barbara Liskov described the substitution principle in a 1987 talk, later formalised with Jeannette Wing (1994). Robert C. Martin collected and named several principles in his writing around 2000, and the acronym SOLID was proposed by Michael Feathers in about 2004.
The reaction came in two forms. In 1996 Peter Norvig showed that 16 of the 23 patterns are invisible or much simpler in a language with first-class functions and dynamic types, such as Lisp or Dylan. Java 8 (2014) added lambdas, and many Java Strategy and Observer classes turned into one-line functions. And critics noted that patterns can be overused, producing "enterprise" code with a factory for every class. Today the patterns are read as vocabulary and trade-offs, not as templates to copy.
how: The mechanism behind almost every pattern is the same: indirection through an interface, plus composition. Take the caller `A` that needs some behaviour. Instead of calling a concrete class `B`, `A` holds a reference to an interface `I`. At run time any object that implements `I` can be given to `A`. This uses dynamic dispatch (L3): the call `i.run()` looks up the real method in the object's virtual table (vtable) and jumps to it. The cost is one extra pointer read and an indirect call, a few nanoseconds, and sometimes a lost inlining chance (which the JIT often recovers with inline caches when only one or two types are seen). The gain is that `A` no longer changes when a new implementation of `I` is added.
Strategy is this idea in its plain form. A `Pacer` interface has one method `allowed(campaign, now)`. Implementations are `EvenPacer`, `AsapPacer`, `BudgetCurvePacer`. The campaign engine holds a `Pacer` and calls it. Adding a pacer touches one new class and one line where it is chosen. Without the pattern, the engine has an `if type == ...` chain, and every new type edits the engine and risks the old branches. With a closure (L3), the same pattern is a function value: `pacer = lambda c, t: ...`. This is why the GoF classes look heavy in languages that have first-class functions.
Observer inverts a dependency in the other direction. A `Campaign` object holds a list of listeners and calls `listener.on_change(event)` when its budget changes. The campaign does not know whether the listener is a cache invalidator, a metrics counter or an audit log. New listeners are added without editing `Campaign`. The same shape appears as event emitters, callbacks, UI bindings (React state subscriptions, Android LiveData), and message topics. The cost is that control flow becomes implicit: to know what happens when a budget changes, you must find all subscribers. There are also subtle failures: a listener that throws, a listener that changes the subject during notification, and subscribers that are never removed and so leak memory.
Decorator uses composition to add behaviour in layers. A `RetryingClient` implements the same interface as `HttpClient` and wraps one. A `LoggingClient` wraps that. Calls pass through the stack, and each layer does its part. Java's `BufferedInputStream` over `FileInputStream`, middleware chains in web servers, and gRPC interceptors are decorators. Factory and Builder deal with creation: they hide which concrete class is made and with what settings, so the caller depends only on the interface. State puts the behaviour of each state in its own object, replacing a large `switch` on a status field. Adapter converts one interface into another, and is the pattern that lets a third-party SDK appear as your own `PaymentGateway` interface. Facade gives one simple entry point to a complicated subsystem.
SOLID states the rules behind these shapes. Single responsibility: a class should have one reason to change; a class that parses files, validates records and writes to a database changes for three different reasons. Open/closed: add behaviour by adding code, not by editing tested code; Strategy and Decorator are the standard means. Liskov substitution: a subtype must work wherever its parent works, which means it keeps the promises of the parent's contract (it accepts at least what the parent accepts, and returns at most what the parent returns). A `Square` that extends `Rectangle` and breaks `setWidth` is the classic violation. Interface segregation: many small interfaces beat one large one, so a client is not forced to depend on methods it does not use. Dependency inversion: the high-level policy (the campaign engine) owns the interface (`EventSink`), and the low-level detail (a Kafka producer) implements it, so the arrow of source-code dependency points from detail to policy. The type system (L3) checks that every implementation satisfies the interface.
The tool that ties these together is testing. Because the engine depends on `EventSink`, a test passes a fake sink that records events in a list. No broker is needed, the test runs in milliseconds, and it checks the behaviour of the engine alone. Much of the practical value of the patterns is that they put seams (places where you can substitute a part) at the right places.

example: Start with a campaign engine that decides how fast to spend a budget. The first version has this method:
```python
def allowed_spend(c, now):
    if c.pacing == "even":
        return c.budget * elapsed_share(c, now)
    elif c.pacing == "asap":
        return c.budget
    else:
        raise ValueError(c.pacing)
```
A product owner asks for a third mode, "curve", which follows a traffic curve. Then a fourth, for sports events. Each time, three things happen: this function is edited, its tests are re-run for all branches, and a typo in one branch can break the other modes. The method also has two reasons to change (the rules of each mode, and the choice among modes), which breaks single responsibility.
Now apply Strategy. Define the interface and one class per mode, and keep a registry that maps names to objects:
```python
class Pacer:                       # the abstraction the engine owns
    def allowed(self, c, now): ...

class EvenPacer(Pacer):
    def allowed(self, c, now):
        return c.budget * elapsed_share(c, now)

class AsapPacer(Pacer):
    def allowed(self, c, now):
        return c.budget

PACERS = {"even": EvenPacer(), "asap": AsapPacer()}

def allowed_spend(c, now):
    return PACERS[c.pacing].allowed(c, now)
```
Adding "curve" is one new class and one entry in `PACERS`. The engine and the old tests are untouched: that is open/closed. In Python, the interface could even be a plain function, `PACERS = {"even": lambda c, t: ...}`, which is the closure version of the same idea.
Count the effect on a team. Suppose 8 new modes arrive in a year. In the `if` version, the same function is edited 8 times, with 8 merge conflicts if two people work at once, and the test file grows by one branch each time. In the Strategy version, 8 new files are added, and each has its own test. The price is 3 extra types now and one level of indirection when reading the code. For two modes that never grow, the `if` version is simpler, and you should keep it. The rule of three is a good guide: the first time write it directly, the second time copy with care, the third time make the abstraction, when you can see what really varies.
Last, a dependency inversion example. The engine publishes a spend event whenever a campaign spends. If it calls `KafkaProducer.send(...)` directly, every test needs a broker or a mock of the Kafka class. If the engine defines `class EventSink: def publish(self, event): ...` and receives one in its constructor, then production passes `KafkaSink`, and tests pass `ListSink`, a class with a list. A test that checks "spending 10 produces one event with amount 10" now takes about one millisecond and has no network.
intuition: A power socket: the appliance and the wall agree on the shape of the plug, so you can change the lamp, the heater or the wiring behind the wall without redesigning the other side.
Software changes in the places where requirements are unsure: the rules, the vendors, the formats. Patterns put an interface at exactly those places, so the uncertain part is a plug and the stable part is a socket. This is natural because it is the same move that engineers use in every other field: find the seam where changes arrive, and standardise the shape of the connection there. SOLID tells you where such seams belong, and the patterns are the common ways to build them.
mistakes: People often think design patterns are recipes to apply wherever possible. In fact they are solutions to specific pressures, and each one adds classes, indirection and names to learn. A pattern used without the pressure (a Factory for a class that has one implementation) makes code longer and harder to read, and gives nothing back.
People often think SOLID means making many small classes and interfaces. In fact the principles are about reasons to change and direction of dependencies, not about count. A class with 30 methods that all change for the same reason may be fine, and 30 tiny classes that all change together are worse than one.
People often think a Singleton is a harmless way to share an object. In fact it is a global variable with a nice name: it hides dependencies, makes tests share state, and makes it impossible to run two configurations side by side. Pass the shared object in through the constructor (dependency injection) and let the application create it once.
People often think inheritance is the main way to reuse code. In fact the GoF advice is to favour composition. A subclass is tied to the parent's internals and breaks when the parent changes (the fragile base class problem). Strategy, Decorator and State use composition and can be changed at run time; inheritance is fixed at compile time.
People sometimes think patterns are about Java and C++ only. In fact the problems are universal, but the form changes with the language. In Python, JavaScript or Go, a Strategy is often a function, an Observer is a callback or a channel, a Decorator is a function wrapper, and Iterator is built into the language. Knowing the problem is more useful than copying the class diagram.
tradeoffs: Choose a Strategy (interface plus implementations) when the number of variants is open-ended or comes from configuration, and each variant has its own state or dependencies. Choose a plain `if` or `switch` when there are two or three fixed variants that rarely change: the code is shorter, everything is visible in one place, and it costs nothing. The line is usually the third variant, or the first time two people edit the same function in one month.
Choose Observer (or an in-process event bus) when the producer should not know its consumers and consumers come and go. You pay with hidden control flow, ordering questions, and error handling inside notification. Choose a direct call when there is exactly one consumer that is part of the same feature, because a direct call can be read, traced and debugged. If the listeners must survive crashes or run in other processes, use a durable queue, such as Kafka, instead of an in-memory Observer.
Choose inheritance when there is a true "is-a" relation, the base class was designed for extension and you control it (Template Method in a framework you own). Choose composition (Strategy, Decorator) in all other cases: it is more flexible, easier to test and does not lock the hierarchy. You pay with more small objects and some forwarding code.
Choose dependency injection (constructors take interfaces) when you need tests without real infrastructure and clear boundaries. A DI container (Spring, Guice, Dagger) helps in large applications with hundreds of wiring points; for a small service, manual wiring in `main` is clearer, has no reflection magic, and fails at compile time. You give up some convenience either way.
Choose to introduce an abstraction after you have two or three real cases, not before. An early abstraction is a guess about the future, and a wrong abstraction is more expensive to remove than duplicated code. The cost of waiting is a small refactoring later.
friction: The main friction is over-engineering. Teams can create layers of interfaces, factories and managers that have one implementation each, so a simple change needs edits in six files and reading is a treasure hunt through indirection. The test is whether each abstraction has at least two real implementations, or a test that needs the seam.
A second problem is that patterns are described in terms of classes and objects, and the world moved on. Functional programming, data-oriented design, actors and microservices solve the same changes with different tools: immutable data, pure functions, messages and service boundaries. The vocabulary still helps, but a pattern from 1994 can mislead when mechanical copying replaces thinking. Reactive libraries show the Observer pattern grown into a whole programming model, with backpressure, which the original did not consider.
Third, the principles cannot be checked automatically. "One reason to change" and "stable abstraction" are judgments, and reasonable engineers disagree. Liskov substitution is the most precise but depends on contracts that are often written only in comments. Languages help partly (types, sealed classes, `final`), but behavioural contracts live in tests.
Finally, there are costs at run time that matter in hot paths: virtual calls, allocation of small objects, and megamorphic call sites (more than a few receiver types at one call, where the JIT stops inlining). In a request path with a budget of microseconds, these costs are small but real, and in an inner loop they can matter. Measure before removing an abstraction for speed, and keep abstractions out of the innermost loops.
practice: Learn the dozen patterns that appear daily: Strategy, Observer, Decorator, Adapter, Factory (and Builder), State, Command, Template Method, Facade, Proxy, Iterator, Composite. For each, learn the problem it solves in one sentence, not only the diagram. When you review code, name what you see: "this is an Adapter around the vendor SDK", and ask whether the pattern is paying for itself.
Refactor towards patterns when the pain appears, not before. Signs: a `switch` on a type field that keeps growing, a class that is changed in every feature, a test that needs a real database to check a rule, a constructor that creates its own collaborators with `new`, or subclasses that override methods to do nothing. Each sign points to a pattern: Strategy or State, a split by responsibility, dependency inversion, constructor injection, composition instead of inheritance.
Keep interfaces small and owned by the caller. Define `EventSink` where the engine is, not where Kafka is. Prefer constructor injection and immutable objects. Write the contract of each interface in its documentation: what it may throw, whether it is thread safe, whether it can be called twice. A subtype that cannot keep the contract should not implement the interface.
In languages with functions as values, use them first: pass a function for a simple Strategy, a callback for a simple Observer. Use a class when the behaviour has state, several methods or a name worth having in the domain. When you meet frameworks (Spring, React, Kafka clients), read them as pattern collections: Spring's `BeanPostProcessor` is a decorator hook, React's `useEffect` subscriptions are Observer, Kafka's `Serializer` interface is a Strategy.

Also an override that throws "not supported", or tightens a precondition, violates it. The fix is composition or a different hierarchy.

Injection is a technique to supply the implementation from outside (constructor, container). You can inject without inverting, if the interface lives in the detail's module, and the policy still depends on it.
Your Observer-based system sometimes updates a cache twice and sometimes never. What could be wrong? || Duplicate subscription (registered twice), a listener removed or never registered because of lifetime issues, an exception in an earlier listener stopping the rest, notifications during modification of the listener list, or ordering assumptions. You gain brevity and fewer types; you lose the named classes that can carry documentation and multiple methods, and sometimes the open extension of a class hierarchy.
How do patterns scale up to services? || Adapter becomes an anti-corruption layer, Facade an API gateway, Decorator a sidecar or middleware, Observer a pub/sub topic, and Strategy a plug-in or feature flag. The costs also scale up: indirection now includes network calls, versioning of the interface, and partial failure.
quiz: What are the three families of GoF patterns? || Creational (how objects are made), structural (how objects are combined) and behavioural (how objects interact and share work).
Why does "favour composition over inheritance" lead to more flexible designs? || With composition the parts are held through interfaces and can be swapped at run time, and each part can be tested alone. Inheritance fixes the relation at compile time and ties the subclass to the parent's internals.
A class has an `if` on a status field with five cases, repeated in four methods. Which pattern and what does it change? || State: each status becomes its own class with the behaviour for that state, and the object forwards calls to its current state. A new status then adds one class instead of editing four methods.

The old providers and their tests are untouched (open/closed).

value: A backup is a copy of data that lets you go back to an earlier state after something went wrong. Disaster recovery (DR) is the wider plan: how to get the whole service running again after a large failure, such as a deleted database, a ransomware attack, a lost data centre or a cloud region outage. Backups protect the data; DR also covers the servers, the network, the people and the order of steps.
Two numbers define the goal. The recovery point objective (RPO) is how much recent data you can afford to lose, measured in time: an RPO of 5 minutes means the restored system may be missing the last 5 minutes of writes. The recovery time objective (RTO) is how long you can be down before the service is back: an RTO of 2 hours means that the whole recovery, from the decision to restore until users are served, must fit into 2 hours. These two numbers come from the business, and they decide the design and the cost. An RPO of zero and an RTO of seconds is possible, but costs far more than an RPO of 24 hours and an RTO of a day.
Replication is not a backup. A replica copies a mistake as faithfully as it copies good data: a `DROP TABLE` or a bad deployment reaches the replica within milliseconds. A backup keeps old versions, in a separate place, under separate credentials, so that one mistake or one attacker cannot destroy both the data and its copies. That is the point of the "another failure domain" in the mechanism.
This concept uses three layers below. The write-ahead log (L2) is the continuous log of changes that makes point-in-time recovery possible: restore a snapshot, then replay the log to the exact moment you want. Replication (L2) supplies copies in other regions for a low RPO and fast failover. Checksums (L3) detect corruption, so that a backup is known to be readable and intact before the day it is needed. Above it, site reliability engineering (L1) owns the drills, PostgreSQL (L0) supplies base backups and WAL archiving, and cloud computing (L1) sells snapshots, versioning and cross-region copies as products.
terms: RPO (recovery point objective) — the maximum amount of data, measured as time, that the business accepts to lose.
RTO (recovery time objective) — the maximum time the service may be unavailable before it must be restored.
Snapshot — a point-in-time image of a volume or database, usually made cheaply with copy-on-write.
Base backup — a full physical copy of a database that is the starting point for log replay.
Point-in-time recovery (PITR) — restoring a base backup and then replaying archived log records up to a chosen moment.
Incremental backup — a backup that holds only what changed since the previous backup; a differential holds what changed since the last full backup.
Immutable backup — a copy that cannot be changed or deleted until a retention date, even by an administrator (for example S3 Object Lock).
3-2-1 rule — keep 3 copies of the data, on 2 different kinds of media or systems, with 1 copy off site.
history: Backups are as old as computers: operators in the 1950s and 1960s copied data to magnetic tape, and tape rotation schemes such as grandfather-father-son (daily, weekly and monthly sets) were common in the 1970s and 1980s. Disaster recovery as a discipline grew in the 1970s with the first large dependence on mainframes. Companies such as SunGard (founded 1978) sold shared "hot sites": computers in another city that a customer could use after a disaster.
The database side was shaped by logging. Write-ahead logging was developed for recovery from crashes (Gray, 1978; the ARIES algorithm of Mohan et al., 1992). Once the log existed, it was clear that archiving it gives more than crash recovery: a base backup plus all later log records can rebuild the database at any point. Oracle offered archive-log mode and PostgreSQL added point-in-time recovery in version 8.0 (2005).
The RPO and RTO vocabulary became standard in the 1990s and 2000s, and the events of September 11, 2001 pushed many firms to build second sites in separate regions. Cloud computing changed the economics. Amazon S3 (2006) made cheap, durable off-site storage available to everyone, and EBS snapshots (2008) made volume backups a single API call. AWS later published four DR strategies (backup and restore, pilot light, warm standby, multi-site active-active) as a cost-versus-RTO ladder.
Incidents taught the key lessons. In January 2017 GitLab.com lost about six hours of data after an engineer deleted the wrong database directory; the team found that of five backup mechanisms, none was usable when needed. The public post-mortem became a standard story about untested backups. Since about 2019 ransomware, which also hunts for and deletes backups, made immutable and offline copies a normal requirement, and regulators in finance and health now ask for tested recovery times.
how: Start from the objectives. For each system, ask the business for RPO and RTO per type of failure, because they differ. A user deletes a row (needs a small RPO, and recovery of one table). A disk dies (a replica takes over, RPO near zero). A bad migration corrupts data (restore to a time before it). A region is lost (failover to another region). Ransomware encrypts everything and deletes the visible backups (restore from an isolated copy). One strategy never fits all of these, so the plan uses layers.
The first layer is the snapshot or base backup. A full copy is taken on a schedule, for example every night. To save time and space, systems use incremental backups, which store only changed blocks or files. Block-level tracking (a changed-block bitmap in a hypervisor, a copy-on-write snapshot in EBS, ZFS or LVM, or file-level comparison with hashes in pgBackRest) finds what changed without reading all the data. A restore of an incremental chain needs the last full backup and all increments after it, so chains are limited in length, and a new full backup is "synthesised" periodically. Deduplication (which uses hashing, L3) stores each unique chunk once across backups.
The second layer is the continuous log, which gives a low RPO. The database writes every change to its write-ahead log (L2) before it changes data files. If the log is also archived to remote storage, the base backup plus the log gives the database at any moment. In PostgreSQL, `archive_command` (or `archive_library`) copies each completed 16 MB WAL segment to storage, and `archive_timeout` forces a segment switch after a set time, for example 60 seconds, which bounds the RPO of the archive to about a minute plus upload time. A restore sets `recovery_target_time` and PostgreSQL replays the log up to that point. Tools such as pgBackRest, WAL-G and Barman handle compression, encryption, retention and parallel transfer. For file stores, the equivalent is object versioning: S3 keeps every older version of an object, so an overwrite or a delete can be undone, and a lifecycle rule expires old versions after a retention time. Delta Lake tables keep a transaction log in the same way, so `RESTORE TABLE ... TO VERSION AS OF` can go back, limited by the retention settings (`VACUUM` removes old data files, with a default retention of 7 days).
The third layer is replication (L2), which gives a low RTO for hardware and site failures. A synchronous replica in another zone has RPO near zero, because a commit returns only after the replica has the log, and takes over in seconds to minutes. An asynchronous replica in another region has an RPO equal to the replication lag (seconds), and costs no commit latency. S3 cross-region replication is asynchronous; with Replication Time Control AWS states that 99.99% of objects replicate within 15 minutes. Remember again that replication copies deletions and corruption, so it complements backups and cannot replace them.
The fourth layer is isolation. A copy only counts if one failure cannot reach it. Put copies in another account and another region, with separate credentials, so a stolen administrator key cannot delete them. Use write-once settings (S3 Object Lock in compliance mode, tape, a vault with a delay on deletes) so that even the owner cannot remove a copy before its retention period ends. Encrypt backups with keys that are also backed up elsewhere, because an encrypted backup without its key is empty.
The fifth layer is verification, where checksums (L3) matter. Store a checksum with each backup file and check it on write, on a schedule and on restore, so silent corruption (bit rot, a truncated upload) is found early. PostgreSQL can check data pages with `pg_checksums`, and `pg_verifybackup` checks a base backup against its manifest. Checksums only prove that bytes did not change; they do not prove the backup is restorable. So restores are tested: automatically restore the latest backup into a scratch environment every day or week, start the database, run queries and compare row counts with production. The time this takes is your measured RTO, and the result is evidence for the plan.
Finally, the plan itself: a runbook with steps, owners and contact lists, stored somewhere that survives the disaster (not only on the system that is down), plus dependencies. Restoring a database is useless if the network, DNS, secrets, identity provider and deployment pipeline need the very systems that are gone. Drills (game days) test the people and the order as well as the tools.

example: A PostgreSQL database holds 2 TB. A base backup is taken every night at 02:00 and takes 1.5 hours to upload. WAL is archived continuously, with `archive_timeout = 60s`. The database writes about 20 GB of WAL per hour on average. At 14:07:31 a developer runs a `DELETE` without a `WHERE` clause on a large table. The monitoring finds it at 14:20. The team decides to restore the whole database to 14:07:30.
The RPO question: what is lost? Everything written after 14:07:30 is lost, in this case on purpose, because it includes the damage. Real writes during the 13 minutes since then are lost too, unless they can be exported from the damaged system and re-applied. This is why "restore to a point before the error" is a business decision as well as a technical one. For a hardware failure, the RPO would be the time since the last archived WAL segment, at most about 60 seconds plus upload delay.
The RTO question: how long until the service is back? Restore the base backup: 2 TB at 500 MB/s is 2,000,000 MB / 500 = 4,000 s, about 67 minutes. Replay WAL from 02:00 to 14:07, which is 12.1 hours × 20 GB = 242 GB. WAL replay is mostly sequential but applies changes page by page, and is often slower than copying. At an effective 50 MB/s this is 242,000 MB / 50 = 4,840 s, about 81 minutes. Add 15 minutes for decision, provisioning a machine and 10 minutes for checks and switching traffic. The total is about 67 + 81 + 25 ≈ 173 minutes, or just under 3 hours. If the RTO target is 1 hour, the plan fails by a factor of three, and now you can see the options: take base backups more often (every 6 hours halves the replay to about 40 minutes), use a standby with delayed replay (`recovery_min_apply_delay = 1h`, which allows going back one hour in minutes), use faster storage or parallel restore, or reduce the data in this database.
A cheaper fix for this specific error: keep a delayed replica that is one hour behind. The bad `DELETE` appears on it only at 15:07. The team stops replay at 14:07:30 on the delayed replica, extracts the deleted rows with a query, and inserts them back into production, in about 20 minutes and with no data loss for other tables. This is a partial restore, and often the right answer for human error.
Then the verification. The next week the team runs the same restore into a test machine and records 2 h 51 min. That number is now in the runbook. They also find that two WAL segments from last month failed to upload, because the archive command had returned success on a full disk. The checksum job reported a gap in the sequence. A gap in WAL means PITR is impossible past that point, and it is exactly what an untested system finds only at the worst moment.
intuition: A fire drill for data: the building may never burn, but the only way to know that people reach the exit in time is to walk the route with a stopwatch.
The design is natural because failures come in different sizes, and each tool covers one size. A log with a base copy covers "go back to before my mistake"; a replica covers "this machine died"; a remote immutable copy covers "this whole account was attacked". The two objectives RPO and RTO turn the vague hope "we have backups" into numbers that can be checked, bought and tested. And the unglamorous truth is that a backup is only a hypothesis until it has been restored.
mistakes: People often think replication is a backup. In fact a replica reproduces every write, including the wrong ones, within milliseconds. A dropped table, a bad migration, an application bug that overwrites data, and ransomware encryption all replicate. Replication protects against hardware failure; backups with history protect against everything else.
People often think that if the backup job shows "success", then the backup works. In fact the job only says that a process finished. The disk may have been full, the files may be empty or corrupt, the encryption key may be lost, or the backup may need a file from a system that no longer exists. Only a restore test proves that the backup works, and a measured restore time proves the RTO.
People often think RPO and RTO are the same as the backup frequency. In fact RPO is determined by the gap between the failure and the last recoverable data (for nightly backups that is up to 24 hours, but with archived WAL it can be one minute). RTO is not frequency at all: it depends on the size of the data, the restore bandwidth, the replay time and the steps around them. A 100 TB backup taken every minute can still take days to restore.
People often think that cloud storage durability means the data is backed up. In fact S3's "eleven nines" durability describes the chance of the service losing an object. It does not protect against you deleting the object, a script overwriting it, a compromised key, or a bad lifecycle rule. Versioning, Object Lock and a copy in another account are what protect against those.
People sometimes think the disaster plan is finished when the data is safe. In fact restoring the data is one step. The service also needs compute, network, DNS, certificates, secrets, and access for the engineers, and the order matters. Many real recoveries were slow because the credentials vault or the deployment tool was among the lost systems.
tradeoffs: Choose a nightly full backup with archived logs when an RPO of minutes and an RTO of hours is acceptable; it is cheap, and storage is the only large cost. Choose a warm standby (a replica that can take over) when the RTO must be minutes: you pay for running capacity all the time, roughly 30% to 100% of production cost, depending on how small you keep it. Choose multi-site active-active only when minutes of downtime cost more than double the infrastructure and the application can handle conflicting writes in two regions; it is the most expensive and the most complex option.
Choose synchronous replication when the RPO must be zero and the second site is close (under about 10 ms away), so commit latency stays small. Choose asynchronous replication across regions: you gain speed and independence from the distance, and you accept an RPO equal to the lag, usually seconds. You cannot get zero RPO and zero latency overhead across a long distance.
Choose full backups more often when restore speed matters more than storage, because a restore needs a shorter chain and less replay. Choose incrementals and long chains when the data is huge and changes slowly, and accept a slower restore and more risk that one broken link spoils the chain. Periodic synthetic fulls give a middle path.
Choose logical backups (`pg_dump`, exports) for small databases, for moving between versions and for restoring one table. Choose physical backups and log archiving for large databases and for PITR: logical restores must rebuild indexes and can be many times slower, but physical backups are tied to the major version and the platform.
Choose a long retention (months or years) when law or audits demand it or when slow corruption could go unnoticed; you pay for storage and you keep personal data longer than you may want. Privacy rules (for example the right to erasure) conflict with long retention, so many teams keep a short retention for personal data and a longer one for aggregated or anonymised data, and document how deletions apply to backups.
friction: Restore time grows with data size, and bandwidth does not keep up: restoring 500 TB at 2 GB/s takes about 70 hours. Large systems therefore avoid full restores; they use replicas, partition-level restore, snapshots on the same storage system (restore by pointer change in minutes) or design for rebuilding from replicas in other zones. The honest RTO of a very large system is often much longer than leadership assumes, until a drill shows the number.
Consistency across systems is hard. A database restored to 14:07:30 and a message queue, a search index and an object store restored to other times no longer agree. Orders may reference files that do not exist, or events may be replayed twice. Systems need a plan for the order of restore, for rebuilding derived data from the source of truth, and for idempotent consumers. Distributed databases with no global snapshot make this harder.
Testing is expensive and gets skipped: it needs spare capacity, realistic data (which brings privacy concerns), time and attention, and it looks like wasted effort until the day it is not. Backups also create their own risk: they hold copies of sensitive data and need the same access control and encryption as production, and a leak of a backup is a leak of the database.
Ransomware and insider threats change the design from "protect against accident" to "protect against an adversary with administrator rights". Then even the backup system's own credentials must be separated, deletion must be delayed or impossible, and recovery must be possible from a clean environment. Finally, cost grows with the retention and with cross-region transfer fees, and teams often discover that they keep hundreds of terabytes of old backups that nobody could restore anyway.
practice: Write the RPO and RTO for each system, by failure type, with the business owner. Tier the systems: a payments database is tier 0 (RPO near zero, RTO minutes to an hour), an analytics warehouse is tier 2 (RPO a day, RTO a day, and it can be rebuilt from source). Spend effort in order of tier.
For databases, use a base backup plus continuous log archiving (pgBackRest or WAL-G for PostgreSQL, binlogs for MySQL), store it in object storage in another account and region, enable object versioning and a retention lock, and monitor archive lag and gaps in the log sequence. Keep at least one delayed replica for human errors where the RTO is tight. For object stores and lakehouse tables, enable versioning; for Delta tables, set the retention (`delta.deletedFileRetentionDuration`, `delta.logRetentionDuration`) so that time travel covers your detection time, and know that `VACUUM` ends the ability to go back beyond it.
Test restores on a schedule. Automate a daily or weekly restore into a scratch environment, run checks (row counts, a few business queries, application smoke test), measure the duration, and alert on failure or on drift above the RTO. Do a full game day at least once a year, including failover of traffic, and practice with the real on-call people at an unplanned time.
Protect the backups: separate credentials and accounts, write-once retention, encryption with keys stored separately and also backed up, and alerts on any attempt to delete or shorten retention. Add a note about which data is excluded (caches, rebuildable data) so nobody expects it back.
Document the runbook where it survives the outage, include the order of restore and the dependencies (secrets, DNS, identity, CI/CD), and keep it short and numbered. After each drill and each real incident, fix the plan, and write down the measured RPO and RTO. The 3-2-1 rule is a good floor: three copies, two kinds of storage, one far away and, today, one that cannot be changed.

@@ raid
value: RAID (Redundant Array of Independent Disks) combines several physical disks into one logical disk, to get two things a single disk cannot give: survival of a disk failure, and more throughput. Disks are mechanical or flash parts with a limited life. In a fleet, a few percent of them fail every year, and a server with 12 disks sees a failure often enough that it must plan for it. RAID lets the server keep running, and keep its data, when one (or two) disks die.
There are three basic tools. Striping (RAID 0) spreads consecutive blocks over many disks, so reads and writes run in parallel; it gives speed and capacity but no protection, and in fact makes failure more likely, because losing any one disk loses everything. Mirroring (RAID 1) writes every block to two or more disks; it costs half the capacity and survives the loss of a disk. Parity (RAID 5 and 6) stores one or two extra blocks per stripe, computed from the data blocks, so that the contents of one or two missing disks can be recomputed. A parity array of 8 disks keeps 7 disks of data (RAID 5) or 6 disks (RAID 6), not 4.
The layers below provide the parts. Storage devices (L3) explain the problem: disks fail, have limited random I/O (an HDD does 100 to 200 random operations per second), and have unrecoverable read errors. Coding theory (L4) gives the maths: parity is an XOR, and RAID 6 adds a second equation over a finite field (Reed–Solomon). Erasure coding (L2) is the general form: RAID 6 is the simplest erasure code, applied within one machine, and distributed storage applies the same idea across machines.
Above it, single-server OLTP databases traditionally run on RAID 10 volumes. Distributed file systems moved the same idea (redundancy through copies or coding) from disks inside a box to machines in a cluster, and today many large systems do not use RAID at all, because the redundancy is in the software above.
terms: Stripe — one row of blocks across all disks in the array; the unit over which parity is computed.
Chunk (stripe unit) — the amount of contiguous data placed on one disk before moving to the next; typically 64 KB to 512 KB.
Parity — a block that is the XOR of the data blocks in a stripe; XOR-ing the survivors with the parity gives back a missing block.
Mirror — an exact copy of a disk's contents on another disk.
Degraded mode — the array state after a disk failure: it still works, but each read of the missing disk needs a reconstruction, and one more failure may lose data.
Rebuild (resilver) — copying or recomputing the data of a failed disk onto a replacement disk.
Write penalty — the number of disk I/Os needed for one small logical write: 2 for RAID 1, 4 for RAID 5, 6 for RAID 6.
URE (unrecoverable read error) — a read that the disk cannot correct; rated at about 1 per 10^14 to 10^16 bits read, depending on the drive class.
Write hole — the inconsistency left when power fails between writing the data block and writing its parity block.
history: In 1987 David Patterson, Garth Gibson and Randy Katz at the University of California, Berkeley, wrote a technical report, later published at SIGMOD in 1988 as "A Case for Redundant Arrays of Inexpensive Disks (RAID)". The situation was this: mainframes used a few huge, expensive disks (the "Single Large Expensive Disk", SLED), while the personal computer market made small 3.5-inch disks cheap. They argued that an array of small cheap disks could match the capacity and performance of one large disk at lower cost and power, but with N disks the failure rate rises N times, so redundancy was needed. The paper defined five levels, RAID 1 to RAID 5. Level 0 (striping without redundancy) and level 6 (two parity blocks) were added later. The word "Inexpensive" was later changed to "Independent" by the industry.
The 1990s made RAID a standard product: hardware controller cards with battery-backed caches, and external arrays from EMC, IBM and others. The 1990s also gave the analysis of the small-write problem and the work on declustered parity. Peter Anvin's note "The mathematics of RAID-6" (2004) described the Reed–Solomon code used by Linux software RAID. ZFS (Sun, 2005) brought RAID-Z, which solved the write hole by using variable-width stripes and copy-on-write.
Around 2007, Robin Harris argued that RAID 5 would stop working by 2009 because disk capacity grew faster than error rates fell: rebuilding a big RAID 5 array was likely to hit a read error on a surviving disk. The argument is simplified, but it moved the industry to dual parity (RAID 6, RAID-Z2) for large disks. Since 2010 the main change has been that the data center moved away from RAID: Google's file system (2003), HDFS and cloud object stores keep copies or erasure-coded fragments across machines, and flash SSDs changed the performance picture.
how: Begin with the layout. Take 4 disks and a chunk size of 128 KB. In RAID 0 the logical address space is cut into chunks, and chunk 0 goes to disk 0, chunk 1 to disk 1, chunk 2 to disk 2, chunk 3 to disk 3, chunk 4 to disk 0 again. A large read touches all disks in parallel and gets about four times the throughput. A small random read touches one disk. Mapping an address to a disk is a division and a modulo, so there is no table.
RAID 1 writes each chunk to two disks and can read from either one, so reads can be balanced over both and even doubled in throughput; writes are as slow as the slower disk. RAID 10 (a stripe over mirrored pairs) gives striping speed with the mirror's simple recovery: after a failure only the partner disk must be copied. It is the common choice for databases with many small writes.
RAID 5 adds parity. For each stripe of N − 1 data chunks, one parity chunk is stored, and the parity position rotates from stripe to stripe (left-symmetric layout), so no single disk takes all the parity writes. Parity is the bitwise XOR (the "exclusive or", which is addition without carry in GF(2) from coding theory (L4)): \( P = D_1 \oplus D_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus D_{N-1} \). XOR has the property that any one term equals the XOR of all the others, so if a disk fails, its chunk is \( D_k = P \oplus \bigoplus_{j \ne k} D_j \). A degraded read of a missing chunk therefore reads all N − 1 survivors, which is why a degraded array is slow.
The write penalty comes from keeping parity in step. To overwrite one chunk, the controller does not read the whole stripe. It reads the old data and old parity (2 reads), computes \( P_{\text{new}} = P_{\text{old}} \oplus D_{\text{old}} \oplus D_{\text{new}} \), then writes the new data and new parity (2 writes). That is 4 I/Os for one logical write, called read-modify-write. If a write covers a whole stripe, the parity is computed from the new data directly, with no reads, and the cost is N writes for N − 1 chunks of data. Big sequential writes are therefore efficient on RAID 5, and small random writes are the bad case. Controllers and the Linux md driver collect writes in a cache to assemble full stripes.
RAID 6 stores two syndromes per stripe, P and Q, and survives two failures. P is the XOR as before. Q is a weighted sum in a finite field, usually GF(2⁸), where each byte is an element and multiplication is defined by a polynomial: \( Q = \sum_i g^{i} D_i \), with \( g \) a generator of the field (for example 2). With two lost disks, P and Q give two linear equations in two unknowns, which are solved in the field. The linear algebra (L4) and the field arithmetic (done with lookup tables or vector instructions such as AVX2) are what make RAID 6 a Reed–Solomon code with two parity symbols, the smallest practical case of erasure coding (L2). A small write costs 6 I/Os (3 reads, 3 writes).
Failure and rebuild follow. When a disk fails, the array goes degraded. The administrator (or a hot spare) adds a new disk, and the rebuild reads all the surviving disks fully and writes the replacement, stripe by stripe. For RAID 1 and 10 a rebuild reads only the partner. The rebuild is limited by the disk's sequential speed: a 20 TB disk at 200 MB/s needs at least 100,000 seconds, about 28 hours, and with the array serving traffic a rebuild often takes days. During this window the array has less (RAID 6) or no (RAID 5, RAID 1) redundancy.
The danger is then the unrecoverable read error. A rebuild of RAID 5 with 8 disks of 10 TB reads 7 × 10 TB = 70 TB = 5.6 × 10¹⁴ bits. With a spec of one URE per 10¹⁴ bits, the expected number of errors is 5.6, and the chance of finishing without any is about \( e^{-5.6} \approx 0.4\% \) in this simple model. Real drives do better than the specification (and enterprise drives are rated at 10¹⁵ or 10¹⁶), but the principle holds: the larger the disks, the more likely that a single parity does not survive a rebuild. This is the main reason for double parity and for scrubbing, which reads the whole array regularly to find and repair bad sectors early, while the redundancy is still intact.
Mean time to data loss can be estimated with the standard model for single parity: \( \text{MTTDL} \approx \dfrac{\text{MTTF}^2}{N(N-1)\,\text{MTTR}} \), where MTTF is the mean time to failure of one disk, MTTR is the repair time and N is the number of disks. It shows the lever: shorter repair time matters as much as better disks. The model assumes independent failures, which is optimistic, since disks from the same batch, in the same chassis, with the same heat and vibration, fail together more often than the formula says.

example: First, parity with small numbers. Use 4-bit chunks. Three data disks hold D1 = 1011, D2 = 0110 and D3 = 1100. Parity is P = 1011 ⊕ 0110 ⊕ 1100. First 1011 ⊕ 0110 = 1101; then 1101 ⊕ 1100 = 0001. The parity disk holds 0001. Now disk 2 fails. To recover D2, XOR the survivors with the parity: 1011 ⊕ 1100 = 0111; 0111 ⊕ 0001 = 0110. This equals the lost D2. (Each bit position is an independent XOR, so a real array does this on whole 128 KB chunks at once.)
Next, a small write. Change D2 from 0110 to 1111. The controller reads D2 (0110) and P (0001). New parity = 0001 ⊕ 0110 ⊕ 1111 = 0111 ⊕ 1111 = 1000. Write D2 = 1111 and P = 1000. Check: D1 ⊕ D2 ⊕ D3 = 1011 ⊕ 1111 ⊕ 1100 = 0100 ⊕ 1100 = 1000. It matches. Four I/Os were needed, and the other disks were not touched.
Now compare configurations for 8 disks of 10 TB with 150 random IOPS each (a typical 7,200 rpm HDD gives about 100 to 200). Raw capacity is 80 TB. RAID 10 gives 40 TB, RAID 5 gives 70 TB, RAID 6 gives 60 TB. Random read IOPS are about 8 × 150 = 1,200 in all three. Random write IOPS are divided by the write penalty: RAID 10, 1,200 / 2 = 600; RAID 5, 1,200 / 4 = 300; RAID 6, 1,200 / 6 = 200. So for a database that does 40% small random writes, RAID 10 is about three times better than RAID 6 on write-heavy load, and costs 20 TB more per 60 TB of usable data. For an archive that is written once in big sequential batches and mostly read, RAID 6 gives the most usable space with safety, and the write penalty hardly appears, because full-stripe writes avoid the read-modify-write.
Last, the rebuild window. In the 8 × 10 TB RAID 6, one disk fails on Monday at 09:00. The new disk is in place at 10:00. Rebuild must read 7 × 10 TB and write 10 TB. At 100 MB/s effective speed (half of sequential, because the array is also serving users), the rebuild of the new disk takes 10 TB / 100 MB/s = 100,000 s, about 28 hours. During this time, a second failure is still survivable, because there is a second parity. A third failure, or a second failure plus bad sectors elsewhere, would lose data. With RAID 5 the same window would have no spare redundancy at all. Rebuild time is the number to know when you choose a level.
intuition: A group of friends who each remember a different part of a long number, plus one friend who remembers only the sum: if any one of them is absent, the others can work out the missing part.
Parity is natural because redundancy does not have to be a full copy. To survive one failure you need to store one extra disk's worth of information, not N. The XOR gives the cheapest possible equation: one extra block in which each bit says only "is the count of ones even or odd?". Together with the surviving blocks, that single bit fixes the one unknown. RAID 6 uses the same logic: two unknowns need two independent equations, and the finite field gives a second equation that is independent of the first. The price is the update cost: to keep the equation true, every change to a data block must also change the parity.
mistakes: People often think RAID is a backup. In fact RAID protects against a disk failing, not against deletion, corruption, ransomware, a controller bug, a fire or an operator error. A `rm -rf` is applied to all mirrors at once. RAID raises availability; backups protect data history.
People often think RAID 5 means you can safely lose one disk and nothing else matters. In fact, when one disk fails, the rebuild reads every other disk completely. A read error on any of them during that time can make a stripe unrecoverable, and a second disk failure (common, because disks of the same age and batch wear together, and the rebuild stresses them) loses the array. This is why large arrays use two parity disks.
People often think a RAID level with more disks is always faster. In fact random small writes in parity RAID are limited by the write penalty, not by the number of disks, and extra disks raise the chance that some disk fails. A wide RAID 5 or 6 group also rebuilds slower and each stripe update touches more devices. Groups of 6 to 12 disks are typical.
People often think a hardware RAID controller makes data safe even when power fails. In fact a controller with a volatile cache can lose acknowledged writes, and any parity array can have the write hole after a crash. Safety depends on a battery- or flash-backed cache, a write journal, or a copy-on-write design like ZFS. Without them, a crash can silently leave a stripe with inconsistent parity.
People sometimes think RAID detects corruption. In fact classic RAID trusts the data a disk returns. If a disk returns wrong data without an error (silent corruption), a mirror cannot say which copy is right, and parity may be recomputed from bad data. File systems with end-to-end checksums (ZFS, Btrfs) detect this and use the redundancy to repair it.
tradeoffs: Choose RAID 10 when the workload has many small random writes (OLTP databases, journals, virtual machines) and a rebuild must be quick and light; you pay with 50% usable capacity. Choose RAID 6 (or RAID-Z2) for large disks and capacity-oriented, mostly sequential or read-heavy data (archives, media, backups); you get 60% to 85% usable capacity and survive two failures, and you pay with a write penalty of 6 and slow rebuilds. Choose RAID 5 only for small disks (a few TB or less), SSDs, or data that can be rebuilt, because the rebuild window with one parity is risky on big spinning disks.
Choose software RAID (Linux md, ZFS) when you want portability, no proprietary controller, and checksums with self-healing (ZFS); you spend some CPU, which today is small. Choose a hardware controller when you need a battery-backed write cache for a database on HDDs and a managed, vendor-supported array; you pay with lock-in to the controller (the disk set may not be readable on another model), a firmware risk, and a single point of failure unless duplicated.
Choose RAID 0 only for scratch space or cases where data is rebuilt or copied elsewhere (a cache, a temporary shuffle area); a stripe of 4 disks fails about 4 times as often as one disk. A common cloud use is striping several network volumes to get more IOPS than one volume offers, on top of a database that replicates at the application level.
Choose to skip RAID and rely on replication across machines when the software already keeps 3 copies or erasure-coded fragments (HDFS, Kafka with replication, Cassandra, object stores). Then a disk failure is repaired from other machines, and RAID inside the box would double the redundancy cost (3 copies × 2 mirrors = 6x). You give up the fast local rebuild, and you must have enough network capacity to re-replicate a disk's worth of data.
Choose NVMe SSD arrays with care: SSDs have far lower random write penalties in time, but parity writes add wear (each small write writes twice as much to flash) and a failed SSD often fails suddenly; many designs use mirrors or erasure-coded pools across servers instead of RAID 5 inside a box.
friction: The first limit is rebuild time and the exposure window. As disk capacity grows faster than disk speed, the time to rebuild a disk grows (hours became days), and the array runs degraded the whole time, with higher load on the survivors. Declustered RAID (as in ZFS dRAID, Ceph and many enterprise arrays) spreads the spare capacity and the rebuild work over all disks, so many disks rebuild a lost disk in parallel and the window shrinks, at the price of more complex layout.
The second limit is the write hole and the cost of crash-consistent parity. A crash between the data write and the parity write can leave a stripe whose parity does not match, and later a disk failure then reconstructs wrong data without any warning. Remedies exist (a write-intent bitmap, a journal device in Linux md, a battery-backed cache, ZFS copy-on-write), but each costs performance or hardware, and many setups run without them.
The third is silent corruption and correlated failures. RAID alone cannot tell which copy is right when copies disagree, and it assumes independent failures. Disks of the same model and age, in the same enclosure, behind the same controller or backplane, may fail together. A controller or firmware bug can take down the whole array at once. Scrubbing helps with bad sectors, not with these.
The fourth is that RAID hides the state of the disks: a degraded array may look healthy to the application until a second failure. Monitoring of SMART data, array state and rebuild progress is essential. And at large scale the model changes: when one machine holds 24 to 60 disks, it is better to treat the machine as the failure unit and put redundancy across machines, which is exactly the move that distributed file systems and erasure-coded object stores made.
practice: Pick the level from the workload and the disk size. For databases on HDDs use RAID 10, and on SSDs a mirror or RAID 10 as well, unless capacity forces parity. For capacity pools on disks of 8 TB and more use RAID 6 or RAID-Z2, in groups of 6 to 12 disks, and keep a hot spare so that the rebuild begins by itself. Never mix random-write databases with RAID 5/6 without checking the write penalty against the IOPS you need.
Align the stripe with the workload. Choose a chunk size near the typical I/O size (a smaller chunk spreads each request over more disks, a larger chunk keeps small requests on one disk). Align partitions and file system blocks with the stripe so that writes are full stripes where possible. Set the file system's `stripe` or `stride` parameters to match the array.
Scrub on a schedule (weekly or monthly) so that bad sectors are found while redundancy is intact. In Linux md, use `check` through `sync_action`; in ZFS, `zpool scrub`. Monitor SMART attributes (reallocated and pending sectors), `mdadm --detail` or `zpool status`, and alert on degraded state, not only on failure. Practise a replacement and a rebuild in a test, and measure the time.
Use a write journal or a battery-backed cache with parity RAID on a database server. Prefer file systems with checksums (ZFS, Btrfs) when data integrity matters more than raw speed. Buy disks from different batches, or at least different manufacturing dates, to reduce correlated failure.
Keep backups. A RAID array in one chassis shares the power supply, the controller, the room and the administrator's mistakes with its data. And in cloud environments remember that a block volume (for example an EBS volume) is already replicated by the provider inside its zone, so adding RAID 1 on top does not give protection against the zone failing; RAID 0 striping across volumes is used for IOPS, not for safety.
interview: Why is the small-write penalty 4 for RAID 5, and how can you avoid it? || An update to one chunk needs the old data and old parity (2 reads) to compute the new parity, then 2 writes. Avoid it by writing full stripes (buffer and coalesce writes, large sequential I/O), by using a log-structured or copy-on-write layout (ZFS), or by choosing RAID 10 for write-heavy loads.
A 12 × 16 TB RAID 5 loses a disk. Why is this dangerous? || The rebuild reads 11 × 16 TB = 176 TB with no redundancy left. With a URE rate of 1 in 10^15 bits, the expected number of read errors is about 1.4, so the chance of a failure during rebuild is high, and the rebuild takes days, during which another disk can fail. Use RAID 6 or smaller groups, scrub regularly, and prefer fast, declustered rebuilds.
What is the RAID write hole and how do you close it? || A crash between writing a data chunk and its parity leaves the stripe inconsistent, and after a later disk failure the reconstruction produces wrong data silently. Close it with a write-ahead journal (Linux md journal or bitmap with resync), a battery-backed write cache, or copy-on-write with variable stripe widths as in ZFS RAID-Z.
How does RAID 6 recover two lost disks? || Each stripe has two independent equations: P is the XOR of the data and Q is a weighted sum in GF(2^8). With two unknown chunks, these give two equations in two unknowns, which are solved in the field. If one lost chunk is parity, it is recomputed from the data, so the cases are data+data, data+P, data+Q, and P+Q.
Your service runs on HDFS or Cassandra with 3 replicas. Do you still use RAID on the nodes? || Usually no, or only RAID 1 for the OS disks. The software already tolerates a lost disk or machine, and RAID adds cost and hides disk failures from the replication layer. Use JBOD (each disk separate) and let the system re-replicate. The exception is when re-replication traffic is too costly, or a failure of a single disk would take down the whole node's data.
How do RAID and erasure coding relate, and why did distributed storage move toward erasure coding? || RAID 5/6 are erasure codes with 1 or 2 parity symbols inside one machine. Distributed systems use codes like Reed–Solomon (10+4, for example) across machines or racks to get the same durability as 3 copies with about 1.4x storage instead of 3x, at the cost of more network traffic for repair and degraded reads.
quiz: What does RAID 5 store besides data, and how is a lost disk recovered? || It stores one parity chunk per stripe, the XOR of the data chunks, with the parity position rotating across disks. A lost chunk is the XOR of the parity and all surviving data chunks.
Why does RAID 5 take 4 I/Os for one small write? || It must read the old data and old parity, compute the new parity, then write the new data and new parity: 2 reads and 2 writes.
Why is RAID 6 preferred over RAID 5 for large disks? || Rebuilds of large disks take many hours or days and read all the surviving data, so the chance of a second failure or a read error during rebuild is high. The second parity lets the array survive that.
Eight 12 TB disks are set up as RAID 6. What is the usable capacity, and how many random write IOPS if each disk gives 150? || Usable: (8 − 2) × 12 = 72 TB. Raw IOPS 8 × 150 = 1,200; with a write penalty of 6, random write IOPS ≈ 200.
When would you choose RAID 10 over RAID 6? || For small random write workloads such as OLTP databases, where the write penalty of parity hurts and rebuilds must be fast and gentle; you pay with 50% usable capacity.

@@ broadcast
value: A broadcast primitive lets one process send a message to a whole group and gives a clear promise about who delivers it, and in what order. The plain network gives you point-to-point sends that can be lost, and a sender that can crash in the middle of sending to five receivers, so that two of them get the message and three never do. Reliable broadcast fixes this: if any correct process delivers a message, all correct processes deliver it, even if the sender crashed half way. Total-order broadcast (also called atomic broadcast) adds one more promise: all processes deliver all messages in the same order.
The second promise is the important one. If every replica of a service starts in the same state and applies the same commands in the same order, they all end in the same state. This is state machine replication, and total-order broadcast is what supplies "the same commands in the same order". Every replicated database log, every Raft or Zab cluster, every Kafka partition and every ledger shared by several writers is a total-order broadcast, in practice.
The famous fact is that total-order broadcast is equivalent to consensus. Consensus lets a group agree on one value; total-order broadcast is consensus repeated for slot 1, slot 2, slot 3, and so on, with the group agreeing on which message fills each slot. Each can be built from the other. So the limits of consensus (the FLP impossibility result, the need for a majority) are the limits of total-order broadcast.
The layers below give the parts. Consensus (L2) gives the agreement on order. Lamport clocks (L2) give a way to stamp messages so that the order respects cause and effect. Vector clocks (L2) let causal broadcast hold back a message until everything it depends on has arrived. Failure detection (L2) tells the group when to stop waiting for a crashed process, and relay its messages. Distributed systems theory (L3) defines the model, asynchronous with crash failures, in which the guarantees are stated and proved. Above them sit ZAB, the distributed commit log and event sourcing.
terms: Best-effort broadcast — the sender sends to everyone; if the sender stays alive every correct process delivers, but a sender crash can leave some without the message.
Reliable broadcast — if any correct process delivers m, then every correct process delivers m (agreement), even if the sender crashed.
Delivery — the moment when the broadcast layer hands a message to the application; receiving a message from the network is not yet delivering it.
FIFO order — messages from one sender are delivered in the order that sender sent them.
Causal order — if message a could have influenced message b (a happened before b), every process delivers a before b.
Total order — all processes deliver the same messages in the same sequence.
Uniform agreement — if any process, even one that later crashes, delivers m, then all correct processes deliver m. Non-uniform agreement only covers correct processes.
State machine replication — keeping copies of a deterministic service in sync by applying the same commands in the same order on each.
history: The problem grew out of fault-tolerant systems in the 1980s. Jo-Mei Chang and N. F. Maxemchuk published "Reliable Broadcast Protocols" in 1984, with a rotating token site that orders messages for a group. Leslie Lamport's 1978 paper "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System" had already shown the idea of state machine replication: if every process applies the same commands in the order of their logical timestamps, all replicas agree. Fred Schneider's 1990 survey "Implementing Fault-Tolerant Services Using the State Machine Approach" made this a general method.
Kenneth Birman and Thomas Joseph built the ISIS system at Cornell (1985 to 1987), which provided process groups with reliable, causal and totally ordered multicast (the CBCAST and ABCAST primitives), and it ran in real settings, such as the New York Stock Exchange and the French air traffic control system. Hadzilacos and Toueg's "A Modular Approach to Fault-Tolerant Broadcasts and Related Problems" (1994) gave the clean hierarchy of reliable, FIFO, causal and atomic broadcast that textbooks still use.
The theory then set the limits. In 1985 Fischer, Lynch and Paterson proved that no deterministic algorithm can reach consensus in an asynchronous system if even one process may crash. In 1996 Chandra and Toueg showed that consensus is solvable with an unreliable failure detector, and that atomic broadcast and consensus are equivalent problems, each reducible to the other. Défago, Schiper and Urbán (2004) surveyed more than 60 total-order algorithms and classified them by how they decide the order: a fixed sequencer, a moving sequencer or token, logical timestamps from the senders, or agreement among the destinations.
In practice the idea moved into products. Google's Chubby (2006) used Paxos for a replicated log. ZooKeeper (2008 to 2010) introduced ZAB, a purpose-built atomic broadcast protocol. Raft (2014) made the replicated log the main idea of the algorithm, and became the base of etcd, Consul and many databases. Kafka (2011) offered a partitioned log with a leader per partition. Blockchains can be seen as total-order broadcast among parties that do not trust each other.
how: Start with the weakest primitive and build up. Best-effort broadcast is a loop: send the message to every process over a reliable point-to-point link. If the sender stays up, every correct process receives it. If the sender crashes after sending to some, others miss it.
Reliable broadcast closes this gap with the eager relay algorithm. When a process first receives message m, it immediately re-sends m to everyone, and then delivers m. So if any correct process received m, it relays m, and every correct process gets it, even if the original sender is gone. The cost is O(n²) messages for each broadcast, since each of n processes sends to n − 1 others. Failure detection (L2) enables a cheaper variant, the lazy algorithm: processes relay m only if the failure detector says the sender crashed. The cost drops to n − 1 messages in the normal case. A caveat is that the eager version, as described, only gives non-uniform agreement, because a process may deliver m and crash before relaying. Uniform reliable broadcast waits until a majority has acknowledged m before delivering.
FIFO broadcast adds a per-sender sequence number: process p tags its messages 1, 2, 3, and receivers deliver p's messages in this order, holding back any message that arrives early. It costs one counter per sender.
Causal broadcast needs to know what each message depends on. Each process keeps a vector clock (L2), an array V with one entry per process, counting how many messages from each sender it has delivered. When p broadcasts, it attaches its current vector. A receiver q delivers a message m from p with vector \( V_m \) only when two conditions hold: \( V_m[p] = V_q[p] + 1 \) (it is the next message from p) and \( V_m[k] \le V_q[k] \) for every other process k (q has already delivered everything that p had delivered when it sent m). Otherwise q holds m in a buffer. This gives exactly "never deliver a reply before the question".
Total-order broadcast has several designs. Using a fixed sequencer is the simplest: all senders send to a sequencer process, which assigns consecutive numbers 1, 2, 3 and broadcasts (number, message); receivers deliver in number order and buffer gaps. It needs only a few messages and one extra hop of latency, but the sequencer is a single point of failure, and electing a new one safely is itself a consensus problem. A second family uses logical timestamps (Lamport clocks, L2): each message is stamped (clock, sender), receivers acknowledge, and a message is delivered when it has the smallest stamp and everyone has been heard from, so a slow or crashed process stalls everyone unless a failure detector removes it. A third family has the destinations agree: for each slot, the processes run a consensus instance to decide which message fills it. This is what Multi-Paxos, Raft and ZAB do, in an optimised form: one stable leader acts as the sequencer, proposes entries, and an entry is committed when a majority has stored it. When the leader fails, a new leader is elected by a majority vote, and the protocol makes sure that any entry that may have been committed is kept. So the sequencer's single point of failure is solved by consensus (L2).
The equivalence with consensus is easy to see in both directions. From total-order broadcast to consensus: every process broadcasts its proposal, and all decide the first delivered proposal; since order is the same everywhere, the decision is the same. From consensus to total-order broadcast: collect messages, and repeatedly run consensus on the next batch to be delivered. Because consensus is impossible in an asynchronous system with a crash (FLP), so is total-order broadcast; real systems assume partial synchrony and use failure detectors or timeouts, which guarantee safety always and progress only when the network behaves well for long enough.
The cost of a majority protocol is a round trip: a leader appends the message locally, sends it to n − 1 followers, and commits when ⌊n/2⌋ followers have acknowledged, so with 5 nodes it waits for the fastest 2 followers. This tolerates 2 crashes. Systems add batching and pipelining to reach hundreds of thousands of messages per second, because the cost per message of the round trip is shared by the batch.

example: Three replicas A, B and C store a counter named x. Two clients send commands at the same moment: client 1 sends "x = x + 1" and client 2 sends "x = x * 2". Initially x = 5. If the replicas apply these in different orders, they diverge: A applies +1 then ×2 and gets (5 + 1) × 2 = 12; B applies ×2 then +1 and gets 5 × 2 + 1 = 11. Both replicas are correct programs and no message was lost, yet they disagree. The reason is the lack of a common order. Reliable broadcast alone, which guarantees that everyone gets both commands, does not fix this.
Now use total-order broadcast with a leader. The leader L receives "+1" first and assigns slot 1, then receives "×2" and assigns slot 2. It sends (1, +1) and (2, ×2) to A, B and C. Suppose the second message reaches C before the first. C puts (2, ×2) into a holdback queue and waits, because slot 1 has not arrived. When (1, +1) arrives, C delivers slot 1 and then slot 2. All three replicas compute (5 + 1) × 2 = 12.
Next, the failure of the sender. In reliable broadcast with five processes, the sender S sends m to P1 and then crashes before sending to P2, P3 and P4. With the eager relay rule, P1 re-sends m to P2, P3 and P4 on first receipt. All correct processes deliver m. The cost: each broadcast uses up to n(n − 1) = 5 × 4 = 20 messages instead of 4. With the failure-detector variant, P1 relays only after the detector reports that S crashed, which costs 4 messages normally and 4 more after a crash.
Now the leader crashes. Raft with 5 nodes: the leader has appended slots 1 to 10 locally and sent slot 10 to two followers before it crashes. Slots 1 to 9 are on a majority; slot 10 is on 3 of 5 nodes (leader and two followers), so it may have been committed. Any new leader must be elected by a majority of 3, and a candidate whose log is not at least as up to date as the voters' logs cannot win. At least one voter has slot 10, so the winner has it as well and keeps it. If instead slot 10 had been on only the old leader, it was not committed, and may be lost; the client never got an acknowledgement and will retry. Order is preserved: nobody ever delivers slot 11 before slot 10.
Finally the causal case. Process A broadcasts "Q" with vector [1,0,0]. B receives it, delivers it, and broadcasts "A" with vector [1,1,0]. Process C receives B's message first. The rule needs V[A] ≤ C's V[A], that is 1 ≤ 0, which is false, so C holds the message back. When Q arrives, C delivers it and updates its vector to [1,0,0]. Now the buffered message passes the test and C delivers "A". C always sees question before answer, without any global order and without a leader.
intuition: A group of clerks who must all write the same minutes of a meeting: the only way to get identical minutes is to agree on one order of speakers, and everything else is how the clerks agree on it.
It is natural because ordering is the one thing that distributed copies cannot get for free. Delivery is easy to repeat and replicate, but the order in which concurrent events happened is not recorded anywhere; it must be decided. Once a group decides an order for each slot, the log is the agreed history, and everything else (the database state, the index, the cache) becomes a deterministic function of it. This explains why consensus is the core problem: any system that wants replicas to agree on a sequence of events, without a single machine that never fails, needs total-order broadcast.
mistakes: People often think reliable broadcast also orders messages. In fact it only guarantees that all correct processes deliver the same set of messages. Two processes may deliver m1 and m2 in opposite orders, and the replicas then diverge. Ordering is a separate guarantee, and total order is the expensive one.
People often think a timestamp from the sender's clock gives a total order. In fact real clocks are not synchronised exactly, so two events from different machines can be stamped in the wrong order, and ties need a rule. Lamport timestamps with a process ID as a tiebreaker give a total order consistent with causality, but delivery must still wait until no smaller timestamp can arrive, which needs messages from every process or a failure detector.
People often think a sequencer, or a leader, makes the total order cheap and solves the problem. In fact it moves the problem: when the sequencer fails, the group must elect a new one and agree on exactly which slots were assigned, and that is consensus. A naive sequencer that is restarted without this can assign the same number to two messages.
People often think total-order broadcast is the same as a message queue. In fact a queue delivers each message to one consumer, while broadcast delivers every message to every member, in a group-wide order, and gives guarantees on failure. A system such as Kafka provides a total order inside one partition, not across partitions.
People sometimes think that delivered means "received from the network". In fact the protocol decides when a received message may be delivered to the application. A message can sit in a holdback queue for a long time. Applications that treat network arrival as delivery lose the ordering guarantees.
tradeoffs: Choose best-effort broadcast when messages are idempotent, loss is tolerable, and later repair exists (a heartbeat or a periodic full state sync); it costs n − 1 messages. Choose reliable broadcast when everyone must eventually see every message even if the sender dies, and the order does not matter (for example, spreading membership changes or CRDT updates). The eager version costs O(n²) messages, the lazy version with a failure detector is cheaper but depends on detection time.
Choose causal broadcast when the data model needs only cause-before-effect (comments before replies, CRDT operations, collaborative editing), because it works without a leader, stays available during partitions and has low latency; you pay with a vector of n counters on each message (or a smaller encoding) and you accept that concurrent messages may be seen in different orders. Choose total-order broadcast when replicas must agree on a single history (a database log, a lock service, a bank ledger); you pay with a majority round trip per batch and unavailability on the minority side of a partition.
Choose a fixed leader or sequencer protocol (Raft, ZAB, Multi-Paxos) for throughput: one round trip from the leader to a majority, batching, and a simple log. You give up the ability to write at any node without a hop to the leader, and you have a short pause during leader changes (typically 1 to 10 seconds with default timeouts, shorter if tuned). Choose a leaderless or multi-leader scheme (EPaxos, per-key ordering, CRDTs) only if you have conflict-free operations or can resolve conflicts, because it gives low latency in many regions at the cost of complexity.
Choose a cluster of 3 nodes to tolerate 1 failure, or 5 nodes to tolerate 2. More nodes make writes slower, since the leader must wait for a larger majority, and do not increase the write throughput. Choose to place nodes within one region or metro area when write latency matters; a majority across continents turns each commit into a 100 to 200 ms round trip.
Choose crash-fault-tolerant protocols inside your own data centre. Choose Byzantine fault-tolerant protocols (PBFT, HotStuff, Tendermint) only when participants may lie, as in a blockchain among organisations; you need 3f + 1 nodes to tolerate f faults and pay more messages (often O(n²)) per decision.
friction: The first limit is the cost of agreement. Every ordered message needs a quorum round trip, so latency is bounded below by the network round trip to the slowest node of the fastest majority. Across regions this is tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Throughput is limited by the leader's CPU, network and disk, since all writes pass through one node; scaling means partitioning the log (Kafka partitions, sharded Raft groups), and then there is no order across partitions, and cross-partition operations need another coordination step.
The second limit is availability. A majority protocol stops making progress when it cannot reach a majority, which is a deliberate choice (the CAP theorem trade). Leader changes cause pauses, and a flapping leader, caused by timeouts shorter than a garbage-collection pause or a network hiccup, can lower availability more than a real crash.
The third is subtle correctness. The guarantees depend on stable storage: a node that acknowledges a message must have written it durably, and must not lose it when it reboots; disks that lie about fsync, or a node restored from an old snapshot, break safety. Reconfiguration (adding or removing nodes) is a known source of bugs in real implementations. Delivery to the application must be tied to the application state: if a process delivers a message, applies it, and crashes before recording that it applied it, replay after restart applies it twice, unless commands are idempotent or applied state is saved together with the log position.
The fourth is the model. Safety holds in the asynchronous model, but liveness needs some timing assumptions, which can fail under overload. And the group is usually fixed and small: the protocols described scale to tens of nodes, not thousands. Large systems use hierarchies, sharding, or probabilistic gossip-based broadcast (with weaker, eventual guarantees) when they need to reach many nodes.
practice: Do not write your own total-order broadcast. Use a replicated log you can trust: etcd or ZooKeeper for small coordination data (configuration, locks, leader election), Kafka for a high-throughput ordered log per partition, or a database with built-in consensus replication. Know which guarantee each provides: order within a partition, durability level (`acks=all` and `min.insync.replicas` in Kafka), and what happens on leader change.
Design the application so order is needed only where it matters. Put all operations that must be ordered with respect to each other on the same partition or key (for example all events of one campaign or one account), and let the rest be concurrent. Use idempotent commands with IDs, so that a retry after a timeout does not apply twice, and store the log position together with the state that was derived from it.
Choose the group size and layout by the failures you want to survive: three nodes in three zones for one failure, five for two, usually in one region. Monitor commit latency, the number of leader elections, replication lag per follower and disk fsync latency; frequent elections are a symptom of timeouts or overloaded nodes. Tune election timeouts to be above typical pauses, and test leader failure on purpose.
For event sourcing and change data capture, use the log as the single source of order and let consumers keep their own offsets; each consumer sees the same order, which is exactly what total-order broadcast promises. When you need causal consistency only (for example in a geo-replicated store), attach dependency metadata or use hybrid logical clocks instead of paying for total order.
interview: What is the relationship between total-order broadcast and consensus? || They are equivalent in the asynchronous crash model. A message is delivered at one replica, which then crashes. Could other replicas never deliver it? || Under non-uniform agreement, yes: only correct processes must agree. Under uniform agreement, no. Majority-based protocols give the uniform version, because a message is delivered only after a majority stored it, and any new leader sees it. This matters when the delivering process has an external effect, like replying to a client.
How would you implement causal broadcast, and what does it cost? || Attach a vector clock to each message and hold back a message at the receiver until it is the next one from its sender and everything it depends on has been delivered. Your Kafka consumers need the same order of events for one account, but ordering across accounts is irrelevant. How do you handle a leader that is partitioned away but still believes it is the leader? || Followers elect a new leader with a higher term, and any request carries the term. quiz: What does reliable broadcast guarantee that best-effort broadcast does not? || If any correct process delivers a message, all correct processes deliver it, even if the sender crashed during sending. Best-effort only guarantees this while the sender stays alive.

How does a vector clock decide whether a received message may be delivered in causal broadcast? || It delivers message m from p only if m's counter for p is exactly one more than the receiver's counter for p, and for every other process the message's counter is not greater than the receiver's. value: A distributed hash table (DHT) is a hash table whose entries are spread over thousands or millions of machines, with no central server and no machine that knows the whole membership. Any node can ask "who stores key K?" and get an answer after a small number of messages, usually about log₂ N hops for N nodes. A node that joins or leaves moves only a small part of the data. This makes the table scale to the size of the whole Internet and survive the constant arrival and departure of nodes (churn).
The idea was invented for peer-to-peer (P2P) file sharing. After Napster's central index was shut down by a court in 2001, and Gnutella's flooding search proved too expensive, researchers wanted a decentralised index with guaranteed lookup. Four systems appeared in 2001: Chord, CAN, Pastry and Tapestry. The design that won in deployment is Kademlia (2002), which runs today inside BitTorrent's Mainline DHT, in IPFS and libp2p, and in node discovery for Ethereum. A Kademlia network does not store files, only small records (who has this torrent, which peers know this ID), so the table stays tiny and the lookups are what matter.
The lower layers supply the ingredients. Hashing and hash tables (L3) give the interface (put, get) and the idea of mapping a key to a slot; here the "slots" are machines. Consistent hashing (L2) gives the way to place keys so that a join or leave moves only about 1/N of them: nodes and keys are hashed into the same ID space, and a key belongs to the nearest node. Random and small-world graphs (L4) explain why a routing table with only O(log N) links can still reach any node in O(log N) hops: links of doubling length behave like a small-world network that can be navigated greedily. Gossip protocols (L2) explain how nodes learn about new peers and repair their tables, by exchanging contacts during ordinary lookups and periodic refreshes.
Above it, blockchains and Ethereum use Kademlia-style tables only to find peers, not to store blocks.
terms: ID space — the set of all identifiers, for example all 160-bit numbers; nodes and keys both get an ID from it, usually by hashing.
Overlay network — a network built on top of the Internet in which nodes link to chosen peers and route by those links instead of by IP routing.
Finger table (Chord) — a node's list of O(log N) contacts, where the i-th entry points at the node responsible for the ID that is 2^(i−1) beyond itself on the ring.
XOR metric (Kademlia) — the distance between two IDs is their bitwise XOR read as a number; it is symmetric and has the triangle property.
k-bucket — a Kademlia list of up to k contacts (k is usually 20) whose IDs share the same length of prefix with the own ID.
Iterative lookup — the querying node itself contacts one node after another, each time asking for closer contacts, instead of letting the nodes forward the query.
Churn — the continuous joining and leaving of nodes; in public P2P networks the median node stays only minutes to hours.
Sybil attack — an attacker creates many fake node identities to control a part of the ID space.
history: Napster (1999) kept a central index of who has which song; a court order closed it in 2001. Gnutella (2000) removed the server and searched by flooding the query across the network, which does not scale: the traffic grows with the number of nodes and a rare file may not be found. Freenet (2000) routed by key closeness but gave no guarantee. The academic question was: can we have the decentralisation without losing the guarantee that an existing key is found in a bounded number of steps?
In 2001 four groups answered. Chord (Stoica, Morris, Karger, Kaashoek and Balakrishnan, at MIT, SIGCOMM 2001) placed nodes on a ring and used finger tables for O(log N) lookup. CAN (Ratnasamy et al., Berkeley) mapped keys to points in a d-dimensional space divided among nodes, with lookup cost O(d · N^(1/d)). Pastry (Rowstron and Druschel, Microsoft Research and Rice) and Tapestry (Zhao et al., Berkeley) used prefix routing, correcting one digit of the ID at a time. Chord's ring was inspired by the consistent hashing of Karger et al. (1997), which had been invented for web caches.
Petar Maymounkov and David Mazières at NYU published Kademlia in 2002. Its XOR distance makes every distance symmetric, so a node learns useful contacts from the queries it receives, which keeps the routing tables fresh at no extra cost. The first widely used DHT in practice was eDonkey's Overnet and then BitTorrent's Mainline DHT (2005 to 2008, formalised in BEP 5), which became the largest DHT in the world, with millions of simultaneous nodes. IPFS (2014 onwards) put a Kademlia DHT into libp2p. Ethereum's node discovery protocol uses a Kademlia-like table to find peers.
Meanwhile, the data-centre world took the ring and dropped the multi-hop routing. Amazon's Dynamo paper (2007) and then Cassandra and Riak used consistent hashing with full membership knowledge by gossip, so every node can route in one hop; this is sometimes called a "zero-hop DHT". It works because the cluster is small, trusted and has little churn. The multi-hop DHT stayed the answer for large, open, unreliable networks.
how: Every DHT answers the same question: given a key K, which node is responsible, and how do I reach it without knowing the whole network? The first step is a shared ID space. Each node gets an ID by hashing something (its public key or IP address); each key gets an ID by hashing the key. With a 160-bit hash such as SHA-1 the IDs are numbers from 0 to 2¹⁶⁰ − 1, and the ID of a node is random, so nodes are spread evenly. A key is stored on the node whose ID is closest to the key's ID, under a defined meaning of "closest". This is consistent hashing (L2): when a node is added, it takes over keys only from its neighbours, so about 1/N of the data moves.
Chord defines closest as "the first node clockwise from the key on the ring", called the successor of K. A node needs to know only its successor to be correct: a lookup can walk along the ring one node at a time, but that takes N/2 steps on average. To speed it up, each node keeps a finger table with m entries (m is the number of bits of the ID). Entry i points to the successor of \( n + 2^{i-1} \) (mod \( 2^m \)). So the fingers jump over 1, 2, 4, 8, ... positions of the ring. To look up K, a node forwards the query to the finger that is closest to K without passing it. This at least halves the remaining distance in each hop, so the lookup ends in at most m hops, and about ½ log₂ N hops on average. The table holds only m entries (say 160, but many fall to the same node, leaving about log₂ N distinct contacts). This is the same idea as binary search or a skip list: choosing a link whose length is a power of two, so each step halves what remains.
Kademlia defines closest by the XOR metric: \( d(x, y) = x \oplus y \), read as an integer. Each node keeps a routing table of up to 160 k-buckets; bucket i holds contacts whose distance from the node is in \( [2^i, 2^{i+1}) \), that is, those sharing exactly the first 159 − i bits of the ID. The buckets near the node's own ID are rarely full (few nodes exist there), while the far buckets hold k contacts out of half of the network. This gives log N knowledge: the node knows many far nodes and a few near ones, like the doubling distances of Chord. To look up K, the node picks the α closest contacts it knows (α is 3) and sends them FIND_NODE(K) in parallel; each replies with the k contacts it knows closest to K. The node merges the results, queries the closest ones it has not asked yet, and repeats until the k closest nodes it has seen have all answered. Each round fixes at least one more bit of the distance, so it needs O(log N) rounds. The final nodes store the value (STORE) or return it (FIND_VALUE).
Kademlia has two small rules that make it robust. First, the iterative lookup is run by the querying node, so a dead or slow node does not stall the chain, and the α parallel queries hide timeouts. Second, in a full bucket, a new contact is not inserted over an old one that still replies: the node pings the least-recently-seen entry, and only if it fails is the new one used. Studies of P2P networks showed that a node that has been online for an hour is likely to stay for another hour, so long-lived contacts are the most reliable, and this rule also makes it hard for an attacker to flush good contacts out of a table by flooding it with new IDs.
Joining and repair use the lookup itself, which is the gossip (L2) element. A new node contacts one known node, then runs a lookup for its own ID, and the nodes along the path learn about it, while it fills its buckets with the contacts that it meets. Nodes refresh each bucket that has not been used for an hour by looking up a random ID in its range. Because messages carry the sender's ID, every request that a node receives is also evidence of a live contact. Stored records are re-published periodically (hourly) by their owners and replicated on the k closest nodes, because nodes leave without warning. Records have a time-to-live (for example 24 hours) and are expired if not refreshed.
Why does this reach any node in O(log N) hops? Think of the routing structure as a graph. Each node has a few random-looking long links and many short ones, with the lengths spread on a log scale: the same structure as in small-world graphs (L4). Greedy routing (always step to the contact closest to the target) works in such a graph because a link at each scale exists. After each hop the remaining distance is at most half the previous one, so after \( \log_2 N \) hops it is zero.

In an hour the chance that all 20 replicas leave is \( 0.05^{20} \), which is negligible, and republishing every hour restores the missing replicas before the next hour's departures. Without republishing, after 10 hours about 40% of the replicas would be gone (1 − 0.95¹⁰), and the record would still survive with overwhelming probability, but after a few days it would not.
intuition: Finding a house in a country without a map by asking people: each person you meet knows many people far away and a few close by, so each answer at least halves the distance.
The design is natural because it is the same trick as binary search, applied to a network. To find one of N things in O(log N) steps you need a sorted order (the ID space) and the ability to jump by halves (links of doubling length). Hashing makes node IDs random, so every node is statistically equal and no node is special: no root, no index server and no single point of failure. The price is that each node knows only a logarithmic slice of the network, so it must be content with local information and ask other nodes for more.
mistakes: People often think a DHT is a database that you can query. In fact it supports one operation well: exact lookup by key. Range queries, prefix search and joins are not possible, because hashing destroys the order of the keys. Systems that need search build an index on top, which is expensive.
People often think DHT lookups are as fast as a hash table or a database. In fact each lookup needs log N network hops, and on the open Internet each hop takes 50 to 200 ms or fails. Lookups take seconds, not milliseconds. This is why DHTs suit discovery (find a peer, find a tracker) and not low-latency data serving.
People often think every distributed hash table is a DHT in this sense. In fact Dynamo, Cassandra and Redis Cluster hash keys onto nodes (consistent hashing or slots), but every node knows the whole membership and routes in one hop. The DHT of the research literature is for settings where no node can know all others. The data-centre systems took the partitioning idea and dropped the multi-hop routing.
People often think a DHT is secure because it is decentralised. In fact open DHTs are weak against Sybil and eclipse attacks. If an attacker can pick many node IDs near a target key (IDs are cheap), the attacker controls who answers lookups for that key and can hide or poison the data. Defences include IDs derived from IP addresses or public keys with proof of work, diversity rules for buckets, and verifying the content by its hash.
People sometimes think data is stored safely once it is put. In fact a record is only stored on nodes that may leave at any time. Without periodic republishing and replication on the k closest nodes, records vanish. A DHT gives soft state with expiry, not durable storage.
tradeoffs: Choose a multi-hop DHT (Kademlia) when the network is large (tens of thousands to millions of nodes), open and unreliable, and no organisation can run the index; you pay with lookups of O(log N) round trips, weak security guarantees and weak consistency. Choose consistent hashing with full membership (Dynamo style, Redis Cluster, Cassandra) when the cluster is under a few thousand trusted nodes: lookups take zero or one hop, membership is shared by gossip, and the system is easy to reason about. The DHT is the answer to "no one can know all nodes"; if someone can, do not use it.
Choose Kademlia's XOR over Chord's ring when churn is high and the table must repair itself from normal traffic: XOR is symmetric, so incoming queries refresh the table, and parallel iterative lookups mask failures. Choose Chord (or a ring) when you value a simple model and easy reasoning about successor lists, and the environment is more stable. CAN needs d dimensions and has O(d N^(1/d)) lookups, so it is mostly of historical interest; choose it only to learn.
Choose a larger bucket size k (20 instead of 8) when churn is high and you want reliability: more replicas and more alternatives per hop, at the cost of more table state and more traffic per lookup. Choose higher parallelism α (3 to 8) when latency matters more than bandwidth, since parallel queries reduce the effect of slow nodes but multiply the messages.
Choose a DHT for discovery and records that can be rebuilt (peer addresses, provider records), where stale answers are harmless. Choose a different system for bulk data and strong guarantees: IPFS uses the DHT to find who holds a block, and a direct transfer to move it. For data that must not be lost, use a replicated store with a proper consensus protocol, because DHT replication gives only best effort.
Choose to run your own bootstrap nodes and keep a cache of recent peers on disk: a DHT needs at least one known entry point to join, and a hard-coded list of a few well-run servers is the usual compromise between pure decentralisation and reliability.
friction: The main problem is security. The open join model means anyone can add any number of nodes. Sybil attacks give an adversary control of the responses in a region of the ID space; eclipse attacks surround a victim node so all its contacts are malicious. Studies of the BitTorrent Mainline DHT found large numbers of nodes that were not behaving correctly, and attacks that could monitor or censor chosen torrents. Mitigations exist, but they all add cost or reduce openness.
Performance is the second problem. Lookup latency is dominated by the slowest hop and by timeouts to dead contacts: in real networks a significant share of the entries in a routing table are stale at any time. Many nodes sit behind NATs and cannot accept incoming connections, so they cannot take part as full members, which lowers the useful size of the network. Latency is also unrelated to geography unless proximity-aware routing is added (Pastry and Tapestry did this, at the price of complexity).
Third is the weak data model. There is no range query, no transaction and no strong consistency; the answer to a get may be missing, old or poisoned. Load is uneven for popular keys, since one key lives on k nodes only, and a hot key (a viral torrent) can overload them; caching along the lookup path is a partial remedy. Balance of data across nodes is also uneven in simple schemes, because random IDs give a spread of about log N between the lightest and heaviest nodes unless virtual nodes are used.
Fourth, DHTs were largely overtaken in the data centre. Cloud object stores, consistent hashing with full membership and consensus-based metadata services solved the storage problem with stronger guarantees and lower latency. What remains are the places where decentralisation is the requirement: file sharing, content-addressed storage networks, peer discovery for blockchains, and some resilient messaging systems.
practice: Use a DHT only where you really cannot run or trust a central index. If you do need one, use an existing, maintained implementation: libp2p's Kademlia (go-libp2p-kad-dht, rust-libp2p), the Mainline DHT libraries for BitTorrent, or the discovery protocol your blockchain client already ships. Do not design the routing yourself.
If you run a cluster of your own machines, use consistent hashing with virtual nodes (100 to 256 per physical node) and gossip membership, as Cassandra and Dynamo do. Understand that this is the DHT's partitioning idea without the multi-hop routing, and you get 1-hop lookups. Keep the replication factor at 3 and the placement rack-aware.
When reading a system that claims to be a DHT, ask these questions. What is the ID space and the distance metric? How big is each routing table and what is the lookup hop count at your N? How are failed contacts detected and replaced? How are values replicated and republished, and what is the record's lifetime? What stops an attacker from choosing IDs near a target? How does a new node find its first contact?
For learning, implement a small Kademlia simulator: 1,000 nodes with 32-bit IDs, k-buckets with k = 8, iterative lookup with α = 3, and measure the average number of hops (it should be close to log₂ 1000 ≈ 10 at worst and often fewer) and what happens when you remove 30% of the nodes. Then add a Sybil node cluster and see the effect on a target key. This builds more intuition than reading three papers.
When a protocol needs only peer discovery, consider lighter options first: a list of well-known bootstrap servers, a gossip-based membership protocol, or DNS seeds, as Bitcoin does. A DHT adds complexity that is worth paying only when the network is large and open.

value: A thread pool is a fixed group of worker threads that live for the whole life of the program and take tasks from a queue. A caller does not start a thread for each piece of work. It hands a task to the pool (the Java word for the interface is an executor) and gets back a handle, usually called a future, to wait for the result. The pool gives two things that "one new thread per task" cannot: the cost of creating a thread is paid once, and the number of threads running at the same time has a ceiling.
The ceiling matters more than the saving. Threads are not free. Each one holds a stack (1 MB of address space by default for a Java thread on Linux x64), is scheduled by the kernel, and competes for the same CPU cores. When 5,000 threads try to run on 8 cores, the machine spends a growing share of its time switching between them, caches are cold after each switch, and memory fills with stacks. A server that starts a thread per request works at 100 requests and falls over at 10,000, in a way that gets worse exactly when load is highest. A pool turns this overload into a queue and a decision: wait, or reject.
The pool is the basic way a program decides how much work to do at once. It sits behind web servers, database drivers, `ExecutorService` in Java, `ThreadPoolExecutor` in Python, Tokio's blocking pool in Rust, the libuv pool in Node.js, and the thread pools of NGINX. It is also the base on which work stealing, fork/join frameworks and async runtimes are built.
It stands on several lower layers. Threads (L3) are the workers. Queues and ring buffers (L3) hold the pending tasks. Condition variables and monitors (L3) put idle workers to sleep and wake them when work arrives, so waiting costs no CPU. Semaphores and bounded buffers (L3) limit how many tasks may wait or run. Queueing theory and Little's law (L4) give the way to choose the size.
terms: Worker thread — a long-lived thread that loops: take a task from the queue, run it, repeat.
Task — a unit of work given to the pool, usually a function or object with a `run` or `call` method.
Executor — the interface that accepts tasks and decides when and on which thread they run.
Future — a handle for a task's result, which can be waited on, cancelled or combined with others.
Work queue — the buffer between submitters and workers; it can be bounded (fixed capacity) or unbounded.
Rejection policy — what the pool does when it is full: throw an error, run the task on the caller's thread, drop the task or drop the oldest.
CPU-bound / I/O-bound — a task is CPU-bound if it mostly computes; I/O-bound if it mostly waits for a network, disk or lock.
Thread starvation deadlock — a state in which all workers wait for results of tasks that are queued behind them in the same pool, so none can run.
history: Early servers handled one connection per process (the Unix `fork` model), and then one per thread when threads became common in the 1990s. Creating a thread or process for each request was costly, and under heavy load the system collapsed. Pre-forked and pre-threaded servers were the response: Apache's prefork and worker modes (1996 onward) keep a pool of workers waiting for connections.
The pattern was named and studied in the 1990s. Douglas Schmidt's work on ACE and the "Half-Sync/Half-Async" and "Leader/Followers" patterns (1995 to 2000) described how a pool of threads can share a set of event sources. Matt Welsh's SEDA (Staged Event-Driven Architecture, SOSP 2001) built servers as stages, each with its own queue and thread pool, and showed how bounded queues give admission control. In the same years Doug Lea's `util.concurrent` library appeared, and in 2004 it became `java.util.concurrent` in Java 5 with `ExecutorService`, `ThreadPoolExecutor` and `Future`. Brian Goetz's "Java Concurrency in Practice" (2006) taught a generation how to use it, and still gives the standard sizing advice.
Later developments moved in two directions. One was lighter workers: green threads and coroutines (Go's goroutines in 2009, Erlang processes, Kotlin coroutines, Rust's async tasks on the Tokio runtime from 2016) run millions of tasks on a small pool of OS threads. The other was smarter pools: Doug Lea's ForkJoinPool (Java 7, 2011) added work stealing so idle workers take tasks from busy ones. Project Loom brought virtual threads to Java 21 (September 2023, JEP 444): threads that are cheap enough to create one per task, so the old advice of pooling threads to save their cost no longer applies to them. The idea of a bounded pool for limiting concurrency remains, but is now done with a semaphore, not by sizing a pool.
how: The structure is a shared queue and N workers. Each worker runs a loop: take the next task from the queue; if the queue is empty, sleep; run the task; catch any exception so the worker does not die; repeat. Submitting a task puts it in the queue and, if a worker is sleeping, wakes one. All the waiting is done with the tools of the layers below. The queue is a thread-safe buffer (queues and ring buffers, L3), protected by a lock. An empty queue makes the worker wait on a condition variable (L3) that the submitter signals, so an idle pool uses no CPU. A bounded queue uses a second condition (or a semaphore, L3) for "not full", so a submitter can block when the buffer is full. This is the bounded-buffer producer-consumer problem, with submitters as producers and workers as consumers.
Java's `ThreadPoolExecutor` shows the real decisions in its constructor: `corePoolSize` (threads kept even when idle), `maximumPoolSize` (the upper limit), `keepAliveTime` (how long extra threads idle before exit), the `workQueue`, a `ThreadFactory` for names, and a `RejectedExecutionHandler`. The rule for a new task is easy to get wrong. If fewer than `corePoolSize` threads exist, start a new thread, even if others are idle. Otherwise, try to put the task in the queue. Only if the queue is full, start a thread up to `maximumPoolSize`. If that is full as well, reject. Thus with an unbounded queue, the pool never grows beyond the core size, and the maximum is ignored. `Executors.newFixedThreadPool(n)` uses an unbounded `LinkedBlockingQueue`, and under overload the queue grows until memory runs out. `newCachedThreadPool()` uses a `SynchronousQueue` (no buffer), so every task above the idle workers creates a new thread, with no limit: the opposite risk.
Choosing the size is queueing theory (L4). Little's law says that the average number of tasks in the system equals the arrival rate times the time each task spends in the system: \( L = \lambda W \). If 1,000 requests arrive per second and each takes 50 ms, then on average 1000 × 0.05 = 50 tasks are in flight, so you need at least 50 concurrent workers (more for bursts). For CPU-bound tasks, extra threads beyond the core count only add switching, so the size is about the number of cores (Goetz suggests N + 1, so one spare thread covers a brief page fault or pause). For tasks that wait, the classic formula is \( N_{\text{threads}} = N_{\text{cpu}} \times U \times (1 + W/C) \), where U is the target CPU use (0 to 1), W the waiting time and C the compute time of a task. A task that computes 5 ms and waits 45 ms has W/C = 9, so 8 cores at full use need 8 × 1 × 10 = 80 threads. Because of this, a pool should be sized by the kind of work. Mixing CPU-bound and blocking tasks in one pool is a common mistake: use separate pools, as Tokio does with its `spawn_blocking` pool and as NGINX does with its `aio threads` pool for file reads.
The queue and the rejection policy define what happens under overload, and so define the failure mode. A bounded queue gives backpressure: when it is full, submitters either block, get an error or run the task themselves. The "caller runs" policy executes the task on the submitting thread; since that thread is then busy, it stops submitting, which slows the sources naturally. An unbounded queue hides overload until memory or latency breaks: with 1,000 tasks waiting at 50 ms each across 50 workers, the last task waits one second. By Little's law, a long queue means high latency for every request, even if all of them would finish. Failing fast with a clear error is often better than serving everyone late.
Shutdown and errors need care. A graceful shutdown stops accepting tasks and lets queued tasks finish (`shutdown` then `awaitTermination`); a forced one interrupts running tasks. Exceptions thrown by a task submitted with `submit` are captured in the future and are lost if nobody calls `get`; with `execute` they reach the thread's uncaught-exception handler. Context such as the request ID, security identity and tracing span is stored in thread-local variables, which do not follow a task from the submitting thread to the worker; the pool must copy it explicitly, or a task will run with the context of the previous task that used the thread.
Variants change the queue structure. A pool with a shared queue has one lock that all workers contend for, which limits throughput to millions of tasks per second. Per-worker deques with work stealing (the next L2 concept) reduce the contention: each worker pushes and pops at its own end, and idle workers steal from the other end of a busy worker's deque. Event-loop runtimes use a small pool of threads (about one per core) for non-blocking work, and a second pool for blocking calls.

example: A service has 8 cores. Each request does 5 ms of CPU work and then waits 45 ms for a database. What pool size is right, and what happens at a burst?
Step 1: the compute time is C = 5 ms and the wait is W = 45 ms, so W/C = 9. For full CPU use the formula gives 8 × 1.0 × (1 + 9) = 80 threads. In practice, you target 70% to 80% CPU use, which gives 8 × 0.75 × 10 = 60 threads. The maximum throughput with this pool is the CPU limit: 8 cores / 5 ms = 1,600 requests per second. At 1,000 requests per second, Little's law gives 1000 × 0.05 = 50 requests in flight, so 60 threads cover the average with a margin of 10.
Step 2: a burst. Suddenly 3,000 requests arrive in one second (3× the pool's capacity of 1,600). In that second 1,600 can complete, and 1,400 remain waiting. With an unbounded queue the backlog grows by about 1,400 tasks per second while the burst lasts, and each new task waits longer. After a 10-second burst the queue holds about 14,000 tasks, and a new request waits about 14,000 / 1,600 ≈ 8.75 seconds before it even starts. Clients time out after 2 seconds and retry, which adds more load, and all of the work done on requests whose clients have given up is wasted. The service looks busy and produces nothing useful.
Step 3: a bounded queue. Set the queue capacity to 200, which is about 125 ms of work at 1,600 per second. Now a request waits at most 125 ms in the queue plus its 50 ms of work. Everything above the capacity is rejected at once with a clear error (HTTP 503, "try again"), and the client or a load balancer can send it elsewhere. The accepted requests keep a good latency, and the rejected ones fail fast. Latency is bounded at about 175 ms at the cost of refusing roughly 1,400 requests per second during the burst.

Step 4: a mistake to avoid. The handler of each request submits a sub-task to the same pool and blocks for its result. Under load, all 60 workers hold a parent task that waits for a child, and the children sit in the queue behind them. No worker is free to run a child, so nothing finishes: a thread starvation deadlock. The fixes are a separate pool for the child tasks, not blocking in a task (use callbacks or futures that compose), or a pool with work stealing that runs the child on the waiting thread.
intuition: A kitchen with a fixed number of cooks and a ticket rail: orders hang on the rail, each cook takes the next ticket, and when the rail is full the waiter tells new guests to wait outside.
It is natural because the machine has a fixed number of things it can do at once (cores, connections, disk heads), and the rest is waiting in line anyway. Starting more threads does not make the machine faster, it only moves the line from a visible queue to the inside of the scheduler and memory, where it is harder to see and to control. A pool puts the line where you can measure its length and decide what to do with it. The queue is the place where you apply policy: order, priority, limit, timeout.
mistakes: People often think more threads mean more throughput. In fact beyond the number of tasks that can make progress (cores for compute, connections for I/O), more threads only add context switches, cache misses, lock contention and memory use. Throughput rises until the bottleneck resource is busy, then stays flat or falls, while latency rises.
People often think `Executors.newFixedThreadPool` is a safe default. In fact its unbounded queue hides overload, and a slow downstream service makes the queue grow until the JVM runs out of memory or tasks wait minutes. Construct `ThreadPoolExecutor` yourself with a bounded queue and a rejection policy that you chose.
People often think the maximum pool size lets the pool grow under load. In fact the pool grows beyond the core only when the queue is full. With an unbounded queue (or a large one) the extra threads are never created. If you want threads before queueing, use a `SynchronousQueue` or set core equal to max.
People often think one shared pool for the whole application is simplest and best. In fact a slow dependency can fill the shared pool with blocked tasks, and then even unrelated fast tasks cannot run. Separate pools (bulkheads) for separate dependencies, and for CPU-bound and blocking work, limit the damage of one failure to one pool.
People sometimes think virtual threads (Java 21) make pools obsolete. In fact they make pooling for the sake of cheap creation unnecessary, since you create one virtual thread per task. You still need limits on the work that reaches a database or a remote service, which is done with a semaphore or a bounded connection pool, and a pool of platform threads is still right for CPU-bound work.
tradeoffs: Choose a fixed-size pool with a bounded queue and fast rejection when you serve latency-sensitive requests and want predictable behaviour under overload; you give up the ability to absorb large bursts and must handle the rejections. Choose a larger queue when the work is a batch or a background job where waiting is fine, and size the queue by the delay you accept (capacity = workers ÷ service time × acceptable wait), not by hope.
Choose one thread per core (about N or N + 1) for CPU-bound tasks; the pool keeps the cores busy without switching. Choose W/C-based sizing (tens or hundreds of threads) for tasks that block on I/O; you pay with more memory (1 MB stack per thread, so 500 threads are about 500 MB of address space, usually only partly committed) and more switching. Choose async I/O with a few threads when you need tens of thousands of concurrent connections, since it removes the thread-per-connection cost; you pay with callback or `async` complexity and the rule that no handler may block.
Choose virtual threads (Java 21 and later) when the code is simple blocking code that waits on I/O and concurrency is high, as they give the thread-per-request style at low cost; you must limit concurrent access to scarce resources with semaphores, and avoid long pinned sections (synchronised blocks that hold a lock while blocking; this was improved in later releases). Choose platform-thread pools for CPU-bound and native work.
Choose separate pools per dependency when failure isolation matters (a slow payment API should not stop search); you pay in idle threads and in tuning effort for each pool. Choose one pool with a semaphore per dependency when the number of pools would be large, since the semaphore limits concurrency without reserving threads.
Choose "caller runs" when you want natural backpressure from a producer you control; choose "abort" when the producer is a remote client that can retry; choose "discard" only for work that is safe to lose, such as sampled metrics.
friction: Sizing is a judgement under changing conditions. W/C changes when a database slows down: if the wait grows from 45 ms to 450 ms, the same 60 threads can serve only 133 requests per second (60 / 0.45), a tenfold drop, and the queue fills at once. A pool sized for one week of data fails in the next. So pool metrics (active threads, queue length, rejections, task wait time and run time) must be watched, and the size should be revisited when dependencies change.
Blocking and dependencies between tasks are hard. Starvation deadlock appears only under load. A single task that blocks forever (a lost response with no timeout) removes a worker from the pool permanently, and a few of them disable the service without any error. Every blocking call needs a timeout, and long tasks should be separated from short ones.
Context and fairness are a problem. Thread-local state leaks between tasks, a task that runs for minutes delays all tasks queued behind it (head-of-line blocking), and a shared FIFO queue treats all tenants alike, so one noisy client can use all capacity. Priority queues and per-tenant limits add complexity. The pool also does not give you an upper limit on latency; it only gives you a place to control it.
The costs at the hardware level also remain: a shared queue is a contended lock that limits scalability on machines with many cores, cache locality is lost when a task moves between threads, and NUMA effects appear on big servers. This is why high-performance runtimes switched to per-worker queues, thread-per-core designs and work stealing. Finally, each runtime has its own defaults, which are often surprising: Node's libuv pool has only 4 threads by default (`UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE`), which becomes a hidden bottleneck for file system and DNS calls; Python's `ThreadPoolExecutor` defaults to `min(32, cpu_count + 4)` workers and is limited by the global interpreter lock for CPU-bound code.
practice: Write down, for each pool, what it is for, what the tasks do (CPU or wait), what the size is and why, how large the queue is and what happens when it is full. Use named threads (`api-1`, `db-3`) so thread dumps and profiles are readable. Never use the unbounded convenience factories in production code for request handling.
Size from numbers. Measure the compute time C and the wait time W of a typical task under realistic load, use the W/C formula as the first guess, check with Little's law that the pool covers your peak rate times latency, and then load test while watching CPU use, queue length and p99 latency. Keep the queue small: its capacity should equal the delay that your callers can tolerate, multiplied by the pool's throughput.
Give every task a timeout and every blocking call a deadline. Set the rejection policy on purpose, and make rejections visible: count them, log them at a low rate, and return a clear error that clients can retry with backoff. Combine the pool with load shedding and circuit breakers so a failing dependency does not use up all the threads.
Isolate by purpose: one pool for request handling, one for blocking I/O calls to each important dependency, one for CPU-heavy work, and a scheduled pool for timers. Copy request context (trace ID, user, locale) into tasks explicitly with a wrapper, or use a library that does this. In Python, use `ThreadPoolExecutor` for I/O waits and `ProcessPoolExecutor` for CPU-bound work. In Node.js, raise `UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE` if file or crypto calls queue up. In Rust with Tokio, keep blocking code inside `spawn_blocking`. In Java 21 and later, consider virtual threads for blocking I/O code, with a semaphore in front of scarce resources.
Monitor: active threads versus pool size, queue depth, task queue wait time (time from submit to start, often the best overload signal), task run time, rejection count, and thread-pool saturation as a share of the time all workers are busy. Alert on queue wait time and rejections, not on CPU alone.

Why is an unbounded queue dangerous even if memory is large? || It converts overload into growing latency: by Little's law the wait equals queue length divided by throughput, so a long queue means every task is late, clients time out and retry, and work for abandoned requests is wasted. quiz: What are the two main benefits of a thread pool over one thread per task? || The cost of creating threads is paid once, and the number of concurrently running threads has a ceiling, so overload becomes a controlled queue and rejection instead of memory exhaustion and thrashing.

A task computes for 10 ms and waits 90 ms. How many threads keep 16 cores busy? || W/C = 9, so threads = 16 × (1 + 9) = 160.
By Little's law, how many requests are in flight at 2,000 requests per second with a mean latency of 80 ms? || L = λW = 2000 × 0.08 = 160 requests, so the pool needs at least 160 concurrent workers (plus margin).
When is a bounded queue with rejection better than an unbounded queue? || When latency matters and clients can retry: bounded queues keep waiting time limited and give early feedback to callers. An unbounded queue only delays the failure and adds latency and memory pressure.

@@ gemm
value: GEMM (GEneral Matrix Multiply) is the operation \( C \leftarrow \alpha AB + \beta C \). It is the most heavily optimised piece of numerical code in the world, because almost all heavy computing reduces to it: the dense layers and attention in neural networks, convolutions (after rearranging the data), linear solvers, the LINPACK benchmark that ranks supercomputers. Training a large language model is, to a first approximation, a very long sequence of GEMMs.
The problem is not the arithmetic, which is simple, but the memory. A naive triple loop computes the right answer, yet it runs at 1% to 5% of what the processor can do, because it keeps waiting for data. Blocked (tiled) GEMM changes the order of the loops so that a small tile of each matrix is loaded once into fast memory (registers, then L1, then L2, then shared memory on a GPU) and used many times before it is evicted. Every value loaded from slow memory is reused about b times, where b is the tile size. This converts the loop from limited by memory bandwidth to limited by arithmetic units, which is the best case.
Matrix multiplication is special because it has a lot of reuse hidden in it. Multiplying two n×n matrices does 2n³ floating-point operations on only 3n² numbers, so each number could be used about n times. Operations like adding two vectors (n operations on 3n numbers) have no such room. GEMM is the one routine for which the hardware's peak speed is reachable, and the rest of the stack is designed to turn its problems into GEMMs.
This concept stands on the memory hierarchy and cache lines (L3), which say what fast memory is, how big it is and how data moves in 64-byte lines; on the roofline model (L3), which tells you when a kernel is limited by bandwidth or by compute; on loop optimisation (L3), which gives the tiling, unrolling and vectorising transformations; on tensor cores and systolic arrays (L3), the hardware units that multiply small tiles in one instruction; and on linear algebra and numerical linear algebra (L4), which define the operation and show that BLAS level 3 is the base of dense algorithms. Above it are FlashAttention, PyTorch's linear layers and the CUDA libraries.
terms: GEMM — general matrix–matrix multiply, \( C = \alpha AB + \beta C \), with A of size m×k, B of size k×n and C of size m×n; it costs 2mnk floating-point operations.
FLOP — one floating-point operation; a fused multiply-add (FMA) counts as two.
Tiling (blocking) — splitting the matrices into small sub-blocks that fit in a cache level, and multiplying block by block.
Microkernel — the innermost routine of a GEMM library, usually hand-written in assembly or intrinsics, that multiplies a small register tile (for example 6×16) and holds C in registers.
Packing — copying a panel of A or B into a contiguous buffer in the order in which the microkernel reads it, so that access is sequential and cache-friendly.
Arithmetic intensity — floating-point operations per byte moved from main memory; the main number of the roofline model.
BLAS — Basic Linear Algebra Subprograms, the standard interface: level 1 (vectors), level 2 (matrix–vector), level 3 (matrix–matrix, which includes GEMM).
Tensor core — a GPU unit that multiplies a small tile (for example 16×8×16) in one instruction, usually with low-precision inputs and higher-precision accumulation.
history: The operation is old, but the idea of making it fast through structure is from the late 1970s. Lawson, Hanson, Kincaid and Krogh defined the BLAS level 1 routines (1979) for vectors, to give libraries a standard set of building blocks that vendors could optimise. LINPACK (1979) used them. But on the vector supercomputers and then on machines with caches, vector operations were not enough: they do O(n) work on O(n) data, so they run at memory speed. Dongarra, Du Croz, Hammarling and Hanson added level 2 (matrix–vector, 1988) and level 3 (matrix–matrix, 1990), and LAPACK (1992) was rewritten as blocked algorithms that call level 3 BLAS for most of their work.
In 1981 Jia-Wei Hong and H. T. Kung proved the theory: with a fast memory of M words, multiplying two n×n matrices by the standard algorithm needs at least on the order of \( n^3/\sqrt{M} \) words moved from slow memory, and blocked algorithms reach this bound. It was the first "red-blue pebble game" result on the cost of data movement, and it explains why tile size follows the cache size.
Vendors in the 1990s hand-tuned GEMM for each chip, and the work was slow. ATLAS (Whaley and Dongarra, 1998) automated it by running a search over tile sizes and loop orders when the library was installed. Kazushige Goto's GotoBLAS (2002 onwards) showed that careful attention to the TLB and cache with packing of panels could beat vendor libraries, and Goto and van de Geijn formalised this in "Anatomy of High-Performance Matrix Multiplication" (2008), the algorithm that OpenBLAS and BLIS follow. BLIS (van de Geijn's group, 2013 onwards) made the structure explicit: five loops around one microkernel, so a new chip needs only a new microkernel.
GPUs brought a second wave. NVIDIA's cuBLAS (2007) with CUDA, the 2016 Pascal generation that added FP16, and the 2017 Volta generation with tensor cores turned GEMM into a hardware instruction. CUTLASS (2017) published the GPU tile hierarchy as open C++ templates. Deep learning, from AlexNet (2012) onwards, made GEMM the main workload of the whole industry. Recent hardware (Hopper, Blackwell) keeps pushing the same idea with larger tiles, asynchronous copies, and lower precisions (FP8, FP4).
how: Begin with the naive algorithm, three nested loops: for i in 0..m, for j in 0..n, for p in 0..k, add \( A_{ip} B_{pj} \) into \( C_{ij} \). It does 2mnk operations. The problem is the memory access pattern. For row-major storage, \( A_{ip} \) walks along a row (good), but \( B_{pj} \) walks down a column (stride n, a new cache line at every step), and for a large n the whole of B is read again for every row of C. Counting traffic without any cache, each multiply-add loads two values from memory: an arithmetic intensity of 1 flop per 4 bytes or less, far below what a modern processor needs (its ridge point is roughly 5 to 20 flops per byte of main memory bandwidth). The loop is memory-bound, as the roofline model (L3) says, and no amount of faster arithmetic helps.
Blocking fixes the reuse. Split C into tiles of b×b and compute each tile as a sum of products of tiles from A and B. Load one tile of A and one of B (2b² values) into fast memory, and perform \( b^3 \) multiply-adds on them, i.e. 2b³ flops. The intensity is 2b³ flops for 2b² values, so each value loaded is used b times. If three tiles must fit in a cache of M words, then \( 3b^2 \le M \), so \( b \approx \sqrt{M/3} \), and traffic is about \( 2n^3/b = O(n^3/\sqrt{M}) \), matching the lower bound of Hong and Kung. A larger fast memory therefore gives a proportionally higher intensity. For 4-byte floats, a tile of size b gives an intensity of about b/4 flops per byte, and when this exceeds the machine's ridge point (peak flops divided by memory bandwidth), the kernel becomes compute-bound.
The Goto algorithm applies tiling at every level of the hierarchy. It has five loops. The outer loops split n into blocks of \( n_c \), k into blocks of \( k_c \) and m into blocks of \( m_c \). A panel of B of size \( k_c \times n_c \) is packed into a contiguous buffer that stays in the L3 cache (or L2), and a block of A of size \( m_c \times k_c \) is packed into a buffer that stays in the L2 cache. The two inner loops step through the packed panels in small pieces of \( m_r \) rows and \( n_r \) columns, and call the microkernel on each. The microkernel keeps an \( m_r \times n_r \) tile of C in vector registers for the whole k loop; each step loads one column of A (as \( m_r \) scalars to broadcast) and one row of B (as vectors) from L1, and does \( m_r \times n_r \) FMAs. On an AVX2 chip with 16 vector registers of 8 floats, a 6×16 tile uses 12 registers for C, 2 for B and 1 for A. So per step it loads 6 + 16 = 22 floats from L1 and does 6 × 16 = 96 FMAs. The loads are hidden behind the FMA units, which is why the kernel can reach 90% to 95% of peak.
Why packing? The tile of A is not contiguous in memory: its rows are n elements apart. Reading them directly causes cache-line waste (only part of each 64-byte line is used) and TLB misses (each row sits in a different page). Copying the tile into a buffer in the exact order the microkernel reads costs O(mk) work, which is small compared to the O(mnk) arithmetic, and makes all accesses sequential and aligned. Cache lines (L3) are then fully used, which is the point of that layer. Prefetching hides the rest of the latency.
Vectorisation and instruction-level parallelism come from the loop-optimisation layer (L3). The microkernel is unrolled so that independent FMAs fill the pipeline: with an FMA latency of 4 cycles and two FMA units, at least 8 independent accumulator registers are needed to keep both busy, which the 12-register C tile provides. Multiple cores are used by splitting the outer m or n loops among threads, so each thread has its own packed buffers.
On a GPU the same hierarchy exists with different names. A thread block computes a tile of C (for example 128×128), looping over k in steps of 8 to 32. Each step copies a tile of A and B from global memory into shared memory (usually through asynchronous copies, double-buffered so loading overlaps computing), then each warp reads its part into registers and issues tensor-core instructions (such as `mma.sync` on 16×8×16 tiles) that accumulate into registers. Tensor cores (L3) change the arithmetic: inputs in FP16, BF16 or FP8 and sums in FP32, which multiplies the peak rate several times. The structure is the same: global memory to shared memory to registers to the multiply units, with each level reusing its tile as much as possible.
Related algorithms. Strassen's algorithm multiplies in \( O(n^{2.807}) \) instead of \( O(n^3) \) by using 7 sub-multiplications instead of 8, but the extra additions, memory traffic and weaker numerical stability mean it pays off only for very large matrices, and mainstream BLAS libraries do not use it. Attention in transformers is two GEMMs with a softmax in between, and FlashAttention tiles both so the large n×n score matrix is never written to main memory.

example: Multiply two 1024×1024 matrices of 4-byte floats. The work is 2n³ = 2 × 1024³ ≈ 2.15 × 10⁹ flops. The three matrices occupy 3 × 1024² × 4 bytes = 12.6 MB, so the minimum traffic to read A and B and write C is 12.6 MB. A perfect algorithm therefore has an intensity of 2.15 × 10⁹ / 12.6 × 10⁶ ≈ 170 flops per byte.
Naive loops. In the worst case, with no useful cache reuse, each of the 1.07 × 10⁹ multiply-adds loads two floats: 2.15 × 10⁹ floats = 8.6 GB. On a machine with 50 GB/s of memory bandwidth this takes 8.6 / 50 ≈ 0.17 s just to move data, which corresponds to 2.15 × 10⁹ / 0.17 ≈ 12 GFLOP/s. In practice, column access in B causes cache-line waste, and the speed is often 1 to 5 GFLOP/s.
Blocked with b = 32. Three 32×32 tiles of floats use 3 × 1024 × 4 = 12 KB, which fits in a 32 KB L1 cache. Each tile product loads 2 × 1024 floats and does 2 × 32³ = 65,536 flops. The traffic (counting L1 loads from L2) is about 2n³/b = 2 × 1.07 × 10⁹ / 32 = 67 million floats = 268 MB, thirty-two times less than the naive 8.6 GB. The intensity from the next level is 2.15 × 10⁹ / 268 × 10⁶ ≈ 8 flops per byte. At 50 GB/s this bounds speed at 400 GFLOP/s, so the memory is no longer the limit for a core that can do 100 GFLOP/s.
What can one core do? An AVX2 core with two FMA units, each working on 8 floats, completes 2 × 8 × 2 = 32 flops per cycle. At 3 GHz this is 96 GFLOP/s. A well-tuned library (OpenBLAS, MKL, BLIS) reaches about 85 to 95 GFLOP/s on this core for a large GEMM, and the 1024³ problem takes about 2.15 × 10⁹ / 90 × 10⁹ ≈ 24 ms. The same problem in a plain Python loop takes minutes, and in naive C about 1 to 2 seconds. The difference between the naive and the blocked version in C is about 50 times, without changing the arithmetic.
A GPU example. An NVIDIA H100 (SXM) has a dense BF16 tensor-core peak of about 990 TFLOP/s and a memory bandwidth of about 3.35 TB/s, which gives a ridge point of 990 / 3.35 ≈ 295 flops per byte. For a square GEMM with n×n BF16 matrices (2 bytes per element), the minimum traffic is 3n² × 2 = 6n² bytes and the work is 2n³, so the intensity is n/3 flops per byte. To exceed 295, we need n > 885. So a 4096×4096 GEMM (intensity 1365) is compute-bound and can reach 70% to 80% of peak, while a 256×256 GEMM (intensity 85) is memory-bound and can reach at most 85 / 295 = 29% of peak. This is why small batch sizes in inference run far below the advertised speed.
intuition: Reading a recipe book while cooking: instead of walking to the shelf for each ingredient, you bring one tray of ingredients to the counter and cook as many dishes as possible before you go back.
Matrix multiplication reuses every number many times, but only if the numbers stay close to the arithmetic units while being reused. Blocking is the plan that chooses which numbers to keep close: the tile that fits in the nearest memory, used as much as it can be. Each level of the memory hierarchy is an answer to the same question with a different size, so the tile sizes follow the cache sizes. The lower bound of Hong and Kung shows this is not a trick but the best possible order of magnitude.
mistakes: People often think GEMM speed depends on the number of operations. In fact the operation count (2mnk) is fixed, and the speed depends on how many bytes move. Two implementations with the same flops can differ by 50 times because one reuses data and the other does not.
People often think a smart compiler optimises a naive triple loop to a fast GEMM. In fact compilers can vectorise the inner loop and may interchange loops, but they do not find the five-loop structure with packing, the register tile sized for the exact number of registers, and the cache-size dependent blocking. A naive loop compiled with `-O3` typically reaches 5% to 15% of a tuned BLAS. Use the library.
People often think a larger tile is always better. In fact the tile must fit in its cache level together with the other operands, and in registers a larger tile spills to the stack and loses all the benefit. For GPUs, larger tiles use more shared memory and registers per block, reducing the number of blocks that can run at once and so lowering occupancy. The best tile is a balance found by search.
People often think GEMM libraries are only for science and not relevant for software engineers. In fact most of the compute cost of machine learning, recommendation and search ranking systems is spent in GEMM. Shapes matter: a matrix with one tiny dimension (a batch of 1) turns into a matrix–vector product, which is memory-bound and runs at a fraction of peak.
People sometimes think lower precision always gives the same result faster. In fact FP16, BF16 and FP8 reduce the memory traffic and use faster units, but they change rounding, so accumulation is done in FP32 (or higher) to keep errors small. Results differ in the last digits between libraries and hardware, and summation order changes the answer, so exact reproducibility across devices is not guaranteed.
tradeoffs: Choose a tuned library (MKL, OpenBLAS, BLIS, cuBLAS) for any dense matrix product in production; it takes hours to call and years to match. Choose to write your own kernel (CUTLASS, Triton, hand-written) only when you need a fusion that libraries cannot offer, such as GEMM plus bias plus activation in one pass, a special data layout, or an unusual precision. The gain comes from not writing the intermediate result to main memory; the price is engineering time and per-architecture tuning.
Choose bigger tiles for large problems, where the intensity is the limit, and smaller tiles (or split-k, which splits the k loop over several blocks and sums the results) for small or skinny matrices that otherwise cannot fill the machine. A 64-core CPU or a GPU with 132 SMs needs enough independent tiles to keep all units busy: a matrix of 512×512 with 128×128 tiles has only 16 tiles, which leaves most of an H100 idle.
Choose lower precision (BF16, FP8, INT8) when memory bandwidth or compute is the bottleneck and the model tolerates the rounding (inference, and training with scaling and FP32 accumulation); you gain 2 to 4 times in speed and memory. Choose FP32 or FP64 when accuracy matters, as in scientific simulation and some solvers; you pay in speed. TF32 is a compromise on NVIDIA GPUs: FP32 range with 10-bit mantissa.
Choose batched or grouped GEMM when you have many small independent products (attention heads, mixture-of-experts experts, small solves): a single call keeps the device busy, and many separate small calls waste launch time. Choose a fused attention kernel (FlashAttention) over two separate GEMMs when the sequence is long, as it avoids writing an n×n matrix to memory; for short sequences, two plain GEMMs are fine.
Choose Strassen-like or other fast algorithms only for very large exact or well-conditioned problems on which you have measured a gain; for almost all workloads the standard algorithm with perfect tiling wins, because of constants and stability.
friction: The first problem is portability of performance. A GEMM tuned for one chip (cache sizes, register count, vector width, FMA latency) is wrong for another, so every new architecture needs a new microkernel and new tile sizes. Autotuning (ATLAS, cuBLAS heuristics, Triton autotune) helps but is itself costly, and the shape space is huge: m, n, k, layouts, transposes, data types, alignments. Libraries ship thousands of kernels and choose among them with heuristics that are sometimes wrong for unusual shapes.
Second, hardware outpaces memory. Compute throughput has grown faster than memory bandwidth for decades; the ridge point of GPUs rose from about 10 flops per byte to nearly 300. As a result, more operations fall on the memory-bound side, and techniques such as fusion, quantisation and sparsity are needed to get the benefit of faster math. Small matrices, low batch sizes, and the decoding phase of language-model inference (matrix–vector products) are memory-bound by construction.
Third, GEMM is not the only thing: the elementwise operations around it (layer norm, activation, softmax) are memory-bound and, in a model, can account for a substantial share of the time unless fused. And a GEMM at peak speed uses a lot of power: clocks fall under heavy tensor-core load, so measured speed is below the number on the data sheet.
Fourth, numerical and reproducibility issues. Different blocking changes the order of additions, and floating-point addition is not associative, so results differ slightly between libraries, thread counts and GPUs. Low-precision formats add overflow and underflow risks that need scaling. And the extreme performance depends on assembly and intrinsics that are hard to read, test and maintain: a small number of experts maintain code that the whole industry depends on.
practice: Never write a matrix product loop for real data in production code; call `numpy.matmul`, `torch.matmul`, `cblas_sgemm` or `cublasGemmEx`. Make sure the library you use is the fast one: NumPy linked with OpenBLAS or MKL, PyTorch built with cuBLAS. Check the number of threads (`OMP_NUM_THREADS`, `MKL_NUM_THREADS`) so that parallel code does not oversubscribe the cores.
Think in shapes. Express your computation as large GEMMs where possible: batch many small products into one (a single (batch × features) times (features × out) multiplication instead of one vector product per item), and make dimensions multiples of the hardware tile (multiples of 8 or 64 for tensor cores, of the vector width on CPUs). A hidden size of 4096 uses the GPU far better than 4001, and padding 4001 up to 4096 is often faster than running it unpadded.
Use the roofline to diagnose. Compute the flops and the minimum bytes for your operation, divide to get the intensity, and compare it with the ridge point of the machine. If you are below it, optimise the traffic (fusion, lower precision, larger batches). If above, you are limited by compute and should check that you use the tensor cores or SIMD units. Measure achieved GFLOP/s against the peak: a large GEMM below 70% of peak on a modern GPU is a sign of a bad shape, layout or library setting.
Pick precision on purpose: BF16 or FP16 with FP32 accumulation for training and most inference; FP8 where the stack supports it with scaling; FP32 or FP64 where accuracy rules. Keep layouts consistent to avoid transposes and copies, and prefer fused library calls (GEMM with epilogue) for bias and activation.
To learn, implement the naive loop, then a tiled loop, then a version with a register tile and vectorisation, and measure each against OpenBLAS on your laptop. Seeing the gap close from 1 GFLOP/s to 80 GFLOP/s teaches more than any description. The papers by Goto and van de Geijn and the BLIS tutorials give the exact path.
interview: Why is a naive triple-loop matrix multiplication slow and what does tiling change? || It does the right number of flops but reuses no data: B is read with stride, and each multiply-add loads two values from memory, giving a tiny arithmetic intensity, so it is memory-bound. Tiling loads b×b tiles that fit in cache and uses each loaded value b times, raising intensity by about b and cutting traffic to O(n³/√M), which makes the kernel compute-bound.
How do you choose tile sizes? || From the memory sizes: the register tile from the number of vector registers and FMA latency (enough independent accumulators), the L1 tile so the B sliver fits, the L2 block so the packed A block fits, and the L3 panel for B. On GPUs, from shared memory and register budget and the occupancy needed. Libraries autotune, and the shapes and dtypes change the best choice.
What is the roofline model and how does it apply to a GEMM of size n on a GPU? || Performance is bounded by min(peak flops, bandwidth × intensity). For BF16 the intensity of an n×n×n GEMM is about n/3 flops per byte; with a ridge point of ~295 on an H100, you need n above ~900 to be compute-bound. Smaller or skinny shapes are limited by bandwidth, which is why small batches underuse the chip.
Why does decoding with batch size 1 in a language model run far below peak FLOPs? || With a batch of 1 each weight matrix is multiplied by a vector, a GEMV: 2 flops per weight, and each weight (2 bytes) is read once, so the intensity is about 1 flop per byte, far below the ridge point. The step is bound by memory bandwidth, so speed is set by how fast weights stream from HBM. Larger batches, speculative decoding and quantisation raise the intensity or cut the bytes.
Why is Strassen not used in BLAS libraries? || It reduces the exponent to about 2.81 but adds many matrix additions (memory-bound), needs temporary buffers, is harder to tile, and is less numerically stable. The cross-over size is large, and at that size tuned standard GEMM is already close to the hardware peak. Some libraries offer it only as an option.
How does GEMM relate to convolution? || A convolution can be rearranged (im2col) into one big matrix multiplication of the unrolled input patches with the filter matrix, at the cost of extra memory, or computed implicitly by a GEMM-like kernel that forms the patches on the fly. This is why convolution speed on GPUs follows GEMM speed, and why tensor cores accelerate it.

When would you write a custom kernel instead of calling cuBLAS? || When you need to fuse operations around the GEMM (bias, activation, dequantisation, attention softmax) to avoid writing intermediate results to memory, or need a layout or precision the library lacks. Otherwise the library is faster and cheaper to maintain.

value: A trusted execution environment (TEE) is a hardware-protected place on a processor where code can run and keep its data secret, even from the operating system, the hypervisor and the administrator of the machine. This reverses the usual trust model. Normally the operating system is the most privileged software, so anything it can read, a hacked OS or a cloud operator can also read. In a TEE, the CPU itself enforces the wall: memory of the protected region is encrypted with keys that live inside the processor, and privileged software sees only encrypted bytes.
The second half is remote attestation. A TEE can produce a signed statement, "this exact code (this hash) is running inside a genuine protected region on a genuine CPU", signed by a key that the CPU maker certified. A remote party can check the signature and the hash before it sends any secret. So the question "is it safe to give my data to that machine?" changes from "do I trust the cloud company?" to "do I trust the CPU maker, and is the code I audited the code that runs?".
This is the base of confidential computing: protecting data in use, in addition to data at rest (disk encryption) and data in transit (TLS). It is used for processing sensitive data in a public cloud, for multi-party data collaboration where no party wants to show its data to the others (ad-tech and healthcare clean rooms), for protecting keys and secrets (phone secure enclaves, key management), and for private AI inference on confidential GPUs.
The layers below provide the tools. Virtual memory and paging (L3) are how the hardware intercepts every memory access to apply encryption and access checks. Symmetric encryption (AES, L3) protects the memory contents at line speed with keys that never leave the CPU. Digital signatures and PKI (L3) turn a measurement into a verifiable attestation. Authorization models (L2) explain the odd policy: the most privileged software on the machine is denied access to the enclave. Side-channel attacks (L3) are the main known weakness. Above it, privacy technology such as clean rooms, security architecture and the confidential-computing GPUs depend on it.
terms: Enclave — a protected region of a process (Intel SGX style) whose memory is encrypted and cannot be read by the rest of the process, the OS or the hypervisor.
Confidential VM — a whole virtual machine whose memory is encrypted and protected from the hypervisor (AMD SEV-SNP, Intel TDX).
Trusted computing base (TCB) — everything you must trust for the security claim to hold; a smaller TCB is better.
Measurement — a cryptographic hash of the code and initial data loaded into the TEE, taken by the hardware or firmware as it loads.
Remote attestation — a signed report, produced by the TEE, that proves what is running and on what hardware, bound to a fresh challenge from the verifier.
Sealing — encrypting data with a key derived from the CPU's secret and the TEE's identity, so only the same code on the same CPU can decrypt it later.
Root of trust — the keys fused into the hardware at manufacture, from which all attestation signatures are derived.
Side channel — information leaking through timing, cache state, power or other effects of execution, not through the intended interface.
history: The idea of hardware-protected code is old. ARM TrustZone (2004) splits a processor into a "secure world" and a "normal world", used for fingerprints, payment keys and DRM on phones. The Trusted Platform Module (TPM), specified by the Trusted Computing Group from 2003 (TPM 2.0 in 2014), is a small chip that stores keys and records hashes of boot software in registers (PCRs), so that a verifier can see what the machine booted; this is "measured boot". Neither could protect an arbitrary application from a hostile operating system on a general-purpose server.
Intel Software Guard Extensions (SGX) did that. The ideas were published at a workshop in 2013 and the instructions shipped with the Skylake processors in 2015. An application could create an enclave, a region of memory that the CPU encrypts and integrity-protects, with a size limited to the Enclave Page Cache (about 128 MB at first, with 93 MB usable). The 2016 paper "Intel SGX Explained" by Costan and Devadas gave an independent description of the architecture. SGX was used for Signal's contact discovery, for the Azure Confidential Computing service (2017), and in research systems that ran whole databases and containers in enclaves, such as VC3, Haven and SCONE.
SGX had two problems: a small memory size and a long list of attacks. Foreshadow (2018), Plundervolt (2019), SGAxe (2020) and others broke its confidentiality guarantees through speculative execution, voltage control and cache side channels; Intel responded with microcode updates. Intel removed SGX from its consumer processors in 2021 to 2022, and kept it for servers. The industry turned to virtual-machine-level designs, which protect whole VMs and so avoid rewriting applications. AMD SEV (2016) encrypted VM memory with a per-VM key; SEV-ES (2017) protected register state; SEV-SNP (2020 to 2021, with the Milan processors) added integrity protection against a malicious hypervisor, with a reverse map table. Intel Trust Domain Extensions (TDX) came with 4th generation Xeon (Sapphire Rapids) in 2023. ARM announced the Confidential Compute Architecture (Realms) in 2021. NVIDIA's H100 (2022 to 2023) was the first GPU with confidential computing, and Blackwell continues it.
The most recent work targets the assumption that the memory bus is safe. In October 2025, several groups published attacks that place a custom interposer (a device costing under about 1,000 US dollars) between the CPU and DDR5 memory to observe the encrypted traffic, and showed that the deterministic encryption used by some server TEEs lets an attacker extract keys. The vendors state that physical attacks are outside the threat model of these products.
how: A TEE needs four building blocks: isolation of memory, a root of trust, measurement and attestation. They fit together as follows.
Isolation. The memory controller sits between the CPU cores and DRAM. In a TEE-capable processor, a memory encryption engine in that path encrypts every cache line written to DRAM with AES, and decrypts it when read, using a key held in the CPU and never exposed to software. The data is plaintext inside the caches and registers, and ciphertext outside the chip, in DRAM, on the bus and in swap. Access control comes from the paging machinery (virtual memory, L3). The processor adds ownership information to its address translation: a page belongs to a particular enclave or confidential VM, and any access from another context, including the OS kernel and the hypervisor, is blocked or returns ciphertext. In SGX, an Enclave Page Cache Map records the owner of each page. In SEV-SNP, a reverse map table (RMP) records for each physical page which guest owns it and at what address it is mapped, so a hypervisor cannot silently remap or replay a guest page. In TDX, the equivalent is a secure EPT managed by a trusted module.
Integrity and freshness. Encryption alone does not stop an attacker from changing ciphertext or replaying an older copy. SGX keeps a Merkle-style integrity tree over the protected memory with counters, which detects tampering and replay, at the cost of limited size and speed. SEV-SNP and TDX use per-line integrity tags or the ownership tables against remapping, but relax the protection against physical replay. This difference is the reason why bus attacks affect some designs more than others.
Root of trust. At manufacturing, the CPU maker burns a unique secret key into fuses of the chip. The maker also certifies the matching public key, directly or through a chain of certificates (for AMD, the chain goes through the AMD Root Key and a per-chip VCEK; for Intel, through a provisioning service and the DCAP infrastructure). All attestation keys are derived from this root, so a signature proves that a real chip of that family created it.
Measurement. When an enclave or a confidential VM is created, the hardware or a small trusted firmware component hashes the initial contents loaded into it (code, initial data, configuration) into a measurement register (MRENCLAVE in SGX, the launch digest in SEV-SNP, MRTD in TDX). Any change to the code changes the hash. Later software can extend the measurement with more values, as in the TPM's PCRs.
Attestation. The verifier (for example a data owner) sends a fresh random nonce. The TEE asks the hardware for a report that contains the measurement, the security version of the CPU firmware, some flags such as "debug mode off", and the nonce, optionally with a hash of a public key that the enclave generated for a secure channel. The hardware signs it with its attestation key. The verifier checks the certificate chain up to the vendor's root, the signature, the freshness (the nonce), the measurement against the value it expects (from a reproducible build or an audit), and the firmware version against known vulnerabilities. If all checks pass, the verifier opens a TLS-like channel whose key is bound to the attested report, and sends its secrets directly into the TEE. Digital signatures and PKI (L3) provide the trust chain; this step is the heart of the design, because without it the TEE would be a locked box with an unknown content.
Sealing and I/O. To keep data between runs, the TEE derives a sealing key from the CPU secret plus its measurement or signer identity, and encrypts data to untrusted storage with it. Only the same code on the same chip can open it again. Network and disk I/O are handled by untrusted software, so the TEE must encrypt and authenticate everything that leaves it (TLS terminated inside the enclave, encrypted storage). For GPUs, NVIDIA's confidential mode encrypts the traffic over PCIe between the CPU's TEE and the GPU with a session key established after attestation of the GPU, and the GPU memory is protected by its own isolation.
Programming models differ. Process-level enclaves (SGX) give a tiny TCB, only the enclave code, but the application must be split into trusted and untrusted parts, and system calls must be proxied out. Library OSes (Gramine, Occlum, SCONE) run an unmodified program inside the enclave with a small OS layer. VM-level TEEs (SEV-SNP, TDX) run a normal guest OS and applications unchanged, with a larger TCB (the whole guest), and are therefore easier to adopt.

example: A bank and an ad-tech company want to compute how many of the bank's 2 million customers saw a campaign (which the ad company logged for 50 million devices), without either side giving its list to the other. They agree to use a confidential VM on a cloud provider's AMD SEV-SNP machine.
Step 1: audit and build. Both parties review the open-source matching program (about 1,500 lines) and build it reproducibly into a VM image. They compute its hash, `H = 9f3a...`, and each stores the same value as the expected measurement.
Step 2: launch. The cloud starts the confidential VM from this image. The hardware measures the image into the launch digest. The cloud operator, who controls the host and the hypervisor, can start the VM, stop it, or look at its memory, but sees only ciphertext.
Step 3: attest. The bank sends a random 32-byte nonce to the VM. The VM asks the AMD secure processor for an attestation report that includes the launch digest, the firmware version, the policy flags (debug disabled) and the nonce, and signed with the chip-specific key (VCEK). The bank fetches the certificate chain from AMD, and checks: the chain leads to AMD's root key; the signature is valid; the nonce is the one it sent (so the report is not replayed); the digest equals H; the firmware version is recent enough to include fixes for known flaws. It repeats this check for the ad company. If the cloud had swapped the program for a modified one that copies the lists, the digest would not equal H and both parties would refuse to continue.
Step 4: provision. Each party encrypts its hashed customer or device list under a key negotiated with the VM, over a channel bound to the attested report, and uploads it. Only the code in the VM can decrypt it. The lists never exist as plaintext outside the CPU.
Step 5: compute and release. The program intersects the lists (hashed identifiers, sorted or using a hash set; 2 million against 50 million takes seconds) and releases only the aggregate count, plus noise or a threshold such as "do not report counts below 100". The VM then wipes the data. Both parties receive the same number. Because the program was audited, they know that nothing else leaves.
Step 6: what the hardware does not protect. The program must not leak through its output (a very small count could reveal one person), its access pattern (if memory access patterns depend on secret values, a cache side-channel attacker on the same machine could infer something), or its network timing. The parties have to trust the CPU vendor, the firmware that is part of the attestation, and the absence of unpatched attacks. They also keep the minimum data in the VM. The overhead of the encrypted memory is typically a few percent for compute-bound code, and higher for workloads with heavy I/O or many page faults, so a 10-minute job on a normal VM would take about 10 to 11 minutes here.
intuition: A sealed room with a glass window and a notary: you cannot see inside, the janitor cannot get in, and the notary stamps a certificate saying exactly which machine is inside.
It is natural because security always needs something that is not itself controlled by the attacker. The classic answer was "trust the operating system", which fails when the operating system or its operator is the threat. A TEE moves the boundary down into the silicon: a small piece of hardware that is hard to modify and is built by a vendor with a reputation to lose. Attestation then answers the second necessary question, which is how a stranger knows that the sealed room is real and holds the code they expect. Without attestation, encryption of memory would protect the data from others but give no reason for the data owner to send it there.
mistakes: People often think a TEE makes the code inside secure. In fact it only protects the code from the software below it. A bug in the code inside, such as a buffer overflow, an unsafe input handler or a secret printed in a log, remains a bug, and the attacker may now exploit it with the stronger guarantee that nobody can inspect the memory. The enclave's own code must be small, audited and tested.
People often think remote attestation proves the program is correct or harmless. In fact it proves only identity: which code (hash) runs on genuine hardware with a given firmware level. If the program is malicious or buggy, attestation will faithfully report exactly that program. The value comes from comparing the measurement with a hash of code you have reviewed.
People often think the cloud provider cannot affect a TEE at all. In fact the provider controls availability (it can stop the VM or delay it), the network, the storage, and the scheduling, and it can mount side-channel attacks and, with physical access, bus attacks. A TEE gives confidentiality and integrity of the computation against software, not guarantees of availability, and not full protection against a physical attacker.
People often think a TEE removes the need to trust anyone. In fact it moves the trust to the CPU vendor (who made the root keys and the firmware), the microcode, and the people who audited the code. Vulnerabilities in the vendor's design, like the series of attacks on SGX and SEV, show that this trust has to be maintained with updates and with checks of the firmware version inside attestation.
People sometimes think enclaves are only for secrets like keys. In fact the VM-level TEEs are used for whole databases, analytics jobs and AI inference, since the memory protection covers any amount of data in the VM. The cost is a performance tax and a harder debugging story, since the usual tools (debugger, core dumps, profilers) are deliberately blocked.
tradeoffs: Choose a process-level enclave (SGX, via Gramine or the Open Enclave SDK) when the sensitive part is small, such as a key store, a signing service or a matching function, and you want the smallest TCB: you must trust only that code and the CPU. You pay with a split application, limited memory, system-call overhead and the more complex programming model. Choose a confidential VM (SEV-SNP, TDX) when you want to lift existing applications and you accept a larger TCB (the guest OS and everything inside): lower effort and small overhead (often 2% to 10%), but a bigger attack surface inside the VM.
Choose attestation-based key release (the key service gives a secret only to a VM with the expected measurement) over putting secrets in the VM image or in environment variables, which any operator can read. You pay in operations: a verifier service, policies for approved measurements and firmware versions, and updates whenever the code changes, because every new build has a new hash.
Choose a TEE when the threat is a privileged insider or a curious cloud operator and the data is regulated or very valuable, and you cannot or do not want to use encryption schemes that work without trust. Choose cryptographic methods (homomorphic encryption, secure multi-party computation, differential privacy) when you cannot trust any hardware vendor, or need to resist physical attacks; they have no hardware root of trust, but are 100 to 100,000 times slower for general computation, and so are practical only for narrow tasks such as sums or intersections. Many designs combine the two: a TEE does the computation, differential privacy limits what the output reveals.
Choose confidential GPUs for sensitive training or inference only when the data or model weights justify some overhead: protection of the CPU–GPU transfers adds encryption cost, which is small for compute-heavy jobs and noticeable for jobs with heavy host–device traffic. Choose to stay on ordinary VMs, with customer-managed keys and strong access control, when the threat model does not include the provider; a TEE adds complexity that you must pay for.
Choose to keep the threat model written down. Decide whether physical access to the machine is in scope (then confidential computing in a public cloud is not enough by itself; use a provider with strong physical controls or a verified data centre), and whether side channels matter (then use constant-time code, no shared cores with untrusted tenants, and the latest microcode).
friction: Side channels and physical attacks remain the biggest problem. Speculative-execution attacks (Foreshadow, then the families around Spectre), page-fault and interrupt-driven attacks (controlled-channel attacks, which let an OS observe which pages an enclave touches), cache attacks, voltage and fault injection (Plundervolt, CacheWarp) and bus interposers (TEE.fail, 2025) have each shown that the isolation is not absolute. Vendors patch many of them through microcode and firmware, and the attestation report includes the version so that verifiers can reject unpatched machines, but the cycle of attack and patch continues, and some designs make a trade of weaker physical protection for lower cost.
The trust in the vendor is a real limit. The root key, the attestation service and the microcode are controlled by one company. A government order, a compromise of the vendor or a bug in the signing infrastructure would affect every device. Verification of the hardware itself is not possible for a customer.
Usability and operation are hard. Reproducible builds, a policy of accepted measurements, firmware upgrade management, key release, and debugging with no access to memory are all new work. Memory limits (the EPC for SGX), the cost of paging, system call and I/O overhead, and the difficulty of using hardware features in containers and Kubernetes slow adoption. Confidential VM images need support from the cloud provider, who offers it only in some regions and instance types.
Finally, a TEE cannot protect against what the program itself reveals. Output leaks, access patterns, timing, and the size of data are visible. Standards are still developing: the Confidential Computing Consortium (Linux Foundation) and the IETF RATS working group define common attestation formats, but verifying evidence from different vendors with one policy is still work. Large deployments, such as clean rooms and private AI, therefore combine TEEs with careful program design, output controls and legal agreements.
practice: Start from the threat model. Write down who you protect the data from (cloud operator, other tenants, your own administrators, a compromised OS), what is out of scope (physical attacks, vendor compromise), and what must be proven to a third party. Choose the TEE type from that: confidential VMs for lifting a service, enclaves for a small secret-handling component.
Do not hand secrets to a TEE before attesting it. Build a verification step: check the certificate chain, the nonce, the measurement against a reproducible build, the debug flag, and a minimum firmware version, then release keys from a key management service only to a TEE that passes. Use the cloud provider's attestation service if you want less work (Azure Attestation, Google's confidential computing attestation, AWS Nitro attestation documents), and understand what it verifies for you.
Keep the trusted code small and review it. Remove debug interfaces, avoid secret-dependent branches and memory access where side channels are a concern, and use constant-time cryptographic libraries. Make the output safe: aggregate, add thresholds or noise, and log what leaves the TEE. Keep all persisted data encrypted with sealing or with keys released after attestation.
Test performance with a realistic load, because the cost depends on the I/O and memory pattern. Check that your language runtime, your container setup and your GPU drivers work in the confidential mode. Plan the operational side: how new builds get approved measurements, how firmware updates are rolled out, how an incident is handled when a new hardware vulnerability is announced.
For learning, read "Intel SGX Explained" for the mechanics, the AMD SEV-SNP white paper and the Confidential Computing Consortium's introductory material for the VM-level view, and try a confidential VM on a cloud: request an attestation report, parse it, and verify the signature chain yourself.
interview: What does a TEE protect against and what does it not? || It protects the confidentiality and integrity of code and data in use against privileged software: OS, hypervisor, other tenants, and admins. It does not protect against bugs in the enclave code, availability attacks, output leaks, many side channels, or in some designs physical attacks on the memory bus. It also requires trusting the CPU vendor and its firmware.
Walk through remote attestation. What is signed, who verifies it, and how is replay prevented? || The hardware signs a report with the measurement of the loaded code, security version numbers and flags, and a verifier-supplied nonce (or a hash of a channel public key), using a key chained to the vendor root. The verifier checks the chain, the signature, the nonce freshness, the measurement against the expected value, and the firmware level. Only then does it release secrets over a channel bound to the report.
Why do cloud vendors favour confidential VMs over SGX-style enclaves? || Lifting an unmodified application is far easier, there is no tiny memory limit, and the performance overhead is small. The price is a larger TCB (the guest OS) and weaker per-process isolation. Enclaves have a tiny TCB but need rewriting and have limited memory and costly system calls.
A new side-channel attack on your TEE is announced. What do you do? || Check the vendor's advisory, apply microcode/firmware updates, update the minimum accepted firmware version in the attestation policy so unpatched hosts cannot get keys, rotate secrets that may have been exposed, and assess whether your code has secret-dependent access patterns. Consider moving the most sensitive operations to constant-time code or off shared hosts.
How would you build a data clean room for two companies using a TEE? || Audited, reproducible matching code in a confidential VM; both parties attest it and release encrypted inputs only to the attested instance; the code computes the join or aggregate, enforces output rules (thresholds, noise, allowed queries), and returns only the result; logs and keys are managed outside the control of either party. Add monitoring, a measurement approval process, and legal terms.
Compare a TEE with homomorphic encryption or multi-party computation for private computation. || A TEE runs ordinary code at near-native speed but relies on hardware trust and is exposed to side and physical channels. HE and MPC rely only on mathematics and have no hardware trust, but cost orders of magnitude more computation or communication, and suit narrow functions. Choose by the threat model and performance needs, or combine them.
quiz: What are the two main things a TEE provides? || Isolation of code and data in use from privileged software (through memory encryption and access control by the CPU), and remote attestation, a signed proof of what code is running on genuine hardware.
What does a measurement in attestation represent? || A cryptographic hash of the code and initial data loaded into the TEE. If any byte changes, the hash changes, so the verifier can tell whether the expected program is running.
Why is the nonce part of the attestation request? || It makes the report fresh. Without it an attacker could replay an old valid report from a genuine TEE while the real program is something else.
A confidential VM costs 5% overhead on a job of 4 hours. How long does it take, and when is it worth it? || About 4.2 hours (4 × 1.05). It is worth it when the data is sensitive enough that protection from the cloud operator and other tenants is required.
Why is a TEE not enough to trust a cloud provider with data forever? || It does not cover availability, many side channels and physical attacks, and it relies on the vendor's keys and firmware; vulnerabilities appear over time, so you must verify firmware versions in attestation, minimize the data and keep the code small.

value: Learning to rank (LTR) trains a model to order a list of documents for a query, so that the best ones come first. A search engine first finds thousands of candidate documents, usually with a text-matching score like BM25. But BM25 uses only word statistics, and a good result depends on much more: freshness, popularity, quality of the source, how well the title matches, the user's language and location, what people clicked in the past. LTR combines dozens or hundreds of such signals (called features) into one score, and learns the weights from data instead of leaving them to hand-tuned formulas.
The central idea is in the loss. A normal classifier or regressor is judged item by item: "is this document relevant, yes or no?". But a user sees a list, and what matters is the order. Putting a perfect document at position 1 is worth far more than moving a mediocre one from position 9 to position 8. So LTR uses a loss that looks at pairs or whole lists, and, in the best methods, is weighted by how much a change of order would move a ranking metric such as NDCG. The model is trained to produce the best order, not the best individual scores.
Today LTR is the middle layer of most search and recommendation systems. A cheap first stage (an inverted index with BM25, or a vector search) retrieves 100 to 1,000 candidates in a few milliseconds. A gradient-boosted tree ranker (LambdaMART) scores each candidate with 100 to 500 features in a few more milliseconds. A neural cross-encoder may re-rank the top 20 to 50 at a higher cost. This funnel gives quality where it matters while keeping latency and cost under control.
The layers below supply the pieces. Search evaluation (L2) provides the metric, NDCG, that the loss imitates. Decision trees and boosting (L2) provide the model, since LambdaMART is gradient-boosted trees. BM25 and TF-IDF (L2) provide the strongest single feature. Gradient descent (L2) trains the model on pairwise or list-wise losses. Statistics and estimation (L4) deal with the biggest data problem: clicks are biased by position, and an unbiased estimate needs a statistical correction. Above it sit search engines, recommender systems, and the LTR plugins in Elasticsearch and OpenSearch.
terms: Feature — a number computed for a (query, document) pair, such as the BM25 score of the title, the document's age, or its click rate.
Relevance label — a grade for how well a document answers a query, often 0 (bad) to 4 (perfect), given by human raters or derived from clicks.
Query group — all candidate documents for one query; ranking losses compare documents only inside the same group.
Pointwise / pairwise / listwise — three loss families: predict each label separately; compare pairs of documents; or optimise a function of the whole list.
NDCG — normalised discounted cumulative gain, a metric between 0 and 1 that rewards putting highly relevant documents near the top.
Lambda — in LambdaRank, a gradient assigned to a document directly, equal to a pairwise gradient scaled by how much the metric would change if the pair swapped.
Position bias — users click higher results more often just because they see them first, so clicks are not a clean measure of relevance.
Reranking — applying a more expensive model only to the top candidates produced by a cheaper first stage.
history: Early web search ranked with hand-made formulas of a few signals (TF-IDF, link analysis such as PageRank). As signals multiplied in the early 2000s, tuning weights by hand stopped working. Thorsten Joachims (2002) proposed Ranking SVM, which learns from click preferences with a pairwise loss: "the result the user clicked is better than the ones above it that were skipped". In 2005 Chris Burges and colleagues at Microsoft published RankNet at ICML, a neural net trained with a pairwise cross-entropy loss, and Microsoft's Live Search used it.
Pairwise losses count wrongly ordered pairs, but all pairs count equally, while the metric cares most about the top. Burges noticed that you do not need to write a loss function at all: it is enough to define the gradient. In LambdaRank (2006) the gradient of each pair is the RankNet gradient multiplied by the change in NDCG that swapping the pair would cause. The "lambdas" are gradients that were designed directly, and were later shown to optimise a bound on the NDCG. In 2010 Qiang Wu, Burges and others combined lambdas with gradient-boosted regression trees (MART) as LambdaMART. It won the Yahoo! Learning to Rank Challenge that year, and has remained a very strong baseline: in practice it often beats neural rankers on tabular features. Burges's overview, "From RankNet to LambdaRank to LambdaMART" (2010), is the standard reference.
Datasets drove the field: Microsoft's LETOR (2007) and MSLR-WEB30K (30,000 queries, 136 features per document), the Yahoo challenge data (2010), and Istella. Listwise methods such as ListNet (Cao et al., 2007) and ListMLE modelled the probability of a whole permutation. XGBoost (2016) and LightGBM (2017) included LambdaMART objectives, so any team could train a ranker on a laptop.
Two shifts came after 2018. Unbiased learning to rank (Joachims, Swaminathan and Schnabel, 2017; Wang et al., 2016) treated click logs with counterfactual statistics to remove position bias. And pre-trained language models changed the top of the funnel: monoBERT (Nogueira and Cho, 2019) used a BERT cross-encoder to re-rank passages and improved the MS MARCO benchmark by a large margin. Retrieval-augmented generation increased the use of rerankers since 2023, because the quality of the few passages that go to the language model decides the answer. Many production systems today use all three: BM25 or vector retrieval, a LambdaMART ranker over rich features, and a neural reranker on the top results.
how: A training set is a collection of queries. For each query there is a group of candidate documents, each with a feature vector and a relevance label. Features come from several families. Query–document features measure the match: BM25 scores on title, body and anchor text, the number of matched terms, phrase matches, an embedding similarity. Document features are independent of the query: PageRank or authority, length, age, spam score, click-through rate. Query features describe the query: length, language, how often it is searched, its intent type. Context features cover the user's device, location or history. In total, there are typically 50 to 500 of them, and BM25 is the strongest single feature, so the ranker starts from it and learns when to trust it less.
Labels come from three sources. Human raters grade query–document pairs on a scale such as 0 to 4: accurate but costly (a few cents per judgment). Click logs give millions of signals, cheap but noisy and biased. Recently, large language models grade documents as a cheaper rater, with checks against human labels. In practice teams mix them.
The loss families differ in what they compare. A pointwise loss treats each document alone, as regression on the label or classification; it is simple, but ignores the list, and it spends effort on getting scores right for irrelevant documents. A pairwise loss takes every pair (i, j) in a group where i has a higher label than j and asks that the score of i be higher. RankNet defines the probability that i ranks above j as \( P_{ij} = \sigma(s_i - s_j) = 1/(1 + e^{-(s_i - s_j)}) \) and minimises the cross-entropy against the true order. A listwise loss looks at the whole list, as ListNet does with a probability distribution over permutations.
LambdaRank and LambdaMART make a key step. NDCG is not a smooth function of the scores, because it depends on the sorted order, so you cannot differentiate it. But you can say what the gradient should be. For a pair (i, j) with i more relevant than j, the lambda is
\[ \lambda_{ij} = \frac{-\sigma\,|\Delta \mathrm{NDCG}_{ij}|}{1 + e^{\sigma (s_i - s_j)}} \]
where \( |\Delta \mathrm{NDCG}_{ij}| \) is the absolute change in NDCG if documents i and j swapped positions. The factor \( 1/(1+e^{\sigma(s_i - s_j)}) \) is large when the pair is in the wrong order (the model scores j above i), and small when it is already right. The factor \( |\Delta \mathrm{NDCG}| \) is large when the swap involves high-gain documents or top positions. Each document's total lambda is the sum over all its pairs in the group, with a sign that pushes it up or down. Documents whose position matters most for the metric get the strongest push.
NDCG itself is defined as follows. The discounted cumulative gain for the top k results is \( \mathrm{DCG@k} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{rel_i} - 1}{\log_2(i+1)} \), where \( rel_i \) is the label at position i. The gain \( 2^{rel} - 1 \) makes a grade-3 document worth 7 and a grade-1 document worth 1, and the log discount makes position 1 count 1.0, position 3 count 0.5, and position 10 about 0.29. Dividing by the DCG of the ideal order gives NDCG between 0 and 1.
The boosting step uses the lambdas as gradients. Gradient-boosted trees (L2) build the model as a sum of small regression trees, each added to correct the errors of those before. At each iteration: compute the current scores for all documents; for each query group sort by score, compute for each pair the lambda and a second-derivative weight; sum them per document to get \( \lambda_i \) and \( w_i \); fit a regression tree of depth 4 to 8 to the \( \lambda_i \) as targets using the feature vectors; set each leaf's output by a Newton step (sum of lambdas divided by sum of weights); add the tree to the model, scaled by a learning rate of 0.05 to 0.1. After 300 to 1,000 trees, the model is stable. Gradient descent (L2) is this function-space descent: the trees are the steps. Because trees are scale-free, no feature normalisation is needed, and they pick useful combinations of features, such as "BM25 matters less for fresh news queries".
At serving time, scoring is cheap. A model with 500 trees of depth 6 needs about 500 × 6 = 3,000 comparisons per document, which is a few microseconds, so scoring 200 candidates takes about 1 ms on one core. Production systems often compile trees into optimised code (QuickScorer-style) to go faster. The expensive part is computing the features, especially the ones that need lookups into stores, which is why feature logging and consistency between training and serving are major engineering topics.
Position bias deserves a mention, since click logs are the cheapest labels. A user is far more likely to click position 1 than position 5, even if both are equally relevant. If you train directly on clicks, the model learns to repeat the existing order. The standard model is the examination hypothesis: \( P(\text{click}) = P(\text{examined} \mid \text{position}) \times P(\text{relevant}) \). Inverse propensity weighting divides each click by the estimated examination probability of its position (estimated from randomisation experiments or from the data), so that clicks at low positions count more. Unbiased LTR (the statistics of L4) makes the loss an unbiased estimate of the loss on true relevance, at the price of a larger variance.

example: One query has four candidate documents. The labels (0 to 4 scale; here 3, 2, 0, 1) are for the documents d1, d2, d3, d4, and the model currently ranks them in that order, so the list is [3, 2, 0, 1].
Step 1: compute the current DCG using the gain \( 2^{rel} - 1 \) and the discount \( \log_2(i+1) \). Position 1: 7 / 1 = 7. Position 2: 3 / 1.585 = 1.893. Position 3: 0. Position 4: 1 / 2.322 = 0.431. DCG = 9.324.
Step 2: compute the ideal DCG with the order [3, 2, 1, 0]: 7 + 1.893 + 1 / 2 + 0 = 9.393. So NDCG = 9.324 / 9.393 = 0.993. The ranking is already almost perfect.
Step 3: how much would each swap change the metric? Swap the documents at positions 3 and 4 (labels 0 and 1): the list becomes [3, 2, 1, 0] with DCG 9.393, a gain of 0.069, so |ΔNDCG| = 0.069 / 9.393 = 0.0073. Now compare a different swap: put the label-3 document at position 4 and the label-1 document at position 1 (list [1, 2, 0, 3]): DCG = 1 + 1.893 + 0 + 7 / 2.322 = 1 + 1.893 + 3.015 = 5.908. The loss is 9.324 − 5.908 = 3.416, so |ΔNDCG| = 3.416 / 9.393 = 0.364. This pair matters about 50 times more than the first.
Step 4: lambdas. Take the pair d4 (label 1) versus d3 (label 0). The model ranks d3 above d4, which is the wrong way, say with scores 0.3 for d3 and 0.1 for d4, so \( s_i - s_j = 0.1 - 0.3 = -0.2 \) for the more relevant d4 (σ = 1). The factor is \( 1/(1 + e^{-0.2}) = 0.550 \), and \( \lambda = -0.550 \times 0.0073 = -0.0040 \). It is a tiny push, because the swap hardly changes NDCG. Now take a pair from the second swap above: a label-3 document scored 0.2 and a label-1 document scored 0.6. Then \( s_i - s_j = -0.4 \), and \( 1/(1 + e^{-0.4}) = 0.599 \). With |ΔNDCG| = 0.364 the lambda is −0.599 × 0.364 = −0.218. This is about 55 times larger than the first, so the next tree concentrates on this pair: it learns features that separate the label-3 document from the label-1 document.
Step 5: train and evaluate with LightGBM. Groups are given as the number of rows per query:
```python
import lightgbm as lgb

train = lgb.Dataset(X_train, label=y_train, group=group_train)
valid = lgb.Dataset(X_valid, label=y_valid, group=group_valid)
params = {
    "objective": "lambdarank",
    "metric": "ndcg",
    "ndcg_eval_at": [10],
    "learning_rate": 0.05,
    "num_leaves": 31,
    "lambdarank_truncation_level": 20,   # focus on the top of the list
}
model = lgb.train(params, train, num_boost_round=800,
                  valid_sets=[valid],
                  callbacks=[lgb.early_stopping(50)])
scores = model.predict(X_test)           # sort docs per query by score
```

Step 6: judge it. On a held-out set of queries, the baseline BM25 alone may give NDCG@10 of 0.40, and the LambdaMART model 0.47. A seven-point improvement is large in search. Then check online with an A/B test or interleaving, because offline NDCG on labels is only a proxy for user satisfaction.
intuition: A talent scout who ranks players not by one statistic but by learning from many past drafts which combination of statistics predicted success, and who is corrected hardest when a star is placed far down the list.
The design is natural because ranking is a decision about order, and most of the value lies in a few top positions. A loss that counts all mistakes equally wastes effort on the bottom of the list. LambdaRank's insight is that you only need the direction and the size of the push for each document, and the size can be set directly by the metric you care about: a swap that would change NDCG a lot gets a big gradient. Boosting is a good partner, because it fits those gradients with trees, which handle features of different scales, missing values and interactions with little preparation.
mistakes: People often think a ranker should predict the relevance label of each document accurately. In fact only the order matters. A model that scores all documents of a query between 0.80 and 0.82 in the right order ranks perfectly, and a model with a low regression error but wrong order at the top ranks badly. This is why pairwise and listwise losses beat pointwise regression on ranking metrics.
People often think click-through data is relevance data. In fact clicks mix relevance with position, presentation (snippets, images), trust in the brand and curiosity. A model trained on raw clicks learns to copy the current ranking. Position-bias correction, randomisation experiments or human labels are needed to separate relevance from exposure.
People often think a neural cross-encoder makes feature-based rankers obsolete. In fact cross-encoders are strong at text matching, but are costly (each query–document pair needs a forward pass), and do not use structured signals such as freshness, price, inventory, popularity or personalisation unless you feed them in. Production stacks use both: LambdaMART or a similar model to combine all features, and a neural model as one feature or as a last-stage reranker.
People often think offline NDCG improvement guarantees better search. In fact the labels reflect an older ranking, a fixed set of judged documents (unjudged documents count as non-relevant), and a rater's view. Always confirm with an online experiment, and watch for leakage: if you split rows randomly instead of by query, documents of the same query appear in both train and test, and the score is inflated.
People sometimes think the features can differ between training and serving without harm. In fact a feature computed with a slightly different definition online (a different click window, a stale cache, a missing value coded as zero instead of null) shifts the score distribution, and quality drops silently. Log the features at serving time and train on the logged values.
tradeoffs: Choose LambdaMART (gradient-boosted trees) as the first learned ranker when you have tabular features, thousands of labelled queries or more, and a latency budget of a few milliseconds: it is strong, fast, needs little tuning and is easy to inspect. Choose a neural ranker (cross-encoder, or a transformer that also takes the features) when text understanding is the main difficulty and you have a GPU budget; you pay for latency (10 to 50 ms for a batch of 50) and cost, and for harder debugging.
Choose a pairwise loss when you train from preference data, such as clicks (clicked above skipped) or when labels are few levels; it is simple and stable. Choose a lambda-weighted or listwise loss when the goal is a top-heavy metric (NDCG@10) and you have graded labels; the lambda weighting focuses effort on the top positions. The gain over pairwise is typically a few NDCG points, at the cost of more complex training and a metric-specific design.
Choose human labels when quality is critical and you can pay: they are the cleanest and the base for evaluation. Choose click logs with bias correction when you need volume and freshness, and accept noise and extra statistics. Choose LLM-generated labels for bootstrapping or for coverage of new domains, and always validate them against a human-labelled sample, since their errors are systematic. A mix is normal: human labels for evaluation, clicks and LLM labels for training.
Choose a small candidate set (50 to 200) for the expensive model and a large one (1,000 or more) for the cheap model. A larger set raises recall but costs latency; since the first stage limits the best possible result, measure recall@k of retrieval before tuning the ranker, because no ranker can place a document that was never retrieved.
Choose the offline-online split of work with care: precompute query-independent features in a store (document features), compute query-dependent ones on the fly, and cache what you can. Feature freshness against cost is the usual trade-off, and the online/offline consistency matters more than another percent of NDCG.
friction: Labels are the first problem. Human judgments are expensive, subjective (raters disagree on many query–document pairs) and stale as the world and the queries change. Clicks are cheap but biased and mean different things for different queries (navigational, informational, shopping). Metrics hide failures: NDCG on average queries can improve while the long tail or an important segment worsens, and it does not measure diversity, freshness or fairness.
The feedback loop is the second. A ranker decides what users see, users click what they see, and the next ranker learns from those clicks. Documents that were never shown cannot gain evidence ("rich get richer"). Exploration (random swaps in a small share of traffic, or contextual bandits) gives the needed data at some short-term cost, and it is hard to justify to the business.
Third, operations: feature pipelines that are consistent between training and serving, model versions, rollbacks, monitoring of score distributions and of feature null rates, and the need to retrain as the data changes. Explaining a ranking ("why is this result at 3?") is harder than for a formula, though tree models offer feature importance and SHAP values. A bad feature can produce odd results for a small group of queries, and these are hard to find.
Fourth, the theory is incomplete. NDCG is not differentiable, and the lambda trick is a heuristic with a partial proof. Pointwise, pairwise and listwise methods differ little when features are rich, which has led some to say that the loss matters less than the features and the data. Neural rankers with raw text gain from pre-training, but on structured features gradient-boosted trees remained hard to beat for years, and large language models as rankers (listwise prompting) bring cost and consistency problems.
practice: Build the funnel first. Measure recall of the retrieval stage on judged queries, then add LambdaMART over a modest feature set (BM25 by field, document quality and freshness, click rate, query–document embedding similarity), and only then add a neural reranker to the top 20 to 50. Keep a simple baseline (BM25 alone) in the evaluation so that every layer shows its gain.
Prepare data carefully. Split by query (and by time if possible), never by row. Keep the group boundaries intact. Use graded labels if you can, and use at least several thousand queries with 20 to 100 documents each. Log the features at serving time with the query ID so that you can train on what the live system saw. Handle missing values explicitly, since trees treat them as a separate case.
Use a good library. LightGBM (`objective="lambdarank"`) and XGBoost (`rank:ndcg` or `rank:pairwise`) train in minutes on millions of rows. Set the NDCG cut-off to the depth that users actually see (usually 10), limit the truncation level of lambdas to the top positions that matter, tune the number of leaves and the learning rate with early stopping on validation NDCG, and compare with a simple linear model to make sure the trees add value.
Evaluate offline with NDCG@10, MRR or recall, then online with interleaving (which usually finds a difference with far fewer queries than an A/B test) and A/B tests on business metrics. Slice the metrics by query type, frequency and market. Check position bias in click-derived labels and use propensity weighting or randomised buckets if you train on clicks.
For Elasticsearch and OpenSearch, use their LTR support: you store the feature definitions as queries, log feature values for judged queries, train outside (XGBoost or RankLib), upload the model, and apply it as a rescore on the top N hits (N of 100 to 500) of the first-stage query. Keep N small, because rescoring cost grows with it, and re-log features whenever the index mapping or analysers change.
interview: What is the difference between pointwise, pairwise and listwise learning to rank? || Pointwise predicts each document's label independently (regression or classification) and ignores the list. Pairwise learns from pairs, penalising a less relevant document that is scored above a more relevant one. Listwise defines the loss on the entire list or on a metric such as NDCG. Pairwise and listwise match the ranking objective better and usually win on ranking metrics.
Explain LambdaMART in a few sentences. || It is gradient-boosted regression trees trained with lambda gradients. For each pair of documents of a query, the gradient is the RankNet pairwise gradient multiplied by the NDCG change from swapping them, so mistakes near the top with high-grade documents get large gradients. Each tree is fit to the summed lambdas of the documents, with Newton-step leaf values.
Why can't you optimise NDCG directly with gradient descent, and how do lambdas get around it? || NDCG depends on a sort of the scores, so it is piecewise constant, and its gradient is zero or undefined. LambdaRank skips the loss and defines the gradient directly, as a pairwise force weighted by the metric change of the swap. It is a heuristic that works well in practice, and can be seen as optimising a bound on NDCG.
You train on click logs and the model just repeats the current ranking. Why, and what do you do? || Position bias: users click higher positions more regardless of relevance, so clicks mostly reflect the old order. Correct with inverse propensity weighting using an examination model, estimate the propensities from randomised swaps, use click models, or use human labels for part of the training and for evaluation.
How would you design the ranking stack for an e-commerce search with 50 million products and a 100 ms budget? || Retrieval with an inverted index (BM25 with filters) plus vector search for 500 to 1,000 candidates in about 20 ms. A LambdaMART ranker with features such as text match, price, inventory, popularity and personalisation for the top 100, in a few ms. Optionally a cross-encoder on the top 20 to 30 on GPU in 20 to 30 ms. Cache query-independent features, log features at serve time, and evaluate with interleaving.
Offline NDCG improved by 3 points but the A/B test is flat. What are the possible reasons? || Labels do not match user behaviour, the improvement is concentrated in queries that matter little, the test is under-powered, the features differ online (training-serving skew), the judged set is biased toward the old ranker's results, or the metric ignores things users care about (diversity, price, freshness). Slice the results and check feature consistency.
quiz: Why is a pairwise or listwise loss better than regression on labels for ranking? || The ranking quality depends on order, not on the score values. Pairwise and listwise losses penalise wrongly ordered documents directly, so the model uses its capacity to get the order right, especially near the top.
What does the lambda in LambdaRank contain? || The RankNet pairwise gradient multiplied by |ΔNDCG|, the change in NDCG if the two documents swapped positions. Pairs that matter more for the metric get bigger pushes.
A list of labels at positions 1 to 3 is [0, 3, 1]. What is the DCG@3 with gain 2^rel − 1 and discount log₂(i+1)? || Position 1: 0. Position 2: 7 / log₂3 = 7 / 1.585 = 4.416. Position 3: 1 / log₂4 = 0.5. DCG@3 = 4.916.
Why must you split a learning-to-rank dataset by query, not by row? || Rows of the same query share candidates and labels. A random row split puts parts of one query in both train and test, leaking information and inflating the metric. Ranking losses also need whole query groups.
When would you add a cross-encoder reranker after LambdaMART? || When text understanding is the main weakness and you can afford extra latency and GPU cost on a small set (the top 20 to 50). It re-reads the query and document together, and often adds the final quality gain, for example in retrieval-augmented generation.
