value: FlashAttention computes exact self-attention (the same numbers as the textbook formula, up to rounding) without ever writing the big N × N score matrix to the GPU's main memory. It does this by working on small tiles that fit in fast on-chip memory. The result is a kernel that is 2 to 4 times faster than a standard implementation at long sequence lengths and uses memory that grows linearly with the sequence length N, not with N². At N = 8,192 the score matrix for one head holds 67 million numbers, 134 MB in 16-bit format; with 32 heads that is over 4 GB for one layer and one sequence, which is why long contexts used to run out of memory.
The key point is that attention on a modern GPU is limited by data movement, not by arithmetic. The GPU can do hundreds of trillions of multiply-adds per second, but the score matrix has to travel to and from high-bandwidth memory (HBM) several times: once to be written after the matrix multiply, again to be read for the softmax, again to be written, and again to be read for the multiplication by V. FlashAttention removes almost all of that traffic. It does the same arithmetic (and even a little more in the backward pass) but moves far fewer bytes, so it finishes sooner. The authors call this "IO-aware": an algorithm designed by counting memory reads and writes between levels of the memory hierarchy, not only counting floating-point operations.
For the layer above, this is what makes LLM training and serving with long context affordable (Transformers and LLMs, GPU computing). The layers below make it possible: the memory hierarchy (L3) explains why a small fast memory in front of a big slow one rewards tiling; DRAM and HBM (L3) explain why the big memory is slow relative to compute; the GPU's SIMT design (L3) gives each tile a block of threads with shared memory; and the roofline model (L3) tells you that the original kernel sat far below the compute roof and that the fix is to raise arithmetic intensity, not to add FLOPs.
terms: Attention — for each query vector, a weighted average of value vectors, where the weights come from a softmax over the dot products of that query with all key vectors.
HBM — high-bandwidth memory, the large (40 to 192 GB) off-chip memory of a data-center GPU, with bandwidth of roughly 1.5 to 8 TB/s depending on the generation.
SRAM (shared memory) — the small on-chip memory inside each GPU streaming multiprocessor, about 100 to 230 KB each, many times faster than HBM and managed by the programmer.
Tiling — splitting a large computation into blocks that fit in fast memory, and finishing all the work on one block before moving to the next.
Online softmax — a way to compute softmax over a long row one chunk at a time by keeping a running maximum and a running sum, and correcting the old partial results when the maximum changes.
Kernel fusion — running several steps (matrix multiply, mask, softmax, matrix multiply) inside one GPU program so intermediate results never go to HBM.
Logsumexp — \( \log\left(\sum_i \exp(s_i)\right) \) over a row of scores; storing one such number per row is enough to rebuild the softmax later.
IO-aware — designed by counting the bytes moved between memory levels, not only the arithmetic operations.
history: Attention itself comes from Bahdanau, Cho and Bengio (2014) and the Transformer paper (Vaswani et al., 2017). Its cost, quadratic in sequence length, was seen as the main obstacle to long inputs, so between 2019 and 2021 many papers proposed approximations: sparse patterns (Sparse Transformer, Longformer), low-rank projections (Linformer), kernel tricks (Performer). They reduced the number of FLOPs, but often did not make real models faster on a GPU, because GPUs were not limited by FLOPs in the first place.
Tri Dao, Daniel Fu, Stefano Ermon, Atri Rudra and Christopher Ré (Stanford) took a different view in "FlashAttention: Fast and Memory-Efficient Exact Attention with IO-Awareness" (NeurIPS 2022). They measured that the time was going into reads and writes of the N × N matrix and proved that tiling needs fewer HBM accesses, and that this is optimal for a range of SRAM sizes. They reported a 15% end-to-end speedup on BERT-large (sequence 512) over the MLPerf 1.1 record and about 3 times on GPT-2 at sequence length 1K. Adoption was very fast: PyTorch 2.0 (2023) included a fused attention path in scaled_dot_product_attention, and most training stacks used it within a year.
FlashAttention-2 (Dao, 2023) fixed poor work distribution across thread blocks and warps, cut work that did not use the tensor cores, and reached about 50 to 73% of the A100's peak matrix throughput, roughly 2 times faster than version 1 (up to 225 TFLOPs/s per A100 in end-to-end GPT training, 72% utilization). FlashAttention-3 (Shah et al., 2024) used new Hopper (H100) features: asynchronous copies through the Tensor Memory Accelerator, asynchronous tensor core instructions, and FP8. It reached up to 740 TFLOPs/s in FP16 (75% utilization) and close to 1.2 PFLOPs/s in FP8. FlashAttention-4 (Zadouri et al., March 2026) targets Blackwell (B200) and reports up to 1,613 TFLOPs/s (71% utilization) in BF16, up to 1.3 times faster than cuDNN 9.13. Each version exists because each new GPU changed which resource was scarce.
how: Standard attention for one head has queries Q, keys K and values V, each N × d (d is typically 64 to 128). It computes \( S = QK^{\top}/\sqrt{d} \), an N × N matrix; then \( P = \text{softmax}(S) \) row by row; then \( O = PV \). In a typical framework these are three or more separate GPU kernels, so S and P are written to and read from HBM each time. The matrix multiplies are fast. The softmax, scaling, masking and dropout are element-wise operations with very low arithmetic intensity (a few operations per byte), so they sit far below the roofline's compute ceiling and are limited by HBM bandwidth. The memory hierarchy is the reason this matters: on an A100, HBM delivers about 1.5 to 2 TB/s, while the on-chip SRAM (about 20 MB in total across 108 multiprocessors) delivers roughly ten times that.
FlashAttention restructures the loop. It splits Q into row blocks and K and V into column blocks, each small enough that a block of Q, a block of K, a block of V and the partial output fit in SRAM together. For one Q block it loops over all K and V blocks: it loads a K block and a V block into SRAM, computes the small score tile \( S = QK^{\top} \) there, and updates the output for those rows. The score tile is thrown away after use. Only Q, K, V and O ever live in HBM, so memory is linear in N.
The difficulty is softmax. A row's softmax needs the maximum and the sum over the whole row, but a tile only sees part of the row. Online softmax solves this. For each row the kernel keeps a running maximum m, a running sum l of exp(s − m), and an unnormalized output o. When a new tile arrives with its own maximum m', set m_new = max(m, m'), multiply the old l and o by exp(m − m_new), add the new tile's contribution computed with m_new, and continue. At the end divide o by l. The result is exactly the same as the full softmax, not an approximation. Because exp(m − m_new) is just a scale factor, the correction costs a few multiplications per row.
The number of HBM accesses falls from \( \Theta(Nd + N^2) \) to \( \Theta(N^2 d^2/M) \), where M is the SRAM size in elements. For d = 128 and M of about 100,000 elements, \( d^2/M \) is well below 1, so traffic drops by a large factor; the paper notes this is optimal for the range of M they consider. The kernel is also fused, so the dropout mask, the causal mask and the scaling are applied while the tile is in SRAM, and no extra pass is needed. For causal (decoder) attention, tiles above the diagonal are skipped entirely, which saves about half of the work.
The backward pass needs the gradients of Q, K and V, and normally that requires P. FlashAttention does not store P. The forward pass saves only O and the logsumexp L of each row (N numbers). In the backward pass the kernel reloads Q, K, V tiles, recomputes S and P for each tile from L, and accumulates the gradients. It is a form of activation checkpointing (recomputing instead of storing, see Backpropagation) applied inside the kernel. It does more FLOPs than the standard backward pass, but it moves far fewer bytes, and since the kernel is memory-bound, it is still faster.
FlashAttention-2 changed how the work is split. Version 1 looped over K and V blocks on the outside, which forced extra writes of partial outputs. Version 2 puts the Q blocks on the outside, so each thread block owns a set of output rows and can run independently, and it also parallelizes over the sequence length, which matters when the batch and head counts are small. It also cuts non-matrix-multiply work, because on an A100 the tensor cores run 16-bit matrix math at 312 TFLOPs/s while ordinary FP32 arithmetic (exponentials, rescaling) runs at about 19.5 TFLOPs/s, so each scalar operation is expensive in comparison. SIMT details matter here: how warps (groups of 32 threads) share the tile decides how much shared memory traffic there is. FlashAttention-3 goes further on Hopper with warp specialization (some warps only load data, others only compute) and overlapping the softmax of one tile with the matrix multiply of the next.
Decoding is a different case: with one new query token, there is almost no work per head, so parallelism must come from splitting the long K and V across thread blocks and merging the partial results (Flash-Decoding). The merge uses the same logsumexp trick as online softmax.

example: Take one query row with four keys. The scores are s = [2, 1, 3, 0] and the value vectors are scalars v = [1, 2, 3, 4] to keep the numbers small. The exact answer: the weights are exp(s) = [7.389, 2.718, 20.086, 1.000]; their sum is 31.193; the output is (7.389×1 + 2.718×2 + 20.086×3 + 1.000×4) / 31.193 = 77.08 / 31.193 = 2.471.
Now process the row in two tiles of two keys, never seeing all four scores together. Tile 1 has scores [2, 1]. The running maximum is m = 2. Using exp(s − m): [1, 0.368]. So l = 1.368 and the unnormalized output is o = 1×1 + 0.368×2 = 1.736. Tile 2 has scores [3, 0]. Its maximum is 3, which is larger than the old m = 2, so the new maximum is 3. Rescale the old values by exp(2 − 3) = 0.368: l becomes 1.368 × 0.368 = 0.503 and o becomes 1.736 × 0.368 = 0.639. Add tile 2's terms using exp(s − 3) = [1, 0.0498]: l = 0.503 + 1 + 0.0498 = 1.553, and o = 0.639 + 1×3 + 0.0498×4 = 3.838. Final output: o / l = 3.838 / 1.553 = 2.471. It matches the exact result, and at no point did we hold all four scores at once.
Now count memory traffic for a realistic case: N = 8,192, d = 128, 16-bit values, one head. Q, K, V and O are each 8,192 × 128 × 2 bytes = 2.1 MB, so 8.4 MB in total. The standard approach also writes S (134 MB), reads it, writes P (134 MB) and reads it: about 536 MB of extra traffic, which is 64 times the useful data. At 1.5 TB/s, 536 MB costs about 360 µs, while the arithmetic is \( 4 \times N^2 \times d \) = 34 GFLOPs, which takes about 110 µs on a 312 TFLOPs/s tensor core. The standard kernel is bound by memory by a factor of three. FlashAttention never writes S or P. Each Q block is loaded once, the K and V blocks are streamed through SRAM (re-reads of K and V mostly hit the GPU's L2 cache), and O is written once. The time then moves close to the arithmetic time of about 110 µs, and a good kernel reaches 50 to 70% of the tensor-core peak, which is the range reported for FlashAttention-2.
intuition: Don't spread all your papers across the floor to add them up; work through a few at a time on your desk and keep only running totals.
The idea is natural once you see the numbers. Doing arithmetic is cheap and moving data is expensive, so the best algorithm is the one that moves the least data, even if it does a little more arithmetic. Softmax looks like it needs the whole row, but a running maximum and a running sum are enough, because exp(a − m) can always be rescaled to exp(a − m') by one multiplication. Once that is seen, the N × N matrix is no longer needed. The backward pass follows the same logic: recomputing a tile from Q, K and the saved row statistic costs a few FLOPs, but reading a stored copy from HBM costs far more time.
mistakes: People often think FlashAttention is an approximation, like sparse or low-rank attention. In fact it computes exact attention. The output equals the standard formula up to normal floating-point rounding, because online softmax is an algebraic rearrangement and not a shortcut.
People often think it reduces the FLOPs from N² to something smaller. In fact the arithmetic is still quadratic: for N = 128K tokens the work is still enormous. It reduces memory traffic and memory use. The backward pass even does extra FLOPs by recomputing tiles.
People often think the speedup comes from a new mathematical trick. In fact the speedup comes from fusion and tiling, and the maths is a rearrangement of softmax. That is also why it needs a hand-tuned kernel per GPU generation: the gain depends on registers, shared memory size, tensor core instructions and asynchronous copy engines.
People often think the speedup is the same for all sequence lengths. In fact at short sequences (a few hundred tokens) the N × N matrix is small, other layers dominate, and the gain is small. The gain grows with N: the memory saving is linear versus quadratic, and the time saving rises from tens of percent to several times at 4K to 16K tokens.
friction: The kernels are hand-written for each GPU architecture, in CUDA C++ with libraries such as CUTLASS and CuTe. A kernel tuned for A100 does not use the Hopper asynchronous features, so each new generation needs months of engineering, and the fastest code arrives some time after the hardware. Support is limited: usually only FP16 or BF16 (plus FP8 on recent GPUs), head dimensions up to 256, and certain GPU generations. Unusual attention variants (a custom mask, relative-position bias, a new score modification) used to need a new kernel; PyTorch's FlexAttention (Dong et al., 2024) now compiles a user-written score function into a fused kernel, with performance often close to hand-written code but not always equal.
The compute is still quadratic. Beyond 100K tokens, even at 70% of peak the attention FLOPs dominate, and that is why sliding-window, sparse and state-space designs, and grouped-query attention which shrinks the KV cache, remain active areas. FlashAttention also does not solve the serving problem: at decode time the cost is dominated by reading the KV cache, a bandwidth problem that needs cache compression and good memory management (see PagedAttention). Numerical differences are another cost: because the order of summation differs from a reference implementation, results are not bit-identical, and FP8 variants need careful scaling to avoid accuracy loss.
practice: Do not write your own kernel. In PyTorch call torch.nn.functional.scaled_dot_product_attention; it selects a flash backend, a memory-efficient backend or a plain math backend depending on dtype, mask and hardware. Use BF16 or FP16, pass is_causal=True instead of building an explicit mask (an explicit mask forces a slower path or loads N² data), and keep head dimension at a supported size. For variable-length batches, avoid padding: use the packed "varlen" interface of the flash-attn library, which takes sequences concatenated with cumulative lengths. Hugging Face models enable it with attn_implementation="flash_attention_2" or "sdpa".
Check what you actually run. On A100-class GPUs FlashAttention-2 is the right target, on H100 FlashAttention-3, and on Blackwell FlashAttention-4 or the vendor's cuDNN kernels; profile with the PyTorch profiler or Nsight Systems and confirm the attention kernel name, not a generic matmul plus softmax. If you use a non-standard attention pattern, try FlexAttention before writing CUDA. For serving, combine a fast attention kernel with paged KV-cache memory and grouped-query attention, since decode speed depends on bytes of KV cache read per token.
When you see a slow model, apply the same IO-aware thinking elsewhere: count the bytes that each operation reads and writes, compare with the compute it does, and ask which intermediate tensors can be fused or recomputed. Layer norm, rotary embeddings and cross-entropy over a large vocabulary are common places where fused kernels give similar wins.
tradeoffs: Choose a fused FlashAttention kernel whenever N is above about 1,000 tokens and the hardware supports it. You gain a 2 to 4 times faster attention and memory linear in N; you give up some flexibility in the attention pattern and depend on a library that must support your GPU and dtype. Below about 512 tokens the gain is small, and a plain implementation is fine and easier to debug.
Choose FlexAttention (compiler-generated) over a hand-written kernel when you have a custom mask or score modification such as sliding windows, document masks or ALiBi, and your team does not write CUDA. You give up a little peak speed and accept compile time and some debugging difficulty. Choose a hand-tuned kernel only when the pattern is fixed and the attention cost is a large share of your GPU bill, since the kernel work can take weeks.
Choose recomputation (the FlashAttention backward pass) over storing P because recomputing a tile costs a few microseconds of compute while storing and reloading it costs more time and N² memory. You pay about 25% more FLOPs in the backward pass (five matrix multiplies instead of four); it is still a net win because the kernel is memory-bound.
Choose FP8 attention (FlashAttention-3 on Hopper, newer kernels on Blackwell) when you are throughput-limited and can tolerate small numerical error, for example in inference. You gain up to about 1.6 times higher throughput than FP16 on the same kernel, and you give up accuracy margin and simplicity, so test the downstream quality. Keep BF16 for training until you have measured the loss curve with FP8.
Choose approximate or sparse attention instead only when N is so long (hundreds of thousands to millions of tokens) that quadratic compute itself is the problem. You lose exactness and often quality; for contexts up to about 128K, exact attention with a fast kernel is usually the better engineering choice.
interview: Why is standard attention slow on a GPU even though matrix multiplies are fast? || The N × N score matrix is written to and read from HBM several times, and the softmax, mask and dropout are element-wise operations with very low arithmetic intensity. The kernel sits on the memory-bandwidth side of the roofline, so time is set by bytes moved (about N² × 2 bytes × 4 passes), not by FLOPs.
Explain online softmax and why it makes tiling possible. || Softmax over a row needs the row maximum and sum. Keep a running max m, sum l and unnormalized output o. When a new tile has a larger max, multiply old l and o by exp(m_old − m_new) and continue. The result is exactly equal to the full softmax, so each tile can be processed once with no N × N storage.
The backward pass does more FLOPs than the standard one. Why is it still faster? || It recomputes S and P tile by tile from Q, K, V and the saved per-row logsumexp, instead of reading a stored N × N matrix. Attention is memory-bound, so a few extra FLOPs on the tensor cores cost less than the HBM traffic they replace. It also keeps memory linear in N.
What changed between FlashAttention 1, 2 and 3? || Version 2 changed the loop order and the work split across thread blocks and warps, reduced non-matmul operations and parallelized over sequence length, about 2 times faster. Version 3 used Hopper-specific hardware: asynchronous TMA copies, warp-specialized producers and consumers, overlap of softmax and matmul, and FP8, reaching about 75% utilization.
You serve a model with a 128K context and decode is slow. Will a better FlashAttention kernel fix it? || Only partly. In decode each step reads the whole KV cache for one new token, so the cost is bandwidth to read the cache, not the N × N matrix. Use Flash-Decoding style splitting for parallelism, and reduce bytes: grouped-query attention, KV quantization, paged memory and prefix sharing.
Where would FlashAttention not help? || At short sequences where attention is a small share of the step; on GPUs without a supported kernel; with unsupported dtypes or head dimensions; and with exotic masks unless expressed through a flexible framework. It also does not reduce quadratic compute.
quiz: What does FlashAttention avoid storing, and what does it keep for the backward pass? || It avoids storing the N × N score and probability matrices in HBM. It keeps the output O and one logsumexp value per row, then recomputes the tiles in the backward pass.
Why does the online softmax need to multiply old partial results by exp(m_old − m_new)? || The old partial sums were computed relative to the old maximum. When a larger maximum appears, all earlier terms must be rescaled to the new reference so that the final sum equals the exact softmax denominator.
For N = 8,192 and 16-bit values, how big is the score matrix of one head, and what does that mean for 32 heads? || 8,192² × 2 bytes ≈ 134 MB per head, so about 4.3 GB for 32 heads in one layer for one sequence, which is why it is not stored.
Is FlashAttention an approximation of attention? Does it reduce FLOPs? || No. It is exact (up to rounding). FLOPs stay quadratic (even slightly higher in the backward pass); the gain comes from moving far fewer bytes between HBM and SRAM.
A teammate wants a custom attention mask for a research model. What are your options and trade-offs? || Use SDPA with a standard mask for small cases, FlexAttention to compile the mask into a fused kernel with near hand-written speed, or write a custom kernel only if the pattern is stable and costly. The first is simplest but slow at large N, the second balances speed and effort, the third has the best speed and the highest engineering cost.

@@ pagedattn
value: PagedAttention is a way to store the key-value (KV) cache of a language model in small fixed-size blocks that can sit anywhere in GPU memory, with a per-request table that maps the request's logical token positions to physical blocks. It is the same idea as virtual memory in an operating system, applied to the GPU memory that holds the KV cache. The vLLM paper (Kwon et al., SOSP 2023) showed that older serving systems used only 20.4% to 38.2% of their KV-cache memory to hold real token data; the rest was reserved but unused, or lost to fragmentation. With paging, waste fell to a few percent (roughly 4%), and throughput rose by 2 to 4 times at the same latency.
The KV cache is large because every generated or processed token keeps one key vector and one value vector in every layer. For OPT-13B this is about 800 KB per token (2 × 5,120 × 40 layers × 2 bytes), so one 2,048-token request needs 1.6 GB. On a 40 GB A100 the model weights take 26 GB, leaving only about 12 GB for the cache, which is about 15,000 tokens in total. How many requests fit at once, the batch size, decides throughput, because decoding is limited by memory bandwidth and a bigger batch shares each read of the weights among more requests. So memory waste in the cache is directly lost money.
For the layer above, LLM serving, this made high-batch serving practical and turned the KV cache into something that can be shared (a common system prompt is stored once), swapped, and managed by a scheduler. The layer below is virtual memory and paging (L3): fixed-size pages remove external fragmentation, a page table separates the logical view from the physical layout, allocation happens on demand, and reference counting with copy-on-write allows sharing. PagedAttention did not invent a new memory idea; it recognised that the KV cache has exactly the problems that paging was invented to solve.
terms: KV cache — the stored key and value vectors of all earlier tokens, kept so that each new token does not recompute them.
Block (page) — a fixed number of token slots (16 by default in vLLM) holding the KV vectors for those tokens in all layers.
Block table — a per-request array mapping logical block numbers to physical block ids, like a page table.
Internal fragmentation — space reserved inside an allocated unit but not used; with paging it is at most one partly filled block per request.
External fragmentation — free memory split into pieces that are too small or not contiguous enough for a new request.
Copy-on-write — blocks shared by several requests carry a reference count; the first writer copies the block and writes to its own copy.
Continuous batching — adding and removing requests from the running batch at every decoding step, not at the end of a whole batch.
Preemption — pausing a request and freeing its blocks (by swapping them to CPU memory or dropping them to recompute later) when GPU memory runs out.
history: Early LLM serving frameworks were built on tensors that must be contiguous: the KV cache of one request was one contiguous array. Because the output length is unknown, systems such as FasterTransformer reserved space for the maximum length (for example 2,048 tokens) when a request arrived. The vLLM paper names three kinds of waste that follow: reservation (slots for future tokens that are idle for a long time), internal fragmentation (the reserved space the request never uses) and external fragmentation (gaps between buffers that the allocator cannot reuse). Orca (Yu et al., OSDI 2022) had already introduced iteration-level scheduling, now called continuous batching, so the scheduler was ready to run more requests, but memory was the limit.
Woosuk Kwon, Zhuohan Li and colleagues at UC Berkeley (Ion Stoica's group, with Hao Zhang, Joseph Gonzalez and others) designed PagedAttention and the vLLM system in 2023. The open-source release in June 2023 came before the SOSP paper (October 2023), and the project grew quickly into one of the most used open-source LLM serving engines. The authors state the OS analogy openly: blocks are pages, tokens are bytes, requests are processes.
After 2023 the idea spread. TensorRT-LLM, Hugging Face Text Generation Inference, SGLang and other engines adopted paged KV caches. SGLang added RadixAttention (Zheng et al., 2023), which keeps the cached blocks in a radix tree so that requests with a common prefix share them automatically. vLLM added automatic prefix caching, chunked prefill, and later a redesigned engine with the same paged memory model and FlashAttention-style kernels. The research direction changed from "how to avoid waste" to "how to share, offload, compress and move cache blocks" across GPUs and across servers.
how: The system starts by carving the GPU memory left after loading the weights into a pool of physical blocks. Each block holds the keys and values of B tokens (B = 16 by default) for all layers. At 800 KB per token for OPT-13B, one block is about 13 MB. A free list tracks the unused blocks, and each block has a reference count. Nothing is reserved per request in advance; this is the virtual memory idea of demand paging.
When a request arrives, the scheduler checks that enough free blocks exist for its prompt, takes them from the free list, and writes the prompt's KV vectors into them in one prefill pass. The request keeps a block table: entry i says which physical block holds tokens 16i to 16i + 15. During decoding, each step appends one token. If the last block has a free slot, the new key and value go there. If it is full, the allocator takes a new block from the free list and adds an entry to the table. So a request with 300 tokens holds 19 blocks (304 slots) and wastes 4 slots, while a contiguous allocation would hold a full 2,048-slot buffer. The only waste is the unfilled part of the last block, on average about half a block.
The attention kernel must now read keys and values that are not contiguous. For each query it loops over the request's logical blocks; for block i it looks up the physical id in the block table, loads that block's keys, computes the scores for those 16 positions, and does the same for the values. Softmax across blocks uses the online softmax technique (a running maximum and sum), the same technique as in FlashAttention, since the full row of scores is never held at once. The extra cost is one table lookup per block and slightly less regular memory access. The paper measured a 20% to 26% higher kernel latency than FasterTransformer's contiguous kernel, which is small compared with the gain from larger batches.
Sharing comes from the reference counts. With parallel sampling (n answers for one prompt) or beam search, all sequences begin with the same prompt, so their block tables point to the same physical blocks and the reference count equals the number of sequences. When one sequence needs to write into a shared block (for example the last, partly filled prompt block), the allocator sees a count above 1, allocates a new block, copies the contents, lowers the old count, and lets the sequence write in its private copy. This is copy-on-write, exactly as an operating system does after fork(). On the Alpaca workload the paper reports memory savings from sharing of 6.1% to 9.8% for parallel sampling and 37.6% to 55.2% for beam search. For prefix caching, each full block is identified by a hash of its tokens plus the tokens before it; a new request whose first blocks match existing hashes reuses those physical blocks and skips recomputing them. Blocks with count zero can stay in an LRU cache and be evicted only when space is needed.
When the pool is empty and a running request needs one more block, the scheduler must preempt. Because a request needs all its blocks to make progress, vLLM preempts whole requests, the latest arrivals first. It can swap the request's blocks to CPU memory, or drop them and recompute the KV cache later as a new prefill. The paper found that recomputation is cheaper at small block sizes (many small CPU-GPU copies are slow), and swapping better at large block sizes. The block size therefore balances three costs: larger blocks make the kernel more efficient and reduce table size, but waste more in the last block and share less precisely. Sizes of 16 to 128 performed well in their tests, and 16 became the default.
With tensor parallelism (the model split across several GPUs), each GPU stores the cache for its own share of the attention heads, but all GPUs use the same block numbers, so there is one block table and one allocator on the scheduler side. The scheduler admits a new request only if the free blocks cover its prompt plus a small reserve for growth, which is the usual watermark rule; this keeps preemption rare without reserving the maximum length.
The memory hierarchy sets the frame: this whole design works because decoding is limited by HBM bandwidth, so the cost of reading through a table is hidden, and because extra batch size turns directly into throughput. Without that bandwidth-bound property the indirection would hurt more.

example: Take OPT-13B on a 40 GB A100. Weights are 26 GB. Reserve some memory for activations, leaving about 12 GB for the KV cache. At 819,200 bytes per token, 12 GB holds about 14,600 tokens. A contiguous system reserves for the maximum length of 2,048 tokens: one request needs 1.6 GB, so 12 / 1.6 = 7 requests fit, whatever their real length. Now suppose real requests have on average 300 tokens (prompt plus output). Each real request uses 300 × 0.8 MB = 240 MB, so only 15% of its reservation is used. That is 85% waste and a batch of 7.
With PagedAttention and 16-token blocks, a 300-token request uses 19 blocks, 304 slots, so it holds 304 × 0.82 MB = 249 MB. Memory for 14,600 tokens divided by 304 gives about 48 requests in the batch. The batch is about 7 times larger. The real gain in the paper is 2 to 4 times, because the baselines did not all reserve the full maximum, because the batch cannot grow without limit (compute and scheduling also matter), and because the kernel is 20% slower.
Now the copy-on-write case with a block size of 4 so you can follow it with a pen. A prompt has 6 tokens, so it fills block 0 (tokens 1 to 4) and half of block 1 (tokens 5 and 6). Two samples, X and Y, are requested. Both block tables point to physical blocks P0 and P1, each with reference count 2: the cache holds 2 blocks, not 4. X generates token 7. It must write into slot 3 of logical block 1, which is shared. The allocator takes a free block P2, copies tokens 5 and 6 into it, sets the count of P1 to 1 and the count of P2 to 1, and X's table now points to P2. Y generates its token 7 next: P1 has count 1, so Y writes straight into P1. The cache now holds P0 (shared, count 2), P1 (Y) and P2 (X): 3 blocks instead of 4 or more. If the prompt were 2,000 tokens long and X and Y each generated 100 tokens, 500 of the blocks would be shared and only about 50 would be private.
intuition: Give each request library shelves one at a time as it needs them, and keep a card that says which shelf holds which pages, instead of reserving a whole aisle on day one.
The design is natural once you see that the KV cache grows unpredictably, one token at a time, and that the kernel only needs to find the next 16 keys, not a whole contiguous array. Contiguity was a convenience of the tensor library, not a requirement of attention. Once you give that up, the 50-year-old answer of operating systems fits: small fixed units, a table, and sharing by reference counts. The cost is a small lookup per block, and it is paid in a part of the system that is waiting for memory anyway.
mistakes: People often think PagedAttention is a faster attention algorithm. In fact it computes the same attention. It is a memory management method; the kernel is slightly slower per call (20 to 26% in the paper), and the benefit comes from a larger batch and from sharing.
People often think vLLM's gains come only from continuous batching. In fact continuous batching and paging work together. Continuous batching lets requests join and leave at each step, but without paging the contiguous reservation limits how many can join. Paging removes that limit.
People often think paging removes all waste. In fact there is still waste in the last block of each request (about half a block on average, 8 slots at B = 16), and memory is still finite: when the pool is empty, requests are preempted and either swapped or recomputed.
People often think paging gives prefix sharing for free. In fact paging only makes sharing possible. Finding a matching prefix needs extra machinery (hashes of full blocks, or a radix tree), and only whole blocks can be shared, so the last partial block of a prefix is always recomputed.
friction: The indirection has a price. The kernel loads from scattered blocks, and fused attention kernels for other features (FlashAttention-style tiling, speculative decoding, sliding windows, multi-query layouts) must all understand block tables, so each new feature needs paged variants. This made the serving engines more complicated and slows the adoption of new attention designs.
Block size is a real tuning knob. Too small and the lookups, table sizes and swap transfers hurt; too large and the last-block waste and the loss of sharing granularity grow (prefix sharing can only reuse whole blocks, so a 15-token shared prefix is not shared at B = 16). Models with several kinds of attention layers, such as sliding-window layers mixed with full layers, or state-space layers with fixed-size state, do not fit one block shape, and engines need hybrid allocators.
Paging does not make memory unlimited. The cache still grows linearly with the context and the number of requests. For long contexts the cache of one request can exceed one GPU, and the active research is on offloading to CPU memory and disk, moving blocks between machines (disaggregated prefill and decode), quantizing the cache to 8 or 4 bits, and compressing it. Preemption by recompute wastes the prefill work already done, and by swapping needs PCIe bandwidth, tens of GB/s, which is tiny compared with HBM. Finally the scheduler policy has to avoid thrashing, where requests are repeatedly admitted and preempted.
practice: Use an engine that already has it (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM, TGI) and tune the memory settings instead of building your own. The main parameter in vLLM is gpu_memory_utilization (the fraction of GPU memory the engine may use; the rest after weights becomes the block pool), together with max_model_len and max_num_seqs. If your requests are shorter than the maximum you set, you do not pay for the difference, which is the whole point, but a very high max_model_len still limits how many long requests can be admitted safely.
Turn on prefix caching when many requests share a long prefix: a system prompt, few-shot examples, a document asked about many times, or a chat history. Put the shared content at the start of the prompt and the changing content at the end, because only a prefix can be reused. Watch the cache hit rate in the engine's metrics. For agents and multi-turn applications this can cut the time to the first token a lot, since the prefill of the shared part is skipped.
Watch the preemption counters. If requests are often preempted, reduce max_num_seqs, shorten the maximum context, use a quantized KV cache (FP8), use grouped-query attention models, or add GPUs with tensor parallelism, which splits the cache across devices. Size capacity with the formula: bytes per token = 2 × layers × KV heads × head dimension × bytes per value. Grouped-query models with 8 KV heads need 4 to 8 times less than full multi-head models of the same size, which is often a bigger lever than any scheduling change.
tradeoffs: Choose paged KV cache over contiguous per-request buffers whenever request lengths vary or are unknown in advance, which is almost always. You gain a 2 to 4 times larger effective batch, sharing and flexible preemption; you give up some kernel speed (20 to 26% for the attention kernel in the original measurement) and simplicity. A contiguous buffer is acceptable only for a fixed-length batch job where every sequence has the same known length.
Choose a small block size (8 to 16) when many requests are short or share prefixes at fine granularity, such as chat with short system prompts, and you want little last-block waste and exact prefix reuse. Choose a larger block size (32 to 128) when contexts are long and uniform, and kernel efficiency and low table overhead matter more. In both cases you give up something: small blocks give more lookups and swap copies, large blocks give more waste per request.
Choose recompute over swap when preemption is rare, the prompt is short, or CPU-GPU bandwidth is poor; recomputation costs one prefill and no extra memory. Choose swap when the prompt is long, so recomputing it takes much longer than moving its cache over PCIe (for example 2 GB at about 25 GB/s is about 80 ms), and you have spare CPU memory.
Choose prefix caching when the share of repeated prefix is high (above roughly 30 to 50% of the prompt tokens); you gain a large drop in prefill compute and latency. Leave it off or accept low hit rates when prompts are unique, because the hashing and the cached blocks occupy memory that could hold live requests.
Choose KV-cache quantization (FP8 or lower) when the cache, not the weights, limits your batch. You gain about 2 times more tokens per GB; you pay some accuracy risk that should be measured on your own long-context tasks.
interview: Where does the memory waste in a naive LLM serving system come from? || Three sources: reservation (slots reserved for future tokens that stay empty for a long time), internal fragmentation (reserving the maximum length when the real output is short) and external fragmentation (gaps between variable-size buffers). In the vLLM paper only 20 to 38% of KV memory held real tokens.
Explain how PagedAttention maps to virtual memory. What is the page, the page table, and what does copy-on-write do here? || A block of 16 tokens is a page; the block table is the page table from logical block index to physical block id; the allocator is the frame allocator. When sequences share blocks (a common prompt), a reference count is kept, and the first write to a shared block makes a private copy, as after fork() in an OS.
Why does a bigger batch give more throughput in decoding? || Decoding one token per request reads all the weights from HBM for little arithmetic, so it is bandwidth-bound. A larger batch reuses each weight read for more requests, so tokens per second rises almost linearly until the cache or compute becomes limiting. That is why the cache size sets the batch size.
How would you size the KV cache for a new model, and what lever helps most? || Bytes per token = 2 × layers × KV heads × head dim × bytes per value. Multiply by the tokens in flight. The strongest levers are fewer KV heads (grouped-query or multi-query attention), lower precision (FP8), and sharing prefixes. Quantizing weights does not help the cache.
Your vLLM server shows many preemptions and a falling throughput. What do you check? || Pool size (gpu_memory_utilization), max_num_seqs, and actual context lengths versus max_model_len; whether prefix caching is evicting useful blocks; whether swap or recompute is used. Fixes: cap concurrent sequences, shorten maximum length, quantize the cache, use tensor parallelism to add memory, or add replicas.
Why not just allocate cache in chunks of, say, 256 tokens? || That is paging with a large block: the waste in the last block grows (up to 255 slots, average about 128), and prefix sharing becomes coarse because only whole blocks can be shared. Small blocks need more table entries and gathers, but 16 is already large enough for the kernel to use the GPU efficiently.
quiz: What are the three kinds of KV-cache memory waste in a contiguous allocator? || Reservation (space held for future tokens), internal fragmentation (unused part of the over-sized reservation), and external fragmentation (unusable gaps between buffers).
How does a PagedAttention kernel find the keys of a request? || It walks the request's logical blocks, looks up each physical block id in the block table, loads that block's keys and values, and combines the partial scores across blocks with an online softmax.
How much KV cache does one 2,048-token request need for OPT-13B, and how many such requests fit in 12 GB? || About 800 KB per token, so 2,048 × 0.8 MB ≈ 1.6 GB, and 12 / 1.6 ≈ 7 requests with maximum-length reservation.
What happens when two sequences share a block and one needs to write? || The block has reference count 2, so the writer gets a new block, the old content is copied into it, the old count drops to 1, and the writer's table points to the copy. The other sequence keeps the original.
When would you choose swap instead of recompute for a preempted request? || When the request has a long context so recomputing the prefill is slow, and CPU memory and PCIe bandwidth are available; for short contexts or small blocks recompute is usually cheaper.

@@ denoising
value: The denoising objective is the training recipe behind diffusion models. Take a real example (an image, a sound, a video latent), mix it with a random amount of Gaussian noise, and train a neural network to predict the noise that was added. To generate something new, start from pure noise and apply the trained network many times, each time removing a little of the predicted noise, until a clean sample appears. The training task is simple: it is plain regression with a squared error, one noise level at a time, with no adversary and no unstable balance between two networks. That is why diffusion replaced GANs for image generation after 2021: training is stable and the models cover the variety of the data, instead of collapsing onto a few modes.
What does the layer above get from it? The diffusion models of L1 (Stable Diffusion, DALL·E, Imagen, video models such as Sora, audio and protein models, and robot-policy models) differ in architecture, text conditioning and sampler, but nearly all of them train with a variant of this one loss. It also gives a clean way to add control: a network that has learned to denoise can be steered by a text prompt, a class label or another image.
Three lower layers explain why it works. Stochastic processes (L4) describe the forward process: adding a little Gaussian noise at each of about 1,000 steps is a Markov chain, and in the limit of many small steps a stochastic differential equation. Probability (L4) supplies the Gaussian algebra that gives a closed formula for any noise level, the variational bound that justifies the loss, and the fact that predicting the noise is the same as estimating the score, the gradient of the log-probability of the data. Differential equations (L4) give the generation side: sampling is the solution of an ODE or SDE run backward in time, and solver theory explains why 20 to 50 steps can replace 1,000.
terms: Forward process — the fixed, non-learned procedure that turns data into noise by adding Gaussian noise over T steps.
Noise schedule — the numbers \( \beta_t \) (how much noise is added at step t) and their running product \( \bar\alpha_t \), which fix how much of the original signal remains at step t.
ε-prediction — the network \( \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \) is trained to output the noise \( \varepsilon \) that was mixed into the clean data to make \( x_t \).
Score — the gradient of the log-density of the data distribution, \( \nabla \log p(x) \); it points toward more likely data.
Reverse process (sampler) — the loop that starts at pure noise and uses the network to step from x_t to x_(t−1).
Classifier-free guidance — a way to make samples follow the prompt more closely by extrapolating from the unconditional prediction toward the conditional one.
Latent diffusion — running the diffusion in the compressed latent space of an autoencoder, not in pixel space.
Flow matching (rectified flow) — a related objective that trains the network to predict a velocity along a straight line between noise and data.
history: The first diffusion model was Sohl-Dickstein, Weiss, Maheswaranathan and Ganguli, "Deep Unsupervised Learning using Nonequilibrium Thermodynamics" (2015): destroy structure with a slow diffusion process, and learn to reverse it. It worked on small images and was mostly ignored. In parallel, Hyvärinen (2005) proposed score matching, and Vincent (2011) showed that training a denoising autoencoder is equivalent to learning the score of noise-smoothed data. Song and Ermon (NeurIPS 2019) built on this: they trained one network to estimate the score at many noise levels and sampled with Langevin dynamics, producing good images.
The two lines joined in Ho, Jain and Abbeel, "Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models" (NeurIPS 2020). They showed that a simplified loss, the plain mean squared error on the noise, and a U-Net network gave images of quality comparable to GANs on CIFAR-10 and 256×256 LSUN, using T = 1,000 steps. Nichol and Dhariwal (2021) improved the schedule and likelihoods, and Dhariwal and Nichol (2021) showed "Diffusion Models Beat GANs on Image Synthesis". Song and co-authors (ICLR 2021) unified both views as stochastic differential equations and derived the probability-flow ODE; DDIM (Song, Meng and Ermon, 2021) gave a deterministic sampler with far fewer steps.
Text-to-image followed quickly: classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans, 2021), GLIDE, DALL·E 2 and Imagen (2022), and latent diffusion (Rombach et al., CVPR 2022), whose open release as Stable Diffusion in August 2022 started the wave of public image generators. Karras et al. (EDM, 2022) cleaned up the design space: parameterization, schedule and solver. In 2022 and 2023, flow matching (Lipman et al.) and rectified flow (Liu et al.) proposed straight-line training paths, and Peebles and Xie (DiT) replaced the U-Net with a Transformer. Stable Diffusion 3 (Esser et al., 2024) combined rectified flow with a Transformer, and many recent image and video models follow this recipe.
how: The forward process is fixed and has a closed form. Choose a small variance \( \beta_t \) at each step \( t = 1, \dots, T \), for example β increasing linearly from 0.0001 to 0.02 over T = 1,000 steps in DDPM. One step is \( x_t = \sqrt{1 - \beta_t}\, x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\, z \), with \( z \) standard Gaussian noise. Write \( \alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t \) and \( \bar\alpha_t = \alpha_1 \alpha_2 \cdots \alpha_t \). Because sums of Gaussians are Gaussian, you can jump directly to any step: \( x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon \), with \( \varepsilon \) a standard Gaussian vector. For the DDPM schedule \( \bar\alpha_T \) is about 0.00004, so at the last step the data is almost entirely gone and \( x_T \) looks like pure noise. This closed form is why training is cheap: you never have to simulate the chain.
Training then takes only a few lines. Pick a real example x_0, pick a random step t, draw ε, compute \( x_t \) with the formula above, and feed \( (x_t, t) \) to the network \( \varepsilon_\theta \). The loss is the squared error \( \lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \rVert^2 \), averaged over the batch. Many noise levels are learned by a single network because t is an input (usually through a sinusoidal embedding, as in Transformers). Why is predicting noise a good target? From the formula, the noise and the clean data are two views of the same information once \( x_t \) is known: \( x_0 = \bigl(x_t - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon\bigr) / \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} \). And the score of the noisy data is \( \nabla \log p_t(x_t) = -\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t] / \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \). So the network that predicts noise is also a score estimator, which is the link to probability theory: it learns which direction in x-space makes the sample more likely under the data distribution at that noise level. The full DDPM objective is a variational bound on the log-likelihood with a weight for each t, and Ho et al. found that dropping the weights (the "simple" loss) gives better samples.
Sampling runs the chain backward. Start with \( x_T \) drawn from a standard Gaussian. For \( t = T \) down to 1, compute the predicted noise \( \hat\varepsilon = \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \), then the mean of the previous step \( \mu = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}} \left( x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}}\, \hat\varepsilon \right) \), and set \( x_{t-1} = \mu + \sigma_t z \) with \( \sigma_t^2 \) around \( \beta_t \) (no noise is added at the last step). Each step needs one forward pass of the network, so 1,000 steps cost 1,000 passes. This is the slow part. The SDE view explains how to cut it. The reverse of the noising SDE needs the score, which the network provides; a deterministic ODE (the probability-flow ODE) has the same marginal distributions. Solving an ODE with a high-order solver (DDIM, Heun, DPM-Solver) works with big steps because the trajectories are smooth, which gives good images in about 10 to 50 evaluations. Distillation methods (progressive distillation, consistency models) compress this to 1 to 4 steps, with some loss in quality or diversity.
Other output parameterizations exist because they change how the loss weights the noise levels. Predicting \( x_0 \) directly is bad at high noise (the answer is a blur) and good at low noise; predicting ε is the opposite; v-prediction (\( v = \sqrt{\bar\alpha}\,\varepsilon - \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha}\, x_0 \)) is a balance used in many systems; flow matching predicts a velocity. In flow matching, \( x_t = (1 - t)\, x_0 + t\varepsilon \) is a straight line from data to noise, the target is the constant velocity \( \varepsilon - x_0 \), and the sampler integrates an ODE along nearly straight paths, which needs fewer steps.
Conditioning is added by giving the network extra inputs: a text embedding from a language or CLIP/T5 encoder through cross-attention, a class label, or another image. During training the condition is dropped (replaced by an empty one) about 10% of the time, so one network learns both conditional and unconditional predictions. At sampling time, classifier-free guidance forms \( \hat\varepsilon = \varepsilon(x, \varnothing) + w\,\bigl(\varepsilon(x, c) - \varepsilon(x, \varnothing)\bigr) \), where \( w \) around 5 to 8 is common in text-to-image. A larger \( w \) follows the prompt more strictly but reduces diversity. This doubles the cost per step (two network calls, often batched).
Finally, pixel-space diffusion at high resolution is expensive. Latent diffusion first trains an autoencoder to compress an image, 512×512×3 = 786,432 numbers into a 64×64×4 = 16,384 numbers latent (48 times fewer), then runs diffusion in that space and decodes at the end. The denoiser sees a smaller problem, and the autoencoder handles fine detail.

example: Work with one number as the "image". Suppose the clean value is \( x_0 = 0.8 \), and at step t the schedule gives \( \bar\alpha_t = 0.64 \). Then \( \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} = 0.8 \) and \( \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} = 0.6 \). Draw \( \varepsilon = 1.0 \). The noisy value is \( x_t = 0.8 \times 0.8 + 0.6 \times 1.0 = 1.24 \). The signal-to-noise ratio is \( \bar\alpha/(1 - \bar\alpha) = 0.64/0.36 = 1.78 \): the signal power is still larger than the noise power.
Suppose the untrained network predicts \( \hat\varepsilon = 0.8 \). The loss is (1.0 − 0.8)² = 0.04. The gradient moves the network so that, for this x_t and t, the prediction gets closer to 1.0. As a check, convert the prediction into an estimate of the clean value: \( \hat x_0 = (x_t - 0.6\,\hat\varepsilon) / 0.8 = (1.24 - 0.48) / 0.8 = 0.95 \), compared with the true 0.8. A perfect noise prediction (\( \hat\varepsilon = 1.0 \)) gives (1.24 − 0.6) / 0.8 = 0.8 exactly. If the whole data set was only the single point 0.8, the network could always do this. Real data has many points, so for a given x_t the network can only predict the average noise over all the clean values that could have produced it, and that average is exactly the score information.
Now one reverse step in the DDPM sampler, with \( \alpha_t = 0.98 \) (so \( \beta_t = 0.02 \)) and the same \( x_t = 1.24 \) and \( \hat\varepsilon = 0.8 \). The mean is \( \mu = (1/\sqrt{0.98}) \times (1.24 - (0.02/0.6) \times 0.8) = 1.0102 \times (1.24 - 0.0267) = 1.0102 \times 1.2133 = 1.2257 \). Then add fresh noise with standard deviation \( \sqrt{\beta_t} \approx 0.141 \): \( x_{t-1} = 1.2257 + 0.141 z \). The step moved the value from 1.24 to about 1.226, a very small shift. That is how the method works: each of the 1,000 steps removes a small part of the noise, and the network, which sees the current x and t, keeps the path heading toward the data. In a deterministic DDIM step the fresh noise is left out, and the step can be made larger.
For scale: a Stable Diffusion image at 512×512 uses a 64×64×4 latent. With 30 solver steps and classifier-free guidance, the denoiser runs 60 times (two passes per step), on a network of around 0.9 billion parameters in the original version. That is why generating one image takes seconds on a GPU, and why step distillation matters for products.
intuition: A sculptor who learned exactly how dust settles on statues can reverse it: begin with a block of dust and brush away a little at a time, always guided by what the dust "wants" to be.
Destroying structure is easy and has a known formula; building structure is hard. The trick is to make building structure a sequence of tiny, easy problems. At very high noise the best guess is only the overall mean; at medium noise the network decides the large shapes; at low noise it fills in texture. Each step needs only a small correction, and a correction at one noise level is a regression problem with a clear target (the noise we added). Learning many easy regressions is much simpler than learning to produce a whole image in one shot, and it avoids the adversarial game of GANs.
mistakes: People often think the network generates the image from the noise in one shot. In fact it only estimates the noise (or the direction toward cleaner data) at the current level. The image emerges from many small steps, and early steps decide the large layout while late steps add detail.
People often think the model memorizes noise patterns or "removes the noise" from a specific picture. In fact the noise is random each time, and the network learns a statistical property of the whole data set: where the data tends to lie at each noise level. That is why it can create new samples, although at small data sizes or with duplicated training images it can reproduce training examples.
People often think more sampling steps always give better images. In fact quality saturates at roughly 20 to 50 steps for good ODE solvers, and with stochastic samplers extra steps may change the style. Over-guidance (w too large) gives oversaturated, less varied images however many steps you use.
People often think diffusion and flow matching are completely different methods. In fact they are close relatives: both train a network by regression to move between noise and data. Flow matching uses a straight-line path and a velocity target; diffusion uses a noise schedule and a noise target. Their parametrizations can be converted into each other, and the main difference is in the path and the loss weighting.
friction: Generation is slow compared with a GAN or an autoregressive decoder that needs one pass per output. Even with good solvers, 20 to 50 network calls per sample (double with guidance) is costly for video, where each call handles many frames. Distillation to 1 to 4 steps is active research, and it commonly loses some diversity or detail. Training also needs large amounts of data and compute: leading models use hundreds of millions to billions of images or clips.
The loss does not measure what we care about. A low squared error on noise says little about whether hands look right, text is spelled correctly or objects are counted right; fine control and compositional prompts remain hard. Evaluation relies on proxies such as FID (a distance between feature statistics) and human preference, which can be gamed. The choice of noise schedule, loss weighting and resolution-dependent shifts is partly empirical, and a schedule tuned for 256×256 images is poor at 1024×1024 because the same noise hides less information at higher resolution.
Diffusion is natural for continuous data. For discrete data such as text or code, adding Gaussian noise does not apply directly; discrete diffusion variants (masking and denoising tokens) exist and are being studied, but autoregressive Transformers are still the main approach for text. Finally, there are open questions on training-data consent and copyright, on memorization of rare training images, and on misuse for deceptive media, none of which a better loss function solves.
practice: If you want to use diffusion, start from a library: Hugging Face diffusers has the schedulers, samplers and pipelines, and many open models are available. Choose a recent flow-matching or rectified-flow model with a Transformer backbone for new work, and use the default sampler first. Tune three things in order: the number of steps (try 20 to 30), the guidance scale (try 3 to 7 for flow models, 5 to 8 for older Stable Diffusion models), and the resolution the model was trained for.
When training your own, the details matter more than the architecture. Use an exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights for sampling, since the raw weights are noisy. Sample the timestep with care (logit-normal sampling, as in SD3, spends more steps on the middle noise levels where learning is richest). Normalize the data and the latent to unit variance, shift the noise schedule when you change resolution, and drop the condition 10% of the time to enable classifier-free guidance. Watch the loss per noise level, not only the average, since the average hides failures at one level.
To fine-tune cheaply, use LoRA adapters or a small number of images with DreamBooth-style training, and keep the base model frozen. Outside images, the same objective works for audio, molecules and protein structures, and for action sequences in robot control (Diffusion Policy), where the network denoises a short trajectory conditioned on camera input. To learn it, derive the closed-form step and the ε-to-score link on paper, then implement a 1-D or 2-D toy version (a mixture of Gaussians) in an afternoon; Lilian Weng's post and the DDPM paper are the shortest reliable path.
tradeoffs: Choose diffusion or flow matching over a GAN when you need coverage of the whole data distribution and stable training, and can pay 20 to 50 network calls per sample. You give up single-pass speed (a GAN generator needs one pass, about 20 to 50 times less compute per sample) and accept more costly serving. Choose a GAN or a distilled one-step model only when latency is the main constraint and you can train adversarially or distill from a diffusion teacher.
Choose latent diffusion over pixel diffusion for images above about 256×256: 48 times fewer values per image means much cheaper training and sampling. You give up the ability to model the finest detail exactly, because the autoencoder limits reconstruction quality (small text and faces can suffer), and you depend on a separate autoencoder. Pixel-space diffusion is the better choice for small images or when reconstruction quality of detail is the product.
Choose ε-prediction (or v-prediction) with a standard schedule when you want a well-tested recipe; choose flow-matching velocity prediction with a Transformer when you want fewer steps and are training a large new model, as SD3 did. You give up the large body of tuned tricks for older recipes, but gain straighter paths and simpler training.
Choose a deterministic ODE sampler with 20 to 30 steps for most applications; choose a stochastic SDE sampler with more steps when sample diversity or error correction matters (stochastic noise can fix earlier errors). Choose classifier-free guidance with a moderate scale (about 3 to 8) when prompt adherence matters, paying double compute per step and some loss of diversity.
Choose few-step distillation when serving cost dominates (a consumer product with millions of images per day); you pay a one-time distillation cost and some quality, and you lose fine control such as negative prompts and variable guidance unless the distilled model keeps them.
interview: Write the DDPM training objective and say why predicting the noise is reasonable. || Sample \( x_0 \), t and \( \varepsilon \); form \( x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon \); minimize \( \lVert \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \rVert^2 \). The noise and the clean data determine each other given x_t, and the expected noise is proportional to the score of the noisy distribution, so this is denoising score matching. The simple unweighted form gave better samples than the exact variational bound.
Why can you train in one step without simulating the whole forward chain? || A sum of independent Gaussian steps is Gaussian, so \( x_t \) given \( x_0 \) is \( \mathcal{N}\bigl(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0, (1 - \bar\alpha_t) I\bigr) \). Sampling it directly costs one formula, so each training example can use a random t. Training cost is one network pass per example, independent of T.
Why does sampling take 1,000 steps in DDPM and how do we reduce it? || The reverse step is a Gaussian approximation that is only accurate for small steps. The same marginals are given by a probability-flow ODE, which is smooth, so higher-order solvers (DDIM, DPM-Solver, Heun) take large steps and reach good quality in 10 to 50 evaluations. Distillation or consistency models go to 1 to 4 steps at some cost in quality.
What is classifier-free guidance, and what does it cost? || Train one network with the condition randomly dropped, then at sampling time extrapolate: \( \varepsilon(x, \varnothing) + w\bigl(\varepsilon(x, c) - \varepsilon(x, \varnothing)\bigr) \). It strengthens prompt adherence at the cost of diversity and doubles the network calls per step. A guidance scale that is too high saturates colors and reduces variety.
How does flow matching differ from the DDPM objective, and why might it need fewer steps? || It defines a straight-line path \( x_t = (1 - t)\, x_0 + t\varepsilon \) and regresses the constant velocity \( \varepsilon - x_0 \). Straighter paths have less curvature, so an ODE solver makes fewer errors per step. The network, data and training loop are nearly the same; the schedule and target change.
Your diffusion model produces good low-resolution images but poor details at 1024×1024 after you scale up. What do you suspect? || The noise schedule is resolution-dependent: the same noise level destroys less information in a larger image, so the schedule needs shifting toward more noise (as in SD3). Also check latent scaling, the autoencoder's reconstruction limit, and whether the training mix contained enough high-resolution examples.
quiz: What does the network predict in DDPM training? || The noise ε that was added to the clean data to produce x_t, given x_t and the step t.
Why does the closed-form \( x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\, x_0 + \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t}\, \varepsilon \) matter? || It lets training jump to any noise level in one step, so each training example uses a random t without running the chain, and training cost does not depend on T.
How are predicting the noise and estimating the score related? || The score of the noisy data equals \( -\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid x_t] / \sqrt{1 - \bar\alpha_t} \), so a noise predictor is a score estimator up to a known scale factor.
With \( \bar\alpha_t = 0.64 \), \( x_0 = 0.8 \) and \( \varepsilon = 1.0 \), what is \( x_t \) and what clean value does a perfect noise prediction give back? || \( x_t = 0.8 \times 0.8 + 0.6 \times 1.0 = 1.24 \); with \( \hat\varepsilon = 1.0 \), \( \hat x_0 = (1.24 - 0.6)/0.8 = 0.8 \).
When would you prefer latent diffusion over pixel-space diffusion? || For larger images (above about 256×256), because a 512×512×3 image becomes a 64×64×4 latent, 48 times fewer values, which makes training and sampling much cheaper, at the cost of a lossy autoencoder.

value: A machine-learning compiler takes a model written in a high-level framework and turns it into a small number of fast programs for a GPU or TPU. The researcher writes plain Python with PyTorch or JAX. The compiler records which tensor operations the code performs, rewrites that record to remove wasted work (the biggest saving is fusing many small operations into one kernel so intermediate results never go to main memory), picks memory layouts, and generates code for the specific hardware. In the PyTorch 2 paper (ASPLOS 2024), torch.compile gave a geometric-mean speedup of 2.27 times for inference and 1.41 times for training on an A100 over more than 180 real models, with no change to the model code.
The need is easy to see. Without a compiler, a framework runs "eagerly": every operation (add, multiply, softmax, layer norm) is one GPU kernel launch that reads its inputs from HBM and writes its output back. Modern GPUs have far more arithmetic power than memory bandwidth, so chains of cheap operations are limited by memory traffic, not by arithmetic. A compiler that sees the whole chain can keep intermediate values in registers or on-chip memory. It also removes the Python and kernel-launch overhead (a few microseconds per operation) that dominates small models and the decode step of language models.
For the layer above, this is what lets PyTorch, JAX and TensorFlow stay easy to write while running well on new hardware; the same model can be lowered to NVIDIA GPUs, AMD GPUs, TPUs and CPUs by changing the back end. The layers below make it work. An intermediate representation in static single assignment form (L3) is the data structure the compiler transforms: a graph in which every value is produced once, so passes like dead-code removal, common-subexpression elimination and fusion are simple graph rewrites. GPU architecture (L3) tells the code generator how to map work to thread blocks, warps and shared memory. The roofline model (L3) explains the goal: fusion raises arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte moved) so that kernels move up toward the compute roof.
terms: Eager execution — running each framework operation immediately as its own kernel, as Python reaches it.
Graph capture (tracing) — recording the sequence of tensor operations a function performs into a graph, so it can be optimized as a whole.
Intermediate representation (IR) — the compiler's internal form of the program; examples are the FX graph in PyTorch, the jaxpr in JAX, HLO/StableHLO in XLA, and MLIR dialects.
Operator fusion — merging several operations into one kernel so intermediate tensors stay in registers or shared memory.
Graph break — a point where the capture mechanism meets Python code it cannot trace and falls back to eager execution, splitting the graph in two.
Guard — a run-time check (on shapes, dtypes or Python values) that the compiled code is still valid for the current inputs; a failed guard triggers recompilation.
Triton — a Python-based language in which a programmer writes a GPU kernel at the level of one block of data, and the compiler handles threads, shared memory and tensor-core instructions.
Autotuning — trying several tile sizes or kernel variants on the real hardware and keeping the fastest.
history: The idea of compiling numerical code for new hardware is older than deep learning: Halide (Ragan-Kelley et al., 2013) separated the algorithm from its schedule (loop order, tiling) for image processing. Theano (Montreal, 2010) compiled symbolic graphs for GPUs. TensorFlow (2015) was built around a static graph, and Google announced XLA (Accelerated Linear Algebra) in 2017 to compile those graphs for CPUs, GPUs and its TPUs. Apache TVM (Chen et al., OSDI 2018) offered an end-to-end stack that searched for fast schedules per hardware target with learned cost models. Facebook's Glow (2018) and Intel's nGraph took related routes for inference.
PyTorch's eager style, with define-by-run graphs, won over researchers, but it made compilation hard because the graph does not exist until the code runs. TorchScript (2018) tried to capture programs with tracing or a restricted Python subset; many users found it too limiting. In March 2023 PyTorch 2.0 introduced torch.compile with a different approach: TorchDynamo hooks into the Python interpreter, captures graphs from bytecode, and falls back safely when it cannot. JAX (Frostig, Johnson and Leary, 2018) took the opposite route, tracing pure functions into jaxprs and sending them to XLA.
For kernels, Philippe Tillet's Triton (Tillet, Kung and Cox, MAPL 2019; open-sourced by OpenAI as Triton 1.0 in 2021) lets people write fused kernels in Python at the block level and made TorchInductor possible: Inductor generates Triton code on GPUs. MLIR (Lattner et al., CGO 2021), from Google and later part of LLVM, supplied shared compiler infrastructure with many "dialects" at different levels, and now underlies StableHLO, newer Triton versions and several hardware compilers. The recent trend is to combine: compiler-generated code for the long tail of operations, vendor libraries (cuBLAS, cuDNN) for large matrix multiplies, and hand-written kernels (FlashAttention) for the hot spots.
how: A compiler for ML has four stages: capture, optimize, generate, run. Take torch.compile. Capture is TorchDynamo. It uses a hook in CPython (PEP 523) to look at the bytecode of a function before it runs, and symbolically executes it: tensor operations are recorded into an FX graph; Python-level things (loops over a list, attribute lookups, Python integers) are evaluated away at capture time, and the values that the graph depends on become guards. If the code does something Dynamo cannot trace, such as calling an unsupported library, reading a tensor value with .item(), or branching on tensor data, Dynamo ends the graph there (a graph break), runs that part eagerly, and starts a new graph afterwards. Dynamo captured 74 of 80 TorchBench models without changes in the paper (and all 46 HuggingFace and 62 TIMM models tested).
Training needs the backward graph too. AOTAutograd traces the forward and backward passes ahead of time using the autograd machinery (see automatic differentiation) and produces a joint graph written in a small set of core operations (a few hundred "ATen" or "prim" operators). This lowering to a smaller operator set is what makes later passes tractable. A partitioner then splits the joint graph into forward and backward programs and decides which intermediate tensors to save and which to recompute in the backward pass, using a min-cut formulation: recomputing a cheap pointwise operation costs less than storing and reloading its output. This is activation checkpointing chosen automatically.
Optimize and generate is TorchInductor. It turns the graph into a loop-level IR and fuses operations. Pointwise operations (add, multiply, GELU, cast) are always worth fusing with each other and with the reduction that follows (softmax, layer norm), because the intermediate result is only needed once. Inductor generates one Triton kernel per fused group; on CPU it generates C++ with OpenMP. Large matrix multiplications and convolutions are normally sent to cuBLAS or cuDNN, or, in max-autotune mode, Inductor tries several Triton matmul templates and library calls and keeps the fastest, including fusing the epilogue (bias and activation) into the matmul kernel. Memory planning reuses buffers whose lifetimes do not overlap, which reduces peak memory.
Triton handles the lowest level. The programmer, or Inductor, writes a kernel for one "program instance" that operates on a tile, for example a block of 1,024 elements or a 128×128 matrix tile, using tensor operations on blocks. The Triton compiler decides how the threads of a thread block (the GPU's SIMT hardware, with warps of 32 threads) divide the tile, inserts coalesced loads, stages data in shared memory, and emits tensor-core matrix instructions. The programmer thus writes about the same amount of code as a CPU loop but gets code close to hand-tuned CUDA for many kernels. Autotuning decorators try several block sizes.
XLA follows a similar plan with a more static design. JAX traces a function into a jaxpr, lowers it to StableHLO, and XLA optimizes the whole program: it fuses operations, assigns layouts, plans buffers, and generates code for GPU, TPU or CPU. With jit, shapes are part of the compile key, so a new shape means a recompile. Whole-program visibility lets XLA also insert communication for sharded arrays (GSPMD), which is why XLA is strong on TPU pods.
Because every graph in this pipeline is in single-assignment form (each value is defined once and has a clear list of users), the classic compiler passes apply directly. Dead-code elimination removes operations whose results nobody reads. Common-subexpression elimination merges two identical computations. Constant folding computes at compile time anything that depends only on constants, such as a causal mask. Layout assignment chooses, for example, channels-last storage for convolutions so that no transposes are needed between layers. Each pass is small and local; the gain comes from running them all on a whole graph.
At run time, the compiled artifacts are cached. In PyTorch each compiled graph is stored with its guards; at each call the guards are checked (cheap Python and C++ checks), and if they pass, the compiled code is used. If shapes change, Dynamo can mark a dimension as dynamic and compile with symbolic shapes, so one program serves many sizes. The reduce-overhead mode uses CUDA Graphs to replay a recorded sequence of kernel launches with a single CPU call, which removes launch overhead for small kernels.

    fused : [ load x -> mul -> add -> relu -> store y ]    (2 passes)
example: Take a simple chain: y = relu(x * a + b), where x is a 4096 × 4096 matrix in 16-bit format. One copy of x is 4096 × 4096 × 2 bytes = 33.5 MB. In eager mode three kernels run. The multiply reads x and writes t1 (2 passes over 33.5 MB). The add reads t1 and writes t2 (2 passes). The ReLU reads t2 and writes y (2 passes). The total is 6 passes, about 201 MB moved. At about 1.5 TB/s of effective HBM bandwidth, that takes 201 / 1,500,000 s ≈ 134 µs. The arithmetic is 3 operations per element, 50 million operations, which a GPU that does 300 trillion per second finishes in about 0.2 µs. The kernel runs at less than 1% of the compute peak; its arithmetic intensity is about 0.25 operations per byte, while an A100 needs roughly 200 operations per byte (312 TFLOPs divided by 1.5 TB/s) to be compute-bound. This is the memory-bound corner of the roofline.
A fused kernel loads x once, does the multiply, add and ReLU in registers, and stores y once. That is 2 passes, 67 MB, about 45 µs, three times faster. Add the launch overheads: each eager kernel launch costs roughly 5 to 10 µs of CPU time, which can be hidden for large tensors but not for small ones; fusing three kernels into one removes two launches.
Now count what the backward pass does if this chain sits in a training graph. Eager PyTorch keeps t1 and t2 for the backward pass (another 67 MB of memory). The AOTAutograd partitioner notices that t1 and t2 are cheap to recompute from x, a and b, so it saves only x (and the ReLU mask can be rebuilt from y). Memory for activations drops from 3 tensors to 1, and the backward kernel recomputes the chain in registers, again reading x once. This is why compiled training often uses less memory as well as running faster. In the same way, softmax followed by dropout followed by a residual add become one kernel, and layer norm (a reduction followed by pointwise scaling) becomes one pass over the data instead of five.
Finally the cost side. The first call of a compiled function runs Dynamo, AOTAutograd, Inductor, and Triton's compiler: for a small model this takes several seconds, for a large Transformer it takes from tens of seconds to several minutes. If you call the function a million times, the compile time is negligible; if you run a script once on small data, the compile time is the whole runtime and the result is slower than eager.
intuition: Instead of carrying each ingredient across the kitchen separately, plan the whole recipe and do as many steps as possible at one bench.
The design is natural because in ML programs most time goes to moving data, and a person writing model code cannot also decide how every pair of operations should be fused. A compiler can see the whole graph and has the time to try several ways. It is a division of labour: the human writes what to compute in the clearest way; the compiler decides how to schedule it on this particular chip. The cost of the approach is that the compiler must understand the program, which is why capturing Python (a very dynamic language) is the hardest part.
mistakes: People often think a compiler makes every model faster. In fact it helps most when a model has many small, memory-bound operations and the shapes are stable. A model dominated by a few huge matrix multiplications already runs near peak in cuBLAS, and the compiler adds little, but adds compile time.
People often think torch.compile is just a faster version of TorchScript or tracing. In fact Dynamo works on bytecode and uses guards and graph breaks to remain correct for general Python. When it cannot capture something it falls back, so the result is correct but may be slower than expected. This is also why "it ran without error" does not mean "it was compiled well".
People often think the compiler produces numerically identical results. In fact fusion changes the order and precision of intermediate values (for example, keeping intermediates in FP32 inside a kernel rather than rounding to FP16 between operations), so results differ slightly from eager mode. They are usually more accurate, but not bit-identical.
People often think Triton or the compiler replaces hand-written CUDA completely. In fact the top-performing kernels for new attention and mixture-of-experts patterns on the newest GPUs are still written with low-level tools. The FlashAttention-4 paper reports its kernel at up to 2.7 times faster than a Triton implementation on Blackwell, because the newest hardware features arrive in vendor tools before high-level compilers expose them.
friction: Graph breaks are the main performance trap. Every .item(), print, data-dependent if statement, unsupported library call or custom Python object can split the graph, and each break means a boundary where intermediates must go to memory and fusion is lost. Recompilation is the second: if shapes, dtypes or Python values change, guards fail and the code is recompiled, and after a limit (eight variants per function by default) PyTorch gives up and runs eagerly. Variable-length inputs, such as sequences of different length in each batch, can cause either many recompiles or the use of dynamic shapes, which can be slightly slower and sometimes unsupported by an operator.
Compile time is real for large models and for CI tests. Debugging is harder: errors point into generated code, the profiler shows kernel names such as triton_poi_fused_add_mul_relu_0, and a bug may appear only after compilation. Tools exist (TORCH_LOGS settings, torch._dynamo.explain, minifier for failures, fullgraph=True to make breaks an error), but they need experience. Performance is not portable: a schedule tuned on one GPU generation may be poor on another, and AMD or other back ends can lag behind NVIDIA.
There are still limits to what the compiler can find. It does not change the algorithm; it will not discover FlashAttention from a naive attention graph (though pattern matchers can replace known patterns), and a custom operator written in CUDA is opaque to it. Finally, the stack is changing fast: APIs, default settings and back ends move between PyTorch releases, so production use should pin versions and keep an eager fallback.
practice: Begin with one line: model = torch.compile(model). Measure before and after, in the right way: run a warm-up (the first call includes compilation), call torch.cuda.synchronize() before taking times, and compare steady-state time per step with a realistic batch. Use the PyTorch profiler to see whether there are many small kernels (the case where compiling helps) or a few big matmuls (where it will not).
If speed is lower than expected, find graph breaks. Run with TORCH_LOGS="graph_breaks,recompiles" or use torch.compile(fullgraph=True) while developing so that a break raises an error. Remove .item() and .cpu() calls inside the model, move Python control flow that depends on tensor values into torch.where or torch.cond, and keep shapes stable (pad to a few bucket sizes, or mark a dimension dynamic). For big Transformers, compile each repeated block (regional compilation) instead of the whole model to cut compile time, since the same block is reused many times.
Use mode="max-autotune" for production inference or long training runs with static shapes (the extra compile time is paid once), and mode="reduce-overhead" with CUDA Graphs for small-batch, latency-sensitive inference. Enable the on-disk compile cache so that restarts do not pay again. In JAX, wrap the whole training step in jit, keep it a pure function, use lax.scan or fori_loop in place of long Python loops (which would unroll and slow compilation), and use static_argnums for Python values. When a hot operation is still slow, write a Triton kernel or use FlexAttention-style APIs before dropping to CUDA, and check the generated code with TORCH_LOGS="output_code" to learn what the compiler did.
tradeoffs: Choose torch.compile when your training or inference runs for hours or serves many requests, shapes are stable, and the model has many pointwise and normalization operations. You gain typically 1.3 to 2 times in throughput (the paper's means were 1.41 times for training and 2.27 times for inference on A100). You give up fast start-up (compile time of seconds to minutes), some debuggability, and exact bit-reproducibility. Stay in eager mode for prototyping, short runs and unusual dynamic code.
Choose static shapes with a few padded bucket sizes over fully dynamic shapes when you need the best kernels and predictable latency; you waste a little compute on padding (for example 10 to 20%). Choose dynamic shapes when sizes vary widely (variable-length text) and recompiling for each size would be worse; you accept slightly slower kernels and more graph-break risk.
Choose a compiler-generated kernel (Inductor, Triton) for the long tail of fused pointwise and reduction ops; choose a vendor library (cuBLAS, cuDNN) for large dense matmuls and convolutions; choose a hand-written kernel (FlashAttention) when one operation takes more than about 20% of the step time and the pattern is stable. Each step down that list gives more speed and costs more engineering and more hardware-specific code.
Choose XLA/JAX when you target TPUs or want whole-program compilation with automatic sharding and can accept static shapes and a functional style. Choose PyTorch with torch.compile when your team values Python flexibility, a large model ecosystem, and incremental adoption, and accept more graph breaks. Choose TVM-style autotuning or a runtime such as TensorRT for deployment on fixed hardware where inference latency is critical and the model is frozen.
interview: What does torch.compile do, stage by stage? || TorchDynamo traces Python bytecode into an FX graph with guards, breaking the graph where it cannot trace. AOTAutograd captures the backward pass and lowers to core ops, with a partitioner choosing what to save or recompute. TorchInductor fuses operations and generates Triton kernels (C++ on CPU), calling cuBLAS or autotuned templates for matmuls. Compiled code is cached and reused while the guards hold.
Why does operator fusion give speedups when the arithmetic count stays the same? || Most ML operations are memory-bound: they have very low arithmetic intensity. Unfused, each operation writes its output to HBM and the next reads it back. Fused, intermediates stay in registers or shared memory, so the bytes moved fall by the number of intermediate tensors. Speed then follows the bytes.
Your compiled model is slower than eager. How do you diagnose it? || Check for graph breaks and recompilations with TORCH_LOGS and fullgraph=True; check that warm-up is excluded from timing; profile to see whether time is in huge matmuls (no gain expected) or in launch overhead; check dynamic shapes causing recompiles or slow kernels; and compare the mode (default versus max-autotune or reduce-overhead).
What is a guard, and what happens when a guard fails? || A guard is a check that the assumptions made during capture still hold (tensor shapes, dtypes, device, a Python flag). If one fails, the cached graph cannot be used, so Dynamo captures and compiles again for the new case; after too many recompiles it falls back to eager mode.
How is JAX's jit different from torch.compile in handling Python control flow? || jit traces the function with abstract values, so Python control flow runs once at trace time and is baked in; data-dependent branches must use lax.cond, scan or while_loop, and new shapes recompile. Dynamo works on bytecode with guards and graph breaks, so arbitrary Python works but may split the graph.
When would you still hand-write a kernel instead of relying on the compiler? || When one operation dominates the runtime and uses a new hardware feature the compiler does not yet expose (FlashAttention on a new GPU generation), or its structure (online softmax, block-sparse patterns) is something no general fusion pass finds. You pay in engineering time and per-architecture maintenance.
quiz: What are the main stages of torch.compile? || TorchDynamo (graph capture with guards), AOTAutograd (forward and backward graph in core ops), TorchInductor (fusion and code generation into Triton or C++), then cached execution.

value: Backpropagation is the algorithm that computes, for a neural network and a loss, the gradient of the loss with respect to every weight in one forward pass and one backward pass. The backward pass costs about two times a forward pass. Without it, you would have to nudge each weight separately and re-run the network to see how the loss changes: for a model with 7 billion weights that means about 7 billion forward passes for one gradient, instead of roughly three passes in total (one forward, one backward at twice the cost). Backpropagation is the reason gradient descent can train models with billions of parameters, and it is why the training cost of a Transformer is commonly estimated as about 6 FLOPs per parameter per token (2 for the forward pass, 4 for the backward pass).
The method is simple to state. Run the network forward and remember what each layer produced. Then walk backward from the loss. Each layer receives one piece of information, "how much does the loss change if my output changes?", and uses it to compute two things: how the loss changes with its own weights (which the optimizer will use) and how it changes with its input (which it hands to the layer before it). Every layer needs only local knowledge: its own inputs, its own formula, and the gradient arriving from above.
Two lower layers carry the idea. Calculus (L4) supplies the chain rule: the derivative of a composition \( f(g(x)) \) is \( f'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) \), so the gradient through a deep stack of layers is a product of per-layer derivatives. Graph algorithms (L3) supply the order of work. A network is a directed acyclic graph of operations, and a node's gradient is the sum of contributions from every node that uses its output. That sum can only be formed when all those consumers have been processed, so the backward pass visits nodes in reverse topological order, each exactly once. This is why the total cost is linear in the size of the graph.
For the layer above, deep learning, backpropagation is the training engine; the variational autoencoder, normalizing flow and GAN reads all rely on gradients passing through sampling, invertible layers or an adversary. Automatic differentiation frameworks implement it for any program, so people no longer derive it by hand.
terms: Gradient — the vector of partial derivatives of the loss with respect to all parameters; it points in the direction of fastest increase of the loss.
Chain rule — the rule for the derivative of a composed function: multiply the derivatives of each step.
Computation graph — the directed acyclic graph of operations that turns inputs and parameters into the loss.
Forward pass — evaluating the graph from inputs to loss while saving the intermediate values the backward pass will need.
Backward pass — walking the graph from the loss back to the parameters, passing along gradients.
Activation — the output of a layer; the activations saved during the forward pass are what use most of the training memory.
Local gradient (Jacobian) — the derivative of one operation's output with respect to its input; backprop multiplies the incoming gradient by it.
Vanishing / exploding gradient — a gradient that shrinks toward zero or grows without limit as it passes through many layers.
Activation checkpointing — saving only some activations and recomputing the rest in the backward pass to save memory.
history: Backpropagation has several parents. Control theorists, notably Henry Kelley (1960) and Arthur Bryson (1961), computed gradients backward through multi-stage systems. Seppo Linnainmaa described reverse-mode automatic differentiation of arbitrary programs in his 1970 master's thesis, to track rounding error. Paul Werbos, in his 1974 Harvard PhD thesis, proposed using it to train neural networks, and developed it in the early 1980s. David Parker (1985) and Yann LeCun (1985) found it independently.
It became famous through David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams, "Learning representations by back-propagating errors" (Nature, 1986). The timing mattered. Minsky and Papert's "Perceptrons" (1969) had shown that single-layer networks cannot learn simple functions such as XOR, and nobody had a good method to train hidden layers. The 1986 paper showed, with examples, that hidden units trained by gradient descent learn useful internal representations. LeCun then used it to train convolutional networks to read handwritten ZIP codes (1989).
Progress stalled in the 1990s for practical reasons. Hochreiter (1991) analysed the vanishing-gradient problem in deep and recurrent networks, which led to the LSTM (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997). Data and compute were small, and other methods such as support vector machines worked well. The revival came from GPUs and large data: AlexNet (2012) trained a deep convolutional network with backpropagation on two GPUs and won ImageNet by a wide margin. Later fixes made very deep networks trainable: ReLU activations, careful initialization, batch normalization (2015), and residual connections (He et al., 2015). Chen et al. (2016) introduced sublinear-memory training with checkpointing, and mixed-precision training (2017) added loss scaling so that 16-bit gradients do not underflow.
how: Take a layer with input a, weights W and bias b. In the forward pass it computes \( z = Wa + b \) and then \( a' = \sigma(z) \), where σ is an activation function. The network ends in a scalar loss L. The backward pass starts with \( \partial L/\partial L = 1 \) and moves toward the input. Suppose a layer receives \( g = \partial L/\partial a' \), the gradient of the loss with respect to its output. It first passes through the activation: \( \delta = \partial L/\partial z = g \odot \sigma'(z) \), an element-wise product. Then, for the linear part: \( \partial L/\partial W = \delta a^{\top} \) (an outer product), \( \partial L/\partial b = \delta \), and \( \partial L/\partial a = W^{\top} \delta \), which is what the previous layer receives as its g. These four lines are the whole algorithm for a fully connected layer. Convolution, attention, normalization and embeddings have their own versions of the same pattern: each operation knows how to turn a gradient on its output into gradients on its inputs and parameters.
Why go backward? There are two ways to apply the chain rule on a graph. Forward mode pushes derivative information from one input toward all outputs: it gives the derivative of every output with respect to one input per pass. Reverse mode starts from one output and gives the derivative of that output with respect to every input in one pass. A network has millions or billions of inputs (the parameters) and one output (the scalar loss), so reverse mode needs one pass where forward mode would need one pass per parameter. The cost of the backward pass is a small constant times the forward pass because each operation's gradient needs about the same arithmetic as the operation itself. For a matrix multiply it needs two matrix multiplies (one for \( \partial L/\partial W \) and one for \( \partial L/\partial a \)), so the backward pass is about twice the forward pass in FLOPs.
With a batch of examples the same equations hold with matrices: a becomes a matrix with one column per example, and \( \partial L/\partial W = \delta a^{\top} \) then sums the contributions of all examples in one matrix multiplication. This is why the backward pass runs as efficiently on a GPU as the forward pass, and why the per-example gradients are averaged for free. In a framework, the graph is recorded while the forward code runs (a tape), and calling backward() replays the tape in reverse; the tape holds references to the saved tensors, so they are freed only after the backward pass finishes.
Where a value is used by several later operations (a residual connection, a shared weight, or the embedding matrix tied to the output layer), the gradients from each use are added. This is the multivariable chain rule, and it is why the graph structure and its reverse topological order matter: the gradient of a node is complete only after all of its consumers have contributed. Frameworks handle it by accumulating into a gradient buffer (in PyTorch, `.grad` is added to on each backward call, which is also why you must zero it between steps).
The forward pass has to save things. The gradient of σ needs z (or its output), the gradient of W needs a, the gradient of a multiplication needs both operands. These saved activations are why training needs much more memory than inference. Per parameter, mixed-precision training with the Adam optimizer holds about 16 bytes: 2 for the 16-bit weight, 2 for its gradient, and 12 for the optimizer's FP32 master weight and two moment estimates. A 7-billion-parameter model therefore needs about 112 GB for these states alone, before a single activation is stored. Activations then scale with batch size, sequence length, width and depth, and are often the largest term. Activation checkpointing saves only some layers' inputs (for example one per Transformer block) and recomputes the rest during the backward pass, trading about one extra forward pass (roughly 33% more compute) for a large memory saving; with the best choice of checkpoints, memory grows like \( \sqrt{\text{number of layers}} \) instead of linearly.
Finally, the numbers that flow backward can shrink or grow. The gradient at an early layer is a product of many per-layer Jacobians. For a sigmoid, \( \sigma'(z) \le 0.25 \), so a chain of 20 sigmoid layers scales the gradient by at most \( 0.25^{20} \approx 10^{-12} \), and early layers cannot learn. If the Jacobians are larger than 1, the product explodes. The practical remedies are structural: ReLU-type activations whose derivative is 1 on the active side, residual connections that give the gradient a path with derivative exactly 1 (since \( d(x + f(x))/dx = 1 + f'(x) \)), normalization layers, weight initialization that keeps the scale near 1 (Glorot, He), and gradient clipping against explosions.
diagram:
  forward  (save what the backward pass needs)

   x -> [ z1 = w1*x ] -> [ h = sig(z1) ] -> [ yhat = w2*h ] -> [ L ]
   keep:     x               z1, h               h, w2        yhat-y

  backward (reverse topological order; each op maps grad-out to grad-in)

   dL/dw1 <-- dL/dz1 <-- dL/dh <-- dL/dyhat <-- 1

example: Take the smallest useful network: input x = 1.5, first weight w1 = 0.4, a sigmoid, second weight w2 = 1.2, target y = 1, and loss \( L = \tfrac{1}{2}(\hat y - y)^2 \). Forward: z = w1 x = 0.4 × 1.5 = 0.6. Then h = σ(0.6) = 0.6457. Then ŷ = w2 h = 1.2 × 0.6457 = 0.7748. The loss is ½ (0.7748 − 1)² = ½ × 0.0507 = 0.02536. Save x, h and w2.
Backward, one line per step. \( dL/d\hat y = \hat y - y = -0.2252 \). For w2: dL/dw2 = dL/dŷ × h = −0.2252 × 0.6457 = −0.1454. Pass back to h: dL/dh = dL/dŷ × w2 = −0.2252 × 1.2 = −0.2703. Through the sigmoid: \( \sigma'(z) = h(1 - h) = 0.6457 \times 0.3543 = 0.2288 \), so dL/dz = −0.2703 × 0.2288 = −0.0618. For w1: dL/dw1 = dL/dz × x = −0.0618 × 1.5 = −0.0927.
Check against the slow method. Nudge w1 by +0.000001 and −0.000001, recompute the loss, and divide the difference by 0.000002: you get −0.09275. It matches the backward value −0.0927. This finite-difference check (gradient checking) is how people test hand-written backward code, and it also shows the cost issue: this check needs two forward passes for each weight.
Take one gradient descent step with learning rate 0.5. w1 becomes 0.4 − 0.5 × (−0.0927) = 0.4464, and w2 becomes 1.2 − 0.5 × (−0.1454) = 1.2727. Re-running the forward pass gives ŷ = 1.2727 × σ(0.6696) = 1.2727 × 0.6616 = 0.8420 and a loss of 0.0125, about half the previous loss, from a single step. Notice that all the multiplications in the backward pass reuse numbers that the forward pass left behind (h, x, w2); this is what saved activations are.
Scale it up. For a layer with a 4,096 × 4,096 weight matrix, a batch of 8,192 tokens: the forward pass does 2 × 8,192 × 4,096 × 4,096 ≈ 275 GFLOPs. The backward pass does the same amount twice: one multiplication for the weight gradient and one for the input gradient, so about 550 GFLOPs. Total training compute for that layer is about 3 times the forward cost, and this is the origin of the "\( 6 \times \text{parameters} \times \text{tokens} \)" rule for whole models.
intuition: Blame flows backward: if the final answer is wrong, each step learns how much it contributed to the error and adjusts in proportion.
The method is natural because a loss is the end of a chain of simple steps, and each step knows its own slope. A small change in the weight of the first layer matters only through everything after it, so its effect is the product of the slopes along the way. Doing this once per weight would repeat the same products again and again; going from the end toward the start computes each shared product once and reuses it for every weight that lies before it. It is the same idea as dynamic programming: store the answer to the sub-problem "how does the loss change with this node?" and let all earlier nodes use it.
mistakes: People often think backpropagation is the learning algorithm. In fact it is only a method to compute gradients. Learning is done by an optimizer (SGD, Adam) that uses the gradients to change the weights. You could compute the same gradients by another method and use the same optimizer.
People often think backpropagation is an approximation, or that it estimates the gradient. In fact it computes the exact gradient of the loss on the current batch (up to floating-point error). The randomness in training comes from sampling the batch, not from backpropagation.
People often think the backward pass costs the same as the forward pass. In fact it costs about twice as much in FLOPs, because each matrix multiplication in the forward pass produces two multiplications in the backward pass (weight gradient and input gradient). With recomputation, training can cost about four times a forward pass.
People often think vanishing gradients mean a bug in backpropagation. In fact the algorithm is correct; the gradient is small because it is a product of many factors smaller than 1. The fix is in the architecture and initialization (residual connections, ReLU-like activations, normalization), not in the algorithm.
friction: The main cost is memory. Training stores activations for the whole forward pass, which for long sequences and large batches can exceed the memory of the weights several times over. Checkpointing, offloading, fusing kernels that recompute cheaply (see FlashAttention), and sharding states across GPUs (ZeRO, FSDP) all exist to cope with it. The backward pass is also harder to parallelize across layers than the forward pass is, because layer k needs the gradient from layer k+1; pipeline parallelism needs careful scheduling to avoid idle time ("bubbles").
Backpropagation needs differentiable operations. Discrete choices (argmax, sampling, rounding, hard routing in mixture-of-experts) give zero or undefined gradients, and people use workarounds: straight-through estimators, the reparameterization trick, or score-function (REINFORCE) estimators with high variance. Gradients through long recurrences (many time steps) explode or vanish, so recurrent models rely on gating or truncated backpropagation through time.
The method is also local and sequential in a way that brains seem not to be. Real neurons do not send exact error signals backward along the same weights, so backprop is considered biologically implausible; research on alternatives (feedback alignment, forward-forward, local learning rules) exists, but none matches backprop at scale. Lastly, low-precision training adds numerical problems: FP16 gradients underflow without loss scaling, and BF16 has fewer mantissa bits, so accumulation is often done in FP32.
practice: Do not write backward code by hand unless you must; let the framework (PyTorch autograd, JAX) derive it. When you do write a custom operation, test its gradient numerically against finite differences with a small input in double precision (torch.autograd.gradcheck). Remember the training-loop details: zero the gradients each step, call backward once per loss, and detach tensors that should not carry gradients (target networks, cached values).
Plan memory before you train. A rule of thumb for mixed-precision Adam is 16 bytes per parameter for weights, gradients and optimizer state, plus activations. For a 7B model that is about 112 GB, so it will not fit on one 80 GB GPU without sharding (FSDP or ZeRO) or 8-bit optimizers. If activations are the problem, enable checkpointing on the repeated blocks (it costs about 30% more time) or reduce the batch and use gradient accumulation: run several small micro-batches, add their gradients, then take one optimizer step.
Watch the gradients as a health signal. Log the global gradient norm: a sudden rise often precedes a loss spike, a norm near zero in early layers shows vanishing gradients. Clip the gradient norm (a common value is 1.0) for Transformers and recurrent models. Initialize with a scheme matched to the activation, use residual connections and normalization for depth, and use loss scaling (or BF16) with half precision. To learn the mechanism deeply, implement a tiny autograd engine yourself (Karpathy's micrograd is about 100 lines) and check it against PyTorch.
tradeoffs: Choose to store all activations when memory is plentiful and you want maximum speed; you pay in memory proportional to batch × sequence × depth. Choose activation checkpointing when activations limit your batch size or sequence length; you pay about one extra forward pass (roughly 30% more time per step) and gain a several-fold reduction in activation memory. Selective recomputation (recompute only cheap, memory-heavy parts such as attention scores) gives most of the saving at a smaller time cost.
Choose large micro-batches with no accumulation when throughput matters and memory allows it; choose gradient accumulation when the target batch size does not fit in memory. You keep the same mathematical gradient but lose some hardware efficiency and add synchronization points.
Choose full backpropagation for training and fine-tuning when you can afford it. Choose parameter-efficient fine-tuning (LoRA) or frozen layers when memory is tight: gradients and optimizer state exist only for the small adapter, cutting the 16 bytes per parameter to a small fraction, though the activations through the frozen layers are still stored. You give up some quality on tasks that need large changes in the model.
Choose zeroth-order or evolutionary methods (see Genetic algorithms) instead of backpropagation only when the objective is not differentiable or you cannot access the model's internals. They need many more evaluations (a number that grows with the number of parameters) and are noisy, so for a differentiable model with a large number of parameters backpropagation is cheaper by orders of magnitude.
Choose to cut a gradient (detach, stop-gradient) when part of the model should act as a fixed target, for example the target network in reinforcement learning or the teacher in distillation. You give up the learning signal through that path, which is the point.
interview: Why is reverse-mode differentiation used for neural networks instead of forward mode? || The network has a huge number of inputs (parameters) and one scalar output (the loss). Reverse mode gives the derivative of that one output with respect to all inputs in a single backward sweep, costing a small constant times the forward pass. Forward mode would need one pass per parameter.
Walk me through the backward pass of a linear layer followed by a ReLU. || With \( z = Wa + b \) and \( a' = \text{relu}(z) \): \( \delta = g \odot \mathbf{1}[z > 0] \); \( \partial L/\partial W = \delta a^{\top} \); \( \partial L/\partial b \) = sum of \( \delta \) over the batch; \( \partial L/\partial a = W^{\top}\delta \). The forward pass must have saved a and the sign of z. Each is a matmul or an element-wise operation of the same size as the forward ones.
Why does training use so much more memory than inference, and what can you do? || Training stores activations for the backward pass and also gradients and optimizer state (about 16 bytes per parameter with mixed-precision Adam). Options: checkpointing (recompute activations, about 30% more compute), smaller micro-batches with accumulation, sharding states across GPUs (ZeRO, FSDP), 8-bit optimizers, and fused kernels.
Why do residual connections help with vanishing gradients? || For \( y = x + f(x) \), the derivative is \( 1 + f'(x) \), so even if f' is small, the gradient has a direct path with factor 1 to earlier layers. Over many layers the product does not collapse the way a product of many numbers below 1 does.
A loss spike appears at step 40,000 and the gradient norm jumped 100 times just before. What do you do? || Treat it as instability: check data in that batch, learning rate and warm-up, add or lower gradient clipping, check for FP16 overflow or loss-scaling issues, and consider restarting from a checkpoint before the spike with the offending batches skipped or a lower learning rate. Look at which layers show the large norm.
How would you check that a custom backward function is correct? || Compare with finite differences on a small input in double precision: \( (f(x+\varepsilon) - f(x-\varepsilon))/2\varepsilon \) for a few coordinates, within a tolerance such as 1e-5 relative error (torch.autograd.gradcheck). Also test shapes, fan-out accumulation, and non-contiguous inputs.
quiz: What two quantities does each layer compute in the backward pass? || The gradient of the loss with respect to its own weights (used by the optimizer) and with respect to its input (passed to the previous layer).

value: Automatic differentiation (AD) is a method that takes a program that computes a function and produces a second program that computes the exact derivatives of that function. It works by breaking the program into elementary operations (add, multiply, exp, sin, matrix multiply), each of which has a known derivative rule, and combining those rules with the chain rule while the program runs. The derivatives are exact up to floating-point rounding, not approximate, and the cost is a small constant times the cost of the original function: in the classic operation-count analysis by Griewank and Walther, the gradient of a scalar function costs at most about 4 times as much as evaluating the function (the book gives a bound between 3 and 4, counted in arithmetic operations), whatever the number of inputs.
The alternatives are worse for this job. Numerical differentiation, (f(x + h) − f(x))/h, is approximate (the best accuracy in double precision is around 10⁻⁸) and needs one extra evaluation per input, so a model with a billion parameters would need a billion evaluations. Symbolic differentiation, as done by computer algebra systems, gives an exact formula, but the formula can grow exponentially larger than the original expression and does not handle loops, branches or data structures well. AD gives exact results at near-function cost for real programs.
For the layer above, deep learning, AD is the part of PyTorch, JAX and TensorFlow that removes hand-derived gradients. A researcher writes only the forward computation of a new architecture, and the framework produces the gradient. This is why trying new ideas takes hours instead of weeks. Backpropagation is the special case of AD (reverse mode) applied to a neural network; AD is the general machinery that also works for physics simulators, probabilistic programs, optimization layers and any numerical code.
Two lower layers explain its structure. Calculus (L4) gives the chain rule and the derivatives of primitive functions: each primitive needs only its local derivative, and the chain rule composes them. The intermediate representation (L3) is the program form that AD transforms. A recorded computation is a graph or a straight-line list of single-assignment statements (\( v_1 = x \cdot y \), \( v_2 = \sin x \), \( v_3 = v_1 + v_2 \)), exactly the form a compiler uses as IR in static single assignment (SSA) style. Because each variable is assigned once, the derivative of each line can be written as a new line, and the program transformation is mechanical.
terms: Primitive — an elementary operation (add, multiply, sin, matmul) whose derivative rule is known to the AD system.
Tangent (forward-mode) value — for each variable, its derivative with respect to a chosen input direction, carried along with the value.
Adjoint (reverse-mode) value — for each variable v, the derivative of the final output with respect to v; computed from the end toward the start.
Jacobian — the matrix of all partial derivatives of a vector function; entry (i, j) is \( \partial y_i/\partial x_j \).
JVP (Jacobian-vector product) — \( Jv \), the effect of a small input change in direction v on all outputs; what forward mode computes in one pass.
VJP (vector-Jacobian product) — \( u^{\top} J \), how a weighted sum of outputs changes with each input; what reverse mode computes in one pass.
Tape (trace) — the recorded list of operations and saved intermediate values that reverse mode replays backward.
Dual number — a number \( a + b\varepsilon \) with \( \varepsilon^2 = 0 \); evaluating a function on it gives \( f(a) + f'(a)\, b\varepsilon \), so the derivative is computed along with the value.
history: The idea is old. R. E. Wengert published a program for automatic derivative evaluation in 1964, which is forward mode. Reverse mode appeared in several places: Seppo Linnainmaa's 1970 thesis on rounding-error analysis, Bert Speelpenning's 1980 thesis, and Baur and Strassen (1983), who proved that the gradient of a function can be computed in a constant multiple of the cost of the function. For decades AD stayed in scientific computing (weather models, aerodynamics, finite elements), with tools such as ADIFOR and Tapenade that transformed Fortran and C source code. Andreas Griewank's work and the book "Evaluating Derivatives" (Griewank and Walther, 2008) set out the theory, including checkpointing schedules for reverse mode.
Machine learning rediscovered it, partly under the name backpropagation. Theano (Bergstra et al., 2010) built a symbolic graph for gradients and compiled it to GPU code. Autograd (Maclaurin, Duvenaud and Adams, 2015) differentiated ordinary NumPy code by recording operations on the fly. Chainer (2015) and then PyTorch (Paszke et al., 2017) made "define-by-run" the mainstream: the graph is built while Python executes, so loops and branches are plain Python. TensorFlow 1.x used static graphs, and added eager mode in 2017 and made it the default in TensorFlow 2 (2019). JAX (Frostig, Johnson and Leary, 2018) turned AD into a composable function transformation: grad, jvp, vjp, vmap, jit can be nested in any order.
The same decade extended AD to new places. Neural ODEs (Chen et al., 2018) used the adjoint method to differentiate through differential-equation solvers without storing all steps. Zygote (Julia) and Enzyme (Moses and Churavy, 2020) differentiate at the compiler IR level (LLVM), so they work across languages and with optimized code. The survey by Baydin, Pearlmutter, Radul and Siskind (JMLR, 2018) pulled the numerical and machine-learning communities together under one vocabulary.
how: Every program run is a sequence of elementary steps. For the function \( f(x, y) = x \cdot y + \sin x \), the steps are \( v_1 = x \cdot y \), \( v_2 = \sin x \), \( v_3 = v_1 + v_2 \). AD attaches derivative information to each step. There are two basic modes.
Forward mode carries a tangent beside each value. Choose an input direction, for example "change x by 1, leave y alone": set \( \dot x = 1, \dot y = 0 \). For each step, compute the tangent from the tangents of the inputs using the primitive's rule: for \( v_1 = x \cdot y \) it is \( \dot v_1 = \dot x \cdot y + x \cdot \dot y \); for \( v_2 = \sin x \) it is \( \dot v_2 = \cos x \cdot \dot x \); for \( v_3 = v_1 + v_2 \) it is \( \dot v_3 = \dot v_1 + \dot v_2 \). At the end \( \dot v_3 \) is the derivative of f along the chosen direction. A dual-number library does this by overloading arithmetic: each number holds (value, tangent). One forward pass costs about 2 to 3 times a normal evaluation and gives one column of the Jacobian (one JVP). For n inputs you need n passes to get the full gradient.
Reverse mode first runs the function normally and records the steps and the values needed (a tape). Then it walks the tape backward. It starts with the output adjoint \( \bar v_3 = 1 \) and for each step it adds to the adjoint of each input: for \( v_3 = v_1 + v_2 \), \( \bar v_1 \mathrel{+}= \bar v_3 \) and \( \bar v_2 \mathrel{+}= \bar v_3 \); for \( v_2 = \sin x \), \( \bar x \mathrel{+}= \cos x \cdot \bar v_2 \); for \( v_1 = x \cdot y \), \( \bar x \mathrel{+}= y \cdot \bar v_1 \) and \( \bar y \mathrel{+}= x \cdot \bar v_1 \). The "+=" is essential: a variable that is used several times (here x appears in two steps) receives a contribution from each use. After one backward pass, \( \bar x \) and \( \bar y \) hold the whole gradient. One reverse pass gives one row of the Jacobian (one VJP) at a cost of a small constant times the function, whatever the number of inputs.
That is the central trade-off. For a function with n inputs and m outputs, the full Jacobian costs n forward passes or m reverse passes. Deep learning has one scalar loss and millions or billions of parameters (m = 1, n huge), so reverse mode wins by a factor of n. Forward mode wins when inputs are few and outputs many, or when you want a directional derivative, and it needs no tape, so its memory is constant. Combining modes gives second-order tools: a Hessian-vector product \( Hv \) is computed by running forward mode over a reverse-mode gradient (or reverse over reverse) at a small constant multiple of the cost of the gradient, without forming the \( n \times n \) Hessian.
Implementations differ in how they find the steps. Operator overloading with a tape (PyTorch autograd) makes each tensor operation record a node holding its inputs and a backward function, as it executes; calling backward() walks these nodes in reverse topological order. This follows the actual control flow of the run, so a Python if or for loop is simply differentiated along the path taken. Tracing and transformation (JAX) runs the function once on abstract values to produce a jaxpr, then rewrites the jaxpr using per-primitive rules (jvp rules, and transpose rules for the backward direction); the result is again a jaxpr, so it can be compiled with jit or vectorized with vmap. Source transformation (Tapenade, Enzyme) generates new source or IR code for the derivative ahead of time, which allows compiler optimization of the derivative program.
The memory cost is the price of reverse mode: the tape holds every intermediate value needed by the backward rules, so memory grows with the number of operations executed (a loop of a million steps stores a million sets of values). Checkpointing saves only some states and recomputes the others, and the "revolve" schedule of Griewank and Walther makes the recomputation near-optimal. The adjoint method for differential equations or fixed-point problems avoids the tape altogether by solving a second equation backward in time, or by using the implicit function theorem at the solution.
Each primitive carries two things: its forward function and its derivative rule (for reverse mode, the rule that maps an output adjoint to input adjoints). Frameworks implement hundreds of such rules. When a user needs a derivative that AD does not give correctly, a custom rule can be supplied: for numerical stability (the gradient of log-sum-exp), for efficiency, for non-differentiable operations (the straight-through estimator pretends the gradient of rounding is 1), or for implicit functions defined by an iteration.

example: Use \( f(x, y) = x \cdot y + \sin x \) at the point x = 2, y = 3 and work all three methods with a pen. First the plain run. v1 = x·y = 6. v2 = sin 2 = 0.9093. v3 = v1 + v2 = 6.9093. The true derivatives (by hand, for checking only) are \( \partial f/\partial x = y + \cos x = 3 - 0.4161 = 2.5839 \) and \( \partial f/\partial y = x = 2 \).
Forward mode, seed \( \dot x = 1, \dot y = 0 \). \( \dot v_1 = \dot x \cdot y + x \cdot \dot y = 3 \). \( \dot v_2 = \cos 2 \cdot \dot x = -0.4161 \). \( \dot v_3 = 3 - 0.4161 = 2.5839 \). That is ∂f/∂x, matching. To get ∂f/∂y we need a second pass with \( \dot x = 0, \dot y = 1 \): \( \dot v_1 = x = 2 \), \( \dot v_2 = 0 \), \( \dot v_3 = 2 \). Two inputs, two passes.
Reverse mode, one pass after the forward run. Seed \( \bar v_3 = 1 \). Step \( v_3 = v_1 + v_2 \) gives \( \bar v_1 = 1 \) and \( \bar v_2 = 1 \). Step \( v_2 = \sin x \) gives \( \bar x = \cos 2 \times 1 = -0.4161 \). Step \( v_1 = x \cdot y \) gives \( \bar x \mathrel{+}= y \times \bar v_1 = 3 \), so \( \bar x = 2.5839 \), and \( \bar y = x \times \bar v_1 = 2 \). Both partial derivatives come out of the single backward sweep, and the "+=" at x added the two paths (through the product and through the sine).
Numerical differentiation of the same function, with h = 10⁻⁸ in double precision: \( (f(2 + h, 3) - f(2, 3))/h \). The values are near 6.9093 and the double-precision spacing near 7 is about 9 × 10⁻¹⁶, so the difference of two almost equal numbers has a rounding error of about 9 × 10⁻¹⁶ / 10⁻⁸ ≈ 10⁻⁷ in the derivative; with a larger step such as h = 10⁻³ the truncation error is about \( \tfrac{h}{2} \times |f''| \) ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁴ instead. You cannot get the 15 digits that AD returns, and it needs a separate evaluation for each input.
Now scale the idea. Suppose a model has n = 10⁶ parameters and the loss costs 1 unit to evaluate. Finite differences need 10⁶ + 1 evaluations. Forward-mode AD needs 10⁶ passes of about 2 to 3 units each. Reverse-mode AD needs 1 forward evaluation and 1 backward pass: about 3 to 4 units in total. Reverse mode is about 300,000 times cheaper than finite differences and about 700,000 times cheaper than forward mode. The price is memory: the tape for the model's intermediate values.
intuition: Every small step knows how it changes its output when its input changes; chain those local facts together, forward or backward, and the whole program knows its own slope.
The method works because a program, however large, is built from a small set of primitives, and the chain rule says that the derivative of a composition is the product of local derivatives. The only decision is the order of multiplication. Multiplying from the input side (forward) carries one direction at a time; multiplying from the output side (reverse) carries one output at a time. Since the loss is one number, starting from the output side is cheapest. AD does not look at the formula or approximate anything: it differentiates the run that actually happened, number by number.
mistakes: People often think AD is the same as symbolic differentiation. In fact AD never builds a formula for the derivative. It evaluates derivative numbers along with (or after) the normal run, so it avoids expression swell and handles loops, branches and data structures.
People often think AD is numerical differentiation done cleverly. In fact there is no step size and no truncation error. The result is exact for the computation performed, up to normal floating-point rounding.
People often think AD differentiates the mathematical function you have in mind. In fact it differentiates the program as executed. A function that is defined with a branch, an iterative solver run for a fixed number of steps, a clipped value or an argmax gives the derivative of that specific path, which can be zero, noisy, or wrong for the mathematical object you wanted (for example, the derivative through a converged fixed-point solver needs the implicit-function approach, not the unrolled steps).
People often think reverse mode is always better. In fact it is better when outputs are few. For a function with few inputs and many outputs, such as a simulation with 3 parameters and a million output values, forward mode costs 3 passes and no tape, while reverse mode needs a million backward passes for the full Jacobian.
friction: Memory is the main cost of reverse mode: the tape grows with the number of operations, so long loops, long sequences and big simulations need checkpointing or the adjoint method, both of which add compute or complexity. Higher-order derivatives are expensive: the full Hessian has \( n^2 \) entries, so practical methods use Hessian-vector products (with conjugate gradients or Lanczos) and never form the matrix.
Non-differentiable pieces break the chain: argmax, sorting, rounding, sampling, discrete choices, and hard attention. Workarounds exist but each changes the problem: straight-through estimators are biased, the reparameterization trick needs a location-scale form, and score-function estimators have high variance. Derivatives at kinks (ReLU at 0, absolute value at 0) are conventions chosen by the framework, and a convention can matter at exactly those points. Floating-point can also destroy the derivative: a function such as sqrt(x²) or x·log(x) at 0, or a stable-looking forward pass that uses a clamp, can return NaN gradients even though the forward value is fine.
Frameworks differ in what they allow. Dynamic frameworks (PyTorch eager) are easy to debug but add per-operation overhead and cannot optimize across the whole function unless compiled. Tracing frameworks (JAX) give composable transformations and compile well, but need pure functions, static shapes, and care with Python control flow (use lax.cond and lax.scan). Mutation, in-place operations and random number state make tape-based AD harder to reason about; in PyTorch, an in-place change of a tensor saved for backward raises an error at backward time. Finally, correctness of custom rules is the user's responsibility, which is why gradient checks matter.
practice: Use the framework's AD; do not hand-derive gradients. In PyTorch, set requires_grad on parameters, call loss.backward(), and call optimizer.zero_grad() each step. Use torch.no_grad() or inference_mode() during evaluation to skip tape recording and save memory. Use detach() to cut a path deliberately. In JAX, write a pure function, then use jax.grad for gradients, jax.value_and_grad when you also need the value, jax.jvp for forward mode and jax.vjp when you need reuse of the backward function, and jax.hessian or hvp tricks for second order.
When you write a custom operation, supply a custom backward (torch.autograd.Function, jax.custom_vjp) and check it with finite differences in double precision (torch.autograd.gradcheck, jax.test_util.check_grads). Use custom rules for numerically delicate functions, for ops that call external code, and for implicit layers (solve the forward problem by any solver, and write the backward from the implicit-function theorem or the adjoint equation).
To save memory use checkpointing (torch.utils.checkpoint, jax.checkpoint), scan for long loops, and mixed precision with care. For a Jacobian with few inputs, prefer forward mode (jax.jacfwd, torch.func.jacfwd); for few outputs, reverse mode (jacrev). Treat NaNs systematically: enable anomaly detection (torch.autograd.set_detect_anomaly, jax_debug_nans) while debugging, and use the "double where" trick to avoid NaN gradients through branches. To understand it deeply, write a 100-line reverse-mode engine for scalars and a forward-mode dual-number class; both fit on a page.
tradeoffs: Choose reverse mode when you have many inputs and few outputs (training a model with one loss). Choose forward mode when you have few inputs and many outputs, when you need directional derivatives or JVPs, or when memory is tight, since it needs no tape. For a square Jacobian of size n × n the two modes cost about the same, and forward mode is simpler.
Choose a dynamic, tape-based framework (PyTorch eager) when you value debugging with ordinary Python tools and fast iteration; choose a tracing and compiling framework (JAX, or torch.compile) when you run the same function many times and want whole-program optimization and composable transformations. You give up some simplicity (pure functions, static shapes) for speed and for transformations such as vmap.
Choose AD over finite differences for any gradient used in training or for more than a handful of parameters. Choose finite differences only to test: they are a simple, independent check of a custom gradient, with a tolerance around 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁷.
Choose the checkpointed or adjoint method when a long computation (a simulation with thousands of steps, an ODE solver) would otherwise hold too much on the tape. You pay extra compute (about one more forward pass for checkpointing) or accept the accuracy of the continuous adjoint, which can differ slightly from the exact gradient of the discretized solver. Choose to differentiate through the solver steps (discretize-then-differentiate) when you need the exact gradient of what you computed.
Choose source-level or compiler-level AD (Enzyme, Tapenade) for existing numerical codes in C++ or Fortran where performance is critical; you give up the convenience of tensors-and-Python frameworks but gain optimized, whole-program derivatives.
interview: Explain forward and reverse mode with one example, and when each is cheaper. || Forward mode carries a tangent with each value and gives a Jacobian column (a JVP) per pass; reverse mode records a tape and propagates adjoints backward, giving a Jacobian row (a VJP) per pass. With n inputs and m outputs, forward costs n passes and reverse costs m. A scalar loss with many parameters makes reverse mode the right choice.
Why does reverse mode need so much memory, and how do you reduce it? || The backward rules need intermediate values from the forward run, so the tape grows with every operation executed. Reduce it with checkpointing (store a subset, recompute the rest), with scan or fused kernels, with reversible layers, and with the adjoint method for ODE or fixed-point layers.
What is the difference between AD, symbolic and numerical differentiation? || Numerical uses finite differences: approximate, error limited near 10⁻⁸ and one evaluation per input. Symbolic manipulates formulas: exact, but can explode in size and handles control flow poorly. AD applies the chain rule to the executed primitives: exact to rounding and of cost close to the function itself.
How does JAX's grad differ from PyTorch's backward()? || PyTorch builds the graph dynamically as operations run and walks it with backward(), storing gradients in .grad. JAX's grad is a function transformation: it traces the function to a jaxpr, transforms it with jvp and transpose rules, and returns a new function that can be jitted, vmapped or differentiated again.
How would you get the derivative of a loss through a root-finding or fixed-point solver with 500 iterations? || Unrolling stores 500 iterations on the tape and differentiates the path taken. Better: solve to convergence without recording, then apply the implicit function theorem at the solution, solving a linear system with the Jacobian (adjoint). It uses constant memory and gives the derivative of the limit.
You see NaN gradients although the forward loss is finite. What do you check? || Operations with a bad derivative at the evaluated point: sqrt or norm at 0, log of 0, division by a small number, exp overflow, a where() that selects a branch whose gradient is NaN (the masked branch still gets 0 × NaN). Use anomaly detection to find the first NaN, apply the double-where trick or a safe version of the function, and clip values.
quiz: What is a JVP and what is a VJP? || A JVP (Jacobian-vector product, \( Jv \)) is the forward-mode result: how all outputs change for one input direction. A VJP (vector-Jacobian product, \( u^{\top} J \)) is the reverse-mode result: how one weighted combination of outputs changes with each input.
Why is reverse mode used for training neural networks? || The loss is one scalar and the parameters are many, so one reverse pass gives the whole gradient, whereas forward mode would need one pass per parameter.
For \( f(x, y) = x \cdot y + \sin x \) at (2, 3), what are the gradient values by AD? || \( \partial f/\partial x = y + \cos x = 3 - 0.4161 = 2.5839 \) and \( \partial f/\partial y = x = 2 \); reverse mode gets both in one backward pass.
Why does reverse-mode AD add (+=) to adjoints? || A variable used in several operations affects the output along several paths, and by the multivariable chain rule its adjoint is the sum of the contributions from each use.
When would you pick finite differences over AD? || Only for testing a custom gradient or when the function is a black box that AD cannot see (external simulator); for training or for more than a few parameters AD is exact and far cheaper.

value: A loss function turns the question "how wrong is the model on this example?" into one number that an optimizer can reduce. Training is nothing more than changing the model's parameters to make the average loss smaller, so the loss defines what the model is actually trying to do. If you grade a model by squared error, it learns to predict averages; if you grade it by absolute error, it learns to predict medians; if you grade it by cross-entropy, it learns to give probabilities that match how often things happen. After the data, the loss is the most important design decision in a machine-learning system.
Cross-entropy is the loss used for almost all classification and for language models. For one example, it is the negative logarithm of the probability the model gave to the correct answer: −log p(correct). If the model gave 0.9 to the right class the loss is 0.105 nats; at 0.5 it is 0.69; at 0.01 it is 4.6. A model that is confidently wrong is punished heavily, a model that is mildly unsure is punished lightly, and a model that is right with high confidence is almost not punished. For a language model with a 50,000-token vocabulary, a model that knows nothing gives every token probability 1/50,000, so its starting loss is ln 50,000 = 10.82 nats; training reduces this to about 2 or lower for good models.
For the layer above (machine learning), the loss is the interface between what people want and what the optimizer can do. The layers below explain why cross-entropy is the right default. Probability (L4) says that choosing the parameters that make the observed labels most likely, maximum likelihood estimation, is the same as minimizing cross-entropy. Information theory (L4) says what the number means: the average number of extra bits (or nats) the model needs to encode the data compared with the best possible code, which is H(p) + KL(p‖q). Convexity (L4) and Jensen's inequality explain why this loss behaves well: it is smallest only when the model's distribution equals the true one, and it is convex in the logits, so gradients always point toward a better answer.
terms: Loss (objective, cost) — a function of the model's prediction and the target, averaged over data; lower is better.
Logit — the raw, unnormalized score the model outputs for a class; softmax turns logits into probabilities.
Softmax — \( p_k = \exp(z_k) / \sum_j \exp(z_j) \); makes the scores positive and sum to 1.
Likelihood — the probability the model assigns to the observed data; maximum likelihood chooses parameters that maximize it, and the negative log-likelihood (NLL) is the loss.
Cross-entropy — \( H(p, q) = -\sum_x p(x) \log q(x) \), the expected code length when data from p is encoded with a code built for q; equals \( H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) \).
Proper scoring rule — a loss whose expected value is lowest exactly when the model reports the true probabilities; log loss and the Brier score are proper.
Calibration — predicted probabilities match observed frequencies: among the cases where the model says 30%, about 30% happen.
Perplexity — exp(cross-entropy in nats); the effective number of equally likely choices the model is torn between per token.
history: Least squares is the oldest loss: Legendre published it in 1805 and Gauss claimed it in 1809, justifying squared error by assuming Gaussian errors. Ronald Fisher developed maximum likelihood in 1912 to 1922, which turned "choose a loss" into "choose a noise model". Claude Shannon defined entropy in 1948, and Solomon Kullback and Richard Leibler defined their divergence in 1951, which gave cross-entropy its meaning as a code length. Brier (1950) introduced a squared-error score for probability forecasts in meteorology, and Huber (1964) proposed a loss that is quadratic for small errors and linear for large ones, to resist outliers.
For neural networks, early work (Rumelhart et al., 1986) used squared error even for classification. The change came when people noticed that squared error on a sigmoid or softmax output learns slowly when the model is confidently wrong, because the derivative of the sigmoid is nearly zero there. Cross-entropy cancels that factor and gives the gradient (prediction − target). John Bridle named the softmax in 1990 and promoted cross-entropy training for classifiers. Support vector machines (Cortes and Vapnik, 1995) used the hinge loss; boosting used the exponential loss (AdaBoost) and later logistic loss.
The modern list of losses grew with the tasks. Label smoothing (Szegedy et al., 2016) softens the one-hot target. Focal loss (Lin et al., 2017) down-weights easy examples for object detection with extreme class imbalance. Contrastive and InfoNCE losses (van den Oord et al., 2018) train embeddings by asking the model to pick the true partner among many negatives. Language models (GPT, 2018 onward) use next-token cross-entropy. Reward models for RLHF use a pairwise Bradley-Terry loss, \( -\log \sigma(r_{\text{chosen}} - r_{\text{rejected}}) \), and DPO (Rafailov et al., 2023) turns preference learning into a similar classification loss.
how: Start with a classifier with K classes. The network produces K logits z for an input. Softmax converts them to probabilities p. The cross-entropy loss with the true class y is \( L = -\log p_y = -z_y + \log \sum_j \exp(z_j) \). The second form is the one implemented in practice. The term \( \log \sum_j \exp(z_j) \) is computed with the "log-sum-exp" trick (subtract the largest logit before exponentiating), so there is no overflow or log of zero, and frameworks provide it as one fused function (cross_entropy on logits). Never compute softmax and then log in two steps; for large logits the probabilities underflow to 0 and the loss becomes infinite.
Look at the gradient with respect to the logits. For cross-entropy with softmax, \( \partial L/\partial z_k = p_k - \mathbf{1}[k = y] \): the predicted probability minus the target (1 for the true class, 0 for the others). This gradient is proportional to the error and does not shrink when the model is confidently wrong, which is the property that makes training fast. It also shows what the loss does: it pushes the correct logit up in proportion to the missing probability, and every wrong logit down in proportion to the probability it wrongly received. Backpropagation then carries these simple signals to all earlier layers.
Why is this the right loss? Treat the model as a probability distribution \( q(y \mid x; \theta) \). Maximum likelihood chooses θ to maximize the product of q over the training examples, which is the same as minimizing the average of −log q, the cross-entropy between the empirical data distribution p and the model. Cross-entropy equals \( H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) \); the entropy \( H(p) \) does not depend on the model, so minimizing the cross-entropy minimizes the KL divergence from the data to the model. Jensen's inequality shows \( \mathrm{KL} \ge 0 \) with equality only when \( q = p \). Therefore the loss has its minimum exactly when the model reports the true conditional probabilities. A loss with this property is called proper, and it is why cross-entropy trained models tend to give meaningful probabilities, which matter in ad bidding, risk scoring and any downstream decision.
Convexity gives the optimizer an easy landscape. The function \( \log \sum_j \exp(z_j) \) is convex in the logits (it is a smooth maximum, and Jensen's inequality is the standard tool for showing this), and \( -z_y \) is linear, so the cross-entropy is convex in the logits: it has no flat plateaus and no local minima in that space. For logistic regression, where the logits are linear in the weights, the whole problem is convex and gradient descent finds the global optimum. For deep networks the loss is not convex in the weights, but the final step from logits to loss is still well behaved. Compare the 0-1 loss (count the mistakes): it is flat almost everywhere, so its gradient is zero and gives no direction to follow. Cross-entropy is a smooth upper bound on the 0-1 loss (when measured in bits), which is why minimizing it also reduces the error count.
The same recipe produces the other classic losses. Assume the target is the prediction plus Gaussian noise with fixed variance: the negative log-likelihood is \( (y - \hat y)^2/(2\sigma^2) \) plus a constant, so mean squared error (MSE) is maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise, and the model learns the conditional mean. Assume Laplace noise: the NLL is \( |y - \hat y|/b \), the mean absolute error (MAE), and the model learns the conditional median, which is robust to outliers. Assume Poisson counts: the loss is \( \hat y - y \log \hat y \). The pinball loss learns a chosen quantile (for example the 90th percentile of delivery time). So choosing a loss means choosing which statistic of the answer distribution you want, and which noise model you believe.
Several practical details decide whether the loss works. Reduction: mean over examples or over tokens? For language models the usual choice is the mean over non-padding tokens, and with gradient accumulation you must normalize by the total token count, or short sequences get too much weight. Class imbalance: for a rare class you can weight the loss, resample, or adjust the decision threshold afterward; weighting changes the model's probabilities, so calibration must be fixed again if you need probabilities. Label noise: a few wrong labels with a confident model produce huge −log p values, so people use label smoothing, a bounded loss or filtering. Auxiliary terms: regularization penalties and other objectives are simply added to the loss with weights.

example: Three classes, logits z = [2, 1, 0]. The exponentials are 7.389, 2.718 and 1.000, which sum to 11.107, so p = [0.665, 0.245, 0.090]. If the true class is class 0, the loss is −ln 0.665 = 0.408 nats, and the gradient on the logits is p − y = [0.665 − 1, 0.245, 0.090] = [−0.335, 0.245, 0.090]. The first logit will be raised, the other two lowered. If instead the true class is class 2, the loss is −ln 0.090 = 2.408 nats and the gradient is [0.665, 0.245, −0.910]: a much larger push on the correct logit.
Now see why cross-entropy beats squared error for a probability output. A binary classifier has logit z = −4 and the true label is 1. The probability is σ(−4) = 0.018, so the model is confidently wrong. Cross-entropy loss is −ln 0.018 = 4.02 and its gradient on the logit is p − y = −0.982: a strong push. With squared error on the probability, \( \tfrac{1}{2}(1 - p)^2 = 0.482 \) and the gradient on the logit is \( -(1 - p) \times p(1 - p) = -0.982 \times 0.018 \times 0.982 = -0.0173 \), about 57 times smaller, because the sigmoid is flat there. The model would barely move; this is the "saturation" problem that led to cross-entropy.
Mean versus median. Take five delivery times in minutes: 1, 2, 3, 4, 100. The value that minimizes the squared error is the mean, 22. The value that minimizes the absolute error is the median, 3. If the 100 is a real, rare event, MSE keeps you aware of it; if it is a data error, MAE ignores it. The loss encodes your attitude to outliers.
Click-through prediction. Suppose 1% of ad impressions are clicked. A model that always predicts 0.01 has log loss −(0.01 ln 0.01 + 0.99 ln 0.99) = 0.0460 + 0.0100 = 0.0560 nats per impression. This is the entropy of the base rate, and it is what "knowing nothing but the base rate" costs. A model that always predicts 0.1 (badly calibrated, ten times too high) has 0.0230 + 0.1043 = 0.127: more than twice as bad, though its ranking is no worse. A real model that reaches 0.0520 has a normalized entropy (log loss divided by the base-rate entropy, a metric used in Facebook's ad system) of 0.0520 / 0.0560 = 0.929, meaning 7% better than the base rate. Because the log loss is proper, improving it also improves calibration, which matters since the auction bid is roughly the click probability times the value of a click.
Finally perplexity: a language model at loss 2.0 nats per token has perplexity e² = 7.39, meaning it is as uncertain as a fair 7.4-way choice at each token; at 1.0 nat it is 2.72. In bits, divide by ln 2.
intuition: The loss is the teacher's grading rubric; students optimize exactly what is graded, so the rubric must reward what you really want.
Cross-entropy is natural because it grades honesty. If the model says "70% rain" and it rains 70% of such days, the log penalty averages out lowest when the model states its true belief, and any exaggeration costs more than it gains. At the same time, −log p grows without limit as p goes to 0, so claiming impossibility for something that happens is the worst mistake. A model trained under this rubric learns to say exactly what it thinks the probabilities are, and the optimizer can see the way toward it because the gradient is simply "what you said minus what happened".
mistakes: People often think that cross-entropy and the accuracy metric measure the same thing. In fact accuracy only counts whether the top class is right, while cross-entropy looks at the probability. Two models with the same accuracy can have very different log loss, and loss often keeps improving after accuracy has stopped changing. Use the loss to train and the business metric to decide.
People often think that minimizing a loss means the model finds the truth. In fact it finds what the loss rewards on the training data. If the labels are biased, noisy or only a proxy for the real goal (clicks for satisfaction, thumbs-up for helpfulness), the model optimizes the proxy; Goodhart's law applies when the optimization is strong.
People often think MSE is a safe default for everything. In fact MSE assumes symmetric Gaussian noise and learns the mean, so it is sensitive to outliers and wrong for skewed targets such as prices or durations, where a log transform or a quantile or Poisson-type loss fits better. For probabilities, MSE on a saturating output learns slowly.
People often think a lower training loss always means a better model. In fact the loss is measured on training data, and a model can reach a near-zero training loss by memorizing. What matters is the loss on held-out data, and whether differences between runs are larger than the noise (see evaluation).
friction: The loss is only a proxy. Next-token cross-entropy does not directly measure truthfulness, helpfulness or safety; click loss does not measure user satisfaction. Pushing a proxy hard can make the real objective worse (reward hacking in reinforcement learning from feedback is a clear example). The more flexible the model, the more completely it satisfies the literal rubric, including its loopholes.
Cross-entropy also has weak spots. It can make models overconfident: modern deep networks trained to near-zero training loss often give probabilities that are too extreme on new data (Guo et al., 2017), so calibration methods such as temperature scaling are used afterwards. It reacts strongly to noisy labels, because a confident prediction on a wrongly labeled example gives a very large loss. It treats all wrong classes the same, although confusing a cat with a dog is milder than confusing it with a truck. Class imbalance can drown the rare class: with 1% positives, a trivial model has a low loss. Weighting or resampling then fixes the loss but shifts the probabilities.
Finally, many tasks do not have a smooth loss. Ranking quality (NDCG), BLEU score, and business measures such as revenue are not differentiable, so a surrogate is used (pairwise or listwise losses, reinforcement learning), and the surrogate may agree with the real metric only roughly. Combining several losses needs weights that are hard to tune because the terms have different scales; multi-task training often needs careful balancing or uncertainty weighting.
practice: Match the loss to the target. Binary or multi-class labels: cross-entropy on logits (BCEWithLogits or CrossEntropyLoss in PyTorch), never softmax followed by log. Probabilities that feed decisions (ad bids, risk): log loss, and check calibration with a reliability plot. Real-valued targets: MSE for symmetric noise, Huber if outliers appear, MAE or quantile loss for medians and quantiles, a log transform for positive skewed targets. Language models: token-level cross-entropy with padding masked out. Embeddings and retrieval: InfoNCE with in-batch negatives. Preference data: a pairwise logistic loss.
Use the loss as a debugging tool. At initialization the loss should be about ln K for K classes (about 10.8 for a 50,000-token vocabulary, 0.69 for balanced binary); if it is much higher, there is a bug in the labels, in the scaling of the last layer or in the learning rate. Try to overfit a single batch: the loss should go close to zero; if it does not, the model or the data pipeline is broken. Watch both the training and the validation loss, and report the loss on a fixed held-out set in bits per token or per byte to compare runs.
For imbalanced data, first measure the base-rate log loss and see how much you gain over it. If you reweight classes, recalibrate afterwards (Platt scaling, isotonic regression, or a prior-shift correction). Report the metric your users care about next to the loss, and keep the offline loss and the online A/B result side by side, since a lower loss does not always move the business metric. Add label smoothing (0.05 to 0.1) when labels are noisy or the model is overconfident, but know that it makes the probabilities less literal.
tradeoffs: Choose cross-entropy (log loss) when you need probabilities and the cost of a wrong decision is computed from them, as in ad bidding or risk. You get proper, calibrated-by-design outputs and strong gradients. You give up robustness to label noise and a direct link to ranking metrics. Choose a hinge or margin loss only when you need a decision boundary and not probabilities, and accept uncalibrated scores.
Choose MSE when noise is roughly Gaussian and you need the mean (total expected spend, energy forecasts). Choose MAE or Huber when outliers are real but you do not want them to dominate: MAE targets the median and has constant gradient magnitude, which can make fine convergence slower; Huber combines a smooth quadratic center with linear tails but adds a threshold δ to tune. Choose quantile loss when the decision depends on a tail (staffing for the 95th percentile of demand).
Choose a pairwise or listwise ranking loss over pointwise cross-entropy when only the order of items matters (search, feed ranking) and positions are biased. You gain direct optimization of order; you lose calibrated probabilities, which you may need again in an auction, so many systems train a calibrated pointwise model and a ranking model separately.
Choose class weights, focal loss or resampling for extreme imbalance (below 1% positives) when the rare class is what matters and a good ranking is the goal. You gain recall on the rare class; you give up calibration, which you must restore (for example by a final calibration layer) if the probabilities are used.
Choose label smoothing or a noise-robust loss when labels are 5 to 20% wrong; you give up some sharpness of the probabilities. Choose a plain loss with cleaner data when you can afford to fix the labels, since no loss repairs systematically wrong ground truth.
interview: Why is cross-entropy the natural loss for classification? || It is the negative log-likelihood of the labels under the model, so minimizing it is maximum likelihood estimation. It equals H(p) + KL(p‖q), so it is minimized exactly when the model's distribution matches the data's. The gradient on the logits is p − y, which stays large when the model is confidently wrong, unlike squared error on saturating outputs.
Derive the gradient of softmax cross-entropy with respect to the logits. || \( L = -z_y + \log \sum_j \exp(z_j) \). The derivative with respect to \( z_k \) is \( -\mathbf{1}[k = y] + \exp(z_k)/\sum_j \exp(z_j) = p_k - \mathbf{1}[k = y] \). That is the prediction minus the one-hot target, which makes the backward pass cheap and numerically stable.
What does MSE learn, and what does MAE learn? When do they differ in practice? || MSE learns the conditional mean (maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise); MAE learns the conditional median (Laplace noise). With skewed targets or outliers the two give different answers, for example the mean of delivery times is dragged up by rare long delays while the median is not.
Your model has low log loss but the business metric does not move. What are the possible reasons? || The loss is a proxy: training labels may be biased or mismatched to the real goal (clicks versus satisfaction); the loss may improve in regions that do not affect decisions; offline data may differ from online traffic; position or selection bias in logged data; calibration may be fine but ranking at the top unchanged. Check by slice, run A/B tests, and examine whether the metric and the loss measure the same thing.
Training loss starts at 25 for a 1,000-class problem. What do you suspect? || The expected initial value is ln 1000 = 6.9. A value of 25 means the initial logits are large (bad initialization or an unnormalized final layer), the learning rate or input scaling is wrong, or labels are misaligned. Check the last layer's scale, the data normalization and the labels.
How would you train a model whose probabilities feed an ad auction when positives are 0.5% of impressions? || Train with log loss (a proper scoring rule) on enough data; if you downsample negatives, correct the predicted probability afterwards (if you keep a fraction w of the negatives, the corrected probability is p / (p + (1 − p)/w)); verify calibration per slice with reliability plots; track normalized entropy against the base rate; and test online since bids depend on absolute probability, not only on ranking.
quiz: What is the cross-entropy loss for one example? || The negative log of the probability the model gave to the correct class, \( -\log p_y \); equivalently \( -z_y + \log \sum_j \exp(z_j) \) in terms of logits.
Why does softmax cross-entropy give the gradient p − y? || The loss is \( -z_y + \log \sum_j \exp(z_j) \); differentiating gives \( -\mathbf{1}[k = y] + \text{softmax}(z)_k \), the prediction minus the one-hot target.
Why is minimizing cross-entropy the same as maximum likelihood, and what does the extra KL term mean? || Cross-entropy is the average negative log-likelihood; it equals H(p) + KL(p‖q), where H(p) is fixed by the data, so minimizing it minimizes the KL divergence between the data and the model.
With logits [2, 1, 0], what is the cross-entropy loss if the true class is class 1? || \( p_1 = e^1 / (e^2 + e^1 + e^0) = 2.718 / 11.107 = 0.245 \), so the loss is −ln 0.245 = 1.41 nats.
When would you choose MAE or Huber over MSE? || When the target has outliers or heavy tails that you do not want to dominate the fit, or when you want the median. MSE is better when you really need the mean and the noise is roughly Gaussian.

@@ regularization
value: Regularization is every technique that makes a model prefer simpler solutions, so that it works on new data and not only on the training set. A flexible model can fit the training data perfectly by memorizing its noise: on 4 points, a model with enough freedom can reach zero training error and still predict badly on the fifth. Regularization adds a force against this: a penalty on large weights (weight decay, L1), random removal of parts of the network during training (dropout), stopping before the model has memorized (early stopping), or more varied data (augmentation).
Every successful model uses some of it. Logistic regression for click prediction uses an L2 or L1 penalty. Gradient-boosted trees limit tree depth and shrink each tree's contribution. Large neural networks use weight decay in the optimizer (AdamW with a decay of about 0.1 is the usual choice for language-model pre-training), dropout in many vision and fine-tuning recipes, and data augmentation in image models. Recommender systems add penalties on user and item factors (see matrix factorization) because most users rate few items.
The idea comes from three lower layers. Statistics (L4) explains the goal: the expected error of a model splits into \( \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance} + \text{irreducible noise} \), and regularization accepts a little more bias in exchange for much less variance. Optimization (L4) explains the mechanism: the penalty is simply another term in the objective, minimized by the same gradient method, and the penalized problem is equivalent to minimizing the loss inside a constraint region (a ball) around zero. Learning theory (L4) explains why it helps: bounds on the gap between training and test error grow with the capacity of the model class (VC dimension, Rademacher complexity, weight norms) and shrink with the number of examples, so limiting capacity tightens the gap.
terms: Overfitting — the model fits noise in the training set; training error is low, error on new data is much higher.
Bias and variance — bias is the error from a model too simple to capture the truth; variance is the sensitivity of the fitted model to which training sample it saw.
L2 penalty (ridge, weight decay) — adds \( \tfrac{\lambda}{2} \sum w^2 \) to the loss; shrinks all weights toward zero.
L1 penalty (lasso) — adds \( \lambda \sum |w| \); drives many weights exactly to zero, giving sparse models.
Dropout — during training, each activation is set to zero with probability p, and the others are scaled by 1/(1 − p).
Early stopping — stop training when validation loss stops improving and keep the best checkpoint.
Data augmentation — creating modified copies of training examples (crops, flips, noise, paraphrases) that keep the label.
Hyperparameter — a setting such as λ or p that is not learned by gradient descent but chosen using validation data.
history: Regularization began as a fix for ill-posed problems. Andrey Tikhonov (1943 and the 1960s) added a penalty to stabilise the solution of inverse problems whose exact solution amplifies noise. In statistics, Hoerl and Kennard (1970) introduced ridge regression for regression with correlated predictors, where ordinary least squares gives huge, unstable coefficients. Tibshirani's lasso (1996) used the L1 penalty to select variables, and Zou and Hastie's elastic net (2005) combined both.
For neural networks, weight decay was used from the late 1980s (Hanson and Pratt, 1988; Krogh and Hertz, 1991). Early stopping was a common practice and was analysed as an implicit L2 penalty (Sjöberg and Ljung, 1995). Dropout was proposed by Hinton and colleagues in 2012 and published by Srivastava et al. in the Journal of Machine Learning Research in 2014; it helped AlexNet-style networks and was a key regularizer of the deep learning revival. Batch normalization (2015) and data augmentation were found to regularize as a side effect.
Two later results changed the picture. Zhang et al. (ICLR 2017) showed that standard networks can fit completely random labels, so their capacity is far above what any classical bound would allow, yet they generalize well on real labels; regularization in the old sense does not explain this. Belkin et al. (2019) described "double descent": test error first follows the classical U-shape, then falls again as models become larger than the data. Loshchilov and Hutter (2017, published at ICLR 2019) showed that for Adam, an L2 penalty added to the loss is not the same as weight decay, and proposed AdamW, now the default optimizer for Transformers. Today the working view is that explicit regularization, the optimizer's implicit bias, the architecture and the data together determine generalization.
how: The core method is to change the objective: minimize \( L(w) + \lambda\,\Omega(w) \), where \( L \) is the training loss, \( \Omega \) measures complexity and \( \lambda \ge 0 \) sets the strength. With \( \Omega(w) = \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert w \rVert^2 \) (the L2 penalty) the gradient of the penalty is \( \lambda w \), so one step of gradient descent with learning rate \( \eta \) is \( w \leftarrow w - \eta(\nabla L + \lambda w) = (1 - \eta\lambda)\, w - \eta \nabla L \). Each step first multiplies the weights by a number slightly less than 1 (they "decay") and then applies the usual gradient step. For linear regression the solution has a closed form, \( w = (X^{\top}X + \lambda I)^{-1} X^{\top}y \), and the effect is clear in the singular-value view: a direction of the data with singular value \( \sigma \) has its coefficient multiplied by \( \sigma^2/(\sigma^2 + \lambda) \). Directions with strong signal (large σ) are barely touched; weak directions, which are mostly noise, are shrunk strongly. This is how a penalty trades a bit of bias for large variance reduction.
The L1 penalty \( \Omega(w) = \sum |w| \) has a constant pull toward zero (gradient \( \lambda\,\text{sign}(w) \)) instead of one proportional to the weight, so small weights are pushed all the way to zero. In one dimension the solution is soft thresholding: if the unpenalized optimum is \( \tilde w \), the penalized one is \( \text{sign}(\tilde w) \times \max(|\tilde w| - \lambda, 0) \). Geometrically, the constraint region \( \lVert w \rVert_1 \le t \) is a diamond with corners on the axes, and the loss contours usually touch it at a corner where some coordinates are zero. L1 therefore gives sparse models and feature selection; L2 gives small, spread-out weights. Statistically, L2 is the MAP estimate under a Gaussian prior on the weights, and L1 under a Laplace prior: the penalty is a prior belief that most weights should be small. The strength λ is chosen on validation data: train with several values (a logarithmic grid, for example 10⁻⁵ to 10⁰), measure the loss on data the training never saw, and keep the value with the lowest validation loss. With little data, k-fold cross-validation repeats this on k splits. A λ that is too small leaves the variance high; a λ that is too large pushes all weights to zero and the model underfits. The validation curve as a function of λ has the same U-shape as the diagram below.
For networks, the decay interacts with the optimizer. Adam divides each gradient by a running estimate of its size, so an L2 penalty put into the loss gets scaled too, and weights with large gradients are decayed less. AdamW applies the decay directly to the weights, \( w \leftarrow w - \eta \lambda w \), outside the adaptive scaling, which restores the intended behavior and makes the best λ nearly independent of the learning rate scale. That is why recipes such as LLaMA use AdamW with a decay of 0.1.
Dropout changes the network, not the loss. In each training step every unit in a layer is kept with probability 1 − p and set to zero otherwise; the kept units are multiplied by 1/(1 − p), so the expected value of each activation is unchanged ("inverted dropout"), and at test time nothing is dropped. A unit cannot rely on any particular partner being present, so features must be useful on their own (the "co-adaptation" argument), and the training behaves like averaging a large family of thinned networks that share weights. Typical values are p = 0.5 for large fully connected layers and 0.1 for Transformers.
Early stopping watches the validation loss during training, and keeps the weights from the step where it was lowest. For a quadratic loss, stopping gradient descent after t steps with step size η is similar to an L2 penalty with \( \lambda \approx 1/(\eta t) \): early in training only the strong directions have been learned, which is the same as shrinking the weak ones. Data augmentation adds knowledge: you declare that a rotated or cropped image has the same label, which forbids the model from using features that change under these edits. Mixup, cutout and label smoothing are further variants of the same principle. Choosing a model class with built-in invariances, such as the shared filters of a convolutional network, is a structural regularizer that removes whole families of functions.
A last mechanism is implicit. Gradient descent from a small initialization tends to find solutions with small norm and, with stochastic mini-batches, flat minima; noise in the gradient acts like a regularizer. This is part of why huge networks generalize, but the theory is still incomplete.

example: Two almost identical features. Training data: x1 = [1, 2, 3, 4], x2 = [1.1, 1.9, 3.1, 3.9], and targets y = [1.3, 1.7, 3.3, 3.7]. The truth behind the data is roughly y ≈ the common value of the two features, and the targets carry noise of about ±0.3. Ordinary least squares fits a weight on each feature. The two features are almost the same, so the data matrix has one strong direction (eigenvalue of \( X^{\top}X \) about 59.6, the sum of the two features) and one weak direction (eigenvalue about 0.0195, their difference). The fit is w = (−2, 3): it passes through all four training points exactly (training error 0), by using the tiny differences between the features to explain the noise.
Test it on a new point with x1 = 5, x2 = 4.6, where the true value is about 4.8. The OLS prediction is −2 × 5 + 3 × 4.6 = 3.8, an error of 1.0. The model amplified a small difference of 0.4 between two features by a factor of 5. Now add the L2 penalty. With λ = 0.1 the solution is w = (0.087, 0.899) and the prediction is 4.57, an error of 0.23; the training error rises from 0 to 0.17 (sum of squares). With λ = 1 the weights are (0.44, 0.53), the prediction is 4.63, and the training error is 0.24. The shrinkage factors \( \sigma^2/(\sigma^2 + \lambda) \) explain it: with λ = 0.1 the strong direction keeps 59.6/59.7 = 99.8% of its coefficient, while the weak, noisy direction keeps only 0.0195/0.1195 = 16%. The penalty removed the noise-fitting part and kept the signal.
Weight decay in a training loop. With η = 0.1 and λ = 0.01 each step multiplies a weight by 1 − 0.001 = 0.999. If the loss gradient were zero, after 1,000 steps a weight of 2.0 becomes 2.0 × 0.999¹⁰⁰⁰ = 2.0 × 0.368 = 0.74. A weight stays large only if the gradient keeps pushing it up; decay removes weights that the data does not support. For AdamW with η = 3 × 10⁻⁴ and λ = 0.1, the multiplier per step is 1 − 3 × 10⁻⁵, and with a constant learning rate 100,000 steps would shrink an unused weight by e⁻³ ≈ 0.05.
Dropout with p = 0.5 on four activations [0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 1.5]: draw a mask, say [1, 0, 1, 0], and scale the survivors by \( 1/(1 - p) = 2 \). The output is [1.0, 0, 4.0, 0]. Over many random masks, each position has expected value equal to the original (0.5 × 2 × a = a), so nothing needs to change at test time, but in every step the next layer must cope with half of its inputs missing.
L1 soft thresholding with λ = 0.5: an unpenalized coefficient of 0.3 becomes 0 (|0.3| ≤ 0.5), and a coefficient of 1.2 becomes 0.7. Small effects vanish, large ones shrink by a constant.
intuition: Prefer the simplest explanation that fits: a rule that needs fewer special cases is more likely to hold tomorrow (Occam's razor).
Fitting noise needs extra freedom: big weights, many unit combinations, or many training epochs. Regularization makes that freedom costly. A signal that appears in many examples is worth its cost; a quirk that appears in one example is not. The penalty therefore works like a budget: weights are spent only where the data pays for them.
There is a second way to see it. Noise is different in each sample, so a model that depends on noise changes when the sample changes; a model that depends only on stable patterns does not. Every technique in this read makes the model less sensitive to the particular sample: a penalty keeps the weights from chasing single points, dropout and augmentation show the model many variants of each example, and early stopping ends training before the weak, noisy directions are learned. Lower sensitivity to the sample is exactly lower variance.
mistakes: People often think regularization always lowers training accuracy and so is a trade of quality for safety. In fact it lowers training fit slightly but usually improves error on new data, which is the goal. When data is plentiful relative to model size, the optimal strength may be small.
People often think L2 penalty and weight decay are the same thing in every optimizer. In fact they are equal for plain SGD, but not for Adam-type optimizers, where the L2 term is rescaled by the adaptive step. AdamW applies true decoupled decay, and using Adam with an L2 penalty gives a different, usually weaker, regularization.
People often think that bigger models must overfit more. In fact modern networks with far more parameters than examples often generalize well, and test error can fall again beyond the interpolation point (double descent). Capacity alone does not decide generalization; the optimizer, the data and the architecture matter too, and classical bounds based on parameter count are often useless for deep networks.
People often think dropout should be used everywhere in deep learning. In fact it is often harmful or unneeded in some settings: with batch normalization the two can conflict; in language-model pre-training where each token is seen about once, there is little to overfit and many recipes use no dropout; in fine-tuning small datasets it is helpful. The choice depends on how many times the model sees each example.
friction: Regularization adds hyperparameters (λ, dropout rate, augmentation strength, patience), and they interact with the learning rate, batch size and training length. A search over them is expensive for large models, and a setting tuned for one scale often does not transfer. Too much regularization causes underfitting, and the error looks like a model that is "not learning". Detecting which of the two failure modes you are in needs both training and validation curves.
Theory lags behind practice for over-parameterized networks. Classical capacity-based bounds are vacuous for them, and we do not have a complete account of why SGD finds solutions that generalize, or why double descent happens. L1 gives sparse weights but sparse networks are not always faster on GPUs, which prefer dense operations. Penalties on weights ignore the function the network computes: two networks with very different weights can compute the same function (scale symmetry with normalization layers), so a weight norm can be a poor measure of complexity.
Regularization can also hide problems: augmentation that does not match the real data changes the distribution; early stopping on a validation set that is reused many times makes the validation score optimistic; and a penalty can reduce accuracy on rare subgroups more than on the majority, which matters for fairness. Finally, data leakage between train and validation sets makes every regularization decision look better than it is.
practice: Start with the defaults of your domain. Gradient-boosted trees: shallow trees (depth 4 to 8), a learning rate of 0.05 to 0.1, row and column subsampling of 0.6 to 0.9, and early stopping on a validation set. Linear and logistic models: L2 with λ chosen on a log scale (10⁻⁶ to 10²), L1 or elastic net when you need feature selection. Transformers: AdamW with decay about 0.01 to 0.1, applied to weight matrices but not to biases and normalization parameters; dropout 0 to 0.1 for pre-training, up to 0.1 to 0.3 for fine-tuning small data. Vision: random crops and flips, mixup or cutmix, label smoothing about 0.1, weight decay about 5 × 10⁻⁴ for SGD-trained convolutional networks.
Tune on validation data, not on test data, and change one thing at a time. Search λ on a logarithmic grid (factors of 3 or 10), use cross-validation when data is small, and plot training and validation curves: a large gap means more regularization or more data helps; both curves high means the model is underfitting and you should reduce regularization or increase capacity. Use early stopping with a patience of a few evaluations and keep the best checkpoint. For small data, prefer simple models, strong priors (pretrained weights, frozen layers), and augmentation that reflects real variation.
Do not forget the strongest regularizer: more and better data. Collecting diverse data, removing duplicates and fixing label noise often helps more than any penalty. In production, regularize toward stability too: when you retrain a model weekly, a penalty toward the previous weights (or a small learning rate) keeps predictions from jumping between versions. For ad-tech click models, L1 with feature hashing is a classic way to keep a huge sparse feature space under control, and calibration should be re-checked after the strength is changed.
tradeoffs: Choose L2 (weight decay) as the default for dense models: it is cheap, smooth, and works with every optimizer (use the decoupled form with Adam). Choose L1 or elastic net when you have thousands of correlated features and want a short list that people can read or a model that is cheap to serve; you give up some accuracy when many features each carry a little signal, and the choice of surviving features among correlated ones is unstable.
Choose dropout when the model is large relative to the data, training runs for many epochs and you cannot get more data (fine-tuning, small vision sets). It costs training time (the network needs more epochs to converge, often 1.5 to 2 times more) and noise in the loss. Skip it for single-epoch pre-training on huge corpora and when a normalization layer already regularizes; there it only slows learning.
Choose early stopping when you have a reliable validation set and want a cheap safeguard: it costs nothing extra except evaluation. You give up the validation data for training, and the stopping point is noisy; combine it with a penalty rather than relying on it alone.
Choose data augmentation when you know invariances of the task (a flipped cat is a cat) or can generate realistic variants; it is often the most effective regularizer for images, audio and small text sets. You pay in training time and in risk that the augmentation is unrealistic (flipping text or digits changes meaning). Choose more real data over augmentation whenever you can collect it.
Choose a smaller or more constrained model over heavy regularization of a big one when serving cost matters or the data is under about 10,000 examples. You give up flexibility and the benefits of pre-training. Choose a big pretrained model with light regularization when data is limited but a related large corpus exists, since pre-training acts as a very strong prior.
interview: What is the bias-variance trade-off and how does regularization affect it? || Expected squared error is \( \text{bias}^2 + \text{variance} + \text{noise} \). A flexible model has low bias but high variance because it changes with the particular sample. Regularization restricts the model, adding some bias and reducing variance; the best λ minimizes the sum on held-out data.
Why does L1 give sparse solutions while L2 does not? || The L1 penalty has a constant gradient magnitude λ near zero, so small weights are pushed all the way to zero (soft thresholding), and its constraint region has corners on the axes where the loss contours often touch. The L2 gradient shrinks with the weight, so weights become small but rarely exactly zero.
Explain the difference between L2 regularization and weight decay, and why AdamW exists. || In SGD adding \( \lambda w \) to the gradient equals decaying the weights by \( 1 - \eta\lambda \). In Adam the gradient is divided by a running estimate of its size, so the L2 term is rescaled and weights with big gradients are decayed less. AdamW applies the decay directly to the weights, decoupled from the adaptive scaling, and its optimal λ is less tied to the learning rate.
How does dropout work at training and at test time, and why scale? || In training each unit is zeroed with probability p and the survivors are multiplied by 1/(1 − p), so each activation keeps the same expected value. At test time all units are used with no scaling. It prevents units from co-adapting and approximates averaging over many thinned networks.
Your training loss is near zero and validation loss is rising. What do you try, in order? || Confirm no leakage and check the validation set. Try early stopping, more data or augmentation, weight decay and dropout, a smaller model, or a lower learning rate or fewer epochs. If both losses are high instead, reduce regularization and increase capacity.
How can a network with more parameters than data points generalize? || Parameter count is a poor measure of effective complexity. SGD from small initialization finds low-norm, flat solutions, architectures encode useful invariances, and the data has structure; explicit regularization and early stopping help further. Double descent shows test error can fall again after the interpolation point, though theory is incomplete.
quiz: What is the update rule that makes L2 regularization "weight decay"? || \( w \leftarrow (1 - \eta\lambda)\, w - \eta \nabla L \): each step shrinks the weights by a factor slightly under 1 before the gradient step.
Why does L1 produce exact zeros and L2 does not? || L1 has a constant pull toward zero, which cancels small weights exactly (soft thresholding); the L2 pull is proportional to the weight and fades near zero.
In the two-feature example, why did ridge help? || The data had a strong direction (the sum of the features) and a weak direction (the difference) that only fitted noise; ridge multiplies each direction by \( \sigma^2/(\sigma^2 + \lambda) \), keeping 99.8% of the strong one and only about 16% of the weak one at λ = 0.1.
Dropout with p = 0.5 produces [1.0, 0, 4.0, 0] from [0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 1.5]. Why is nothing scaled at test time? || The kept units were multiplied by 1/(1 − p) = 2 in training, so the expected activation equals the original; at test time all units are on with their normal values.
When would you prefer early stopping and augmentation over a stronger weight penalty? || When you have a trustworthy validation set and known invariances in the data (images, audio): both are cheap and add information or limit training time without forcing small weights on the useful features.

@@ genetic
value: A genetic algorithm searches for a good solution by keeping a population of candidate solutions and improving it over generations with three operations copied from biology: selection (better candidates are more likely to become parents), crossover (a child takes parts from two parents) and mutation (a child gets small random changes). It needs only one thing from the problem: a fitness function that can score a candidate. It does not need a gradient, a model of the problem or even a smooth landscape. That is why it is used when the objective is discrete, noisy, a black-box simulation or a program: the shape of an antenna, a schedule for 500 jobs, the structure of a neural network, a trading rule, or the parameters of a game-playing agent.
The price is the number of evaluations. A population of 100 run for 200 generations makes 20,000 fitness evaluations. If one evaluation is a simulation that takes a millisecond, this is cheap. If it is the training of a neural network that takes 10 minutes, it is 3,300 hours of compute (about 140 GPU-days) unless you run many evaluations in parallel. So genetic methods fit best when evaluations are cheap or highly parallel, and when gradients do not exist or cannot be trusted.
For the layer above (machine learning), evolutionary search is the tool when gradients are not available: tuning hyperparameters, searching architectures, optimizing policies on non-differentiable simulators, and, more recently, evolving programs with language models. Two lower layers define it. Optimization (L4) provides the problem statement (maximize f(x) over a space) and the vocabulary of local optima, exploration versus exploitation and convergence; a genetic algorithm is a stochastic, population-based search method for it. Evolution by natural selection (L5) provides the inspiration: heritable variation plus selection over many generations produces complex solutions. The algorithm keeps only the abstract loop of that process, not its biology.
terms: Population — the set of candidate solutions (individuals) kept at each generation, typically 20 to a few thousand.
Chromosome (genotype) — the encoding of one candidate, such as a bit string, a vector of numbers, a permutation or a tree.
Fitness function — a function that scores a candidate; the only link to the problem.
Selection — choosing parents with a probability that increases with fitness, for example by tournament or rank.
Crossover (recombination) — building a child by combining parts of two parents, for example one-point crossover at a random cut position.
Mutation — a small random change to a child, for example flipping each bit with probability 1/L.
Elitism — copying the best individuals unchanged into the next generation so progress is never lost.
Premature convergence — the population becomes too similar and gets stuck on a local optimum before finding a better region.
history: In the 1950s and 1960s several groups invented evolution-inspired search independently. Ingo Rechenberg and Hans-Paul Schwefel in Berlin developed evolution strategies (1960s) for engineering design with real-valued parameters, for example shapes in a wind tunnel, using mutation and a simple rule for adapting the step size. Lawrence Fogel proposed evolutionary programming (1966) to evolve finite-state machines. John Holland at the University of Michigan developed genetic algorithms, described in "Adaptation in Natural and Artificial Systems" (1975), with a binary encoding, crossover, and a theory (the schema theorem) about how short, good building blocks spread. David Goldberg's 1989 book made them popular, and John Koza's genetic programming (1992) evolved computer programs as trees.
Theory and practice then met some limits. Wolpert and Macready (1997) proved the "no free lunch" theorems: averaged over all possible problems, no search method beats any other, so a method wins only by matching the structure of the problem. The schema theorem turned out to be a weak explanation of why genetic algorithms work. The field moved toward real-valued methods that exploit structure: CMA-ES (Hansen and Ostermeier, 2001) adapts a full covariance matrix of the search distribution and became a standard tool for black-box optimization of continuous parameters up to a few hundred dimensions. NEAT (Stanley and Miikkulainen, 2002) evolved both the weights and the structure of small neural networks.
Evolution came back in deep learning. Real et al. (2019) used regularized (aging) evolution to search neural architectures, and the AmoebaNet model matched the best hand-designed image classifiers. Salimans et al. (OpenAI, 2017) showed that a simple evolution strategy can train policy networks on reinforcement-learning tasks, solving 3D humanoid walking in about 10 minutes with over a thousand parallel workers and reaching competitive Atari results in about an hour. In 2023 and 2025, DeepMind's FunSearch and AlphaEvolve combined an evolutionary loop with large language models acting as the mutation operator, and found new results in mathematics (AlphaEvolve found a way to multiply two 4×4 complex matrices with 48 scalar multiplications, the first improvement over Strassen's approach in this setting after 56 years) and in the efficiency of Google's own systems.
how: Define three things: a representation, a fitness function and the variation operators. The representation turns a candidate into a data structure that can be mixed and changed: a bit string for "which of 40 features to use", a vector of real numbers for controller parameters, a permutation for the order of cities in a tour, a tree for a program. The fitness function takes the decoded candidate, runs the simulation or model, and returns a number (higher is better). Everything else is generic.
The loop is simple. (1) Create an initial population, often at random, of size N. (2) Evaluate the fitness of each individual. (3) Select parents. Tournament selection draws k individuals at random (k = 2 to 5) and takes the best; larger k means stronger selection pressure. Roulette selection picks in proportion to fitness; rank selection picks by rank, which is robust to the scale of the fitness values. (4) Create children: with probability about 0.6 to 0.9, apply crossover to two parents (one-point, uniform, or a permutation-safe form such as order crossover), otherwise copy a parent. (5) Mutate: flip each bit with probability 1/L (so about one flip per child), add Gaussian noise to real numbers, swap two cities in a tour. (6) Form the next generation from the children, keeping the best one or two parents unchanged (elitism). (7) Repeat until the budget is spent or fitness stops improving.
Why does this work? Selection concentrates the population around good regions (exploitation). Mutation adds new variants near existing ones, so the search takes small exploratory steps like a randomized hill climber. Crossover is the distinctive operator: if two parents each solved a different part of the problem (one chose good values for the first half of the genes, the other for the second half), a child can inherit both. This only pays off when the representation is such that parts of the solution can be good independently ("building blocks"); for tightly coupled problems crossover mostly destroys good solutions and mutation does the work. The population also gives some protection against local optima, since different individuals may sit on different hills, although it does not remove the problem. Optimization theory describes this as a trade-off between exploration and exploitation, and the settings of N, k and the mutation rate control it.
Selection pressure can be put in numbers. In a tournament of size k, the best individual of a population of N takes part in about k tournaments per generation and wins all of them, so its copies multiply by roughly k each generation. A single good individual then takes over a population of N after about ln N / ln k generations: about 6.6 generations for N = 100 and k = 2, but only 2.9 for k = 5. Fast takeover is good for speed and bad for diversity, which is why k is kept small (2 to 5) and mutation keeps feeding in new variants. Constraints are handled by penalizing the fitness of infeasible children, by repair operators, or by an encoding that cannot express invalid solutions.
Evolution strategies (ES) are the real-valued branch, and they have a clear statistical meaning. Keep a mean vector θ and a step size σ. In each generation sample n perturbations \( \varepsilon_i \) from a standard Gaussian, evaluate the fitness \( F(\theta + \sigma \varepsilon_i) \), and move the mean toward the better samples: \( \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha \, \frac{1}{n\sigma} \sum_{i=1}^{n} F(\theta + \sigma \varepsilon_i)\, \varepsilon_i \). This is a Monte-Carlo estimate of the gradient of the smoothed objective \( \mathbb{E}[F(\theta + \sigma\varepsilon)] \). CMA-ES also adapts the covariance, so the sampling cloud stretches along valleys. Because workers only need to share a random seed and one number (the score), ES parallelizes across thousands of machines with almost no communication. The estimate gets noisier as the number of parameters grows: with millions of parameters, you need many samples where backpropagation would give an exact gradient from one pass.
Among newer variations: multi-objective methods (NSGA-II) keep a set of trade-off solutions (the Pareto front); quality-diversity methods (MAP-Elites) keep the best solution in each cell of a behavior grid, for a map of different good solutions; island models run several populations and exchange a few migrants; and LLM-guided evolution replaces random mutation by asking a language model to rewrite the best programs so far, with an automatic evaluator as fitness.

example: Solve the toy problem "OneMax": find a bit string of length 8 with as many ones as possible (fitness = number of ones; the best is 11111111, fitness 8). Population size 4. Generation 0: A = 10010010 (fitness 3), B = 01101100 (4), C = 11100001 (4), D = 00011000 (2). The average fitness is (3 + 4 + 4 + 2)/4 = 3.25.
Selection by tournaments of size 2. Draw (A, B): B wins. Draw (C, D): C wins. Draw (B, C): tie, take C. Draw (A, D): A wins. Parents: B, C, C, A. Crossover. Pair B × C with a cut after position 4: B = 0110|1100 and C = 1110|0001 give children 0110 0001 (fitness 3) and 1110 1100 (fitness 5). Pair C × A with a cut after position 2: C = 11|100001 and A = 10|010010 give 11 010010 (fitness 4) and 10 100001 (fitness 3). Mutation flips each bit with probability 1/8, so on average one bit per child. By chance: child 1 flips its bit 7 (counting from 0) from 0 to 1, giving 01100011 (4); child 3 flips bit 5 from 0 to 1, giving 11010110 (5); child 4 flips bit 0 from 1 to 0, giving 00100001 (2); child 2 stays 11101100 (5).
The new population has fitness 4, 5, 5, 2, an average of 4.0, up from 3.25, and the best individual has gone from 4 to 5. Notice three things. Crossover produced the 5 by combining the good left half of C with the good right half of B. Mutation helped twice and harmed once; selection will remove the harmful one soon. And randomness means a different run gives different numbers, so results are reported as averages over many runs. On this easy problem a GA is not the right tool: a one-individual evolutionary algorithm that mutates one parent and keeps the better of parent and child needs about \( e \cdot L \ln L \approx 1{,}250 \) evaluations for \( L = 100 \) (a known result of Droste, Jansen and Wegener), and the population adds cost without adding value. Populations pay off when the landscape has many local optima.
Cost accounting for a realistic case: tuning 12 hyperparameters of a ranking model, where one evaluation is a 20-minute training run on one GPU. A population of 24 for 30 generations is 720 evaluations. On 24 GPUs, each generation takes one 20-minute wave, so the whole search takes 30 × 20 minutes = 10 hours of wall-clock time (240 GPU-hours). A grid with 5 values per hyperparameter would need \( 5^{12} \) = 244 million runs; random search with the same 720 runs is a strong competitor, and an honest comparison should include it.
intuition: Breed many guesses, keep the fittest, and let small random changes explore; good parts found in different guesses get a chance to meet in one child.
It works the way a plant breeder improves a crop: no one needs to understand the genetics of the plant, only to measure each plant and choose which ones seed the next season. The method makes only weak assumptions about the problem, so it can be pointed at nearly anything that can be scored. The same weakness is its cost: because it knows nothing about the problem's structure, it uses many trials to find what a method with gradients or a model would find in a few steps.
mistakes: People often think a genetic algorithm is a smarter or more general optimizer than gradient descent. In fact when gradients exist and the model is large, gradient descent is far more efficient, by orders of magnitude. Evolution is for problems where gradients are unavailable, misleading or meaningless, not a replacement.
People often think crossover always helps. In fact crossover helps only when the encoding makes parts of the solution independent. For tightly coupled problems, mixing two good parents usually produces a broken child; many successful systems (such as evolution strategies and CMA-ES) use no crossover at all.
People often think the algorithm finds the global optimum. In fact it is a heuristic with no guarantee. It can converge prematurely when the population loses diversity, and the no-free-lunch theorems state that no search method wins on all problems. Results depend heavily on representation and operators.
People often think a bigger population or more generations always fixes poor results. In fact a bad fitness function, an encoding that makes small changes in the genotype cause large jumps in behavior, or a deceptive landscape defeat any budget. Also, the algorithm optimizes exactly the fitness you wrote, including loopholes: evolved simulators often "cheat" by exploiting bugs in the physics engine.
friction: The main cost is the number of fitness evaluations; there is no information reuse like the gradient provides. This limits genetic methods to cheap or parallel evaluations, and the search becomes noisy and slow as the dimension grows: with a million parameters an ES needs very large populations for a useful gradient estimate, which is why deep networks are trained by backpropagation and not by evolution, except in special settings.
The design burden is large. You must pick an encoding, operators that preserve validity (a tour must stay a permutation), a population size, mutation rate and selection pressure, and each affects the result. Constraints are awkward: infeasible children must be repaired or penalized. Fitness evaluation noise (a stochastic simulation) needs repeated evaluations or careful selection. Loss of diversity, deception (where short good building blocks lead away from the optimum) and plateaus make behavior hard to predict, and there is little theory that is both rigorous and useful in practice. Results across papers are hard to compare because of different budgets and tuning effort, and many reports lacked a fair baseline such as random search or Bayesian optimization.
Evolved solutions can be hard to understand and may exploit modelling errors: an antenna design that is excellent in the simulator may fail in the real world if the simulation is inexact. Verification is therefore part of the pipeline. For modern LLM-guided evolution, the evaluator must be fast, automatic and hard to fool, which limits the problems it can address to those with checkable scores.
practice: First ask whether you need evolution at all. If the objective is differentiable, use gradients. If there are fewer than about 20 continuous parameters and evaluations are expensive, Bayesian optimization is usually more sample-efficient. If you have a discrete or structural search with cheap, parallel evaluations, or a black-box simulator, an evolutionary method is a good choice. Always run random search with the same budget as a baseline.
For continuous parameters use CMA-ES (the `cma` Python package, or Nevergrad, Optuna's CMA-ES sampler, or Evosax and EvoTorch for GPU-based ES). For discrete or mixed problems use a library such as DEAP, pymoo (for multi-objective problems) or pygad. Settings that work as defaults: population 50 to 200, tournament size 3, crossover probability 0.7 to 0.9, mutation rate 1/L per gene, elitism of 1 to 2 individuals. Make evaluation cheap: cache fitness values for repeated genotypes, evaluate in parallel, use short proxy runs (train for 1 epoch instead of 20) and then re-evaluate the best candidates fully.
Watch the population: log best, mean and diversity (for example average pairwise distance) each generation. If diversity collapses early, lower selection pressure, raise the mutation rate, or use islands. Use several random seeds and report the median and spread. Normalize or rank-transform fitness values for ES, and use mirrored (antithetic) sampling to reduce variance. For production, prefer to evolve a few well-chosen parameters of a known-good system over evolving everything from scratch, and validate every result on data or a simulator the search never saw.
tradeoffs: Choose an evolutionary method over gradient descent when the objective is non-differentiable, discrete, or a black box (a simulator, a compiler flag choice, a game score); you pay in evaluations, typically thousands to millions. Choose gradient descent whenever gradients exist: it is cheaper by a factor that grows with the number of parameters.
Choose CMA-ES over a binary GA for continuous parameters up to a few hundred dimensions: it adapts step sizes and correlations automatically and has few settings. You give up crossover and discrete-structure flexibility. Choose a GA with a custom encoding for combinatorial structure (schedules, layouts, programs) where problem-specific operators exist.
Choose Bayesian optimization over evolution when evaluations are very expensive (minutes to hours each), the budget is below a few hundred evaluations and the dimension is low (under about 20). It models the objective and picks each point carefully. Choose evolution when you can run thousands of evaluations in parallel, the space is discrete or structured, or the dimension is high, because a surrogate model becomes unreliable there.
Choose a large population with weak selection pressure when the landscape has many local optima and you have the budget; choose a small population with strong selection and elitism when evaluations are limited and the landscape is smooth. In both cases you trade exploration against speed of convergence.
Choose evolutionary strategies for reinforcement learning when you have thousands of cheap CPU workers, rewards come only at the end of a long episode, or the policy is not differentiable; you give up sample efficiency (policy-gradient methods usually need far fewer environment steps) and gain simplicity and near-linear parallel scaling.
interview: When would you use a genetic algorithm or evolution strategy instead of gradient descent? || When the objective cannot be differentiated or its gradient is uninformative: discrete choices, black-box simulators, noisy or non-smooth rewards, program or architecture search. It needs only fitness values, parallelizes easily, but costs many more evaluations, so it suits cheap or highly parallel evaluation.
Describe the roles of selection, crossover and mutation. || Selection increases the share of good solutions (exploitation); mutation creates small random variations (exploration); crossover combines parts of two good solutions, which helps when sub-parts of the solution can be good independently. Too much selection pressure loses diversity; too much mutation turns the search into random walk.
What is premature convergence and how do you handle it? || The population becomes nearly identical and stalls on a local optimum. Remedies: lower selection pressure, higher mutation rate, larger population, fitness sharing or niching, island models with migration, restarts, or novelty and quality-diversity methods.
How does the OpenAI evolution strategy estimate a gradient, and why does it scale to thousands of workers? || It samples Gaussian perturbations of the parameters, evaluates the reward of each, and updates the mean by the reward-weighted average of the perturbations, which estimates the gradient of the smoothed reward. Workers share only a random seed and a scalar score, so communication is tiny. The cost is noisy estimates in high dimensions.
You must tune 10 hyperparameters, each trial takes 30 minutes on one GPU, and you have 40 GPUs for one day. Design the search. || Budget: 40 GPUs × 24 hours × 2 trials per hour = 1,920 trials. Use random search as the baseline, then a population-based or CMA-ES search (population 40, so each generation fills the GPUs for 30 minutes, about 48 generations), with early stopping on poor trials. Why do evolved designs sometimes fail on real hardware, and what is the general lesson? || The search optimizes the fitness as written, including simulator errors and loopholes. The general lesson is Goodhart's law: validate on a held-out simulator or the real system, add noise and constraints to the fitness, and treat the search result as a candidate.
quiz: What three operators form the core loop of a genetic algorithm? || Selection (choose parents by fitness), crossover (combine two parents), and mutation (random small changes), repeated over generations with fitness evaluation.
What is the main practical cost of evolutionary search? || The number of fitness evaluations (population size × generations), since there is no gradient to guide the search; it is feasible when evaluation is cheap or parallel.
For a bit string of length 40, what mutation rate is a sensible default and why? || 1/L = 1/40 per bit, so on average one bit changes per child: enough to explore nearby solutions without destroying most of what selection has found.
A GA uses population 50 for 100 generations and each evaluation takes 2 minutes on one machine. How long does it take on 50 machines in parallel? || 50 × 100 = 5,000 evaluations; with 50 in parallel it is 100 waves of 2 minutes = 200 minutes, about 3.3 hours (versus 167 hours on one machine).
Why is CMA-ES often preferred to a bit-string GA for continuous parameters? || It samples from a Gaussian whose mean, step size and covariance adapt to the landscape, so it handles scaling and correlations between parameters with few settings, while a bit-string GA needs encoding choices and tuned operators.

@@ boosting
value: Gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDT) build a strong predictor by adding many small decision trees one after another, where each new tree is trained to correct the mistakes of the trees before it. On tabular data (rows of numeric and categorical columns, such as ad-click logs, transactions or user profiles) this is still the strongest general-purpose method. XGBoost, LightGBM and CatBoost win most tabular competitions, train on millions of rows in minutes on a CPU, and predict in microseconds per row. In ad tech they are used for click and conversion prediction, fraud and invalid-traffic detection, churn, bid shading and ranking.
Why do they work so well on this kind of data? A single decision tree asks a chain of yes/no questions about the features ("is the age above 35? is the device a TV?") and ends in a leaf with a prediction. It handles mixed feature types, missing values, outliers and feature interactions without scaling or encoding, and it is easy to read. But one tree is a weak, unstable model. Boosting fixes that: a hundred or a thousand shallow trees, each adding a small correction, form an additive model that can represent complicated interactions and is stable because each step is small.
Three lower layers explain the method. Trees (L3) are the weak learners: binary trees stored as arrays of (feature, threshold, left, right) nodes, evaluated by walking from the root to a leaf in about depth steps, and built by scanning sorted feature values to find the best split. Optimization (L4) gives the training rule: boosting is gradient descent in function space, where each tree approximates the negative gradient of the loss, and the second-order version used by XGBoost is a Newton step. Statistics (L4) gives the control of overfitting: a small learning rate (shrinkage), random subsampling of rows and columns, and limits on tree size are bias-variance tools, and the bias-variance view explains why boosting (which lowers bias with shallow trees) differs from random forests (which lower variance with deep trees).
terms: Decision tree — a model that splits the data by a threshold on one feature at each internal node and predicts a constant in each leaf.
Weak learner — a model only slightly better than the baseline, here a shallow tree of depth 3 to 8.
Boosting — building an ensemble in sequence, each model trained on what the previous ones got wrong.
Pseudo-residual (negative gradient) — for each row, the direction in which its prediction should move to reduce the loss; for squared error it is simply target minus prediction.
Learning rate (shrinkage) — the factor ν (often 0.03 to 0.3) by which each tree's output is scaled before being added.
Split gain — the reduction in loss obtained by splitting a node into two children; the split with the largest gain is chosen.
Histogram binning — grouping each feature's values into about 255 bins so that split search scans bins and not individual values.
Bagging / random forest — averaging many deep trees, each trained independently on a random sample of rows and features; reduces variance.
history: Tree learning began with Morgan and Sonquist (1963) and became a standard method with CART (Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone, 1984) and Quinlan's ID3 (1986) and C4.5 (1993). Trees were interpretable but unstable. Boosting arose from learning theory: could many weak learners be combined into a strong one? Schapire (1990) showed they could, and Freund and Schapire's AdaBoost (1995; Journal of Computer and System Sciences, 1997) did it in practice by re-weighting examples so that each new learner focuses on the mistakes of the earlier ones. It won the Gödel Prize in 2003. Leo Breiman (1998 to 2001) and then Friedman, Hastie and Tibshirani (2000) showed that AdaBoost minimizes the exponential loss by stagewise additive fitting.
Jerome Friedman's "Greedy Function Approximation: A Gradient Boosting Machine" (Annals of Statistics, 2001) generalized this to any differentiable loss: fit each new tree to the negative gradient of the loss. He added shrinkage and, in 2002, random subsampling ("stochastic gradient boosting"). Breiman's random forests (2001) took the other path, averaging deep independent trees. In the 2000s gradient boosting was adopted in web search ranking (LambdaMART, Burges, won the Yahoo Learning-to-Rank challenge in 2010), and at Facebook, where He et al. (ADKDD 2014) used boosted trees to turn raw features into inputs for a logistic regression for ad click prediction.
Engineering made it the default. Tianqi Chen's XGBoost (2014 release, KDD 2016 paper with Guestrin) added second-order gradients, explicit regularization of leaf weights, sparsity-aware split finding, and a scalable system design, and spread through Kaggle after the Higgs challenge. LightGBM (Microsoft, Ke et al., NeurIPS 2017) added histogram-based split finding, leaf-wise tree growth, gradient-based one-side sampling and exclusive feature bundling. CatBoost (Yandex, Prokhorenkova et al., NeurIPS 2018) added ordered boosting and target statistics for categorical features, to reduce target leakage. Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux (NeurIPS 2022) confirmed on a large benchmark that tree-based models still beat deep networks on medium-sized tabular data.
how: Start with a loss \( L(y, F(x)) \) and an initial constant model \( F_0 \) (the mean of y for squared error, the log-odds of the base rate for log loss). Repeat for \( m = 1, \dots, M \). (1) For every training row compute the pseudo-residual \( r_i = -\partial L(y_i, F)/\partial F \) at the current prediction \( F_{m-1}(x_i) \). For squared error \( L = \tfrac{1}{2}(y - F)^2 \) this is \( y - F \), the ordinary residual. For log loss with \( p = \sigma(F) \) it is \( y - p \). (2) Fit a small regression tree \( h_m \) to the pseudo-residuals \( r_i \). (3) Update \( F_m(x) = F_{m-1}(x) + \nu\, h_m(x) \), with learning rate \( \nu \). The final model is the sum of all trees plus \( F_0 \). Every tree is a step in the direction of steepest descent of the loss, but the "direction" is a function, approximated by a tree. This is the optimization concept applied to a space of functions.
How does a tree choose its splits? For a node with a set of rows, try each feature and each threshold and compute how much the loss falls if the node is split into left and right children. XGBoost expresses this with gradients \( g_i \) (first derivative of the loss for row i) and hessians \( h_i \) (second derivative). Sum them over a node: \( G = \sum g_i \) and \( H = \sum h_i \). The best constant output for a leaf is \( w = -G/(H + \lambda) \), where \( \lambda \) is an L2 penalty on leaf weights. The gain of a split is
\[ \text{gain} = \frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L + \lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R + \lambda} - \frac{(G_L + G_R)^2}{H_L + H_R + \lambda}\right] - \gamma \]
where \( \gamma \) is a penalty for adding a leaf. Splits with negative gain are not made, which prunes the tree. For squared error \( g_i \) = prediction − target and \( h_i = 1 \), so the formulas reduce to means and counts. The hessian is what makes the step a Newton step rather than a plain gradient step; for log loss, \( h_i = p(1 - p) \) automatically gives smaller steps for rows the model is already sure about.
The cost is in the search for splits. A naive method sorts each feature at each node, which is O(n log n) per feature. Histogram methods (LightGBM, XGBoost "hist", CatBoost) first bin each feature into at most 255 bins, once. Then for each node they build a histogram of (G, H) sums per bin in one pass over the rows, and scan the bins to find the best threshold: O(n) to build and O(bins) to scan. A further trick: build the histogram only for the smaller child, and obtain the larger child's histogram by subtracting it from the parent's. Trees can be grown level by level (XGBoost default) or leaf by leaf, always splitting the leaf with the highest gain (LightGBM), which gives lower loss for the same number of leaves but can overfit small data, so min-data-in-leaf must be set.
Overfitting is controlled with four kinds of settings, all of them statistics. Shrinkage: a small \( \nu \) (0.03 to 0.1) with more trees. Tree size: depth 4 to 8, or 15 to 255 leaves, and a minimum of rows or hessian-weight per leaf. Subsampling: each tree uses a random 50 to 90% of the rows and of the columns (this decorrelates the trees and speeds training). Penalties: \( \lambda \), \( \gamma \) and early stopping on a validation set, which picks the number of trees M. Missing values are handled by learning a default direction at each split. Categorical features are handled by target encoding with ordering (CatBoost), by grouping categories by gradient statistics (LightGBM), or by one-hot codes.
The same loop serves different tasks only by changing the loss. For binary classification the trees add up to a log-odds score \( F \) and the probability is \( \sigma(F) \); the pseudo-residual is \( y - p \) and the hessian \( p(1 - p) \). For K classes, each round grows K trees, one per class, with a softmax loss. For ranking, LambdaMART uses pairwise gradients weighted by how much swapping two items would change NDCG. For counts or durations, Poisson or Tweedie losses fit directly. Because the algorithm needs only the first and second derivatives of the loss at each row, any twice-differentiable loss can be plugged in (see Loss functions).
Prediction is fast: for each tree, follow comparisons from the root to a leaf (about depth steps) and add the leaf value; a model with 500 trees of depth 6 does about 3,000 comparisons per row, a few microseconds when the nodes are in cache. Trees can be compiled into native code or evaluated in batches (Treelite, FIL on GPU).

example: Six rows with one feature x = 1 … 6 and targets y = [1, 2, 2, 8, 9, 12]. Use squared error, trees with a single split (stumps), learning rate \( \nu = 0.5 \), \( \lambda = 0 \).
Start: F₀ = mean(y) = 34/6 = 5.667 for all rows. Residuals: [−4.667, −3.667, −3.667, 2.333, 3.333, 6.333]. The sum of squared errors is 105.3. Round 1: try each split. Splitting after x = 3 gives left residuals mean −4.0 (rows 1 to 3) and right mean +4.0 (rows 4 to 6). The leftover squared error is 9.33, so this split removes 96.0 (check with the gain formula: G_L = +12, H_L = 3, G_R = −12, H_R = 3, G = 0, H = 6, gain = ½ [144/3 + 144/3 − 0] = 48, which is half of 96 because the formula's ½ scales the loss). Other splits are worse: after x = 2 removes 52.1, after x = 4 removes 70.1. New predictions: F₁ = 5.667 + 0.5 × (−4.0) = 3.667 for rows 1 to 3 and 5.667 + 0.5 × 4.0 = 7.667 for rows 4 to 6. Squared error is now 33.3 (mean 5.56), down from 105.3.
Round 2: the new residuals are [−2.667, −1.667, −1.667, 0.333, 1.333, 4.333]. The best stump now splits after x = 4: left mean −1.417, right mean +2.833. Add 0.5 times these: F₂ = [2.958, 2.958, 2.958, 6.958, 9.083, 9.083]. Squared error: 15.3. After round 3 (split after x = 5, leaf values −0.583 and +2.917) the error is 7.6. Each stump is crude, but the sum is a staircase that follows the data.
Effect of regularization on round 1 with \( \lambda = 1 \): leaf values become \( -G/(H + \lambda) = -12/4 = -3 \) and +3 (instead of −4 and +4), and the gain is \( \tfrac{1}{2}[144/4 + 144/4] = 36 \). Leaves with few rows are shrunk more: a leaf with H = 1 would have its value cut in half. This is why λ protects against leaves that rest on one or two rows.
Now the cost. A dataset with 10 million rows and 200 features, histogram method with 255 bins: each tree level makes one pass over the rows, 10 million × 200 histogram updates = 2 billion updates, a few seconds on a multi-core CPU, and the subtraction trick halves it. A depth-6 tree needs 6 such passes, so one tree takes roughly seconds on a multi-core machine and a 500-tree model trains in minutes to tens of minutes, depending on the hardware and settings. Inference for one row: 500 trees × 6 comparisons = 3,000 comparisons, a few microseconds.
intuition: Each new student concentrates on the questions the class still gets wrong, and the class's answer is the sum of everyone's corrections.
Boosting is natural because it is gradient descent where the steps are trees. A single shallow tree is a poor model, but it can find the one rough pattern that explains most of what is left. Taking only a small fraction of its advice (the learning rate) avoids overreacting to noise, and letting later trees see only the remaining error makes them specialize. Since a sum of trees can represent any interaction a tree can, depth 6 is enough to capture interactions among a few features. The tree structure itself is a good fit for tables: features have different scales, meanings and many irrelevant ones, and a threshold rule on a single feature ignores scale and focuses on order.
mistakes: People often think boosting and random forests are two names for the same thing. In fact they are opposite strategies. A random forest averages many deep, independent trees to reduce variance; boosting adds many shallow trees in sequence to reduce bias. Boosting is more accurate when tuned and more sensitive to noise and settings.
People often think more trees always give a better model. In fact, because each tree corrects the previous errors, with enough trees the model starts to fit noise and validation loss rises. The number of trees must be chosen with early stopping on a validation set, and it depends on the learning rate (halving ν roughly doubles the trees needed).
People often think trees need feature scaling or one-hot encoding like linear models and neural nets. In fact trees depend only on the ordering of numeric values, so scaling does nothing, and good implementations handle missing values and categories directly. Feature engineering still matters, but of another kind: ratios, counts over time windows, and target statistics.
People often think trees can extrapolate. In fact a tree predicts a constant in each leaf, so outside the range of the training data the prediction stays flat at the value of the last leaf. For a trend that continues (time-based growth) use a model with a linear component, or predict a detrended target.
friction: Boosted trees depend on good features. They do not learn representations from raw pixels, audio or text, so on those data types neural networks win, and on tabular data they need engineered inputs such as aggregates over time windows. For high-cardinality categorical features (user id, device id with millions of values) they overfit or lose information unless the encoding is done with care, and for sparse, extremely wide data (hashed features with billions of dimensions) linear models or factorization machines scale better.
Training is sequential by nature: tree m needs the residuals from tree m − 1, so parallelism is within a tree (across features, rows or histograms), not across trees. Online learning is awkward: adding new data usually means retraining, or adding trees on top, which drifts. Models are piecewise-constant, so predictions are not smooth in the features, and calibration of probabilities from boosting with log loss is usually good but can be distorted by heavy shrinkage or class weighting. Large ensembles are hard to interpret; tools like SHAP give per-feature attributions for each prediction, but they explain the model, not the cause.
Hyperparameters interact (learning rate, number of trees, depth, minimum leaf size, subsampling, regularization) and require a search; different libraries use slightly different defaults and the same names mean different things. Recent work challenges the status quo on small data: TabPFN (Hollmann et al., Nature 2025), a Transformer pre-trained on synthetic tables, reports better accuracy than tuned boosted-tree ensembles on datasets up to about 10,000 rows, within seconds. It is not (yet) a replacement for GBDT on large tables or when latency and cost per prediction matter.
practice: Start with LightGBM or XGBoost histogram mode with sensible defaults: learning rate 0.05, max depth 6 (or 31 to 63 leaves), min child weight or min data in leaf 20 to 100, row subsample 0.8, column subsample 0.8, L2 λ of 1, and up to 5,000 trees with early stopping (patience 50 to 200) on a validation set. Use CatBoost when you have many categorical features and little time for encoding. Split the data the way production will use it: by time for anything that predicts the future, and by user or group when rows of one user are correlated; a random split leaks and gives a model that looks better than it is.
Spend effort on features: ratios, counts and recency over windows (clicks in the last 1, 7, 30 days), target encodings computed out-of-fold, and interactions that a human knows matter. Check feature importance by permutation or SHAP, not only by split counts, which favor high-cardinality features. For imbalanced problems keep log loss as the objective and only then adjust the decision threshold, or use scale_pos_weight and re-calibrate. Monitor calibration (reliability plots) if the output is used as a probability in an auction.
In production, convert the model to a fast runtime, keep the feature pipeline identical in training and serving (feature skew is a common cause of silent degradation), and retrain on a schedule with a time-based hold-out to catch drift. Use monotonic constraints when domain knowledge says a feature should only push the score one way (price, bid), which improves robustness and explainability at a small cost in accuracy. A common pattern in ads (He et al., 2014): use the leaf indices of a boosted model as categorical features for a logistic regression that can be updated online.
tradeoffs: Choose gradient-boosted trees over deep learning for tabular data with fewer than a few million rows, mixed feature types and no raw text or images: you get strong accuracy with little tuning, minutes of training and microsecond inference. You give up learned representations, easy multi-task training and online updates. Choose a neural network when the inputs are raw images, text or audio, when you need embeddings for huge ID spaces trained end-to-end, or when you have hundreds of millions of rows and rich sparse features (large ad-ranking systems), accepting more engineering and compute.
Choose boosting over a random forest when you want the best accuracy and can tune; choose a random forest when you need a robust baseline with almost no tuning, parallel training and some protection against label noise, and accept lower peak accuracy. Choose a linear or logistic model when the features are already good, you need an interpretable and online-updatable model, or the feature space is extremely wide and sparse.
Choose a small learning rate with many trees (0.03 with 2,000 trees) for the final model and a large one (0.1 to 0.3) for fast experiments. You pay training time (about 3 to 10 times) for a usually small gain in accuracy and robustness. Choose shallow trees (depth 3 to 5) when interactions are weak or data is small, and deeper trees (depth 8 to 12) with strong regularization when many interactions matter and data is large.
Choose leaf-wise growth (LightGBM) for speed and accuracy on large data; choose level-wise growth or symmetric trees (CatBoost) when data is small or you want more stable behavior and faster inference. Choose CatBoost for many high-cardinality categoricals and when out-of-the-box quality matters; choose LightGBM for large data and speed; choose XGBoost for a mature ecosystem and GPU or distributed training support.
interview: Explain gradient boosting in two minutes, with the algorithm. || Start with a constant prediction. Repeat: compute the negative gradient of the loss for each row (the residual for squared error, y − p for log loss), fit a small regression tree to it, and add the tree scaled by a learning rate. The final model is the sum. It is gradient descent in function space, with trees as the step direction. XGBoost adds second-order information and regularized leaf weights.
How does XGBoost choose a split, and what do λ and γ do? || It sums gradients G and hessians H in the left and right children and computes \( \text{gain} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left[\frac{G_L^2}{H_L+\lambda} + \frac{G_R^2}{H_R+\lambda} - \frac{G^2}{H+\lambda}\right] - \gamma \). The best leaf value is \( -G/(H+\lambda) \). \( \lambda \) shrinks leaf values (more for leaves with small H), and \( \gamma \) is the minimum gain required for a split, which prunes trees.
Why do histogram-based methods train faster, and what do they lose? || They bin each feature into at most 256 bins once, then build per-node (G, H) histograms in a pass over the rows and scan only the bins; the subtraction trick gets one child from the parent. Cost per level becomes O(rows × features) plus O(bins × features) instead of sorting. The loss is the exact threshold position, which rarely matters in practice.
Your model has an AUC of 0.85 offline but performs worse online. What do you check? || Time leakage in features or in the split (random instead of time-based split), target leakage through encodings or aggregates computed with future data, training-serving feature skew, population shift, and calibration. Rebuild the validation by time, recompute features as they would be available at serving time, and compare feature distributions online and offline.
Why can't a tree-based model extrapolate, and what do you do about it? || Each leaf holds a constant, so predictions outside the training range equal the nearest leaf values. Use a linear or parametric model for the trend and boost on the residual, add features like time since a reference date with caution, or model ratios and differences instead of levels.
How do boosting and bagging differ in terms of bias and variance? || Bagging (random forest) averages deep, low-bias, high-variance trees trained independently; averaging reduces variance. Boosting adds shallow, high-bias trees sequentially, each reducing the remaining error, which lowers bias; the learning rate, subsampling and early stopping control the variance it adds.
quiz: What does each new tree in gradient boosting fit? || The negative gradient of the loss with respect to the current prediction for each row (the residual for squared error).
Why is the learning rate (shrinkage) used? || Taking only a fraction of each tree's correction prevents overreacting to noise and gives smoother, more general models; it needs more trees, chosen by early stopping.
In the example with y = [1, 2, 2, 8, 9, 12] and \( F_0 = 5.667 \), what are the leaf values of the first stump and the new predictions with \( \nu = 0.5 \)? || The split after x = 3 gives leaf means −4.0 and +4.0, so the predictions become 5.667 − 2.0 = 3.667 for the first three rows and 5.667 + 2.0 = 7.667 for the last three.
Why do trees not need feature scaling? || A split uses only the order of values of one feature (a threshold), so monotone transformations like scaling or log do not change the chosen splits.
When would you pick a neural network over gradient-boosted trees? || For raw images, text or audio, for huge sparse ID features that need learned embeddings, for multi-task or online learning at very large scale; for medium-sized tabular data boosted trees are usually better.

value: Evaluation answers two questions about a model or an AI system: is it good enough to ship, and did this change make it better? The method is to run the system on a set of test cases that it did not train on, score each output with a rule, a person or another model, and summarize the scores with an honest estimate of uncertainty. For LLM products, evals have become the central engineering practice: a prompt edit, a new model version or a retrieval change can improve one kind of case and quietly break another, and without a repeatable eval the team is guessing.
The hard part is not running the code. It is that every part of the pipeline can mislead. The test set may overlap with the training data (contamination), so the score measures memory, not skill. The test set may be small, so a 1.5-point gain is noise: on 500 questions the 95% confidence interval of a single accuracy is about ±3.5 points. The scoring rule may reward the wrong thing (length, style, a judge model's preferences). And the benchmark may stop measuring what you care about once everyone optimizes for it. An eval is useful to the extent that you can say how much to trust it.
For the layer above (machine learning, and large language models in particular), evals decide which model to use, which training run to keep and which release to ship. Two lower layers do the real work. Statistics (L4) gives the estimates and error bars: the standard error of an accuracy is \( \sqrt{p(1 - p)/n} \), and paired designs on the same items shrink the noise when comparing models. Hypothesis testing (L4) gives the decision rule: whether model B is better than model A is a test of a difference, with a stated false-alarm rate and enough items to have power to detect the size of improvement you care about.
terms: Benchmark — a fixed dataset and scoring protocol shared by many people so results can be compared (MMLU, HumanEval, SWE-bench).
Held-out set — examples never used for training or tuning; the only data on which a score means generalization.
Contamination (leakage) — test examples, or close copies, appear in the training data, so the score is inflated.
LLM-as-judge — using a language model with a rubric to grade another model's outputs.
pass@k — the probability that at least one of k sampled answers is correct (for code, passes the unit tests).
Confidence interval — a range that would contain the true score in 95% of repeated experiments; for accuracy, \( \pm 1.96 \times \sqrt{p(1 - p)/n} \).
Paired comparison — comparing two models on the same items, so item difficulty cancels and only the disagreements count.
Saturation — a benchmark where top models score near the ceiling so it no longer separates them.
history: Measuring systems on shared test sets is old in speech and information retrieval (the TREC evaluations began in 1992 and NIST speech tests before that). In machine learning, ImageNet (Deng et al., 2009) and its annual challenge showed how a benchmark can drive a field: top-5 error fell from about 28% in 2010 to about 15% with AlexNet in 2012 and below 4% by 2015. In language, BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002) offered an automatic score for translation, and GLUE (Wang et al., 2018) and SuperGLUE (2019) collected tasks into one leaderboard; models passed human-level baselines within a year or two, which showed how quickly benchmarks saturate.
For large language models, a new set arrived: MMLU (Hendrycks et al., 2020; 57 subjects, 15,908 multiple-choice questions), HumanEval (Chen et al., 2021; 164 programming problems with unit tests, scored with pass@k), BIG-bench (2022), HELM (Liang et al., 2022, which measured many metrics across many scenarios), GPQA (Rein et al., 2023; expert-written science questions), and SWE-bench (Jimenez et al., ICLR 2024; real GitHub issues that the model must fix, checked by the project's tests). Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al., 2023) collects human pairwise votes and ranks models with a Bradley-Terry model. The paper "Judging LLM-as-a-Judge" (Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023) showed that a strong model judge agrees with human preferences at about the level humans agree with each other, but with systematic biases.
Two cautionary results shaped the field. Recht et al. (2018) built new test sets for CIFAR-10 and ImageNet and found that accuracies of the best models dropped by about 11 to 14 points, which showed that years of tuning against the same test set leaks information. And for LLMs, contamination became a standing worry as web-scale pre-training data came to include benchmark questions; GPT-3's paper already reported a 13-gram overlap check. Responses include private held-out sets, continuously refreshed benchmarks (such as LiveCodeBench), human-verified subsets (SWE-bench Verified, 2024), and error bars (Miller, 2024, "Adding Error Bars to Evals").
how: An eval has four parts: cases, a way to run the system, a scorer and an analysis. Cases: a list of inputs, each with whatever is needed to score it (a reference answer, a unit test, a rubric). For a product, the best cases come from real usage: sample production traces, read them, and turn failures into test cases. Run: call the system under the exact conditions of production (same prompt, tools, model version, temperature), and log inputs, outputs, versions and seeds. Because sampling is random, run each case several times or fix the seed, and report the variation.
Scoring has three families. Programmatic checks are the most reliable: exact match, regular expressions, a JSON schema, unit tests passing, a SQL query returning the same rows. Use them whenever the task allows. Human grading is slow and costly but sets the ground truth; use it to build a small gold set and to audit the other scorers. Model grading (LLM-as-judge) scales: give a strong model the input, the output, optionally a reference, and a written rubric, and ask for a score or for a choice between two outputs. Known biases are position bias (the first answer in a pair is favored), verbosity bias (longer answers score higher), and self-preference (a model likes its own style). The remedies are: swap the order and require agreement, control for length, use a rubric with concrete criteria, ask for the reason before the score, use several judges, and above all measure the judge: label 50 to 100 examples by hand and check the agreement (for example Cohen's κ) between the judge and the people before you trust it.
Statistics decides what the numbers mean. For n independent cases with true success rate p, the observed accuracy has standard error \( \sqrt{p(1 - p)/n} \). With n = 500 and p = 0.8 this is 1.8 points, so a 95% interval is about ±3.5 points. To compare two models, use the same cases for both. Only the cases where the models disagree carry information about which is better: let b be the number where only B is right and c where only A is right. The difference in accuracy is \( (b - c)/n \) and its standard error is \( \sqrt{b + c - (b - c)^2/n}\,/n \); McNemar's test or a paired bootstrap give the same conclusion. Pairing is a variance reduction: it removes the item difficulty that both models share. Cases that come in groups (many questions from the same document, many tests from the same repository) are not independent, so standard errors should be clustered by group, or the interval is too narrow.
Sample size follows from the test. To detect a difference \( \delta \) in accuracy (80% power, 5% false-alarm rate) with two independent samples, you need about \( n \approx 15.7\, p(1 - p)/\delta^2 \) cases per model: for \( p = 0.8 \), \( \delta = 0.05 \) this is about 1,000; for \( \delta = 0.02 \) it is about 6,300. This is why differences of one or two points on benchmarks with a few hundred items are usually meaningless. For code and reasoning tasks, pass@k uses n samples per problem with c correct: \( \text{pass@}k = 1 - \binom{n - c}{k} \big/ \binom{n}{k} \), an unbiased estimator. Reporting pass@1 over many samples also reveals reliability; for agents, "all k attempts succeed" (pass^k) is the stricter product measure.
Contamination and overfitting need process rules. Keep a test set that is touched only for final decisions and a separate development set for iteration; each look at the test set leaks a little information. Check for overlap between test items and training data with n-gram matching (13-grams in GPT-3). Prefer tasks whose answers cannot be memorized: freshly written problems, private sets, or execution-based tasks. For public benchmarks, treat the score as a rough signal and give weight to the ones whose creators maintain them and publish error analyses, since audits found a few percent of questions in widely used sets with wrong or ambiguous answers.
For online systems, an A/B test is the final eval. Randomly assign users (or requests) to the old and new system, measure the business metric, and test the difference. Offline metrics (AUC, log loss, calibration for ad models; win rate for chat) are a cheap filter that decides what is worth an A/B test; they do not replace it. Sample size again matters: with a per-user metric of mean 1 and standard deviation 5, detecting a 1% relative change needs about 16 × 25/0.01² = 4 million users per arm. Variance reduction methods (CUPED, which adjusts by pre-experiment behavior) and sequential testing cut this.
Finally, read the failures. Averages hide structure. Sort the failures, label them with the cause (retrieval miss, wrong format, refusal, reasoning error), count the categories, and fix the biggest one; this "error analysis" loop usually beats chasing the aggregate score. Slice the scores by user segment, language, input length and difficulty, since a gain on average can hide a loss on a group that matters.

example: Two versions of an assistant are tested on the same 500 questions. Model A answers 410 correctly (82.0%), model B 417 (83.4%). The difference is 1.4 points. Is B better?
Look at each model alone: the standard error of A is √(0.82 × 0.18/500) = 1.7 points, so its interval is 82.0 ± 3.4. The intervals overlap heavily. A naive test that treats the two samples as independent gives a standard error of the difference of √(0.0172² + 0.0166²) = 2.4 points, so 1.4/2.4 = 0.59 standard errors: no evidence.
Now use the pairing. Count how the models disagree: both right on 386, both wrong on 59, only B right on b = 31, only A right on c = 24. Check: 386 + 24 = 410 for A, 386 + 31 = 417 for B. Only the 55 disagreements matter. McNemar's statistic is \( (b - c)^2/(b + c) = 49/55 = 0.89 \), and with one degree of freedom p = 0.35. The standard error of the paired difference is \( \sqrt{55 - 49/500}\,/500 \) = 1.48 points, so the 95% interval for the difference is 1.4 ± 2.9, which is [−1.5, +4.3] points. We cannot say B is better; we cannot say it is not either.
How many questions would settle it? If the real difference is 1.4 points and about 11% of cases are disagreements, the paired standard error is about \( \sqrt{0.11}/\sqrt{n} = 0.33/\sqrt{n} \). To have 80% power at the 5% level the effect must be 2.8 standard errors, so the standard error must be 0.5 points: \( n = (0.33/0.005)^2 \approx 4{,}400 \) cases. A set of 500 could never have detected it.
A second view, pass@k. A coding problem is sampled n = 10 times and c = 3 pass the tests. pass@1 = 3/10 = 0.30. pass@5 = 1 − C(7, 5)/C(10, 5) = 1 − 21/252 = 0.917. The same model looks weak at pass@1 and strong at pass@5; which number matters depends on whether the product can verify answers (run the tests, pick the one that passes) or must commit to its first answer.
An A/B test sizing: if your current click-through metric per user has mean 1.0 and standard deviation 5.0, to detect a 1% change (0.01) at 80% power you need \( n \approx 16\sigma^2/\Delta^2 = 16 \times 25/0.0001 = 4{,}000{,}000 \) users per arm. If CUPED reduces the variance by 40%, the requirement falls to 2.4 million.
intuition: An eval is a unit test for behavior that cannot be specified exactly: a sample of cases, a way to grade, and an error bar.
The idea is natural because we cannot inspect a model's mind; we can only watch what it does. Watching a few cases is anecdote. Watching a large, representative sample that the model has not seen, with a scoring rule that matches the goal, and with an honest estimate of the noise, is measurement. The statistics is the same as in a poll: a small sample gives a wide margin, and asking the same people twice is better than asking different people.
mistakes: People often think a higher score on a leaderboard means a better model for their task. In fact a benchmark measures its own distribution and scoring rule. A model may rank first on a public benchmark and lose on your data, because of different domain, language, format or contamination. Build an eval from your own traces.
People often think a one- or two-point gain proves an improvement. In fact on a set of a few hundred items, gains of that size are inside the noise (a 500-item accuracy has a 95% interval of about ±3.5 points). Report confidence intervals, use paired comparisons, and increase the number of cases before claiming a win.
People often think LLM judges are objective. In fact they have systematic biases (position, length, self-preference) and can be wrong on the type of cases you care about. They are valid only after you have measured their agreement with human labels on a sample of your own data.
People often think contamination only matters for cheating. In fact it happens by accident when benchmark data spreads over the web and into training sets, and it also happens inside a team, when developers repeatedly tune prompts against the same test cases. A score on cases you have looked at and tuned against is a training score.
friction: Benchmarks saturate and decay. Once top models score above about 90% on a test, the remaining differences are inside the noise or caused by label errors, so a new harder benchmark is needed, and the cycle repeats every year or two. Public test sets leak into training data, and the incentive to rank high encourages optimizing for the benchmark itself (Goodhart's law), so published numbers can overstate real ability.
Offline scores often fail to predict user satisfaction. A model that wins on multiple-choice questions may be worse at long, open-ended tasks, and a model that wins pairwise human votes may be preferred for style and length rather than correctness. Agents and long-horizon tasks are hard to evaluate: the outcome depends on tools, environment state and many steps; runs are expensive and variable, so each configuration needs many repeats. Safety, robustness and rare-failure behavior need red-teaming and targeted sets, because average accuracy hides the tail.
Evals cost real effort and money: building a good dataset takes domain experts, judge calls multiply token costs, and every model change means a rerun. They also go stale: user behavior and the world move, so a static set stops being representative. And there is a limit to what any score can show: with a finite test set you can disprove a claim ("the model is at least 90% accurate") but never prove the model is safe on all inputs.
practice: Start small and real. Collect 50 to 200 real or realistic cases, read every output yourself, and write down what good and bad look like. Turn that into a first scorer: programmatic wherever possible, an LLM judge with a written rubric where not. Check the judge against your own labels on 50 cases. Version the dataset, the prompt, the model and the scorer together, and run the suite automatically on every change (in CI), with a threshold on the key metrics and a report of which cases flipped.
Make the statistics routine. Report each metric with a confidence interval (a bootstrap over cases is easy), compare variants on the same cases with a paired test, run several samples per case when outputs are random, and decide the sample size before the experiment from the smallest difference you care about. Slice results by category and difficulty. Keep a development set for iteration and a held-out set that you look at rarely; refresh it with fresh cases from production, since stale sets reward overfitting.
Close the loop with production. Log real traffic, sample and review it weekly, add each new failure type to the suite, and track online metrics next to the offline ones; when they disagree, believe the online one and find out why. For ad and ranking models, evaluate with log loss, calibration and AUC offline, and then A/B test with variance reduction and guardrail metrics (latency, revenue per request, fairness slices). For public benchmarks, use them to shortlist models, check the date and the contamination notes, and prefer execution-based or recently created tasks.
tradeoffs: Choose programmatic scoring (tests pass, exact match, schema valid) over an LLM judge whenever the task has a checkable answer: it is cheap, deterministic and unbiased. You give up coverage of open-ended qualities (tone, helpfulness). Choose an LLM judge for open-ended outputs when you have validated it against 50 to 100 human labels and accept its cost (about as many tokens as the task itself) and biases. Choose human graders for the gold set, for high-stakes launches and for judge audits, and pay in time and money.
Choose a small, high-quality, hand-curated eval for fast iteration (50 to 300 cases, results in minutes); choose a large eval (thousands of cases) for release decisions and for detecting small differences (a 1 to 2 point gain needs thousands of paired cases). You give up speed with the large set, and you accept noise with the small one, so use the small set to explore and the large one to confirm.
Choose a public benchmark to shortlist and compare general ability across vendors; choose your own task-specific eval to decide what to ship. Public benchmarks are cheap and comparable but contaminated and unrepresentative; your own eval is representative but costs effort and cannot be compared with others.
Choose offline evaluation to filter many candidates cheaply and to run before every merge; choose an online A/B test for the final decision on user impact, which needs days or weeks and millions of users for small effects (about 4 million per arm in the example), and exposes real users to the new version. Use interleaving (mixing the two rankings in one list) when you compare rankers and need far fewer samples (often an order of magnitude).
Choose pass@k with verification when your product can check candidate answers (run tests, validate output) and pass@1 when it must commit to one answer. Choose a pairwise preference setup when absolute scores are hard to define, and absolute rubrics when you need to track a metric over time.
interview: How would you build an eval for a new LLM feature from scratch? || Collect real or realistic inputs (production traces, support tickets) and read outputs to define failure categories. Write programmatic checks where possible, and an LLM-judge rubric for the rest, validated on 50 to 100 human-labeled cases. Fix a held-out split, run in CI with confidence intervals and paired comparisons, slice by category, and feed new production failures back in.
A change improves your benchmark accuracy from 71.0% to 72.4% on 600 questions. Do you ship? || Not on that evidence alone. The standard error of each accuracy is about 1.8 points, and the paired difference needs the number of disagreements to compute; probably the interval includes zero. Run a paired test or bootstrap, enlarge the set, check slices for regressions, and test online if it matters.
What is data contamination and how do you detect and avoid it? || Test items (or close copies) appear in the training data, inflating scores. Detect it with n-gram overlap (13-grams in GPT-3), canary strings, or by checking that performance drops on paraphrased or newly written items. Avoid it with private or fresh test sets, execution-based tasks, and by not tuning on the test set.
What biases does an LLM judge have, and how do you handle them? || Position bias, verbosity bias, self-preference and sensitivity to formatting. Swap the order of pairs and require consistency, control for length, use specific rubrics, prefer a different model family as judge, ask for reasoning before the score, and calibrate against human labels, reporting agreement.
Offline AUC improved by 0.5 points but the A/B test showed no revenue gain. Why might that be? || The offline gain may be within noise or limited to regions that do not affect decisions (the top-ranked items), the model may be worse calibrated so bids shift, training data may be biased from the old policy (feedback loops), the A/B test may be under-powered, or latency and coverage may differ in production. Check calibration, slices, power and the logging policy.
Explain pass@k and when it is the wrong metric. || pass@k is the probability that at least one of k samples is correct, estimated as 1 − C(n − c, k)/C(n, k) from n samples with c correct. It fits tasks where a verifier can pick the right sample. It is the wrong metric when the product must give one answer without verification, where pass@1 (or reliability across repeated tries) is what users experience.
quiz: Why is a paired comparison of two models better than comparing their separate confidence intervals? || Both models see the same items, so item difficulty cancels; only the disagreements carry information. The standard error of the difference is smaller than that from two independent samples.
What is the 95% confidence interval for an accuracy of 80% on 500 items? || The standard error is √(0.8 × 0.2/500) = 1.8 points, so the interval is about ±3.5 points: roughly 76.5% to 83.5%.
Model A gets 410/500 and B gets 417/500, with 31 cases only B right and 24 only A right. What is McNemar's statistic? || (31 − 24)²/(31 + 24) = 49/55 ≈ 0.89, p ≈ 0.35: no significant difference.
With 10 samples per problem and 3 correct, what is pass@5? || 1 − C(7, 5)/C(10, 5) = 1 − 21/252 ≈ 0.917.
When would you choose an A/B test over an offline benchmark, and what is the cost? || When you need to know the effect on real users or business metrics, the final decision before launch. The cost is time (days to weeks), large traffic for small effects (millions of users), and exposure of real users to the candidate.
