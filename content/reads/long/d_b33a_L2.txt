@@ hll
value: HyperLogLog (HLL) counts how many distinct items a stream contains, approximately, using a tiny fixed amount of memory. Counting distinct users, devices, IP addresses or search queries exactly needs a set holding every item seen: a billion 16-byte IDs need at least 16 GB. HyperLogLog answers the same question with about 12 KB and a typical error of under 1%, whether the true answer is a thousand or a billion.
Two further properties make it a workhorse of analytics. First, sketches merge: the sketch of the union of two streams is computed from the two sketches alone, by taking the larger value in each register. So you can store one sketch per day, per campaign or per server, and later combine any subset (a week, a set of campaigns, all servers) without touching raw data. Second, it is fast and streaming: each item costs one hash and one memory update. That is why approx_count_distinct in Spark, BigQuery and Snowflake, Redis's PFCOUNT, Druid and Pinot's distinct-count sketches, and reach metrics in advertising (unique households reached by a campaign) all use HLL or its descendants.
It rests on four lower concepts. Hashing (L3) turns every item into a string of bits that looks random and uniformly distributed. Probability (L4) explains why the longest run of leading zeros in those hashes reveals how many distinct values were seen. Statistics and the central limit theorem (L4) explain why averaging over many registers, with a harmonic mean, shrinks the error to about \( 1.04/\sqrt{m} \). And streaming algorithms (L3) supply the framework: one pass, small memory, provable error.
terms: Cardinality — the number of distinct items in a set or stream.
Register — one small counter (usually 6 bits) in the sketch; m registers in total, m a power of two.
ρ (rho) — the position of the first 1 bit in a hash's remaining bits; ρ = 3 means the bits start 001.
Harmonic mean — the reciprocal of the average of reciprocals; it limits the influence of rare large values.
Relative standard error — the typical size of the error as a fraction of the true count, about \( 1.04/\sqrt{m} \).
Merge (union) — combining two sketches by taking the maximum of each pair of registers.
Linear counting — the estimate m × ln(m/V) used when many registers (V of them) are still zero.
HLL++ — Google's improved variant with a 64-bit hash, bias correction and a sparse mode for small counts.
history: Philippe Flajolet and G. Nigel Martin published "Probabilistic Counting Algorithms for Data Base Applications" in 1985 (FM sketches), introducing the key observation: in random binary strings, seeing a hash that starts with k zeros suggests you have seen about 2^k distinct values. Their method kept bitmaps of observed patterns and averaged several of them. Durand and Flajolet's LogLog (2003) reduced memory by storing only the maximum ρ per register, using log log n bits each, hence the name.
HyperLogLog (Flajolet, Fusy, Gandouet and Meunier, 2007) replaced LogLog's arithmetic mean with a harmonic mean and added corrections for small and large ranges, reaching a standard error of about \( 1.04/\sqrt{m} \), very close to the theoretical optimum for this kind of sketch. Flajolet died in 2011, a few years before the algorithm became standard infrastructure.
Google engineers Heule, Nunkesser and Hall published "HyperLogLog in Practice" (EDBT 2013), describing HLL++: a 64-bit hash to remove the large-range limit, empirical bias correction for medium counts, and a sparse representation that uses far less memory when few items have been seen. HLL++ became the basis of distinct counts in BigQuery and many other systems. Redis added HyperLogLog commands in 2014 (12 KB per key). Yahoo open-sourced the DataSketches library in 2015 (now Apache DataSketches), which offers HLL alongside theta sketches that also support intersections, and Spark, Druid, Pinot and others integrated these sketches for analytics.
how: Start with a hash. Each item (user ID, device ID) is hashed to 64 bits that, for a good hash function, behave like independent fair coin flips. This is what hashing contributes: whatever the input, the output bits are uniform, and the same item always gives the same bits, so duplicates do not change anything.
The core probabilistic idea: in a uniform random bit string, the chance that it starts with exactly k − 1 zeros followed by a one is \( 2^{-k} \). If you have looked at n distinct random strings, the largest such ρ you are likely to have seen is about \( \log_2 n \). Seeing a hash that begins with 20 zeros is a sign you have seen roughly a million distinct items. One register storing the maximum ρ is therefore a (very noisy) estimate of \( \log_2 n \). Duplicates produce identical hashes and cannot raise the maximum, so only distinct items count.
One register is far too noisy, so HLL splits the stream into m buckets. The first p bits of the hash choose a register (\( m = 2^p \), for example p = 14 gives 16,384 registers); the remaining bits give ρ. Each register keeps the largest ρ seen in its bucket. Each bucket sees about n/m distinct items, so each register is an independent, noisy estimate of \( \log_2(n/m) \). Six bits per register suffice because ρ never exceeds 64.
The estimate combines registers with a harmonic mean, the statistics concept at work. Compute \( Z = \sum 2^{-M_j} \) over all registers M_j, then \( E = \alpha_m \times m^2 / Z \), where \( \alpha_m \approx 0.7213 / (1 + 1.079/m) \) corrects a systematic bias (about 0.673 for m = 16 and 0.709 for m = 64). The harmonic mean matters because the 2^M values are skewed: one register that happened to see an unusually long run of zeros would dominate an arithmetic mean, while the harmonic mean limits its weight. By the central-limit behaviour of many independent registers, the relative standard error is about \( 1.04/\sqrt{m} \): 1.6% with 4,096 registers, 0.81% with 16,384, 0.41% with 65,536.
Two corrections handle the extremes. For small cardinalities many registers are still zero and the formula is biased; if E is below 2.5m and V registers are zero, HLL uses linear counting, \( E = m \times \ln(m/V) \), which is accurate when the sketch is mostly empty. HLL++ improves the middle range with an empirical bias table and avoids the large-range problem entirely with a 64-bit hash. It also stores small sketches sparsely, as a list of (register, value) pairs, switching to the dense array only when that is smaller.
Merging is the property that makes HLL so useful in data systems. The register for bucket j holds the maximum ρ among items in that bucket. For the union of two streams, the maximum over the union is the larger of the two maxima. So merge(A, B)[j] = max(A[j], B[j]), and the merged sketch is exactly the sketch you would have built from the combined stream. It is associative and commutative, so sketches can be combined in any order, in parallel, across machines, which fits distributed aggregation perfectly.
What HLL cannot do well is intersection. The size of A ∩ B can be estimated by inclusion–exclusion, |A| + |B| − |A ∪ B|, but the error of each term is relative to the large sets, so a small overlap disappears in the noise. For overlap questions (how many households saw both campaigns?), theta sketches or exact methods on smaller data work better.
Implementations care about a few practical details. Registers are packed: 16,384 six-bit registers fit in 12,288 bytes, and Redis stores exactly this dense form, switching from a sparse encoding as the count grows. Adding an item costs one hash (a fast non-cryptographic hash such as MurmurHash3 or xxHash), one shift to find the register, one count-leading-zeros instruction to find ρ, and one compare-and-store, a few nanoseconds in total, so a single core can absorb hundreds of millions of items per second. Estimating needs one pass over the registers, microseconds. In SQL engines, approx_count_distinct builds one sketch per group per partition, merges the partial sketches during the shuffle, and estimates at the end; the shuffle moves kilobytes per group instead of every distinct ID, which is where most of the speed-up over COUNT(DISTINCT) comes from.

example: Take a toy sketch with m = 16 registers (p = 4). The device ID "device-42" hashes to bits 0110 0001 0110 1101 and so on. The first 4 bits, 0110, choose register 6. The remaining bits start 0001, so the first 1 is at position 4 and ρ = 4. If register 6 holds 3, it becomes 4; if it already holds 5, nothing changes. Seeing "device-42" again produces the same hash and changes nothing.
After a stream, suppose the registers hold 3, 2, 4, 1, 2, 3, 5, 2, 1, 3, 2, 4, 2, 3, 1, 2. Then \( Z = 2^{-3} + 2^{-2} + 2^{-4} + \cdots = 3.656 \). With α₁₆ = 0.673, E = 0.673 × 256 / 3.656 ≈ 47 distinct devices. With only 16 registers the standard error is 1.04/4 = 26%, so the true count is very likely between about 35 and 60. Toy sketches are noisy; real ones are not.
Now realistic sizing. A CTV campaign must report unique households reached per day and over its 30-day flight. With p = 14, each sketch has 16,384 registers of 6 bits: 12 KB, with a standard error of 0.81%. One sketch per campaign per day for 50,000 campaigns is 50,000 × 30 × 12 KB ≈ 18 GB for the month, or much less with sparse encoding for small campaigns. To get flight-to-date reach, merge the campaign's 30 daily sketches by register-wise max: 30 × 16,384 comparisons, microseconds. Summing daily reach would be wrong: if a campaign reaches 2.0 million households each day but mostly the same ones, the 30-day sum says 60 million, while the merged sketch might say 6.4 million ± about 52,000.
Finally, the intersection trap. Two campaigns each reached 10 million households, and their union sketch estimates 19.9 million. Inclusion–exclusion gives an overlap of 10 + 10 − 19.9 = 0.1 million. But the union estimate alone has a standard error of 0.81% × 19.9 million ≈ 161,000, larger than the overlap itself. The overlap estimate could easily be negative. Use theta sketches or an exact join for overlaps.
intuition: If you flip coins in runs and someone tells you the longest run of tails you ever got was 20, you know you must have flipped a lot of runs, roughly a million. HyperLogLog asks thousands of independent people for their longest runs and averages carefully.
The idea is natural once you notice what is rare. You cannot remember every item, but you can remember the most unusual thing you have seen, and how unusual it is tells you how much you have seen. Hashing makes "unusual" a property of random bits rather than of the items, so it works for any kind of data. Splitting into many registers turns one lucky guess into a reliable average, and keeping maxima makes the sketch naturally mergeable, which is exactly what distributed systems need.
mistakes: People often think HyperLogLog stores a sample of the items. It stores only small maxima per register; you cannot list, look up or remove items, and you cannot ask whether one specific user was counted.
People often think distinct counts from sketches can be added across days or groups. Distinct counts are not additive; you must merge the sketches (register-wise max) and estimate from the merged sketch. Adding daily estimates double-counts repeat visitors.
People often think a 1% error on unions means overlaps are also accurate to 1%. Inclusion–exclusion inherits the error of the large sets, so small intersections can be meaningless. Use theta sketches or exact methods for overlap.
People often think sketches from different systems can be merged. Sketches merge only if they use the same hash function, the same precision p and compatible formats; a Spark HLL++ sketch and a Redis HLL cannot simply be combined.
friction: HLL gives approximate answers, which some uses cannot accept: billing, legal reporting and small-number audits need exact counts. The error is relative, so for small counts (a few dozen) the absolute error is small but the relative error and bias corrections matter, and for very large counts 1% can still be hundreds of thousands of units, which must be explained to business users.
Sketch formats are not standardized across systems, which complicates moving sketches between Spark, Druid, BigQuery and Redis; Apache DataSketches helps but is not universal. Deleting individuals is impossible: you cannot remove one user's contribution from a sketch, so privacy deletion requests require rebuilding sketches from raw data that excludes them, which conflicts with keeping only sketches. Intersections and complex set expressions need other sketch types (theta, tuple sketches) with different error behaviour. Finally, choosing precision is a cost trade-off at scale: each extra bit of p doubles memory and cuts error by \( \sqrt{2} \).
practice: Use HLL wherever you need distinct counts over large data and can accept about 1% error: daily active users, unique devices, reach and frequency in advertising, distinct series in monitoring. Store sketches, not counts, in aggregate tables (per day, campaign, region), so any combination can be merged later; this turns reach from a nightly full scan into a cheap merge.
Pick precision from the error you need: \( \text{error} \approx 1.04/\sqrt{m} \), so 1% needs m ≈ 10,800 (use p = 14). Keep the same library and precision everywhere sketches will be merged; Apache DataSketches (available in Spark, Druid, Pinot and Hive) is a good common choice. Use theta sketches when you need intersections or differences, and exact counts when the numbers are small, legally significant or must be audited.
Document the error to users, for example "reach ±1%", and keep raw data long enough to rebuild sketches when deletions or corrections require it.
quiz: Why does a long run of leading zeros in a hash suggest many distinct items? || In uniform random bits, a hash starting with k − 1 zeros then a one has probability \( 2^{-k} \), so seeing one is likely only after about \( 2^k \) distinct values. Duplicates repeat the same hash and add nothing.
How do you merge two HyperLogLog sketches, and why is the result exact? || Take the maximum of each pair of registers. Each register stores the maximum ρ in its bucket, and the maximum over a union equals the larger of the two maxima, so the merged sketch equals the sketch of the combined stream.
What error do you expect with 4,096 registers, and how much memory do they use at 6 bits each? || About 1.04/√4096 = 1.04/64 ≈ 1.6%. Memory is 4,096 × 6 bits = 24,576 bits, about 3 KB.
A campaign reaches 1.5 million households each day for 7 days. Why can't you report 10.5 million as weekly reach? || Reach counts distinct households, and many households are reached on several days, so daily counts overlap. Merge the seven daily sketches and estimate from the merged sketch.
When should you not use HyperLogLog for a distinct count? || When you need exact numbers (billing, audits, small counts), when you need overlaps between sets (use theta sketches or exact joins), or when you must delete individuals from the count without rebuilding from raw data.

@@ cms
value: A count-min sketch estimates how many times each item has appeared in a stream, using a small fixed table of counters instead of one counter per distinct item. With millions or billions of distinct keys (URLs, IP addresses, search queries, ad domains, device IDs), exact per-key counting needs a huge hash map. A count-min sketch with about 14,000 counters (55 KB) can answer "how many times did key x appear?" for any x, with an error of at most 0.1% of the total stream size, 99% of the time. Its answers are never too low, only sometimes too high.
That trade fits many streaming problems. Network devices and observability pipelines find heavy hitters (the IP addresses or customers generating most traffic). Databases use sketches to estimate the frequency of values for query planning. Caches use them to decide which keys are popular enough to keep: the Caffeine cache library's W-TinyLFU policy keeps a count-min sketch of recent access frequencies. Fraud and abuse systems use them to spot sudden bursts from one source. Like HyperLogLog, sketches from different machines can be combined, here by adding their tables cell by cell.
It stands on three lower concepts. Hashing (L3) spreads items over counters so that each item shares its counters with a random, small set of others. Probability (L4) bounds how much those collisions inflate an estimate, and shows why taking the minimum over several independent rows makes large errors unlikely. Streaming algorithms (L3) provide the setting: one pass over unbounded data, memory independent of the number of distinct items.
terms: Frequency — how many times an item has appeared in the stream.
Width (w) — the number of counters in each row; it controls the size of the error.
Depth (d) — the number of rows, each with its own hash function; it controls the probability of a large error.
ε (epsilon) — the error bound as a fraction of the total count N; \( w = \lceil e/\varepsilon \rceil \).
δ (delta) — the probability that the error bound fails; \( d = \lceil \ln(1/\delta) \rceil \).
Point query — asking for the estimated frequency of one item.
Heavy hitter — an item whose frequency exceeds a chosen fraction of the stream, such as 1%.
Conservative update — incrementing only the counters that currently hold the minimum, which reduces overestimation.
Count sketch — a related sketch that adds and subtracts with random signs, giving unbiased estimates.
history: Counting frequent items in streams with little memory is an old problem. Misra and Gries (1982) gave a deterministic algorithm that keeps k counters and finds every item occurring more than N/(k+1) times. In the late 1990s and early 2000s, data streams became a research field of their own, driven by network monitoring and database query optimization: Alon, Matias and Szegedy's 1996 paper on frequency moments founded the area and won the Gödel Prize; Charikar, Chen and Farach-Colton introduced the count sketch in 2002 for finding frequent items.
Graham Cormode and S. Muthukrishnan introduced the count-min sketch in 2004 (journal version in 2005, "An Improved Data Stream Summary: The Count-Min Sketch and its Applications"). Its appeal was simplicity: only additions and a minimum, with clean error bounds in terms of ε and δ, and support for point queries, range queries, inner products and heavy hitters. It spread quickly to network equipment, databases and stream processors.
Later work applied it widely. Conservative update (Estan and Varghese's work on traffic measurement, 2002) reduced overestimation. Spark's DataFrame API offers countMinSketch, RedisBloom provides CMS commands, and Apache DataSketches includes frequent-items sketches. TinyLFU (Einziger, Friedman and Manes, 2015; W-TinyLFU in 2017) used a compact count-min sketch with small counters as a frequency filter for cache admission, adopted by Caffeine and later by other caches.
how: The structure is a table of d rows and w columns of counters, all starting at zero, with d independent hash functions h₁ … h_d, each mapping an item to a column. To record an occurrence of item x (or c occurrences), add c to one counter in each row: counter[i][h_i(x)] for i = 1 … d. To query x, read the same d counters and return the minimum.
Why the minimum? Every counter that x touches contains x's true count plus the counts of whatever other items hashed to the same cell in that row. Counts are never negative, so every counter is at least the true count: the estimate can only be too high. Different rows use independent hashes, so x collides with different items in each row. The row with the fewest or smallest collisions gives the most accurate value, and the minimum picks it automatically.
Probability turns this into a guarantee. Let N be the total count of the stream. In one row, the other items' counts spread over w cells, so the expected extra count in x's cell is at most \( N/w \). By Markov's inequality, the chance that the extra exceeds \( e \times N/w \) (that is, \( \varepsilon N \) when \( w = e/\varepsilon \)) is at most \( 1/e \). The rows are independent, so the chance that all d rows exceed it is at most \( (1/e)^d = \delta \) when \( d = \ln(1/\delta) \). Result: with probability at least \( 1 - \delta \), the estimate is between the true count a and \( a + \varepsilon N \). For ε = 0.001 and δ = 0.01: w = ⌈e/0.001⌉ = 2,719 and d = ⌈ln 100⌉ = 5, so 13,595 counters, about 54 KB with 4-byte counters, regardless of how many distinct items appear.
Notice what the guarantee says and does not say. The error is relative to N, the whole stream, not to the item's own count. For frequent items, an error of 0.1% of N is small compared with their counts; for rare items, the error can be larger than the true count. Count-min sketches are therefore good at answering "is this item frequent, and roughly how frequent?" and bad at precise counts of rare items.
Conservative update improves accuracy cheaply. When adding 1 for x, increment only those of its d counters whose value equals the current minimum. Counters already above the minimum must be inflated by collisions, so incrementing them only makes things worse. This keeps the never-underestimate property and often cuts the error substantially on skewed real data, at the cost of losing the ability to merge sketches by simple addition in all cases and of not supporting decrements.
Finding heavy hitters needs one more piece, because the sketch alone cannot list items. As items stream past, query each item's estimate after updating it, and keep a small heap or map of the items whose estimates exceed a threshold (for example 0.5% of N so far). At the end, the heap holds the candidates; because estimates never undercount, no true heavy hitter is missed, though a few false positives may appear and can be checked exactly in a second pass.
Sketches combine naturally. Two count-min sketches with the same w, d and hash functions can be added cell by cell, giving the sketch of the combined streams; this makes distributed counting easy (one sketch per server or partition, summed centrally). Time windows are handled by keeping one sketch per interval (for example per minute) and summing the ones in the window, or by periodically halving all counters to age out old counts, which is what TinyLFU does.
The count sketch is the close relative worth knowing. Each row also has a random sign function, adds ±c, and the estimate is the median of the signed counters. Its errors cancel on average, giving unbiased estimates with error bounds based on the stream's second moment, which is better for heavy-tailed data, at the cost of possible underestimates.

The domain "ads.example" hashes to column 1 in row 1, column 3 in row 2 and column 2 in row 3. After adding its latest occurrence, those counters read 10, 9 and 7, as in the diagram. Now realistic sizing. A monitoring pipeline sees N = 50 million requests per hour from about 3 million distinct client IP addresses and wants the request count of any IP, plus the heavy hitters above 0.1% of traffic. Choose ε = 0.0002 and δ = 0.001: w = ⌈2.718 / 0.0002⌉ = 13,592 and d = ⌈ln 1000⌉ = 7, giving 95,144 counters, about 380 KB with 4-byte counters. An exact hash map would hold 3 million entries, roughly 100 MB with typical overheads.
Guarantee: every estimate is at most 0.0002 × 50 million = 10,000 requests too high, with probability 99.9%. An IP sending 200,000 requests (0.4%) is estimated between 200,000 and 210,000: good enough to flag it. An IP sending 30 requests might be estimated at several hundred or more, useless for that IP, but irrelevant for heavy-hitter detection. Heavy hitters: the threshold is 0.1% of N = 50,000. Keeping a small heap of IPs whose estimates exceed 50,000 finds every true heavy hitter (estimates never undercount) plus perhaps a handful of near-threshold false positives, which a second exact count over just those IPs removes.
Merging: 40 ingest servers each keep their own sketch for the hour; adding the 40 tables cell by cell gives the global sketch, 40 × 380 KB of data moved instead of 40 hash maps of millions of entries.
intuition: Several tally sheets with only a few hundred boxes each, where every name is assigned a random box on each sheet; boxes get shared, so tallies only grow too big, and the smallest of a name's tallies is the most trustworthy.
The design is natural once you accept collisions instead of fighting them. With fewer counters than items, sharing is unavoidable, and shared counters can only over-count. Using several independent sheets means a given item is unlikely to share a crowded box on every one, so the minimum filters out most of the damage. The guarantee is relative to the whole stream, which matches what people usually need to know: which items matter at the scale of the total, not exact counts of everything.
mistakes: People often think a count-min sketch gives accurate counts for every item. Its error bound is a fraction of the whole stream, so frequent items are estimated well and rare items can be badly overestimated, sometimes many times their true count.
People often think the sketch can list the most frequent items. It answers queries for items you name; finding heavy hitters needs an extra structure (such as a heap of candidates) maintained while the stream passes.
People often think estimates may be too low as well as too high. With non-negative updates, count-min estimates are never below the true count. Underestimates indicate deletions, decrements or a bug; count sketches, not count-min, can underestimate.
People often think any two sketches can be added together. Only sketches with the same width, depth and hash functions combine, and conservative-update sketches are not exactly mergeable by addition in all cases.
friction: The error grows with total stream size, so long-running sketches become less precise unless they are windowed or decayed, which adds design choices (per-interval sketches, halving, sliding windows). Rare items are effectively invisible behind collision noise, which matters for long-tail analysis. Choosing ε and δ requires knowing roughly how large N will be and how small a frequency you care about.
Adversarial inputs can hurt: someone who knows the hash functions can craft keys that collide with a target and inflate its count, so sketches used for abuse detection need secret seeds. Deletions and negative updates break the never-underestimate guarantee. And in many practical systems, simpler exact approaches (a hash map with a top-k cutoff, or Misra–Gries counters) are good enough and easier to explain, so the sketch's benefits must justify its approximations.
practice: Use a count-min sketch when you need approximate frequencies or heavy hitters over high-volume streams with many distinct keys and limited memory: per-IP or per-customer traffic, popular queries or URLs, cache admission decisions, early detection of bursts. Size it from your accuracy needs: \( w = \lceil e/\varepsilon \rceil \) and \( d = \lceil \ln(1/\delta) \rceil \), with ε chosen relative to the smallest frequency you care about as a fraction of N.
Pair it with a candidate heap for heavy hitters, and confirm top items exactly when the numbers will be reported. Use conservative update when you do not need merging by addition, and per-interval sketches or periodic halving for time windows. Use library implementations (Spark's countMinSketch, RedisBloom, Apache DataSketches' frequent-items sketches) and secret hash seeds for anything exposed to adversaries.
quiz: Why can a count-min estimate never be lower than the true count? || Each of the item's counters holds its true count plus the non-negative counts of colliding items. The minimum of numbers that are all at least the true count is also at least the true count.
Why does taking the minimum over several rows make large errors unlikely? || Each row has independent hashes, so the item collides with different items in each row. A large overcount must happen in every row at once, which has probability at most (1/e)^d.
What width and depth give an error of at most 0.5% of the stream with 99% confidence, and how many counters is that? || \( w = \lceil e/0.005 \rceil \) = 544 and \( d = \lceil \ln 100 \rceil \) = 5, so 2,720 counters.
A stream has N = 10 million events and the sketch has ε = 0.001. Is an estimate of 4,200 for a rare item trustworthy? || No. The error bound is 0.001 × 10 million = 10,000, larger than the estimate, so the item's true count could be anywhere from 0 to 4,200.
When would you choose an exact hash map or Misra–Gries counters instead of a count-min sketch? || When the number of distinct keys fits comfortably in memory, when you need exact counts or explainable results, or when you only need the top-k items, where Misra–Gries gives deterministic guarantees with k counters.

@@ eviction
value: A cache keeps copies of data in a faster, smaller place: CPU caches in front of DRAM, the operating system's page cache in front of disk, Redis in front of a database, a CDN edge in front of an origin server, a browser cache in front of the network. Because the fast place is smaller than the data behind it, it fills up, and every new item forces a decision: which existing item to throw away? That decision is the eviction policy, and it decides the cache's hit ratio, the fraction of requests served from the fast place.
Small changes in hit ratio have large effects. If a cache hit costs 0.5 ms and a miss costs 20 ms (a database query), raising the hit ratio from 90% to 95% halves the misses, cuts the average latency from about 2.45 ms to about 1.48 ms, and halves the load on the database behind it. Better eviction is often the cheapest way to get that: no new hardware, just better choices about what to keep.
It stands on four lower concepts. The memory hierarchy (L3) is the reason caches exist and the source of the speed gaps that make hit ratio matter. Page replacement in operating systems (L3) is the classic eviction problem, where LRU, CLOCK and the optimal policy were first studied. Hash tables (L3) make cache lookups O(1), and LRU itself is a hash map plus a linked list. Power laws (L4) describe real access patterns: a few keys get most requests (Zipf-like skew), which is why a small cache can serve most traffic, and which eviction policies try to exploit.
terms: Hit ratio — the fraction of requests served from the cache; miss ratio is one minus this.
Working set — the set of items a workload actively uses over a period.
LRU (least recently used) — evict the item whose last access is oldest.
LFU (least frequently used) — evict the item with the fewest accesses, usually with aging.
CLOCK — an approximation of LRU using one reference bit per item and a circular pointer.
Belady's OPT — the optimal offline policy: evict the item whose next use is furthest in the future.
Scan — a long run of one-time accesses (a backup, a batch job) that can flush useful items from a cache.
Admission policy — a rule deciding whether a new item should enter the cache at all.
One-hit wonder — an item accessed once and never again; caching it is pure waste.
history: Eviction began with virtual memory. The Atlas computer (early 1960s) needed to choose which page to move from core memory to drum. László Bélády showed in 1966 that the optimal policy evicts the page used furthest in the future (OPT), which needs knowledge of the future but gives a bound to compare against. LRU became the practical standard, and Fernando Corbató's CLOCK algorithm (described for Multics in 1968–1969) approximated it cheaply with reference bits. Peter Denning's working-set model (1968) explained thrashing.
Databases and file systems found LRU's weaknesses with scans and mixed workloads. LRU-K (O'Neil, O'Neil and Weikum, 1993) used the time of the K-th most recent access; 2Q (Johnson and Shasha, 1994) put new items on probation before promoting them; LIRS (Jiang and Zhang, 2002) used reuse distance; ARC (Megiddo and Modha at IBM, 2003) balanced recency and frequency adaptively and became famous through ZFS. Operating systems adopted variants: Linux's page cache uses active and inactive lists, and the multi-generational LRU (MGLRU) merged in Linux 6.1 (2022).
Web-scale caches added new ideas. Redis implemented approximated LRU by sampling (2010s) and added LFU with logarithmic counters in version 4.0 (2017). TinyLFU and W-TinyLFU (Einziger, Friedman and Manes, 2015–2017) used a compact frequency sketch to admit only items more popular than the one they would replace; the Java library Caffeine made it widely used. Analyses of CDN traffic showed that a large share of objects are one-hit wonders. Recent research found that simple FIFO-based designs can match or beat LRU: S3-FIFO (Yang and others, SOSP 2023) and SIEVE (NSDI 2024), which are easier to make fast and scalable.
how: Start with why eviction matters, which comes from the memory hierarchy: each level is roughly an order of magnitude or more faster and smaller than the next (L1 cache about 1 ns, DRAM about 100 ns, NVMe about 100 µs, a network round trip within a data centre about 0.5 ms, a database query milliseconds). The average access time is h × t_hit + (1 − h) × t_miss, and because t_miss is so much larger, the miss ratio dominates. A policy's job is to keep the items most likely to be used soon.
The benchmark is Bélády's OPT: evict the item whose next use is furthest in the future. It is impossible online, since it needs the future, but it shows how far a real policy is from the best possible hit ratio on a recorded trace. Real policies guess the future from the past using two signals: recency (items used recently tend to be used again soon) and frequency (items used often tend to be used again).
LRU uses recency, and hash tables make it O(1). Keep a hash map from key to node, and a doubly linked list ordered by last access. On a hit, look up the node through the hash map and move it to the head of the list. On a miss, insert the new item at the head and, if the cache is full, remove the item at the tail and delete it from the map. Every operation is constant time, but every hit modifies the list, which requires a lock in multi-threaded caches and makes very high-throughput caches hard to scale. CLOCK, the page-replacement solution, avoids this: items sit in a circular buffer with a reference bit set on access; to evict, a hand sweeps around clearing set bits and evicts the first item whose bit is already clear. Hits only set a bit, which is cheap and needs no lock.
Power laws explain both why caching works and where LRU fails. Requests in web, database and CDN workloads often follow a Zipf-like distribution: the k-th most popular item gets a share proportional to \( 1/k^{\alpha} \), with α near 1. Under Zipf with α = 1 and a million items, the top 10% of items receive about 84% of requests, so a cache holding the right 10% achieves an 84% hit ratio. LRU approximates "the right 10%" by recency, which works for steady skew but fails when a scan of one-time items (a backup, a crawler, a batch export) passes through: each new item enters at the head and pushes popular items out, even though the new items will never be used again.
Frequency-aware and scan-resistant designs fix this. LFU counts accesses, but plain counts never forget, so a formerly popular item stays forever; practical LFU uses aging, such as Redis's 8-bit logarithmic counters that decay over time. 2Q and S3-FIFO put new items in a small probationary FIFO queue (about 10% of the space); items accessed again while on probation move to the main queue, while one-hit wonders fall out quickly without disturbing the main queue. ARC keeps two LRU lists, one for items seen once and one for items seen at least twice, plus "ghost" lists of recently evicted keys, and adapts the space split between them based on which ghost list gets hits. W-TinyLFU adds an admission filter: a count-min sketch with small counters estimates the frequency of each key, and a new item replaces the eviction victim only if it is estimated to be more popular.
Distributed and approximate implementations trade exactness for speed. Redis does not keep a global LRU list; when it needs memory, it samples a few keys (the maxmemory-samples setting, 5 by default) and evicts the best candidate among them and a small pool of earlier candidates, which closely approximates LRU at a fraction of the cost. CDNs combine eviction with admission ("cache only on the second request") because many objects are requested once. CPU caches implement pseudo-LRU in hardware with a few bits per set.
Finally, eviction interacts with expiry and size. Time-to-live (TTL) expiry removes stale items regardless of popularity; size-aware policies weigh hit value against object size, because one large object can occupy the space of a thousand small ones; and cost-aware policies prefer to keep items that are expensive to recompute.

example: A product-catalog service has 1 million items with Zipf-like popularity (α ≈ 1), a database lookup costing 20 ms and a Redis cache lookup costing 0.5 ms. Memory allows caching 100,000 items, 10% of the catalog.
Ideal steady state: if the cache holds the 100,000 most popular items, the hit ratio is about H(100,000) / H(1,000,000) ≈ 12.09 / 14.39 ≈ 84%, where H(n) is the n-th harmonic number. Average latency = 0.84 × 0.5 + 0.16 × 20 = 3.62 ms, and the database sees 16% of requests. LRU on a steady Zipf workload gets close to this, typically within a few percentage points.
Now a nightly export job reads all 1 million items once, in order, through the same cache. With LRU, each export read inserts an item at the head and pushes out the tail. After 100,000 export reads, every popular item has been evicted. For the next several minutes, the hit ratio for real users collapses toward the probability of hitting items the export just loaded (about 10% of traffic, by chance), so roughly 90% of requests go to the database: average latency rises to about 18 ms and the database receives over five times its normal load.
With S3-FIFO (or 2Q, ARC or W-TinyLFU), export items enter the small probationary queue (10,000 slots). They are not accessed again, so they are evicted from probation without ever touching the main queue that holds the popular items. The users' hit ratio stays near 84% throughout the export. The same memory and the same data give a 5× difference in database load, entirely from the eviction policy.
Last, the value of a few points. If a better admission filter raises the steady hit ratio from 84% to 90%, average latency falls from 3.62 ms to 2.45 ms, and database traffic falls from 16% to 10% of requests, a 37% reduction, without adding memory.
intuition: A small desk next to a large library: you keep the books you will need soon on the desk, and the art is guessing which ones, using what you used recently and what you use often, without letting a one-time stack of reference books push your daily tools off the desk.
The policy space follows from two simple predictions about the future. Recency says the near future looks like the recent past; frequency says popular things stay popular. Each fails in a recognizable way (recency falls for scans, frequency clings to yesterday's favourites), so good modern policies combine them: a probation area that newcomers must pass through, and a memory of how often keys have been seen. The optimal policy, knowing the future, reminds us that every policy is only a forecast.
mistakes: People often think LRU is always the best practical policy. LRU is simple and often good, but it is not scan-resistant and keeps one-hit wonders; on many real traces, 2Q, ARC, W-TinyLFU or S3-FIFO achieve noticeably higher hit ratios.
People often think a bigger cache is the only way to raise the hit ratio. Admission and eviction choices can raise hit ratio substantially for the same memory, especially under scans or many one-time items.
People often think LFU means counting forever. Without aging, LFU keeps items that were popular long ago; practical LFU variants decay counts or use windows.
People often think hit ratio alone measures cache quality. Item sizes and miss costs differ: missing one expensive or large object can cost more than missing many cheap ones. Byte hit ratio and cost-weighted metrics matter too.
friction: Workloads change over time (daily cycles, launches, batch jobs), so a policy tuned for one pattern may suffer under another. Exact LRU needs list updates on every hit, which limits concurrency in multi-threaded caches; approximations (CLOCK, sampling, FIFO variants) scale better but complicate reasoning. Frequency tracking costs memory and needs aging parameters.
Distributed caches add problems: hot keys overload one shard, consistent hashing moves keys during resizing (causing temporary misses), and cache stampedes happen when a popular key expires and many requests rebuild it at once. Invalidation, keeping cached data consistent with the source, is often harder than eviction. And evaluating policies needs real traces; synthetic Zipf workloads miss the scans, bursts and correlations that decide real performance.
practice: Measure before tuning: record hit ratio, byte hit ratio, eviction rate and miss latency, and if possible capture an access trace to simulate policies offline (libCacheSim and similar tools make this easy). Start with a scan-resistant policy where available: Caffeine's W-TinyLFU for Java, S3-FIFO or SIEVE implementations, Redis's allkeys-lfu for skewed workloads, or allkeys-lru when recency dominates.
Protect caches from scans and one-time traffic: route batch jobs around the cache, use admission rules (cache on second access) for CDNs and large objects, and set TTLs that match data freshness needs. Prevent stampedes with request coalescing and jittered expiry. For distributed caches, watch for hot keys and replicate or locally cache them.
Size the cache from the hit-ratio curve, not a guess: plot hit ratio against cache size from your trace; the knee of the curve shows where extra memory stops paying.
quiz: How does an LRU cache achieve O(1) get and put? || A hash map finds a key's node directly, and a doubly linked list ordered by recency lets you move a node to the head or remove the tail in constant time.
Why does a one-time scan hurt LRU but not S3-FIFO or 2Q? || Under LRU every scanned item enters at the most-recent end and pushes popular items out. Scan-resistant designs put new items in a small probationary queue, and items not reused there are evicted before reaching the main cache.
A cache hit costs 1 ms and a miss 30 ms. What is the average latency at 80% and at 90% hit ratio? || 0.8 × 1 + 0.2 × 30 = 6.8 ms at 80%, and 0.9 × 1 + 0.1 × 30 = 3.9 ms at 90%: ten more points of hit ratio cut latency by about 43%.
Why can't real systems use Bélády's optimal policy, and why is it still useful? || It requires knowing which item will be used furthest in the future. It is useful as an upper bound on hit ratio for a recorded trace, showing how much room a real policy leaves.
When would you choose exact LRU over an approximate policy like CLOCK or Redis-style sampling? || When the cache is small or single-threaded and exact behaviour matters, for example in tests or embedded code. At high concurrency and large sizes, approximations avoid lock contention and memory overhead with very similar hit ratios.
