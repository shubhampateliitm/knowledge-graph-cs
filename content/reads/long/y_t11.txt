@@+ dimmodel
tradeoffs: Choose a star schema when many analysts and dashboards ask the same "measure by context" questions and numbers must agree across teams; choose one wide denormalized table (or a real-time OLAP store such as Pinot or ClickHouse) when one serving path needs sub-second answers and the OLAP engine joins poorly. A star keeps a campaign name in one 50,000-row table, so a rename touches 50,000 rows; a wide table repeats it on 2 billion rows a day, so a rename is a rewrite of billions. The wide table is often 2 to 5 times faster to scan because it has no join. You give up one source of truth with the wide table, and you give up some query speed with the star.
Choose a star (denormalized dimensions) over a snowflake (normalized sub-dimensions) almost always: the extra tables in a snowflake save a few megabytes in dimensions that are already tiny, and cost an extra join in every query and a harder model for analysts. Choose a snowflake only when a sub-dimension is shared and changes on its own schedule, or when a hierarchy is huge (millions of products under thousands of brands) and must be maintained by a separate team. You give up simplicity for the maintenance benefit.
Choose Kimball (marts built from business processes, joined by conformed dimensions) when you need first reports in weeks and teams can agree on shared dimensions; choose Inmon (a normalized enterprise warehouse first, marts later) or a data-vault style raw layer when many source systems change often and you need full history and auditability before anyone decides the reports. Kimball is faster to deliver but fails quietly if two teams build "customer" differently; Inmon is slower by months and needs a modeling team. In a lakehouse the two are often combined: a raw/silver layer for fidelity and a Kimball star in gold.
Choose SCD type 2 for attributes where history must not change (sales region, account owner, price tier) and type 1 for corrections (fixing a typo in a name). Type 2 multiplies dimension rows by the number of changes: a dimension with 10 million customers changing monthly can reach 100 million rows in a year, and every fact load needs a validity-date lookup. Use type 1 when nobody will ever ask "what was true then", and accept that old reports shift when the attribute changes.
interview: Your daily dashboard sums daily unique households and the number is 2.6 times the true monthly reach. What happened and how do you fix the model? || Distinct counts are non-additive: households seen on several days are counted every day. Store a HyperLogLog (or theta) sketch per campaign per day in the aggregate table, and merge sketches for the month; error is about 1 to 2% for a few kilobytes per sketch. Where exactness matters for billing, compute distinct counts from the event-grain fact.
A late-arriving impression references a campaign whose region changed three days ago. Which dimension row should it point to? || The row that was valid at the event time, not at load time. The loader looks up the dimension by natural key and the event timestamp against valid_from and valid_to. If the dimension row has not arrived yet, insert a placeholder (inferred) member and fix it when the real row loads, so facts never drop or point to unknown keys.
Why not just use one big table in Databricks or ClickHouse? When would you? || One big table is right for a specific serving path where joins are slow or unsupported and latency is tight. It is wrong as the only model: renames rewrite billions of rows, and each team copies its own definitions of metrics. Build the wide table from the star as a derived output, so the star stays the single source of truth.
Two teams own the impression fact and the conversion fact. How do you let a marketer compare them by campaign and month? || Conformed dimensions: both facts use the same dim_campaign and dim_date with the same keys and meanings, so the query aggregates each fact to the shared grain separately and then joins the results (drill-across). Joining the two facts directly would multiply rows and double-count; the bus matrix is the planning tool that makes the sharing explicit.
How do you handle many-to-many, such as a household with several segments, without breaking the star? || Use a bridge table between the fact (or dimension) and the multi-valued dimension, with a weighting factor so sums stay correct when an impression belongs to several segments. The cost is an extra join and a risk of double-counting if the weight is forgotten, so for heavy use teams often precompute a segment-level aggregate instead.
@@+ blockchain
tradeoffs: Choose a blockchain only when several parties that do not trust each other, and will not accept one operator, must share a record none can change alone. Choose an ordinary database with an append-only audit log and a published hash chain (the Certificate Transparency design) when one party is trusted or can be made accountable. A Postgres log handles 10,000 to 50,000 writes per second at a cost of cents per million; Bitcoin handles about 7 per second and Ethereum mainnet about 15 to 30, at fees that can reach dollars per transaction. You give up throughput, latency and privacy for the removal of a trusted operator, and most business cases do not need that.
Choose proof of work (Bitcoin) when you need an open network with a simple security argument tied to real-world cost, and accept about 10 minutes per block and an energy bill near that of a medium-sized country. Choose proof of stake (Ethereum) when you want finality in about 13 minutes and roughly 99.9% less energy, and accept a security argument based on slashing and on the concentration of staked value. Choose a permissioned BFT chain (a PBFT-family protocol such as Hyperledger Fabric's ordering) when the 5 to 50 participants are known and can run validators: you get finality in 1 to 2 seconds and thousands of transactions per second, but you give up open participation, and a coalition of one third of validators can halt or one more than two thirds can rewrite it.
Choose layer 1 only for settlement, and choose rollups (optimistic, with a 7-day challenge window, or ZK, with proofs in minutes to hours) when you need a hundred times more throughput at cents per transaction. Rollups inherit layer-1 security but add sequencer centralization, bridge risk and proving cost; ZK rollups cost heavy proving compute (a GPU-hours-per-batch scale) and optimistic ones cost long withdrawal delays. You give up simplicity and some trust assumptions for scale.
Choose on-chain storage only for tiny, high-value state (balances, hashes, ownership). Store documents off-chain and put only a 32-byte Merkle root on-chain: storing 1 MB directly on Ethereum would cost thousands of dollars, while a hash costs cents. You give up automatic availability (the data must be hosted and pinned elsewhere) for cost, and you must make sure the off-chain data matches the hash.
interview: A bank consortium proposes a blockchain for shared trade finance records. How do you decide? || Ask who the adversary is and who runs the operator. If a trusted or regulated third party exists, a database with signed, hash-chained entries gives tamper evidence at a hundredth of the cost. A chain makes sense only if no party will accept another as operator, and then a permissioned BFT network (known validators, finality in seconds) beats a public chain on throughput and privacy.
What does "6 confirmations" actually protect against, and what could still go wrong? || Each block makes a reorganization exponentially harder: with an attacker holding 10% of hash power, the chance of catching up from 6 blocks behind is around 0.02%. It does not protect against a 51% attacker, against double-spend on small chains with cheap hash rental, or against bugs in the contract and exchange layers, which are the real source of most losses.
Why can't you just raise the block size to get more throughput? || Bigger blocks raise bandwidth and storage needs for every full node, so fewer people can verify, and propagation delay rises, which increases orphaned blocks and favors large miners. Scaling is instead done by moving execution off layer 1 (rollups) and sampling data availability, so verification stays cheap.
How do light clients trust a chain without downloading it? || They download only block headers (80 bytes each in Bitcoin) and ask for a Merkle proof of a transaction: about log2(n) hashes, so around 12 hashes (384 bytes) for 4,000 transactions in a block. They trust that the longest chain of headers reflects honest majority work or stake; they do not validate rules themselves, so they depend on at least one honest full node.
Where do smart-contract systems fail in practice? || Mostly in code and incentives: reentrancy and arithmetic bugs, oracle manipulation (feeding a wrong price), and bridge key compromises, which have lost billions. Mitigations are audits plus formal verification of critical invariants, rate limits and timelocks on admin keys, and keeping contract logic small. Immutability makes each bug permanent unless an upgrade path was designed in.
@@+ mediastream
tradeoffs: Choose H.264 when you must reach every device including old TVs and want cheap hardware decode and fast encoding; choose HEVC or AV1 when the audience is large enough that bandwidth savings outweigh encoding cost. HEVC saves about 30 to 40% bitrate over H.264 at equal quality, AV1 about 40 to 50%, but AV1 software encoding can take 10 to 50 times longer, and hardware decode is missing on devices older than about 2020. At 100 million viewing hours a month and 4 Mbit/s average, a 30% saving is about 50 PB a month less egress, which pays for the extra encoding many times; for a catalog with a few thousand views per title it does not.
Choose per-title or content-aware encoding (a bitrate ladder fitted to each video's complexity) for a stable catalog watched many times: it saves 20 to 30% bitrate on average, and a cartoon may need 1.5 Mbit/s where a football match needs 6. Choose a fixed ladder for live and user-generated content, where you cannot afford a multi-pass analysis before publishing. You give up the encoding time and compute (3 to 10 times) for the saving.
Choose longer segments (6 to 10 seconds) when you care about cache efficiency, fewer requests and better compression (each segment starts with a keyframe); choose short segments (2 seconds) or low-latency chunked CMAF (parts of 200 to 500 ms) when live latency matters. A 6-second segment with a 3-segment buffer means about 18 to 30 seconds behind live; low-latency modes get to 3 to 5 seconds but raise request rates 5 to 10 times and make adaptive-bitrate decisions noisier. You give up latency or efficiency; there is no setting that gives both.
Choose a larger player buffer (30 to 60 seconds) for on-demand video on unstable mobile networks, and a small buffer (5 to 10 seconds) for live and for memory-limited TVs. A big buffer cuts rebuffering by a large factor but increases start-up time if the player waits to fill it, wastes bandwidth when the viewer leaves, and delays switching to a better rendition. Most players start at a low rendition, play after 2 to 4 seconds of data, and then grow the buffer.
interview: Estimate the monthly CDN egress for a service with 5 million viewers each watching 20 hours a month. How would you reduce it? || 5 million × 20 hours × 3,600 s × average 3 Mbit/s ÷ 8 ≈ 40 PB a month. Reduce with a better codec on devices that support it (30 to 40% off), a per-title ladder (20 to 30%), caps on the top rendition for small screens, and a multi-tier CDN with high cache hit ratios; at this size, negotiate or use your own edge.
Users on one TV model complain about buffering, but average QoE looks fine. How do you find the cause? || Averages hide segments, so slice rebuffering ratio, start-up time and bitrate by device model, OS version, ISP, CDN and rendition. Likely causes are an unsupported codec profile that forces software decode, a bad ABR heuristic on that firmware, or a CDN path problem for one ISP. Reproduce with a test device and compare manifests and segment timings.
Why not send one high-quality file and let the client downscale? || Bandwidth: a client on 4 Mbit/s cannot receive a 15 Mbit/s stream, so playback stalls. Adaptive bitrate keeps several renditions (for example 240p to 4K) in short segments so the player can pick per segment from measured throughput and buffer level, trading storage (about 2 to 3 times the top rendition) for smooth playback.
Why does a live stream sit 20 to 40 seconds behind the event and how would you cut it to 5 seconds? || Delay comes from encoding and packaging (2 to 6 s), segment duration times buffered segments (often 3 × 6 s), CDN propagation and player buffering. Use shorter segments, chunked CMAF with partial segments, HTTP chunked transfer, a smaller player buffer with careful ABR, and a CDN that supports low-latency; expect higher origin load and more rebuffering risk.
What does VMAF measure and where does it mislead? || It predicts perceived quality by combining several visual metrics learned from human ratings, on a 0 to 100 scale; 93 is good for streaming and a difference under 1 point is not noticeable. It misleads for content far from its training (animation, screen content), for HDR, and it ignores audio and stalls, so combine it with rebuffering ratio and start-up time.
@@+ contentid
tradeoffs: Choose audio fingerprinting when the content has a clear soundtrack and you can capture audio (a smart TV or phone microphone); choose video (frame) fingerprinting or perceptual hashing when the content is silent, music-heavy or has localized audio, as with ads and sports. Audio fingerprints are tiny (about 1 KB for 10 seconds) and robust to noise, but fail on muted TVs and on the same broadcast with different commentary; frame hashes need screen access (an ACR client on the TV) and are heavier on a CPU, yet survive muting. You give up coverage with one and device cost with the other.
Choose exact lookup (hash of a quantized fingerprint into a table) when you need low latency and the set of references is large: a 10 billion-entry hash index returns in under 5 ms. Choose locality-sensitive hashing or approximate nearest neighbor search (product-quantized vector indexes) when fingerprints are noisy and you must find close, not equal, matches. LSH reaches 95 to 99% recall with 5 to 20 times more index memory than a single exact table; exact lookup is cheaper but misses any clip with a bit flipped by compression. You give up memory for recall.
Choose a high match threshold when a false positive is costly (a copyright takedown, an ad billed as shown) and a low threshold when a miss is costly (finding any match for a rare piece of content). Moving a threshold from 0.9 to 0.95 may cut false matches from 1 in 10,000 to 1 in a million but lose 5 to 10% of true matches in noisy conditions. Report precision and recall separately, and use a second verification stage (more samples over time) to get both.
Choose client-side fingerprinting (the device sends a few hundred bytes every second or so) over sending raw audio or video to the cloud for privacy and bandwidth: 300 bytes at 1 per second is about 26 MB per device-day against hundreds of gigabytes for raw frames. You give up flexibility, because changing the algorithm means updating every client, and you give up some accuracy because the client has less compute than a server.
interview: A user pauses live TV for 10 minutes. How does your content recognition still work? || Matching must be by content offset, not wall-clock time. The service holds a reference timeline for each channel and returns the matched position; fingerprints from delayed playback match earlier reference positions, so the system can tell live from time-shifted viewing. The extra index time window costs memory, and rights rules may differ for delayed viewing.
Your match rate on a channel drops from 92% to 40% overnight. What do you check? || First the reference feed (a broken ingest or a new channel logo or aspect change); then the client version (a changed sampling algorithm), a new overlay such as score tickers, and time sync. Alert on per-channel match rate, compare against the schedule, and keep versioned fingerprints so you can re-run matching on stored client data once references are fixed.
How do you keep false positives from poisoning viewership data used for billing? || Require multiple consecutive matches before declaring an identification, keep a minimum confidence, and store raw match scores. Run offline audits against labelled ground truth per content type and channel. Treat low-confidence events as unknown instead of guessing, and design downstream aggregates to tolerate late corrections.
What are the privacy issues of ACR and how do you design around them? || The device effectively reports what a household watches. Use opt-in consent, regional rules (GDPR, CCPA, DPDP), on-device fingerprinting so no raw frames leave, short retention, hashed device IDs, and deletion workflows. Avoid capturing content during sensitive inputs such as an HDMI-connected camera or private apps.
Why not use embedded watermarks instead of fingerprints? || Watermarks give exact, cheap identification, but only for content you control at production or distribution. Fingerprints work on any existing content without modification. Many systems use both: watermark where possible, fingerprint as the general fallback.
@@+ adtech
tradeoffs: Choose open-exchange programmatic buying when you need scale and reach at the lowest cost per thousand impressions; choose private marketplaces or programmatic guaranteed deals when brand safety, premium inventory and predictable delivery matter more. Open exchange often has CPMs of $1 to $5 for CTV with 30 to 50% lost to the supply chain (SSP, exchange, DSP, data fees), plus more invalid traffic; private deals cost 20 to 100% more but cut intermediaries and fraud. You give up price or control; fixed-price guaranteed deals also give up the efficiency of real-time bidding.
Choose real-time bidding with a tight deadline (100 to 150 ms in total, 30 to 80 ms inside a DSP) and in-memory lookups when the value of one impression varies a lot by user and context; choose batch-planned buying (precomputed target lists, daily budgets split by segment) when the signals are simple. Real-time decisions can lift return by 20 to 50% over static targeting, but need a serving system sized for 1 to 10 million requests per second, and most bid requests (95% or more) are lost; batch is cheaper and simpler but cannot react to context.
Choose first-party and consented identifiers (logins, hashed emails, CTV device IDs) and contextual targeting as cookies disappear; choose probabilistic or modelled audiences when scale matters more than precision. Deterministic matches are precise but cover perhaps 20 to 40% of traffic; modelled audiences cover 80% or more with 70 to 90% accuracy. Cleaner data costs reach, and wider reach costs measurement quality and regulatory risk.
Choose last-touch or click-based attribution when you want cheap, quick reporting; choose incrementality (holdout or geo experiments) when you need to know what the ad caused. Last-touch can credit an ad with 2 to 5 times its true effect because the people who clicked were already likely to buy; a lift test needs 1 to 2 weeks and thousands of conversions per arm and withholds ads from a control group. You give up speed and some revenue in the test for the truth.
interview: Walk through what must happen inside 100 ms when a bid request arrives, and what you cut first when you are too slow. || Parse and validate the request (1 to 2 ms), look up audience and frequency in memory or a key-value store (5 to 10 ms), score value with a model (5 to 20 ms), apply pacing and shading, return the bid. Under pressure, drop low-value requests early (prefiltering by supply path), use cached features, shrink the model or run it on a subset, and set hard internal timeouts so a late bid is skipped instead of timing out the exchange.
Impressions in your system and in the SSP's report differ by 8%. Is that a bug? || Some difference is normal: different counting points (bid won, ad served, beacon fired, viewable), timeouts, bot filtering and clock cut-offs. Over 5 to 10% needs a reconciliation: compare by hour, by creative and by device, check pixel loss and duplicate firing, and agree contractually which source bills. Make pipelines idempotent so replays do not double-count.
How would you detect and limit ad fraud in an open exchange? || Use ads.txt and sellers.json to verify supply paths, filter known data-center and non-human traffic, check for impossible patterns (too many impressions per device, wrong app and device pairs), and compare viewability and conversions by seller. Pay only for verified paths, drop low-quality sellers, and use post-bid verification vendors; remember that filtering has false positives that cost reach.
Why do campaigns underdeliver even with budget left? || Targeting is too narrow for available supply, bids are below clearing prices, pacing is throttled by a forecast error, frequency caps and brand-safety rules remove inventory, or the identifier match rate is low. Diagnose with a funnel: requests seen, eligible, bid, won, rendered; the stage with the largest loss tells you whether to widen targeting, raise bids or fix the pipeline.
How do you measure whether a CTV campaign increased sales? || Randomize at the household level, with a holdout that is not shown the ad, and compare purchases in matched clean-room data; or run geo experiments when household matching is poor. Report incremental lift with confidence intervals, size the test for the expected effect (often 1 to 5%), and do not rely on click-based attribution because TV viewers rarely click.
@@+ identity
tradeoffs: Choose deterministic matching (same login, same hashed email) when an error is expensive: billing, suppression of customers, regulated audiences. Choose probabilistic matching (shared IP at night, co-viewing patterns, scored by a model) when reach matters and a wrong link only mildly hurts. Deterministic links are right 95 to 99% of the time but cover perhaps 20 to 40% of CTV households; modelled links cover 70 to 90% at 80 to 90% precision. You give up reach with the first and accuracy and explainability with the second, and a good system stores both types with a score so each use can pick its threshold.
Choose household-level identity for CTV and TV measurement (one screen shared by several people) and person-level identity for mobile and web personalization, where devices are personal. Household IDs are more stable and easier to link (a TV and its router IP) but cannot tell the 8-year-old from the parent, so demographics are probabilities; person-level graphs support frequency capping and attribution across devices but need logins or a panel and raise privacy burden. You give up precision about who watched in return for stability and coverage.
Choose building your own graph from first-party data (logins, ACR, pixels) when you have scale (tens of millions of devices) and own the data rights; choose licensing a graph (LiveRamp, UID 2.0, vendor graphs) or using a clean room when you lack scale or need a partner's coverage. In-house gives you control and lower per-match cost but needs engineering for union-find at billions of nodes and constant link maintenance; a licensed graph costs about $0.5 to $2 per thousand matched records or a platform fee and you inherit its quality, delays and contract limits.
Choose clean-room or aggregate matching when partner data can't leave its owner, and direct file-level matching (hashed email lists) when both sides trust each other and the use is activation. Clean rooms add days to set up, restrict queries to aggregates (often minimum cohort sizes of 50 to 100), and cut some use cases such as individual-level modelling. You give up flexibility for compliance and reduced leakage risk. Hashing alone is not protection: a SHA-256 of an email can be reversed by trying a list of known addresses.
interview: Match rate between your graph and an advertiser's CRM is 15%. How do you tell whether this is low? || Compare with the overlap you'd expect: the share of the CRM that could be in your population (CTV owners in the right country), the share of your households with a hashed email, and normalization differences (case, whitespace, plus-addressing). If 30% of the CRM can plausibly appear, 15% is half the ceiling. Test with a known sample, and fix normalization before blaming coverage.
Your graph merges two unrelated households into one giant component. What happened and how do you fix it? || Usually a shared or non-residential IP (office, café, carrier-grade NAT), or a mislabeled device linking two components. Union-find has no notion of confidence, so one bad edge merges both. Classify IPs by distinct-device counts, cap component size (for example 15 devices), use edge scores and only merge above a threshold, store provenance per edge, and split components with community detection.
How would you remove a person from the graph after a deletion request, including derived segments? || Keep every edge with its source and identifiers; delete the person's identifiers and edges, recompute the component, drop their profile rows, and propagate to audience tables and partner feeds through suppression lists. Because segments are built from the graph by jobs, deletion must also hit caches and trained-model training sets according to policy. Audit logs record the deletion without the data.
Why separate the identity graph, the profile table and the segment definitions? || They change at different speeds and owners: links change daily, profiles accumulate from events, segments are defined by marketers. Separate contracts let you rebuild one layer, test it, apply consent per layer and explain results. A single monolith makes a bad link or a bad rule impossible to isolate.
A segment of "sports fans" reaches 3 million households in your estimate but the advertiser sees only 1.2 million in a delivery report. Why? || Reach is cut by activation steps: only part of the households is addressable at the target DSP, consent excludes some, frequency and budget limit delivery, and the buyer's own targeting overlaps are filtered. Report the funnel (defined, matched, addressable, bid on, delivered) so each loss is visible.
@@+ experimentation
tradeoffs: Choose a fixed-horizon test (decide the sample size and duration in advance and look once) when decisions are cheap to delay a week or two and you want clean error rates. Choose sequential testing (always-valid p-values, mSPRT) or group-sequential boundaries when stakeholders will peek anyway, or when a harmful change must stop early. Peeking daily at a fixed test with a 5% threshold inflates false positives to 20 to 30%; sequential methods fix that but need about 10 to 30% more samples to reach the same power. You give up sample efficiency for the freedom to stop.
Choose an A/B test with a fixed split (50/50) when you need an unbiased estimate of effect size, for example to size a business decision or to compare to a cost. Choose a multi-armed bandit (Thompson sampling) when the goal is to maximize reward during the test with many variants and short-lived value (headlines, creatives, banners). A bandit can cut the regret by half or more but gives biased effect estimates and shaky intervals, and it adapts badly to time effects such as weekday and weekend. You give up measurement quality for the gain during the experiment.
Choose a larger minimum detectable effect (say 5% relative lift) when traffic is small, and variance reduction (CUPED using the pre-experiment metric, stratification) when it is not. CUPED with a pre-period correlation of 0.7 cuts variance by about 50%, so it halves the required sample at no extra traffic; going from detecting a 5% to a 2.5% effect needs 4 times more users. With 2% baseline conversion, detecting a 5% relative lift needs roughly 300,000 users per arm; there are many cases where the honest answer is that the test cannot be done in your traffic.
Choose randomization by user when effects are individual, by household or device group when screens are shared, and by geo or time slice when units interfere (marketplaces, ad budgets, auctions, network effects). Higher-level randomization removes bias but cuts sample size sharply: 50 geo regions have far less power than 5 million users, often needing 4 to 8 weeks and synthetic controls. You give up statistical power for validity; a user-level test of an ad auction change would show an effect that disappears at launch because treated and control compete for one budget.
interview: Your test shows a 3% lift with p = 0.04, but the sample ratio is 52/48 when you planned 50/50. What do you do? || Stop and investigate. A sample ratio mismatch (chi-square p value tiny, e.g. 10^-6 at that size) means the assignment or logging is broken: bots, crashes in one arm, redirects or filtering. The lift is not trustworthy; fix the cause and rerun. Alert on SRM automatically in the platform.
You test 20 metrics and two are significant. Do you ship? || One expects one false positive in 20 at 5%. Pre-register one primary metric and a few guardrails, correct for multiple comparisons (Bonferroni or Benjamini–Hochberg) for the rest, and treat the other results as hypotheses for a follow-up test. Ship on the primary metric and guardrails, not on the lucky one.
How do you test a feature with network or marketplace interference, such as a new bidding algorithm? || User-level randomization is biased because both arms share budgets and inventory. Randomize by campaign, by budget split (each campaign has its budget cut into halves across arms), by geo, or by time-slice (switchback), and use larger effects and longer runs. Always compare with a holdback or an observational check after launch.
The experiment won but the effect shrank by half after launch. Why? || Winner's curse from selecting the best of many tests, novelty effect, a short test not covering weekly cycles, or a difference between the tested and launched population. Use confidence intervals not point estimates, run holdouts after launch, and replicate big wins.
How do you decide the sample size and duration? || Pick the primary metric, baseline, standard deviation, minimum effect worth detecting, alpha 5% and power 80%; n per arm ≈ 16σ²/δ². Round the duration up to full weekly cycles, include a ramp and exposure delay, and check that traffic supports it before starting.
@@+ oauth
tradeoffs: Choose the authorization code flow with PKCE for every interactive client (browser apps, mobile apps, server-side web apps) and the client credentials flow for service-to-service calls; do not use the implicit or password grants. PKCE adds one hash and one round trip and removes the interception attack on public clients; the implicit flow put tokens in URLs and browser history and was deprecated in OAuth 2.1. For devices without a browser (TVs, consoles), use the device authorization flow, which lets the user approve on a phone; you give up a smooth experience (the user types a code) for not typing a password on a remote.
Choose self-contained JWT access tokens when many services must verify a request without calling the auth server (a verification is a local signature check in about 50 microseconds) and short lifetimes (5 to 15 minutes) are acceptable; choose opaque tokens with introspection when you need immediate revocation or hide claims from clients. A JWT cannot be revoked before it expires without extra state (a deny list), and a leaked 1-hour JWT is valid for the hour; an opaque token needs a call to the auth server on each check (1 to 5 ms with caching) and makes the auth server a dependency and a bottleneck. You give up immediate control with JWTs and independence with opaque tokens.
Choose short-lived access tokens (5 to 60 minutes) with rotating refresh tokens (days to weeks, one-time use, with reuse detection) over long-lived access tokens. A stolen refresh token can still be abused but reuse of an old one reveals the theft and lets the server revoke the family. The cost is more token requests (a refresh every 15 minutes per session) and a need to store refresh-token state; for batch services use client credentials with mTLS or private-key JWT instead of long-lived secrets.
Choose bearer tokens for simplicity; choose sender-constrained tokens (DPoP, or mTLS-bound tokens) when a token leak is costly (banking, admin APIs). A bearer token works for whoever holds it; a sender-constrained token also needs a proof key held by the client, so a stolen token alone is useless. You give up simplicity, key management on clients and some proxy compatibility. Choose OIDC (an ID token for who you are) for login and OAuth scopes for API access, and do not use an access token as proof of identity: it does not say who the user is, only what the bearer may do.
interview: Why is the implicit flow deprecated, and what replaces it for a single-page app? || It returned the access token in the URL fragment, exposing it to browser history, referrers, extensions and scripts, with no way to bind it to the client that asked. A single-page app should use authorization code with PKCE, receive tokens from the token endpoint over a back-channel POST, keep them in memory (or use a backend-for-frontend with an HttpOnly cookie), and use short lifetimes and refresh rotation.
A JWT access token has been stolen. What can you do? || Not much before it expires, which is why lifetimes are short. Options: a deny list or token ID checked by gateways (adds state), revoking the refresh token, rotating signing keys in an emergency (invalidates everything), or sender-constrained tokens so a stolen copy cannot be used. Alert on use from new IPs and impossible travel.
What is the confused deputy problem in OAuth and how do audience and scope help? || A service accepts a token meant for another service, or an app uses a user's grant for something outside what the user consented to. Check the aud claim equals your API, the issuer, expiry and scopes, and use the minimum scopes for each client. Without audience checks any token from the same issuer works anywhere.
How do you protect the redirect step? || Register exact redirect URIs (no wildcards), send and check an unguessable state tied to the session against CSRF, and use PKCE so a stolen code is useless without the verifier; for OIDC add nonce to bind the ID token to the request. Open redirects at the client are common ways to leak codes.
Where do you put signing keys and how do services verify tokens? || The auth server signs with a private key held in an HSM or KMS; services fetch public keys from a JWKS endpoint and cache them, selecting by kid, and refetch on an unknown kid. Rotate keys every 30 to 90 days with an overlap so old tokens still verify; reject alg none and pin allowed algorithms.
@@+ mtls
tradeoffs: Choose mTLS between services when you need strong, automatic, per-workload identity inside your network (zero trust) and can run a certificate authority; choose bearer tokens (OAuth, JWT) at the edge for end users and third parties who cannot hold managed certificates. mTLS authenticates the machine and encrypts the connection, with a TLS handshake overhead of about 1 to 2 ms on a new connection and little on reused ones; tokens carry the user and permissions for each request and survive proxies that terminate TLS. Most mature systems use both: mTLS for who is calling, a token for on whose behalf. You give up simplicity of operation (certificates expire) for stronger identity.
Choose a service mesh (Istio, Linkerd) to get mTLS without code changes when you run many services on Kubernetes: sidecars add 0.5 to 3 ms per hop and 50 to 100 MB of memory per pod, or in ambient mode a node-level proxy costs less per pod but shares failure domains. Choose library-level mTLS (gRPC credentials with a SPIFFE library) when you have a few services, tight latency budgets such as an ad bidder with a 100 ms deadline, or many non-Kubernetes workloads. You give up transparent coverage and uniform policy with libraries; you give up latency and resources with sidecars.
Choose short-lived certificates (1 to 24 hours, automatically rotated) over long-lived ones (1 year) when you can run automation: a stolen key is valid for hours, and revocation lists become unnecessary, but the CA and the issuing path become a critical dependency, and an outage longer than the lifetime takes everything down. Choose longer lifetimes (30 to 90 days) when the CA is less reliable or you lack monitoring; then you need revocation (CRL or OCSP) that actually works, and these are often skipped in practice. You give up safety margin or operational tolerance.
Choose authorization by identity (service A may call GET /budget on service B) on top of mTLS; encryption and authentication alone do not limit what a compromised service can do. Default-deny between namespaces and allow-list by SPIFFE ID adds policy maintenance (hundreds of rules) but limits lateral movement. In permissive mode services accept both plaintext and mTLS: use it only during a migration of days to weeks, not forever.
interview: A service mesh rollout causes random 503s at 02:00 each night. What would you check? || Certificate rotation or expiry: the CA, an intermediate or workload certificates failing to renew, clock skew, or sidecar restart ordering. Check certificate NotAfter dates, the issuing logs and the proxy's error counters; fix by alerting on remaining lifetime (below 50%), making rotation overlap, and testing the failure of the CA.
Why isn't mTLS enough on its own to secure service-to-service calls? || It proves which workload connected, not what it may do or for which user. Without authorization policy any workload in the mesh can call any other, so one compromised pod can reach everything. Add allow-lists by identity and method, and pass the end-user token for per-user decisions.
How do you issue a certificate to a new pod, without a human or a shared secret? || Workload attestation: the node agent proves the pod's properties (namespace, service account) using the platform (Kubernetes API, cloud instance identity), then asks the CA for a short-lived certificate with a SPIFFE ID in the SAN. The private key is generated on the workload and never leaves it.
What happens to long-lived connections when certificates rotate? || The TLS session is authenticated at handshake time, so an existing connection continues with the old certificate until it is closed. Servers should cap connection age (for example 10 to 30 minutes) so rotation and revocation take effect, and clients must pick up the new certificate for new connections without a restart.
Where does mTLS break at the edge? || L7 load balancers and CDNs terminate TLS, so the backend sees the balancer, not the client; pass the verified identity in headers (and trust only the balancer), or use TLS passthrough at L4. Mobile or browser clients rarely hold managed certificates, so use tokens there.
@@+ privacytech
tradeoffs: Choose aggregation thresholds and query restrictions (a minimum of 50 to 100 users per result, no row-level output) when you share data with one known partner and the main risk is accidental disclosure; choose differential privacy when results go to many analysts or the public and an attacker may combine many queries. Thresholds are simple and keep accuracy exact, but repeated overlapping queries can still reveal an individual (differencing). Differential privacy gives a provable limit with a privacy budget ε (often 1 to 8 per release), but adds noise: for a count the noise is about 1/ε, so ε = 1 gives ±1 on a count of 10,000 (harmless) and ±1 on a count of 20 (50% error). You give up exactness or simplicity.
Choose central differential privacy (a trusted server adds noise to true aggregates) when you can trust the collector and want the best accuracy; choose local differential privacy (each device adds noise before sending, as in Apple and Chrome telemetry) when you cannot. Local DP needs about 100 to 10,000 times more users for the same accuracy, with error growing like √n/ε, so it fits only huge populations and simple statistics (frequent items, counts). You give up accuracy for removing the trust assumption.
Choose a clean room (a controlled environment where two parties join their data and only approved aggregates leave) when the question is "how many of our exposed households bought" and both sides are known; choose private set intersection or secure multi-party computation when even the clean room operator must not see identifiers, and a trusted execution environment (TEE) as a middle path. A SQL clean room is deployed in weeks with minimal engineering but you must trust its operator and rules; PSI on 100 million records takes minutes to hours and gigabytes of traffic; TEEs run near native speed but have a history of side-channel attacks. You give up trust-free guarantees or convenience.
Choose pseudonymization (hashed or tokenized IDs) only as a way to reduce accidental exposure, not as anonymization: a hashed email can be reversed by hashing a list of likely emails, and a few attributes (zip code, birth date, gender) identify about 87% of people in the US. Choose k-anonymity or generalization for static releases only with care, since it is broken by linkage with other data. Choose synthetic data when you must share a dataset with analysts and can accept distortion of rare patterns; unless it is trained with DP, it can leak records the model memorized.
interview: A clean room returns a conversion rate for a segment of 12 households. What is wrong and what do you do? || The cohort is too small to publish: results on tiny groups are noisy and reveal individuals. Enforce minimum thresholds (for example 50 to 100), suppress or merge small cells, add noise, and limit repeated queries that differ by one person so an analyst cannot subtract one result from another.
What does epsilon mean in practice and how do you pick it? || It bounds how much any one person's data can change the probability of any output: the ratio is at most e^ε. Smaller is more private and noisier. Pick it from the use: ε about 1 for sensitive public releases, up to 8 or more for analytics with large populations, and track the total budget across all releases, since budgets add up.
Why isn't hashing emails before sharing enough? || The email space is small and guessable: attackers hash lists of known emails or use rainbow tables and match, and the same hash links the person across datasets. Use keyed hashing (HMAC with a secret), salting only with a trusted party, PSI, or a clean room to avoid sharing identifiers.
When would you choose a federated or on-device approach over a clean room? || When data should not leave the device at all (keyboard predictions, ACR event pre-processing), and you can tolerate noisy aggregated updates; add secure aggregation and DP so the server cannot see single updates. A clean room fits two organizations with databases, not millions of devices.
How do you measure ad campaign impact with a partner without sharing user-level data? || A clean-room join on hashed identifiers produces aggregate counts for exposed and holdout groups; add minimum thresholds, noise if needed, and only approved query templates. Compare conversion rates between randomized groups and report lift with confidence intervals; log all queries for audit.
@@+ specdecode
tradeoffs: Choose speculative decoding when latency of a single request matters and batch size is small (1 to 8): interactive chat, coding assistants, agents. In that regime the GPU waits for weights to be read from memory, so verifying 4 to 8 drafted tokens costs about as much as one token, and speedups of 2 to 3 times are common. Choose continuous batching with large batches (32 to 256) and no speculation when throughput per GPU is the goal: the GPU is already close to compute-bound, extra verification work is not free, and speculation can lower total throughput by 10 to 30%. You give up peak throughput for latency.
Choose a separate small draft model (for example 1B drafting for 70B, same tokenizer) when a good same-family model exists and you can host both; choose EAGLE or Medusa-style heads (small prediction heads trained on top of the target) for higher acceptance, 3 to 4 tokens accepted per step against 2 to 3, without a second model's memory, but they need training; choose prompt lookup (copy n-grams from the prompt) for editing, summarization and code rewriting where output copies input, at zero training and almost zero cost. A draft model needs 1 to 2 GB or more extra memory and a KV cache of its own; heads are cheaper at serving time and costlier in engineering.
Choose a longer speculation length k only when the acceptance rate α is high. With α = 0.8, the expected tokens per verification are (1 − α^(k+1))/(1 − α): about 3.4 for k = 4, 4.0 for k = 8. With α = 0.5, k = 4 gives only 1.9 and the draft cost eats the gain. Acceptance drops on creative or high-temperature sampling, on code the draft was not trained for, and in non-English text; measure it on your own traffic and adapt k dynamically, or turn speculation off when the server is busy.
Choose speculation only when exactness matters and you can pay for it: with correct rejection sampling the output distribution is identical to the target model's, so you do not trade quality, unlike distillation to a smaller model or quantization. What you give up is GPU memory, engineering complexity (two KV caches, rollback of rejected tokens) and predictability: latency depends on acceptance, which varies by prompt, so p99 improves less than the mean.
interview: Why does verifying k tokens in one pass cost almost the same as generating one? || Decode at small batch is memory-bound: each step streams all weights (140 GB for a 70B model in fp16) from memory, and the arithmetic is tiny. Verifying k tokens reuses the same weight reads with k times more arithmetic, which is still below the compute limit, so time grows only slightly, about 10% for k = 4 to 8.
Walk through the acceptance rule and why the output distribution is unchanged. || For each drafted token with draft probability q and target probability p, accept with probability min(1, p/q). At the first rejection, sample from the normalized residual max(0, p − q), and stop. This is rejection sampling, so the accepted tokens follow exactly p. If all k are accepted, sample one more token from the target for free.
At what batch size does speculative decoding stop paying, and why? || When the target pass becomes compute-bound, usually around batch 32 to 64 on modern GPUs for a 70B model, verifying extra tokens costs real time. Use the roofline: if arithmetic intensity is already above the ridge point, extra tokens cost proportionally. Servers should enable it adaptively with queue depth.
How do you choose the draft model? || Same tokenizer and training family, 10 to 20 times smaller, ideally fine-tuned or distilled on the target's outputs for your domain; speed ratio c = draft time/target time under 0.1. Evaluate acceptance and tokens per second end to end, since a better but slower draft can reduce the total.
Does speculative decoding help time to first token? || No. It speeds the decode phase, not prefill. For long prompts the first token is dominated by prefill, which chunked prefill and prompt caching address instead.
@@+ promptcache
tradeoffs: Choose automatic prefix caching (block hashing in vLLM, a radix tree in SGLang) when many requests share a long, stable beginning: system prompts, tool definitions, a reference document, or a growing chat history. Cached tokens cost about 10% of the normal input price in hosted APIs and skip the prefill compute, so a 30,000-token manual cut from a 3-second prefill to well under 0.5 seconds. Choose no caching, or a small cache, when prompts are mostly unique (each request has its own 500 tokens): the hit rate is near zero, and the cache only occupies GPU memory that could hold more running requests. You give up KV memory for the saved compute.
Choose block hashing with fixed-size blocks (16 or 32 tokens) for simple, fast lookup and easy eviction; choose a radix tree (SGLang) when many requests branch from shared prefixes, as in agents, few-shot templates or tree-of-thought search, because it shares exact common parts at token granularity and evicts leaves first. Block hashing wastes a partial block at the end of each prefix (up to 15 tokens) and treats a request that differs in the first block as a total miss; the radix tree has more bookkeeping, but the hit rate is higher for branching workloads. You give up simplicity for hit rate.
Choose to keep cached KV on GPU for hot prefixes with minutes of reuse, and offload to CPU memory or SSD (LMCache, Mooncake-style stores) when the prefix pool is large and reuse is hours apart. GPU memory for one 30,000-token prefix of a 70B model with grouped-query attention is about 10 GB (about 320 KB per token), so 80 GB holds only a handful; a CPU tier of 1 TB holds around a hundred, and loading 10 GB over PCIe takes 0.2 to 0.4 s, still faster than 3 s recompute. Offloading adds latency and network or disk bandwidth demand; you give up some speed for much higher capacity.
Choose a prompt layout with stable content first and variable content last; a timestamp or user name in the first line destroys all reuse. Choose routing by prefix (sending requests with the same system prompt to the same replica) when you run many replicas: random load balancing across 8 replicas drops the hit rate to about 1/8 of its potential, but prefix-aware routing can create hot spots and uneven load. Caching is exact: the output is the same as without it, so you trade only memory, routing complexity and some operational surprise (time-to-live of 5 to 60 minutes in hosted services).
interview: A team adds the current time to the top of the system prompt and their bill rises 5 times. Why? || Cache lookup is by exact token prefix, so a changing first line makes every request a miss from the first block; all the long prompt is prefilled and billed at full input price. Move variable fields to the end, or into the user message, and keep tools and instructions in a fixed order.
How does the server decide that two prompts share a prefix? || In vLLM-style systems, each block of tokens is hashed together with the hash of the preceding block, so a block's identity depends on the whole prefix before it; equal hashes mean identical prefix (with collision checks or strong hashes). In SGLang a radix tree matches tokens along edges. Multi-tenant servers also include a salt or tenant ID in the hash to avoid cross-user timing leaks.
What happens under memory pressure with cached prefixes? || Cached blocks with zero active references are kept in an LRU (or leaf-first) list and evicted when new requests need blocks. Running requests are never evicted, so a burst of unique long requests can flush a hot prefix and cause a latency spike. Reserve capacity, tier to CPU, or use priority for system prompts.
Does caching change the model's answer? || No for exact prefix reuse: the keys and values are the same ones that would be computed. A small numerical difference can arise from different batch shapes and kernels, but not from caching itself. Approximate reuse of non-prefix chunks (CacheBlend-style) does change outputs and needs quality tests.
How would you estimate savings for a chatbot with 2,000-token system prompt and 20 turns? || Each turn re-sends all previous turns; without caching total prefill is the sum 2,000 + 2,500 + ... about 2,000×20 + 500×190 = 135,000 tokens per conversation; with caching only the new 500 per turn plus the first prompt are computed: about 12,000 tokens. That is roughly 10 times less compute for prefill, minus misses after cache expiry between turns.
@@+ auction
tradeoffs: Choose a second-price auction when bidders are unsophisticated, you want truthful bidding and simple strategies for buyers; choose first-price when you want transparency and revenue to follow actual bids, and accept bid shading by buyers. Second price has a dominant strategy (bid your true value) in a single, isolated auction, but it relies on the auctioneer being honest about the second bid, which hidden floors and header-bidding wrappers made hard to verify; the industry moved to first price around 2017 to 2019 for that reason. In first price, the buyer needs a model of the win-rate curve and typically shades by 10 to 40% below value; you give up bidding simplicity for transparency, and revenue may rise or fall depending on shading quality.
Choose a model-based shading (estimate P(win | bid) from logs and maximize (value − bid) × P(win)) when you have the data, at a cost of one extra low-latency model call of 1 to 3 ms and a risk of drift when competitors change; choose a fixed shading factor only when you lack data. In the example where competing bids are uniform on $0 to $12 CPM and value is $10, expected profit (10 − b) × b / 12 peaks at b = $5, half the value; real curves are not uniform, so the optimum often sits at 60 to 85% of value. You give up some wins for higher margin, and need continuous testing with holdouts.
Choose a reserve (floor) price when supply is scarce and demand is thin, to protect yield; choose low or dynamic floors when fill rate matters. Raising the floor by 20% on inventory with 5 bidders may raise revenue per impression but lose 10 to 30% of the impressions that do not clear; the optimum depends on the bid distribution (Myerson's optimal reserve), which publishers estimate from logs. You give up volume for price, and floors that are too high push buyers to other paths.
Choose a single exchange auction when simplicity and low latency matter (under 100 ms), and header bidding or unified auctions when you want several demand sources to compete directly (adds 100 to 300 ms of page latency in web contexts, and duplicated bids). Choose GSP/VCG-style multi-slot auctions in search-like settings where several ads are shown; an ad pod in CTV (several slots in a break) is closer to a knapsack or allocation problem than a single-item auction, and brings complexity of competitive separation and fill order.
interview: Why did exchanges switch from second price to first price and what did buyers have to change? || Second price made it hard for buyers to trust the clearing price since multiple layers (exchange, header-bidding wrappers) could insert hidden bids or floors; first price is transparent. Buyers had to stop bidding their value and start shading: model the win probability for each context and maximize expected surplus, using minimum-bid-to-win signals when available.
Derive the optimal bid in the uniform example. || Win probability w(b) = b/12 for 0 ≤ b ≤ 12, value 10. Surplus = (10 − b) × b/12. Derivative: (10 − 2b)/12 = 0, so b = 5, giving expected surplus 25/12 ≈ 2.08 per thousand. Bidding the true value of 10 gives zero surplus. In practice the distribution is estimated from logs, not uniform.
How do you detect that competitors changed their strategy and your shading is stale? || Monitor the win rate and the price paid relative to the minimum bid to win by supply path. A sudden drop of win rate at constant bids means higher clearing prices; a rise with falling margin means overbidding. Retrain on a short window, keep exploration traffic (random bid variations on 1 to 5% of requests) to refresh the curve, and alert on the drift.
Why can't you just maximize win rate? || Winning costs money and not every win is valuable. A bid that wins 90% at 95% of value earns almost nothing; the objective is expected value minus price, subject to budget and pacing. Maximizing wins also overpays on easy inventory and drains budget early.
What is the effect of a bid floor on a single bidder vs five bidders? || With one bidder, a floor extracts surplus up to the bidder's value (a monopoly price); with five, competition already pushes price up and a floor above the second-highest bid only helps when it is binding. Estimate the revenue curve using the log of bids, and A/B test floor changes at the inventory level.
@@+ pacing
tradeoffs: Choose bid modification (scale the bid by a multiplier λ) when you can change prices freely, because it keeps buying the best impressions and drops the least valuable ones first, giving a lower cost per result than random throttling by 10 to 25% in typical simulations. Choose probabilistic throttling (bid on a fraction p of requests) when prices are fixed (guaranteed deals), when the exchange forbids price changes, or when you need a controller that is simple to reason about; it also saves compute because skipped requests are not scored. You give up efficiency with throttling and give up simplicity with bid modification, which must be tuned with the auction format (first price makes the effect of a multiplier less direct).
Choose a feedback controller (PI or PID on the spend error, updated every 1 to 10 minutes) when traffic is predictable and you can tolerate lag; choose forecast-based planning (an optimization over predicted traffic, computing a bid multiplier for each time slot) when traffic is spiky, such as live sports. A PI controller is robust and easy, but slow to react: a 3-minute update interval misses a 90-second spike. Plan-based pacing reacts in advance but depends on forecast error; a 20% miss leaves large errors. Most systems combine them: the plan sets a target curve, the controller trims the error.
Choose even pacing over the day for steady-value inventory, and a traffic-shaped target (proportional to forecast eligible traffic or to forecast conversion value) when value varies by time. Even pacing wastes budget in a low-value 3 a.m. slot and starves prime time, which in CTV is often 3 to 5 times more valuable; shaping needs a forecast and is exposed to its error. Choose "as fast as possible" delivery only for short flash sales or when the advertiser wants the first impressions, because it buys at the highest prices and ends early.
Choose a conservative pacing target (aim for 95 to 98% of the budget) when overspend carries contractual cost (many exchanges bill what you win, and you may not recover overdelivery), and an aggressive one (100%) when underspend loses the advertiser's goals. Latency matters too: a bidder cluster with 50 instances each pacing locally can overshoot by up to the budget they can win between synchronizations; central counters (a fast key-value store at about 1 ms) give accurate control but are a single point of load and failure, and local budgets with periodic rebalancing are cheaper but looser.
interview: Your campaign spends 80% of its budget by noon. What do you check and fix? || Whether the pacing multiplier is working: the controller update lag, a wrong traffic forecast (traffic arrived early), a bug in the spend signal (delayed impression reports), or a campaign with a very high bid ceiling. Fix by correcting the signal delay (use won-bid events, not rendered ones), lower the control gain, add a hard cap per interval, and improve the forecast by hour and day of week.
Why does a PI controller oscillate and how do you stop it? || Delay in the spend signal plus a high gain makes it overshoot, then overcorrect. Lower the gain, add damping (the derivative term or smoothing), update more slowly than the delay, and clamp the multiplier change per step (for example ±10%). Test with simulated traffic and delayed feedback.
How would you pace 50,000 campaigns across a 200-instance bidder fleet? || Do not synchronize every bid. Give each campaign a per-instance budget slice refreshed every few seconds from a central store, estimate spend with local counters, and let a controller compute the multiplier per campaign centrally (cheap: 50,000 numbers every 10 s). Accept 1 to 3% overspend risk and use overdelivery caps for hard-budget campaigns.
When is pacing a linear program rather than a controller? || When many campaigns compete for limited impressions and each has constraints (budget, frequency, targeting). The LP maximizes total value subject to budgets; the dual variables are the pacing multipliers (shadow prices), and the solution gives the target multipliers that a controller then tracks. It needs forecasts of supply and is re-solved every few minutes.
What metrics tell you pacing is good? || Delivery against the target curve (error percent per hour), end-of-day spend (97 to 100%), cost per result against an unpaced baseline, and win rate stability. Report by campaign size, because small campaigns have noisy feedback and big ones may saturate supply.
@@+ idgraph
tradeoffs: Choose deterministic edges (same login, same hashed email) as the backbone and add probabilistic edges (shared IP, co-viewing, model score) only above a stated threshold, with the score stored on the edge. Deterministic edges are 98%+ accurate but cover a minority of devices; probabilistic edges at a 0.9 threshold may be 85 to 90% precise and extend coverage two to three times. You give up precision for reach, and a single bad edge can merge two households, so keep the threshold high for any use where mistakes hurt (suppression, billing) and lower for broad reach campaigns.
Choose union-find (connected components) when you need a fast, simple, incremental graph: near-constant time per edge, easily run at 10 billion edges on Spark with a few passes. Choose community detection or clustering (label propagation, Louvain, correlation clustering) when components get too big because of noisy edges; they split weakly connected parts but cost more compute (10 to 100 times), and give less stable IDs. A component of 40 devices is almost certainly a mistake and should be split; union-find has no way to do it, because it only merges.
Choose household-level and person-level graphs separately: a household graph (devices at one residence) is more robust, and a person graph needs deterministic logins. Choose persistent IDs (an ID that survives the graph being rebuilt, mapped through a lineage table) over IDs recomputed on each run; a rebuilt graph with fresh IDs breaks every audience, frequency cap and measurement study that references old IDs. Stable IDs need merge and split rules (the older ID wins on merge, the biggest part keeps the ID on split) and a table of changes, at the price of more complexity.
Choose batch rebuilds (daily, on all edges, simple to audit) for most use, and incremental updates (stream of edges into a store) when freshness within minutes matters, for example for a newly logged-in device. Incremental union-find is easy for merges but hard for deletions and expiry: removing an edge may split a component, which needs recomputation of the affected component. Edge expiry (dropping IP links not seen for 30 to 90 days) is important, since households move and devices change owners; keeping stale edges inflates the graph by 20 to 40% a year and decreases accuracy.
interview: How would you compute households from billions of ID pairs? || Treat IDs as nodes and links as edges and compute connected components: union-find in a distributed form (Spark GraphFrames, or iterative min-label propagation with log(diameter) passes). Pre-filter edges by score and drop high-degree nodes (shared IPs). Output (id → component_id) and keep edge provenance. For 10 billion edges, expect tens of minutes on a large cluster.
One shared public Wi-Fi IP links 5,000 devices into one household. How do you prevent it? || Never link on an IP seen with many devices: count distinct devices per IP per day, mark IPs above a threshold (for example 6) as shared, exclude hosting and carrier ranges, require repeated nighttime co-occurrence, and cap component size. Monitor the size distribution of components daily, and alert on outliers.
How do you evaluate an identity graph without ground truth? || Use a labelled sample (a deterministic login set held out from model training), measure pairwise precision and recall, compare household size distribution to census (average 3 to 4 people in India, 2.5 in the US), and check downstream metrics such as cross-device frequency and conversion consistency. Use panel data if available.
How do you handle a deletion request in a graph with derived IDs? || Remove the identifiers and edges for the person's IDs, recompute the affected component (it may split), assign new household IDs or keep the stable ID for the remaining part, and propagate suppression to audiences. Keep an audit entry of the deletion with only a hash of the request.
Why store provenance on each edge? || To explain, audit and remove links: you can drop all edges from one data source if its consent is withdrawn or its quality is poor, retrain scores per edge type, and debug why two devices were joined.
@@+ cookies
tradeoffs: Choose first-party cookies (set by your own domain, HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite=Lax) for sessions and preferences: they work in every browser, and are not blocked by tracking prevention. Choose third-party cookies (SameSite=None; Secure) only for embedded functions that need state across sites (a payment widget, an embedded video player), knowing that Safari and Firefox block them by default and Chrome offers users a choice. Cross-site tracking via third-party cookies has fallen from the main identifier of the open web to perhaps 30 to 60% of browser traffic being addressable. You give up cross-site reach for working at all in browsers that block them.
Choose cookies (server-side session ID in an HttpOnly cookie) over tokens in localStorage for browser sessions because JavaScript cannot read HttpOnly cookies, so an XSS attack cannot steal them; choose bearer tokens in headers for APIs and native apps where there is no cookie jar. Cookies are sent automatically, which creates CSRF risk (needs SameSite and CSRF tokens); tokens in localStorage avoid CSRF but are exposed to XSS. A cookie has a 4 KB limit per cookie and is sent on every request (adding 1 to 2 KB to each request), so large state belongs on the server.
Choose a stable device ID (CTV platform IDs, mobile advertising IDs) for ad targeting and measurement on apps and TVs, where the user or platform controls reset and opt-out; choose a first-party ID (your login, a hashed email with consent) for cross-device continuity. Mobile IDs (IDFA) are available to only 20 to 30% of iOS users after App Tracking Transparency, whereas Android's GAID is available for most; CTV IDs (Roku, Samsung, LG) are stable but are not shared across platforms and may be resettable. You give up cross-platform linking for compliance and stability.
Choose server-side ID syncing (cookie matching through redirects between SSP and DSP) only if you accept its cost: each sync adds a redirect and often 100 to 300 ms of delay on the page, leaks IDs to partners, and the match rate is rarely above 40 to 60%. Choose probabilistic or contextual approaches when matching is missing, and partitioned cookies (CHIPS) or Storage Access API for embedded state. Fingerprinting (combining browser attributes) is not an acceptable substitute: it is not stable across changes, is opaque to users, and is restricted by browser vendors and privacy laws.
interview: A login works on desktop Chrome but loops on Safari inside an embedded iframe. What is happening? || Safari's tracking prevention blocks third-party cookies, so the cookie set by the framed domain is dropped and the session never sticks. Use a first-party top-level redirect for login, the Storage Access API, partitioned cookies, or move the login to the parent domain; do not rely on SameSite=None cookies in third-party context.
Explain SameSite=Lax, Strict and None, and which attack each prevents. || Strict sends the cookie only for requests that start on the same site, Lax also on top-level GET navigations, None on every context but requires Secure. Lax (the browser default) blocks most CSRF POSTs from other sites; Strict also breaks logging in from links to your site; None is needed for cross-site embeds. Add CSRF tokens for defence in depth.
How does cookie syncing between an SSP and a DSP work and why is the match rate low? || The SSP redirects the browser to the DSP's match URL with its ID; the DSP reads its own cookie and stores the pair. Rates are low because users clear cookies, many browsers block third-party cookies, syncs only happen on pages where the tag fires, and IDs expire; mappings need refresh every few weeks.
What replaces third-party cookies for CTV and mobile measurement? || Platform device IDs with consent, hashed emails or phone numbers from logins, ACR-derived household identity, publisher first-party data in clean rooms, and contextual signals. Measurement moves to aggregate and experiment-based approaches (geo tests, incrementality).
How would you size the impact of cookie loss on a campaign's reach? || Measure the share of bid requests with a usable ID by browser and device, multiply by frequency of visits, and compare reach for ID-based vs contextual segments in a test. If 40% of requests lack an ID, an audience campaign loses up to 40% of its addressable inventory, and frequency capping fails on the rest.
@@+ declpipelines
tradeoffs: Choose declarative pipelines (Lakeflow Declarative Pipelines / DLT, Spark Declarative Pipelines, dbt) when most of the work is "this table is a query over those tables", and you want dependency order, retries, checkpoints, incremental refresh and data-quality expectations handled for you. A team can run 100 tables with a few hundred lines of SQL instead of a few thousand lines of orchestration code. Choose imperative jobs (Spark code in Airflow tasks) when you need arbitrary side effects, calls to external services, custom partition layouts, or logic that cannot be written as a query. You give up control and sometimes cost transparency: the engine decides the plan.
Choose a streaming table for append-only inputs such as Kafka events and files, where each record is processed once and the state is a checkpoint; choose a materialized view when the result must stay correct as inputs change (aggregates, joins, updates, deletes). A streaming table is cheap (work proportional to new data) but cannot fix history without a full refresh; a materialized view with incremental refresh costs work proportional to changes, when the query supports it (simple filters, joins, aggregates), and falls back to a full recompute (cost proportional to all data, sometimes 10 to 100 times more) when it does not. You give up some query freedom for incremental refresh.
Choose triggered (batch) pipelines when freshness of 15 minutes to a day is acceptable: the cluster runs for minutes and stops, often 3 to 10 times cheaper than keeping compute on; choose continuous mode when you need seconds to a minute of latency, paying for compute 24 hours a day. A pipeline with 25,000 events per second can often run as a 5-minute trigger at a fraction of continuous cost; most dashboards do not need real time. Serverless modes reduce operations but you give up control of cluster sizing and tend to cost more at steady high loads.
Choose a declarative framework within a lakehouse when you can adopt its limits; choose dbt when the logic is SQL transformations on a warehouse or lakehouse and the team already uses it, accepting that it does not manage streaming ingestion or checkpoints by itself. Choose full refresh with idempotent overwrite for small tables (under a few GB), since incrementality is complex; choose incremental models (MERGE) for tables that grow by billions of rows a day. Whichever you pick, be ready for the fixes: late data, schema changes and backfills are where a declared graph can be expensive or surprising.
interview: A materialized view that refreshed in 2 minutes now takes 90 minutes. What happened? || The engine probably fell back from incremental to full recompute: a non-deterministic function, an unsupported join or aggregate, a change in the upstream table (a full rewrite or a schema change) or too many changed rows. Check the refresh explain output, simplify the query, move complex logic to an earlier layer, or maintain the table with MERGE yourself.
How do declarative pipelines get exactly-once results after a failure? || Streaming tables checkpoint source offsets and state, and writes are transactional (Delta commits), so on restart the engine resumes from the last committed checkpoint and re-runs the uncommitted batch; the writes are idempotent. Materialized views rely on table versions and change feeds to compute exactly the delta. You still need to handle non-idempotent side effects outside the pipeline.
When would you not use a declarative pipeline? || When the work is mostly external calls or custom code (ML training, calling an API), needs very fine control of partitioning or timing, requires cross-pipeline transactions, or must run on engines or in places the framework does not support. Use a workflow orchestrator with ordinary jobs there.
How do you backfill a bronze table with three months of data without breaking downstream tables? || Run a one-time backfill flow into the streaming table (append), or full refresh in a controlled window; downstream streaming tables see new rows incrementally, and materialized views recompute only affected partitions if supported. Pause alerts, check quotas, and validate row counts against the source.
How do data-quality expectations change failure handling? || You declare rules (not null, ranges) with an action: record the violation and keep the rows, drop bad rows, or fail the update. Dropping hides problems unless you track counts; failing protects gold tables but halts pipelines. Common practice: warn at bronze, drop and quarantine at silver, fail on critical gold invariants.

tradeoffs: Choose chunked prefill on a single pool of GPUs (the default in recent vLLM) when the traffic mixes long prompts with streaming decodes and the goal is to keep inter-token latency smooth. Splitting a 32,000-token prompt into 2,048-token chunks lets each step carry about 2,048 prompt tokens plus all running decodes, so streaming users see steps of 60 to 90 ms instead of a stall of several seconds, while the long prompt takes 16 steps (about 1 to 1.5 seconds more in total). Choose bigger chunks (8,192 and above) when time to first token matters more than smoothness, since prefill throughput is best with large matrices; smaller chunks (512 to 1,024) when p99 time per output token is the SLO. You give up some prefill efficiency (5 to 15%) for predictable decoding.
Choose disaggregated serving (separate prefill and decode GPUs, as in DistServe, Splitwise, Mooncake, Dynamo) when you have high volume (tens of GPUs or more), strict SLOs for both TTFT and TPOT, and a fast interconnect, so you can size and tune each pool separately: prefill GPUs for compute (large tensor parallel, high batch of tokens), decode GPUs for memory bandwidth and capacity (large batch). Reported gains are 1.5 to 4 times more goodput under SLOs. Choose chunked prefill on a combined pool when you have fewer GPUs, a short typical prompt, or slow networking. Disaggregation costs: the KV cache must move, a 10,000-token prompt of a 70B model is about 3 GB, which takes about 60 ms on 400 Gbit/s links but seconds on slower networks, plus a more complex scheduler and two pools to autoscale.
Choose the prefill-to-decode GPU ratio from workload shape: short prompts and long outputs (chat, reasoning) need more decode capacity, perhaps 1 prefill to 3 decode; long prompts with short answers (summarization, RAG) invert it. A wrong ratio leaves one pool idle and the other queued, so total goodput can drop below a combined pool; adding adaptive routing of short prompts directly to decode nodes (local prefill) lowers the transfer overhead. You give up the simplicity and high average utilization of a single pool for tuned control.
Choose prefix caching in combination: a prompt that mostly hits cache has little to prefill, so chunking and disaggregation matter less; a service with high cache hit rates (agents with long fixed prefixes) may be better served by a single pool with caching. Choose a larger token budget per step when GPUs are underused at low traffic, and a smaller one at peak. Measure p50 and p99 of TTFT and TPOT under your real mix; synthetic uniform prompts hide the problems chunking solves.
interview: Why do long prompts hurt other users' streaming on a shared GPU, and what does chunking change? || A whole-prompt prefill occupies the GPU for seconds, and decode steps for all other requests wait, so their tokens stall. Chunking limits each step to a token budget, mixing a chunk of prompt with the running decodes, so each step stays tens of milliseconds. The long prompt finishes a bit later but nobody stalls.
Prefill is compute-bound and decode is memory-bound. How does this lead to disaggregation? || Mixing them wastes both: decode steps barely use the arithmetic units, and prefill barely uses memory bandwidth, and their latency goals differ. Giving each its own GPUs lets you pick the parallelism, batch size and even hardware type per phase, at the price of transferring the KV cache.
How much bandwidth do you need to move KV cache between prefill and decode GPUs? || KV size per token for a 70B model with grouped-query attention is about 320 KB, so 10,000 tokens is 3.2 GB. At 50 GB/s (400 Gbit/s) that is 64 ms; at 3 GB/s (25 Gbit/s Ethernet) it is about a second, which would break a TTFT target. Overlap transfer with compute layer by layer, and compress or quantize KV.
How do you choose the chunk size? || Start at 2,048 to 4,096 tokens, then sweep while measuring p99 TPOT and TTFT on production-like traffic. Smaller chunks protect streaming but add attention and scheduling overhead, since each chunk re-reads the earlier KV; larger chunks finish prompts sooner. Choose the largest value that meets the TPOT SLO.
When does disaggregation not pay off? || With small clusters (under about 8 GPUs), slow interconnects, short prompts where the transfer is comparable to prefill, or traffic with high prefix cache hits. Also when load is bursty enough that dedicated pools sit idle; a combined pool shares capacity better.
