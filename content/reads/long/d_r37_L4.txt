@@ powerlaws
value: A power law is a pattern where the size of something and how often it occurs are linked by a simple rule: the probability of seeing a value larger than x falls like x raised to a negative power, P(X > x) ∝ x^(−α). The fall is slow, so very large values are not rare accidents. A few items are huge and a very long list of items are tiny. Word frequencies, city sizes, file sizes, website traffic, follower counts, key popularity in a database and the length of network flows all follow this shape, at least roughly. Zipf's law is the version for ranked lists: the item at rank k has frequency about proportional to 1/k^s, with s near 1.
This matters to a systems engineer because almost all intuition built from the bell curve is wrong here. With a bell curve, the average describes a typical item and extreme values almost never happen. With a power law, the average can be dominated by a handful of items, the maximum grows as the data grows, and in the heavier cases the variance or even the mean does not exist. The same fact helps and hurts. It helps because a small cache holds the popular items and serves most requests. It hurts because one hot key can overload one partition, one huge customer can stall one Spark task, and one slow request can dominate the p99 latency.
For the layer above, probability (L4) supplies the vocabulary: tail probabilities, expected value, variance and the conditions under which they are finite. Power laws are the case where those tools behave unusually. Caching and eviction (L2), partitioning and sharding (L2), the caching layer (L1) and scaling laws for neural networks (L2) all depend on this shape, so knowing the exponent tells you how big a cache must be, how bad skew will be, and how fast returns diminish.
terms: Power law — a distribution where P(X > x) = (x_min / x)^α for x ≥ x_min; a straight line on a log-log plot.
Exponent (α) — the slope of the tail on a log-log plot; smaller α means a heavier tail with more extreme values.
Zipf's law — the item of rank k in a sorted list has frequency proportional to 1/k^s, with s often close to 1.
Heavy tail — a tail that decays more slowly than any exponential, so large values keep appearing.
Harmonic number (H_N) — the sum 1 + 1/2 + … + 1/N; it is about ln N + 0.577 and normalises Zipf's law with s = 1.
Hot key — a key that receives a large share of all requests, so the partition holding it becomes overloaded.
Log-normal — the distribution of a quantity whose logarithm is normal; it can look like a power law over a limited range.
Preferential attachment — a growth process where new links go to items in proportion to what they already have; it produces power laws.
history: The first systematic observation was by Vilfredo Pareto in 1896. He found that the number of people with income above x fell like a power of x in several countries, and that a small share of people held most of the wealth. In 1913 Felix Auerbach noticed the same shape in city sizes. George Kingsley Zipf, a Harvard linguist, made the pattern famous in "The Psycho-Biology of Language" (1935) and "Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort" (1949): in English text the most frequent word, "the", is about 7% of all words, the second, "of", is about half that, and so on down the ranked list. Alfred Lotka found a similar inverse-square rule for how many papers scientists publish (1926), and the Gutenberg-Richter law (1944) says the number of earthquakes falls as a power of their energy.
The next question was why. Herbert Simon (1955) showed that a process of "the rich get richer" produces Zipf-like distributions. Benoit Mandelbrot argued from word-coding efficiency and, in the 1960s, promoted heavy-tailed models for market prices and other data. Derek de Solla Price (1965, 1976) described cumulative advantage in citations. In 1999 two papers moved the topic into computing. Breslau, Cao, Fan, Phillips and Shenker measured web proxy traces and found that page requests are Zipf-like with exponent between about 0.64 and 0.83, which set the hit rates of web caches. Barabási and Albert showed that preferential attachment makes a scale-free network, and the Faloutsos brothers found power laws in Internet topology.
The statistics also matured, and warned of over-claiming. Clauset, Shalizi and Newman (SIAM Review, 2009) gave a careful method to fit and test power laws, and found that many famous data sets fit poorly or are no better than a log-normal. Broido and Clauset (2019) argued that strictly scale-free networks are rare. In machine learning, Kaplan et al. (2020) found that language-model loss falls as a power law in parameters, data and compute, which created a new use of the same mathematics: a straight line on a log-log plot used for planning.
how: Start with the definition. In this read, α always means the tail exponent: P(X > x) = (x_min/x)^α for x ≥ x_min. This is the Pareto distribution. (Some papers write the density exponent, which is α + 1; be careful when comparing numbers.) Take logarithms: log P = α log x_min − α log x. This is a straight line with slope −α, which is why a log-log plot is the standard picture.
The role of probability is clearest in the moments. The mean of a Pareto distribution is α·x_min/(α − 1) if α > 1 and infinite otherwise. The variance is finite only if α > 2. Many real systems sit near these edges. If α = 1.5, the mean exists but the variance is infinite. Averaging more samples still converges, but slowly and with large jumps, because the next sample may be larger than all earlier ones combined. Example: for α = 1.5 and x_min = 1, the mean is 3, but a typical sample is about 1.6 (the median is 2^(1/1.5) ≈ 1.59). The mean is carried by rare large values. That is why a monitoring dashboard showing averages of file sizes or request costs jumps around, and why the central limit theorem, which needs finite variance, does not give the usual comfort.
Zipf's law is the same idea in rank form. Sort N items by popularity. The item at rank k gets a share f(k) = (1/k^s) / H_{N,s}, where H_{N,s} = Σ 1/k^s is the normaliser. For s = 1 and N = one million, H_N ≈ ln N + 0.577 ≈ 14.39, so the top item gets 1/14.39 ≈ 6.9% of all traffic, and the top 100 items together get H_100/H_N = 5.19/14.39 ≈ 36%. The two forms are linked: if the rank-frequency exponent is s, the distribution of item frequencies has tail exponent α = 1/s. For s = 1 that is α = 1, exactly the boundary where the mean stops being finite, which is why totals are dominated by the head.
Why does caching work so well under this law? A cache of C items that always holds the C most popular items has hit rate H_C / H_N for s = 1. With N = 1,000,000 keys, a cache of 1,000 items (0.1%) gets 7.49/14.39 ≈ 52%, and a cache of 10,000 items (1%) gets 9.79/14.39 ≈ 68%. Hit rate grows with the logarithm of cache size. Each tenfold increase in cache adds a fixed slice of about 16 percentage points (ln 10 / 14.39), and the gain per byte of memory falls quickly. For s < 1, as in many web traces, the curve is flatter and caches need to be larger. For s > 1, the head is stronger and small caches do very well.
Skew has the opposite effect on partitioning. Hashing keys to P partitions spreads the keys evenly, but not the traffic. If the top key has share 6.9% and P = 100, the partition holding it receives at least 6.9% of the load against a fair share of 1%, about 7 times the average, and hashing cannot fix this because one key lives in one place. The standard repairs are: split a hot key into several sub-keys (salting, key + random suffix 0…7, aggregated later), replicate hot items to several nodes, cache them in front of the store, or detect hot keys and handle them separately. In Spark, adaptive query execution detects a skewed join partition and splits it; a partition counts as skewed when it is more than 5 times the median size and above 256 MB by default (spark.sql.adaptive.skewJoin settings).
Finally, fitting. To estimate the exponent from n samples above x_min, the maximum likelihood estimator of the tail exponent is α̂ = n / Σ ln(x_i / x_min), with standard error about α̂/√n. Fitting a straight line to a log-log histogram is biased and should be avoided. After fitting, compare with alternatives such as log-normal and exponential using a likelihood-ratio test, because a log-normal looks straight over one or two decades. The deeper question is the generating mechanism: preferential attachment, multiplicative growth with a lower barrier, or optimisation under constraints. A mechanism you can name is better evidence than a straight line.

example: Let us size a cache and a partition plan for a key-value store that serves ad metadata for 1,000,000 campaigns and creatives, with Zipf traffic of exponent s = 1. First compute the normaliser: H_N = ln(1,000,000) + 0.577 = 13.82 + 0.577 = 14.39. Key at rank 1 has share 1/14.39 = 6.9%. If the service handles 100,000 requests per second, that one key alone receives 6,900 requests per second.
Cache. You can afford to cache 5,000 items. The hit rate is H_5000 / H_N = (ln 5000 + 0.577) / 14.39 = (8.52 + 0.577)/14.39 = 9.10/14.39 ≈ 63%. So 63,000 requests per second never reach the store, and the store sees 37,000. Doubling the cache to 10,000 items gives (9.21 + 0.577)/14.39 = 68%, only 5 points more for twice the memory. That is the diminishing return: gain per doubling is ln 2 / 14.39 ≈ 4.8 points.
Partitions. The store has 50 partitions and hashes keys uniformly. With the cache, the hot keys are absorbed and the store sees only 37,000 requests per second, so the hot-key problem moves to the cache tier. Without the cache, the partition with the top key gets 6,900 + (the other keys it holds, about 1/50 of the remaining 93,100) = 6,900 + 1,862 = 8,762 requests per second, against a fair share of 2,000: 4.4 times too high. If you split the top key into 8 sub-keys, each sub-key gets 6,900/8 ≈ 863 requests per second, and the worst partition falls to about 863 + 1,862 ≈ 2,725 requests per second, only 1.4 times the fair share. The cost is that every write to that key must update 8 copies and every read must pick one of them.
Fitting. Suppose you measured five request sizes above x_min = 1 KB: 1.2, 1.5, 2, 4 and 10 KB. The logs of x/x_min are 0.182, 0.405, 0.693, 1.386 and 2.303, which sum to 4.97. The estimate is α̂ = 5/4.97 ≈ 1.01, with standard error about 1.01/√5 ≈ 0.45. Five points is far too few to say anything, and the error shows it: the true exponent could plausibly be anywhere from 0.1 to 1.9. With 5,000 points the error would be about 0.014. Always report the uncertainty, and always compare with a log-normal fit before saying "power law".
intuition: A city where the biggest street gets as many visitors as the next ten streets together, and a long tail of lanes that almost nobody uses.
The shape is natural once you see that popularity feeds itself. A popular key is cached, linked and recommended more, so it becomes more popular. Items that start slightly ahead grow faster, and the gap compounds. Mathematically, a quantity that grows by random multiplicative steps, with a floor under it, spreads over many scales, and spreading over many scales is exactly what a straight line on a log-log plot means. In a bell curve all scales are close to the average, so one scale describes everything. In a power law there is no typical scale, so the average and the typical item are different things, and you must design for the head and the tail separately.
mistakes: People often think a straight line on a log-log plot proves a power law. In fact several distributions, especially the log-normal, look straight over one or two orders of magnitude. Only a proper fit with maximum likelihood, a goodness-of-fit test and a comparison with alternatives supports the claim.
People often think the 80/20 rule is a law with a fixed 80 and 20. In fact the split depends on the exponent. A Pareto distribution with tail exponent α ≈ 1.16 gives the 80/20 split; a different exponent gives 90/10 or 70/30. The rule is a memorable example, not a constant.
People often think that adding more samples always makes the average stable. In fact for a tail exponent below 2 the variance is infinite, and for an exponent below 1 the mean is infinite, so the sample mean keeps jumping as new extreme values arrive. Use medians, quantiles and trimmed means, and plan capacity from the tail, not the average.
People often think hashing removes skew. In fact hashing spreads distinct keys evenly but cannot split the load of a single key. If one key has 7% of the traffic, whatever partition owns it carries at least 7%.
People often think a Zipf exponent measured once stays fixed. In fact popularity shifts over time (a new release, a news event, a campaign launch), so the head changes and the exponent drifts. Measure it on recent traffic and re-check.
tradeoffs: Choose a bigger cache when the exponent is near or above 1 and the working set is large: the first few thousand keys carry half of the traffic, so a small cache pays off at once. Choose other levers when the exponent is below about 0.8: the head is weak, hit rates grow slowly with cache size, and you may need hundreds of times more memory for the same gain. Either way, expect gains of a fixed number of points per tenfold cache increase, not per unit of memory.
Choose key salting or replication of hot keys when you know a small set of keys carries a large share and the reads dominate. You give up simplicity and some write cost, because every update must reach all copies. Choose range or consistent hashing with more partitions when the skew is mild (the top key under about 1% of the load), because splitting costs more than it saves.
Choose medians and p99 instead of means for heavy-tailed measurements such as latencies, bytes per request or revenue per user, and report them with confidence intervals from bootstrap resampling. Choose the mean only when you really need total volume (for billing, total bytes), where the mean times count is the right quantity, and then expect large uncertainty. Choose a log-normal model when your goal is only to describe the bulk; choose a Pareto tail when you must predict extremes, and accept that tail estimates from limited data are very uncertain.
friction: Estimation is hard. The tail is the least observed part of any data set, so the exponent is estimated from the fewest points, and the fitted x_min changes the answer. Real data often follow a power law only over a range, with a cut-off at the top (finite systems cannot have infinite values) and flattening at the bottom. Different generating mechanisms give the same straight line, so a good fit does not tell you why the data look this way, and a model based on the wrong mechanism will extrapolate wrongly.
For operations, heavy tails make testing and capacity planning difficult. A load test with uniform keys hides hot-key behaviour; a load test with a sampled production trace may miss rare giants entirely. Benchmarks that report average latency say little about the user experience at p99.9. Tail latency grows with fan-out: if a request calls 100 services, each with a 1% chance of a slow response, about 63% of requests see at least one slow response.
Skew moves. A fix that handles today's hot key does not handle tomorrow's, and automatic detection (sampling request counts per key, count-min sketches) adds its own cost and delay. Finally, treating a power law as a natural law can be misleading in social contexts: popularity may be produced by the ranking algorithm itself, in which case the "law" can be changed by changing the system.
practice: Always look at the distribution, not only the mean. For any metric you care about (requests per key, bytes per file, rows per join key, latency per call) plot the log-log rank-frequency graph and the CDF, and compute p50, p90, p99 and max. If max is 1,000 times the median, you have a heavy tail.
For caches, measure the real hit-rate curve by replaying a trace at different cache sizes, or use a simulator, instead of trusting a Zipf fit. Expect gains from the first small cache and then flat returns; if the curve is flat from the start, the workload has little reuse and a cache will not help. Prefer eviction policies that use frequency (such as TinyLFU admission, as in Caffeine) when popularity is stable, and recency-based ones when it drifts.
For data skew in Spark, check task time and shuffle read size per task in the UI; one task far above the median points to a hot key. Turn on adaptive query execution and skew join handling, salt the hot key when needed, filter out null and default keys (often the largest "key" is a placeholder such as null or "unknown"), and broadcast the small side of a join. For Kafka and similar systems, choose a partition key with high cardinality and watch per-partition byte rates.
When you must fit a power law, use a maximum likelihood method such as the Python package powerlaw (Alstott, Bullmore and Plenz), report the uncertainty, and compare with log-normal. For study, read Newman's short review first, then Clauset, Shalizi and Newman for the method, and Mitzenmacher for the history of mechanisms.

@@ clt
value: Two theorems explain why measuring a lot of noisy things gives a stable answer. The law of large numbers (LLN) says that the average of n independent samples gets closer and closer to the true mean as n grows. The central limit theorem (CLT) says more: the error of that average has a known shape. It is a bell curve (normal distribution) centred on zero with width σ/√n, where σ is the standard deviation of one sample. This holds almost whatever the shape of the original data, as long as the variance is finite.
These two facts are the reason that polls, quality checks, A/B tests and monitoring dashboards work. One ad impression either gets a click or does not, which is pure noise. A million impressions give a click rate that is correct to about ±0.03 percentage points. You do not need to know how people decide to click; you only need independent samples, a finite variance and a large enough n. The CLT also tells you the price of accuracy: to make the error ten times smaller you need one hundred times more data.
For the layer above, probability (L4) provides the objects the theorems are about: random variables, expected value and variance, and the idea of convergence of distributions. The CLT then feeds confidence intervals in statistics (L4), the tests in hypothesis testing (L4), the 1/√n error of Monte Carlo (L4), the sample sizes in A/B testing (L1) and the estimates of sampling (L2) and HyperLogLog (L2). Concentration inequalities (L4) are the finite-sample, exponential version of the same story.
terms: Sample mean — the average X̄ₙ = (X₁ + … + Xₙ)/n of n observations.
Expected value (μ) and variance (σ²) — the true mean of one observation and its average squared distance from that mean.
Standard error — the standard deviation of the sample mean, σ/√n; it measures how far an average is likely to be from the truth.
i.i.d. — independent and identically distributed: each sample is drawn separately from the same distribution.
Convergence in probability — for any small ε, the chance that the average differs from μ by more than ε goes to 0 as n grows (weak LLN).
Convergence in distribution — the shape of the distribution of a standardised quantity approaches a limit shape; this is the CLT's sense of convergence.
Confidence interval — a range computed from data, such as X̄ ± 1.96 × SE, built so that it contains the truth 95% of the time over repeated experiments.
Skewness — a measure of how lopsided a distribution is; large skewness slows the CLT.
history: Jacob Bernoulli proved the first law of large numbers for coin-like events in "Ars Conjectandi" (published 1713, after his death). He had worked on it for about twenty years, and he wanted to know how many trials are needed to be morally certain that observed frequency matches the true probability. Abraham de Moivre then asked what the error looks like. In 1733 he showed that the number of heads in many fair coin flips follows a bell-shaped curve, which is the first case of the CLT. Pierre-Simon Laplace generalised it in 1810 and used it for errors in astronomical measurements; Gauss had meanwhile used the same curve for measurement error, which is why it is also called the Gaussian.
A rigorous general proof came later. Chebyshev, Markov and Lyapunov in Russia, around 1900, proved CLTs under weaker and weaker conditions; Lindeberg (1922) and Lévy gave the nearly final form. Borel (1909) and Kolmogorov (1930s) proved the strong law, where the average converges to μ with probability 1 along a single long run. Berry (1941) and Esseen (1942) answered the practical question of how fast: the error of the normal approximation is at most a constant times ρ/(σ³√n), where ρ is the third absolute moment, and the constant is below 0.5.
In recent decades the main interest moved to cases where the conditions fail. Heavy-tailed data (Pareto, Lévy) have infinite variance and obey different limit laws, the stable laws. Dependent data (time series, users with many events) need modified versions. And in online experimentation, the CLT became a daily working tool: Kohavi and colleagues describe checking the skewness of a metric to decide whether the normal approximation is safe at the sample size of an experiment.
how: The law of large numbers has a short proof in the weak form. The sample mean has expected value μ and variance σ²/n, because variances of independent variables add and dividing by n divides the variance by n². Chebyshev's inequality (from probability) then says P(|X̄ₙ − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²), which goes to 0 as n grows. So independence gives cancellation: positive and negative errors partly offset each other, and the spread of the average shrinks like 1/√n rather than staying fixed. Notice that this is much weaker than the full story; Chebyshev only says the chance of a big miss falls like 1/n.
The CLT says what the miss looks like. Standardise the mean: Zₙ = √n (X̄ₙ − μ)/σ. Then P(Zₙ ≤ z) → Φ(z), where Φ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. In practice: X̄ₙ is approximately normal with mean μ and standard deviation σ/√n. About 68% of the time the average lies within one standard error of μ, 95% within 1.96, and 99.7% within 3. You can also state it for sums: a sum of n samples has mean nμ and standard deviation σ√n, so the sum grows faster than its noise.
Why a bell curve, whatever the starting shape? One way to see it is through the moment generating function or characteristic function. The characteristic function of a sum of independent variables is the product of their characteristic functions. Take the logarithm and the product becomes a sum of n terms, each of which is, for a standardised variable scaled by 1/√n, about −t²/(2n) plus terms of order n^(−3/2). Adding n of them gives −t²/2, which is the log of the normal characteristic function. Only mean and variance survive, because every higher moment contribution is multiplied by a negative power of n. This is why the shape of the original distribution is forgotten and only μ and σ² matter.
Speed of convergence is given by Berry–Esseen: the largest gap between the true distribution function of Zₙ and Φ is at most C ρ/(σ³√n). For a symmetric, light-tailed distribution (a die, a bounded score) this gap is small by n = 30. For a skewed distribution it can be large. The usual rule "n ≥ 30" is a rule of thumb for mild skew only. A metric with skewness 5 (revenue per user, time on page) may need thousands of samples before 95% intervals have 95% coverage in both tails, and the rule grows with the square of the skewness.
Three conditions are easy to forget. First, independence: if each of m events from one user is correlated with the others, the effective sample size is n/(1 + (m − 1)ρ) (the design effect), where ρ is the correlation inside a group. Second, finite variance: if σ² is infinite (Pareto tail exponent below 2), the CLT fails, and the average of n samples is dominated by the largest ones and moves like n^(1/α − 1) instead of n^(−1/2). Third, identical distribution is not essential (Lindeberg's version allows different distributions if none dominates), but a drifting population breaks the idea of one μ.
Applications follow from the same formulas. A proportion p estimated from n yes/no samples has SE = √(p(1 − p)/n). The difference between two independent group means has SE = √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂), which is the basis of the two-sample z-test used in A/B testing. For a ratio metric such as click-through rate, where the denominator is also random, the delta method (a first-order Taylor expansion from calculus) gives the standard error. For a function g of an average, SE(g(X̄)) ≈ |g′(μ)| σ/√n. The bootstrap uses the same idea without formulas: resample the data many times, and the spread of the resampled means estimates the standard error.

example: Let us estimate the click-through rate (CTR) of an ad and then compare two ads. Suppose the true CTR is about 2%, so one impression is a Bernoulli variable with p = 0.02 and variance p(1 − p) = 0.0196, σ = 0.14. After n = 1,000,000 impressions, the standard error of the observed rate is 0.14/√1,000,000 = 0.00014, or 0.014 percentage points. The 95% interval is the observed rate ± 1.96 × 0.00014 = ± 0.027 points. If we observe 2.00%, the true CTR lies between about 1.973% and 2.027%. To halve this interval we need four times as many impressions: 4,000,000.
Now compare ad A and ad B, each shown to 100,000 users. A has 2.00% CTR and B has 2.10%. Each rate has SE = √(0.02 × 0.98/100,000) = 0.000443, i.e. 0.044 points. The difference has SE = √2 × 0.000443 = 0.000626, i.e. 0.063 points. The observed difference is 0.10 points, which is 0.10/0.063 = 1.6 standard errors. That is below 1.96, so it is not significant at the 5% level. The 95% interval for the true difference is 0.10 ± 0.123 points, which contains zero. The data are consistent with B being slightly better, equal or slightly worse. To detect a 0.1 point difference reliably you need roughly 7.85 × 2 × 0.0196 / (0.001)² ≈ 308,000 users per arm for 80% power at the 5% level, three times what we had.
Now see how the assumptions fail. Take 1,000 users with 100 events each, and suppose events from the same user are correlated with ρ = 0.5. The nominal sample size is 100,000, but the effective sample size is 100,000/(1 + 99 × 0.5) = 100,000/50.5 ≈ 1,980. The true standard error is about 7 times larger than the naive formula gives, and a naive test would call noise "significant" far too often. The fix is to treat the user as the unit: average events within each user first and then use the 1,000 user means.
Finally the heavy tail. Take samples from a Pareto distribution with α = 1.5 (mean 3, infinite variance). The CLT does not apply. The error of the sample mean shrinks like n^(−1/3), so going from 1,000 to 1,000,000 samples reduces typical error by a factor of 10, not 31.6 (√1000). Moreover the errors are lopsided: most sample means fall below 3, and occasionally one jumps well above it.
intuition: A crowd of people each guessing the weight of an ox: each guess is far off, but the errors point in random directions and cancel, so the average is close.
The reason the shape is always a bell is that the errors of many independent small pushes combine by addition, and adding independent random effects forgets all details except their typical size. Many different microscopic causes give one macroscopic shape, in the same way that the details of individual molecule collisions disappear in the smooth law of gas pressure. The width is σ/√n because independent errors add as squares (like the sides of a right triangle), not as plain sums: n errors of size σ combine to size σ√n, and dividing by n gives σ/√n.
mistakes: People often think the CLT says that data become normally distributed when there is a lot of data. In fact it says that the average of the data becomes approximately normal. The raw observations keep their own shape, however many you collect. A million revenue values are still very skewed; their mean is the quantity that is nearly normal.
People often think n = 30 is always enough. In fact it is a rule for light-tailed, nearly symmetric data. For skewed metrics, such as revenue per user, you may need thousands or more, and for infinite-variance data no n is enough for the normal approximation. Check by bootstrapping or simulation.
People often think the law of large numbers means that after a run of heads, tails becomes more likely (the gambler's fallacy). In fact the law says the average converges, not that past deviations are corrected. Later flips are independent; the early excess of heads is not cancelled, it is only diluted by the growing total.
People often think more data always fixes a bias. In fact the LLN makes the average converge to the mean of the distribution you are sampling, which may not be the one you want. A biased sample (only users who open the app, only logged-in users) converges confidently to the wrong answer.
People often think the CLT needs identical distributions or that a single big source is fine. In fact it needs that no single term dominates the sum. One giant customer in a sum of revenues breaks it, even when the other terms are many and well behaved.
tradeoffs: Choose the normal approximation (z or t intervals) when the metric is light-tailed, the units are independent and n is large, because it is cheap, fast and standard. Choose the bootstrap when the metric is skewed, a ratio or a quantile (median, p95) and you have a few thousand units or more: it costs several hundred to a few thousand resamples of compute, but it needs no formula and shows skew directly. Neither helps when the sample is biased or the observations are correlated.
Choose a finite-sample bound (Hoeffding, Chernoff, from concentration inequalities) when you need a guarantee that holds for the given n, for example a safety check or a randomized algorithm with a failure budget. Choose the CLT interval when you want the tightest practical estimate and can accept an approximation error that shrinks with n. At 95%, a Hoeffding interval for a rate in [0, 1] has half-width √(ln(40)/2)/√n = 1.36/√n. The CLT interval is 0.98/√n when p = 0.5, so Hoeffding is about 1.4 times wider there, and 0.27/√n when p = 0.02, so Hoeffding is about 5 times wider, because Hoeffding ignores that the variance is small. That is the price of the guarantee.
Choose to randomise and analyse at the level of the user (or the household, device, or whatever unit the treatment is applied to) rather than the event, even though it reduces the nominal sample size by a large factor. You give up apparent precision and gain correct error bars. Choose robust summaries (median, trimmed mean, capping extreme values at the 99th percentile) when the tail is heavy: they converge fast and have small variance, but they estimate a different quantity than the mean, and capping adds bias that you must accept explicitly.
friction: The CLT is an asymptotic statement: it tells you what happens as n goes to infinity, and the practical question is whether your n is large enough. Berry–Esseen gives a bound, but with the third moment, which is itself hard to estimate when the tail is heavy. The coverage of the intervals in the tails (probability of an error beyond 3 standard errors) is where the approximation is worst, and these are the cases that matter for rare events and for multiple testing with a small p-value threshold.
Real data break the assumptions often. Users come back and are correlated with themselves; neighbours in a network influence each other, so treatment spills over; traffic changes during the day, so samples are not identically distributed; and peeking at results while the sample grows changes the meaning of the interval. Heavy-tailed metrics are common in revenue, session length and latency, and for them the sample variance is itself unstable, so the estimated standard error is unreliable.
Finally, the CLT gives a good answer to "where is the mean?" and a poor answer to "what is the worst case?". Extreme values of a sum follow different laws, so it is the wrong tool for tail risk, capacity planning at p99.99, or security guarantees.
practice: In every experiment or estimate, write down the unit of independence first (user, device, household, request) and compute the metric per unit before taking averages. Report the estimate with its standard error, not a bare average. For a proportion use √(p(1 − p)/n); for a difference of means use √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂); for ratio metrics use the delta method or a bootstrap over units.
Before trusting a normal interval, look at the skewness and the largest few values of the metric. If the maximum is a large fraction of the total sum, the CLT will not help. Simulate it: take your own data, resample groups of your planned size, and check whether the histogram of the means looks like a bell. Cap or winsorise extreme values if the business question allows it, and say so.
Plan the sample size before the test: n per arm ≈ 2 (z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² σ²/δ², where δ is the smallest difference that matters; with α = 0.05 and 80% power this is about 15.7 σ²/δ². Remember that halving δ quadruples n. Use variance reduction (CUPED, stratification) to reduce σ² and get the same precision with fewer users. For study, work through Wasserman's chapter 5, then see the 3Blue1Brown videos on the CLT for intuition, and simulate the dice, coin and Pareto cases yourself in Python.
quiz: What is the difference between the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem? || The LLN says the sample mean converges to the true mean as n grows. The CLT describes the error: the standardised mean √n (X̄ − μ)/σ approaches a standard normal distribution, so the error has width about σ/√n.
Why does the standard error shrink like 1/√n and not 1/n? || The variance of a sum of n independent samples is nσ², so the standard deviation of the sum is σ√n. Dividing by n to form the mean gives σ√n/n = σ/√n. Independent errors partly cancel but do not cancel completely.
A click-through rate of 2% is measured on 1,000,000 impressions. What is the 95% interval, and how many impressions are needed to halve it? || SE = √(0.02 × 0.98/1,000,000) ≈ 0.000140, so the interval is about ±0.027 percentage points (1.973% to 2.027%). Halving it needs four times as many impressions, 4,000,000.
You have 1,000 users with 100 events each, with within-user correlation 0.5. What is the effective sample size, and what should you do? || About 100,000/(1 + 99 × 0.5) ≈ 1,980. Compute one number per user and analyse the 1,000 user means, or use cluster-robust standard errors.
Revenue per user has a very heavy tail and your dashboard average jumps by 20% between days. Would you add more days of data, or change the method? || Adding data helps only slowly, and not at all for infinite variance. Check skewness and the top values, then use a robust or capped metric, medians or quantiles, or a bootstrap over users, and report the cost of that choice (bias from capping).

@@ hypothesis
value: Hypothesis testing is a procedure for deciding whether a difference in data is a real effect or only the result of random noise. You have two ad creatives, one with a 5.00% conversion rate and one with 5.40%. Is the second better, or did it get lucky users? The test answers by asking a precise, backwards question: if there were truly no difference, how often would random variation alone produce a gap at least this large? If the answer is very rarely, such as less than 1 time in 20, you treat the difference as real.
This is the statistical core of A/B testing and experiment platforms, model evaluation (is model B really better than model A on this benchmark?), anomaly alerts, fraud checks and data-quality checks. It gives a shared rule for decisions that many people make: a fixed false-alarm rate that is stated in advance, and a sample size chosen so that real effects of a useful size are detected. Without it, teams ship noise, because any metric will move a little in every comparison.
For the layer above, probability (L4) gives the meaning of "how often would this happen": a p-value is a conditional probability computed under the assumption of no effect. The central limit theorem (L4) gives the shape of the test statistic: for large samples, an average or a difference of averages is approximately normal, so we can compute the p-value from a table or one function call. Epistemology (L5) gives the reason for the odd backward logic: evidence can contradict a hypothesis much more clearly than it can prove one, and the question of how much evidence justifies a belief has no purely mathematical answer.
terms: Null hypothesis (H₀) — the default claim of "no effect", for example the two variants have the same conversion rate.
Alternative hypothesis (H₁) — the claim that there is an effect, either in any direction (two-sided) or in one direction (one-sided).
Test statistic — a number computed from the data, such as z = (difference) / (standard error), that measures how far the data are from H₀.
p-value — the probability, assuming H₀ is true, of getting a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed.
Significance level (α) — the false-positive rate you accept in advance, often 0.05: the chance of rejecting H₀ when H₀ is true (Type I error).
Power (1 − β) — the probability of rejecting H₀ when a real effect of a given size exists; β is the Type II error (missing a real effect).
Minimum detectable effect (MDE) — the smallest true difference you want the test to find with the planned power.
Confidence interval — a range of effect sizes consistent with the data; it shows both the size and the uncertainty of the effect.
history: The modern test began with small samples and beer. In 1908 William Sealy Gosset, a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, published under the name "Student" the t-distribution, which gives the right answer when the standard deviation is estimated from only a few barley or hop samples. Ronald Fisher, in "Statistical Methods for Research Workers" (1925), turned this into a general practice: compute a p-value and treat p < 0.05 as a convenient threshold for "worth a second look". Fisher's 1935 book "The Design of Experiments" introduced the randomisation of treatments, and the story of a woman who claimed to taste whether milk or tea was poured first.
Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson, in 1933, framed the problem differently: choose a decision rule in advance, with a fixed Type I error rate, and pick the test that has the highest power at that rate (the Neyman–Pearson lemma shows the likelihood-ratio test is best for simple hypotheses). Modern textbooks mix the two views, although Fisher and Neyman disagreed bitterly. Abraham Wald (1945) developed sequential testing, where data are examined as they arrive and the test stops when evidence is enough, which is the root of today's methods for safe early stopping.
Concerns grew from the 1990s. Benjamini and Hochberg (1995) controlled the false discovery rate instead of the chance of any error, which suits many comparisons. Ioannidis (2005) argued that most published research findings are false, because of small samples, many comparisons and selective reporting, and the replication crisis in psychology confirmed part of this. In 2016 the American Statistical Association issued a statement on p-values, listing six principles, including that a p-value does not measure the probability that the hypothesis is true. For online experiments, Kohavi and colleagues (book 2020) made the practice standard in industry, Deng et al. (2013) introduced CUPED for variance reduction, and Johari et al. (2017) gave always-valid p-values that remain correct when results are checked continuously.
how: A test has five steps. First, state H₀ and H₁ and the smallest effect that matters. Second, choose α (say 0.05) and the power (say 80%), and compute the sample size n before collecting data. Third, collect the data without changing the plan. Fourth, compute the test statistic. Fifth, compute the p-value and compare with α, and also report an effect size with a confidence interval.
For comparing two proportions or means, the statistic is z = (observed difference)/SE, where SE is the standard error of the difference. By the central limit theorem, under H₀ this z is approximately standard normal for large n. The two-sided p-value is the area of the standard normal beyond ±|z|. Some anchors: |z| = 1.64 gives p = 0.10, |z| = 1.96 gives p = 0.05, |z| = 2.58 gives p = 0.01 and |z| = 3.29 gives p = 0.001. When the standard deviation is estimated from a small sample, Student's t-distribution replaces the normal, which has heavier tails; for n above about 100 the two are nearly equal.
The probability tools matter in how to read the p-value. It is P(data this extreme | H₀), not P(H₀ | data). To convert one to the other you also need the prior chance that the effect is real and the power, through Bayes' rule. If only 10% of your ideas truly work, a test with α = 0.05 and 80% power gives, out of 1,000 ideas, 80 true positives and 45 false positives (5% of the 900 ideas that do not work). So 36% of "significant" results are false, even though each test was run correctly. The p-value controls errors when H₀ is true; it does not tell you what fraction of your wins are real.
Sample size comes from requiring that the sampling distributions under H₀ and under the true effect δ are separated enough. For two groups with equal size, n per group ≈ 2 (z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² σ²/δ². With α = 0.05 (z = 1.96) and 80% power (z = 0.84), the constant is 2 × (2.80)² = 15.7. Two consequences are worth remembering. The sample size scales with 1/δ², so detecting an effect half as large needs four times the users. And it scales with the variance σ², so anything that reduces variance cuts the cost. CUPED does this by subtracting a part of the metric that can be predicted from pre-experiment behaviour: Y′ = Y − θ(X − mean of X), where X is the same user's pre-period value and θ = cov(X, Y)/var(X). The variance falls by the factor (1 − ρ²), where ρ is the correlation between X and Y. With ρ = 0.7, variance falls by 51%, so the required sample is about half.
Several problems need corrections. Multiple comparisons: with m independent tests at α = 0.05, the chance of at least one false positive is 1 − 0.95^m, which is 64% for m = 20. Bonferroni tests each at α/m (simple, conservative); Benjamini–Hochberg sorts the p-values and controls the false discovery rate, which is more powerful when there are many tests. Peeking: if you check a fixed-horizon test every day and stop when p < 0.05, the true false-positive rate is about 14% after five looks and about 19% after ten, and it keeps growing. Sequential methods (group sequential boundaries, mSPRT, always-valid p-values) spend the error budget over time to allow monitoring safely. Finally, check the setup before the result: a sample ratio mismatch (the traffic split is 50.7/49.3 when 50/50 was intended, with a very small p-value from a chi-square test) signals a bug that invalidates the experiment.

example: Take an A/B test of a new ad layout. Control A has a conversion rate of 5.00% and treatment B shows 5.40%, with 50,000 users in each group. H₀ says the true rates are equal.
Step 1, standard errors. For A: √(0.05 × 0.95/50,000) = √(9.5 × 10⁻⁷) = 0.000975. For B: √(0.054 × 0.946/50,000) = √(1.022 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.001011. The difference has SE = √(9.5 × 10⁻⁷ + 1.022 × 10⁻⁶) = √(1.972 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.001404, that is 0.14 percentage points.
Step 2, statistic. The observed difference is 0.004 (0.40 points). z = 0.004/0.001404 = 2.85. The two-sided p-value is 2 × 0.0022 = 0.0044. Because 0.0044 < 0.05, we reject H₀. The 95% confidence interval for the true lift is 0.004 ± 1.96 × 0.001404 = 0.004 ± 0.00275, so from 0.125 to 0.675 points. Read this correctly: the lift is positive but its size is uncertain by a factor of five. In relative terms it is a 2.5% to 13.5% improvement on the base of 5%.
Step 3, was the test planned well? For a minimum detectable effect δ = 0.004 and pooled rate 0.052, the variance of one user is 0.052 × 0.948 = 0.0493. The required n per group is 15.7 × 0.0493/(0.004)² = 15.7 × 0.0493/0.000016 ≈ 48,400. We used 50,000, so the power is a little above 80%, as designed. Had the true lift been 0.2 points, the same test would have had power of only about 30%, and most likely would show "not significant" even though B is better. "Not significant" does not mean "no effect".
Step 4, multiple metrics. The team also looked at 19 other metrics (clicks, revenue, session length, and so on). If all were unrelated to the change, the chance that at least one shows p < 0.05 is 1 − 0.95²⁰ = 64%. Bonferroni would require p < 0.05/20 = 0.0025 for each metric. The conversion lift with p = 0.0044 would not pass that strict bar. The remedy is to decide in advance which one metric is primary (here conversion), treat the others as secondary with a stricter rule, and report them honestly.
Step 5, decision. The result is evidence that B improves conversion, but it is not a proof. If B costs engineering effort or lowers revenue per conversion, you compare the interval (0.125 to 0.675 points) against the cost, not only the p-value.
intuition: A court that assumes the defendant is innocent and convicts only when the evidence would be very unlikely under innocence.
The backward logic is natural because "no difference" is a single precise claim, so you can compute what data it predicts, while "some difference" is a whole family of claims with no single prediction. So you test the one claim you can compute, and if the data look very strange under it, you stop believing it. This follows the old idea from philosophy of science that a theory is tested by trying to refute it. The price is that a small p-value only says the data are strange under H₀. It does not say that your preferred explanation is true, and it says nothing about whether the effect is large enough to matter.
mistakes: People often think the p-value is the probability that the null hypothesis is true, or that the result is due to chance. In fact it is the probability of data this extreme if H₀ were true. Getting from there to the chance that H₀ is true needs a prior and the power, as the 36% false-wins calculation shows.
People often think p > 0.05 means there is no effect. In fact it means the data do not rule out zero. With low power, a real effect often gives p > 0.05. Look at the confidence interval: a wide one that includes both zero and a useful effect means "we do not know yet".
People often think a smaller p-value means a larger effect. In fact p depends on both effect size and sample size. With 50 million users a lift of 0.01 points can give p < 0.001 and still be worthless for the business. Statistical significance is not practical significance, so always report the effect with its interval.
People often think peeking at the dashboard is harmless if you only decide at the end. In fact repeated looks give extra chances for a false positive. Stopping the first time p < 0.05 gives a false-positive rate far above 5%. Either fix the sample size and look once, or use a sequential method designed for repeated looks.
People often think you can change the analysis after seeing the data. In fact choosing the metric, the subgroup or the cutoff after seeing which one gives a small p-value is multiple testing in disguise (p-hacking). Each of those choices multiplies the number of implicit tests.
tradeoffs: Choose a fixed-horizon test when the sample size can be set in advance, the cost of a delayed decision is small and the team will not peek. It is the simplest and has the most power for a given n. Choose a sequential or always-valid method when stakeholders will look at results every day, or when stopping early on a harmful change matters; you pay with some loss of power (more samples are needed to reach the same power) in exchange for valid error rates under monitoring.
Choose Bonferroni when a few tests matter and a single false positive is costly (a safety metric, a launch gate). Choose Benjamini–Hochberg when you screen many hypotheses, such as hundreds of segments or features, and can tolerate a known fraction (say 5 to 10%) of false discoveries among the findings; it finds more true effects but accepts some mistakes. Choose a smaller α (0.01 or 0.005) when the prior chance that an idea works is low or when a wrong launch is expensive; you give up power or need more users.
Choose a Bayesian analysis (a posterior probability that B beats A, expected loss) when decisions are made continuously, there is useful prior information, and the audience wants the answer "what is the chance B is better". You gain a direct statement and flexible stopping, and you give up the guarantee on long-run error rates unless you check it by simulation, and you must defend the prior. Choose non-parametric or permutation tests, or the bootstrap, for skewed or small samples, at the cost of more compute and less simple formulas.
Choose variance reduction (CUPED, stratification, a better metric) before choosing a larger sample. The reduction can be large when pre-period behaviour predicts the metric well, and it is cheaper than waiting for twice the traffic.
friction: The method rests on assumptions that online data often break. Units must be independent, but users have several devices, households share TVs and neighbours influence each other (network effects), so the treatment spills over into the control group. In a two-sided marketplace or an ad auction, giving one group a higher bid changes prices for the other group. Effects change over time: a novelty effect makes new things attract clicks at first, and a long-term effect may be different from a two-week effect.
Interpretation is the weakest point. The p-value is misread by students and experts alike, which is why the ASA issued its statement. Binary "significant or not" thinking throws away information; a result of p = 0.049 and p = 0.051 are almost the same evidence. Winners' curse is a quiet problem: among experiments chosen because they were significant, the estimated lift is biased upward, and the true lift after launch is on average smaller. Holdouts and replications usually show a smaller lift than the original test.
Metrics also cause trouble. Ratio metrics (revenue per session) need the delta method or a bootstrap; heavy-tailed metrics need capping or robust methods; short-term metrics may not predict long-term value. And the sample-size formula needs σ² and δ as inputs, both of which are guesses before the experiment. A wrong guess of σ² by a factor of 2 changes n by a factor of 2.
practice: Write a pre-registration note for each experiment: the hypothesis, the primary metric, the guardrail metrics (latency, errors, revenue), the unit of randomisation, α, power, MDE and the planned duration. Compute n from the MDE and a variance estimated from historic data, and run for at least one full weekly cycle to cover day-of-week effects, even if n is reached sooner.
Before reading results, run the checks: sample ratio mismatch test, A/A test history (a platform run on identical variants should show p < 0.05 about 5% of the time), and balance on pre-experiment variables. Report the effect with a confidence interval, in the units the business uses, together with the p-value. Use CUPED when you have a pre-period. Use a stricter threshold or a correction for secondary metrics and segments, and label post-hoc findings as hypotheses for a follow-up test.
Use a holdout group to measure the long-term cumulative effect of many launches, since each small win is overestimated. For model comparison, use paired tests on the same examples (a paired bootstrap or McNemar's test) rather than comparing two separate averages, because the paired difference has much smaller variance. To study, read Kohavi, Tang and Xu for the practice, Wasserman for the theory, and the ASA statement for the cautions.

@@ montecarlo
value: Monte Carlo methods answer a hard question by running a random experiment many times and averaging the results. If you cannot compute the chance that a complex system fails, simulate it ten thousand times and count the failures. If you cannot compute an integral over 50 dimensions, pick random points and average the function. The answer is not exact, but its error is known, and it shrinks in a predictable way as you add samples: the error is about σ/√n, whatever the number of dimensions.
That last property is the reason the method is so widely used. Classical grid methods for integration need a number of points that grows exponentially with the number of dimensions (10 points per axis in 10 dimensions is 10 billion points). Monte Carlo needs the same number of samples in 10 dimensions as in 1 for the same error, as long as the variance of the function stays similar. The cost is slow convergence: one more digit of accuracy needs 100 times more samples.
For the layer above, probability (L4) tells us that an expectation can be estimated by an average of samples, and that estimators are random variables with their own mean and variance. The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem (L4) tell us the estimate converges and what its error looks like, so each Monte Carlo result can come with a confidence interval. Deterministic simulation testing (L2) uses random fault schedules to explore rare interleavings of events. Monte Carlo tree search (L2) uses random game playouts to estimate how good a position is. The same machinery sits behind Bayesian inference, risk models, rendering and reinforcement learning.
terms: Estimator — a rule that turns random samples into an estimate; the Monte Carlo estimator of E[f(X)] is the average of f over samples.
Standard error — the typical size of the estimator's error, σ/√n, where σ is the standard deviation of f(X).
Variance reduction — techniques that lower σ (or the effective σ) without adding bias, so that fewer samples give the same accuracy.
Importance sampling — sampling from a different distribution q that puts more samples where f matters, and correcting with weights p(x)/q(x).
Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) — a way to draw samples from a distribution that is hard to sample directly, by running a Markov chain whose long-run distribution is the target.
Burn-in — the first steps of a chain, discarded because the chain has not yet reached its long-run distribution.
Effective sample size — the number of independent samples that would give the same accuracy as n correlated chain samples; it is smaller than n.
Quasi-Monte Carlo — using deterministic, evenly spread points (such as Sobol sequences) instead of random ones, which can converge faster for smooth functions.
history: The method was invented in 1946 at Los Alamos. Stanislaw Ulam, recovering from illness, played solitaire and wondered how to estimate the chance of winning a game. Counting all arrangements was too hard, but playing many random games and counting wins was easy. He saw that the same idea would work for the physics problems of the nuclear weapons project, such as how neutrons travel through material, where the equations were too hard to solve. He discussed it with John von Neumann, who designed the first computer programs for it on the ENIAC in 1948. Nicholas Metropolis suggested the name "Monte Carlo", after the casino where Ulam's uncle used to borrow money to gamble. Metropolis and Ulam published "The Monte Carlo Method" in the Journal of the American Statistical Association in 1949.
Variance reduction came early, because computers were slow. Herman Kahn and Theodore Harris, in the early 1950s, described stratified sampling, "Russian roulette" and splitting for particle transport. The second major step was to sample from distributions that cannot be sampled directly. In 1953 Metropolis, the Rosenbluths and the Tellers published the algorithm for simulating the equation of state of a liquid, which is now called Metropolis sampling. W. K. Hastings generalised it in 1970, and Geman and Geman (1984) introduced Gibbs sampling for image restoration. Bayesian statistics adopted these around 1990 (Gelfand and Smith), which turned previously impossible models into routine ones.
More recent advances are Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (Duane et al., 1987, later popularised by Neal) and the No-U-Turn Sampler (Hoffman and Gelman, 2014) used in the Stan package. Kocsis and Szepesvári (2006) combined random playouts with a bandit rule to make Monte Carlo tree search, and the same idea was a core part of AlphaGo in 2016. In software engineering, simulation with random seeds has become a way to test distributed systems, where a recorded seed replays any failing run exactly.
how: The basic estimator is simple. To estimate μ = E[f(X)] where X has distribution p, draw X₁ … Xₙ independently from p and compute μ̂ = (1/n) Σ f(Xᵢ). The estimator is unbiased, because the expected value of each f(Xᵢ) is μ. Its variance is σ²/n, where σ² = Var(f(X)), so the standard error is σ/√n. By the central limit theorem, μ̂ is approximately normal for large n, and an approximate 95% interval is μ̂ ± 1.96 σ̂/√n, where σ̂ is the sample standard deviation computed from the same samples. This gives the first useful property: every Monte Carlo answer can report its own error.
Why is the rate independent of dimension? Nothing in the argument depends on the dimension of X. The variance of f(X) is a property of the function and the distribution. A grid method with m points per axis uses n = m^d points, and a good rule such as the trapezoid method has error about m⁻² = n^(−2/d). In d = 10 that is n^(−0.2), much worse than n^(−0.5). The honest caveat is that σ itself may grow with dimension or be dominated by rare regions, so "independent of dimension" refers to the rate, not to the constant.
Because the rate is fixed at 1/√n, the main way to improve is to reduce σ. Importance sampling is the most important tool. We want E_p[f] = ∫ f(x) p(x) dx = ∫ f(x) (p(x)/q(x)) q(x) dx = E_q[f · w], where w = p/q. Draw from q, and average f(x) w(x). If q is large where |f| p is large, the weighted values have a small variance. The ideal q is proportional to |f| p, but that needs the answer. A bad q (thinner tails than f p) can give infinite variance, so q should have heavier tails than the product.
Other tools are: control variates (subtract a correlated quantity with a known mean), antithetic variates (pair a sample with its mirror image so that errors cancel), stratified sampling (divide the space into regions and sample each, which removes the between-region variance) and quasi-Monte Carlo (low-discrepancy sequences, error about (log n)^d/n for smooth functions, better than 1/√n in moderate dimensions). Random number generators must be good and reproducible: use a seeded, well-tested generator (PCG, Philox, Xoshiro), give each parallel worker a separate stream, and record the seed so any run can be replayed.
When p cannot be sampled directly, as with a Bayesian posterior known only up to a constant (p(x) ∝ π(x)), use MCMC. The Metropolis–Hastings algorithm builds a Markov chain: from the current x propose x′ from a proposal q(x′ | x), and accept with probability min(1, π(x′) q(x | x′) / (π(x) q(x′ | x))). If rejected, stay at x. The normalising constant cancels in the ratio, which is the whole point. This rule satisfies detailed balance, so π is the stationary distribution of the chain, and from the Markov chain's ergodic theorem, averages along the chain converge to expectations under π. The samples are correlated, so the effective sample size is n divided by the integrated autocorrelation time. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo uses the gradient of log π (from calculus) to propose far-away moves with high acceptance, which is why it works well in hundreds of dimensions.
Diagnosing MCMC is harder than diagnosing plain Monte Carlo. There is no certificate that the chain has converged. Practice is to run several chains from dispersed starting points and compare them with the R-hat statistic (values near 1.00, below about 1.01, are reassuring), check trace plots, discard burn-in, and report the effective sample size.
Two practical rules complete the picture. First, you can plan the run: measure σ̂ on a small pilot batch of, say, 1,000 samples, then compute the n that gives the target error, n = (1.96 σ̂/ε)² for a half-width ε. Because samples are independent, the work splits across machines with no coordination except combining sums, so Monte Carlo suits Spark and GPUs well. Second, the same idea applies to data you already have. The bootstrap treats your sample as the population, resamples it with replacement many times, recomputes a statistic each time, and reads the standard error from the spread. This is Monte Carlo applied to the sampling distribution, and it works for medians, ratios and model metrics where no formula for the standard error exists.

example: Take the classic estimate of π. Throw n random points into the unit square. A point (x, y) is inside the quarter circle if x² + y² ≤ 1, which happens with probability p = π/4 ≈ 0.7854. Our estimator is π̂ = 4 × (fraction inside). Each indicator has variance p(1 − p) = 0.7854 × 0.2146 = 0.1686, so σ = 0.411. The standard error of π̂ is 4 × 0.411/√n = 1.64/√n. With n = 10,000 points the typical error is 0.016, so you might get 3.14 or 3.17. With n = 1,000,000 the error is 0.0016. To get an error of 0.001 you need n = (1.64/0.001)² ≈ 2.7 million points. This is a poor way to compute π, but it shows the law: each digit costs 100 times more work.
Now a case where plain sampling fails, and importance sampling helps. We want the tail probability P(Z > 4) for a standard normal Z. The exact value is 3.17 × 10⁻⁵. With plain Monte Carlo, n = 1,000,000 draws give about 32 hits. The relative standard error is √((1 − p)/(np)) = √(1/31.7) ≈ 18%. To get 1% relative error you need n = (1 − p)/(p × 0.01²) ≈ 3.2 × 10⁸ draws. Most of that work produces zeros.
Instead, sample from q = N(4, 1), which is centred on the rare region. The weight is w(x) = φ(x)/φ(x − 4) = exp(−4x + 8). The estimator is the average of 1{x > 4} × w(x). Half of the draws now land above 4. The second moment of one weighted sample is ∫ from 4 to ∞ of φ(x) e^(−4x+8) dx. Completing the square, φ(x) e^(−4x+8) = e^16 × φ(x + 4), so the integral is e^16 × P(Z > 8) = 8.89 × 10⁶ × 6.22 × 10⁻¹⁶ = 5.53 × 10⁻⁹. The variance is 5.53 × 10⁻⁹ − (3.17 × 10⁻⁵)² = 4.53 × 10⁻⁹, so σ = 6.7 × 10⁻⁵. The relative standard deviation of one sample is 6.7 × 10⁻⁵/3.17 × 10⁻⁵ ≈ 2.1, against 178 for plain sampling. For 1% relative error you need n = (2.1/0.01)² ≈ 45,000 draws. That is about 7,000 times fewer draws than 3.2 × 10⁸, from one change of sampling distribution.
Last, a one-line MCMC picture. Target π(x) ∝ exp(−x²/2) (a normal, unnormalised). Start at x = 0 and propose x′ = x + uniform(−1, 1). If x = 0.5 and x′ = 1.2, the ratio is exp(−0.72)/exp(−0.125) = exp(−0.595) = 0.55, so accept with probability 0.55. Moves to higher density are always accepted; moves to lower density are accepted sometimes. After a few thousand steps, the histogram of visited x values matches the normal curve.
intuition: To learn the average height of a country, you do not measure everyone; you measure a few thousand random people and the average settles quickly.
The method is natural because an integral is just an average over a distribution, and an average can be estimated by a sample. The surprising part is that the accuracy depends on how many samples you take, and not on how complicated the space is. Each new random point gives an independent piece of information about the whole function, in every dimension at once. A grid spends its points on exploring the shape of the space; random sampling spends them on the answer. Importance sampling goes further: put the samples where they carry the most information.
mistakes: People often think Monte Carlo is a method of last resort because it is random and approximate. In fact in high dimensions it is often the only method with a reasonable cost, and it comes with an error estimate that deterministic methods often lack.
People often think more samples always fix the problem. In fact the error shrinks only as 1/√n, so rare events and tails need a different approach. If the event has probability 10⁻⁶, you need about 10⁸ samples for a 10% error with plain sampling. Use importance sampling, splitting or a different formulation.
People often think the samples from MCMC are independent. In fact consecutive samples are correlated, sometimes strongly. Ten thousand correlated draws might be worth only 100 independent ones. Report the effective sample size, not the raw count.
People often think a chain that looks stable has converged. In fact a single chain can sit in one mode of a multi-modal distribution and look perfectly stable while missing other modes. Run several chains from different starting points and compare them.
People often think that using the same random seed everywhere is a bug. In fact fixed seeds are good practice for debugging and for reproducible tests; what matters is that production estimates use enough independent streams, and that parallel workers do not accidentally share a stream.
tradeoffs: Choose plain Monte Carlo when the integrand is cheap, the dimension is more than a handful and the quantity is not extreme. It is simple, parallel by nature and has a built-in error bar. Choose deterministic quadrature when the dimension is 1 to 3 and the function is smooth, because a few hundred points can give 10 digits where Monte Carlo would need 10²⁰ samples.
Choose quasi-Monte Carlo (Sobol or Halton points with random scrambling) when the function is smooth and the effective dimension is moderate; you often get error close to 1/n instead of 1/√n, at the price of losing the simple independent-sample error bar unless you randomise and repeat. Choose importance sampling, splitting or stratification when the quantity depends on rare regions, and accept that a poor proposal can make things worse by creating infinite variance or a few huge weights.
Choose MCMC when you need samples from a distribution known only up to a constant, such as a Bayesian posterior; choose variational inference or Laplace approximation when you need speed and can accept a biased approximation, as in large machine-learning models. MCMC is asymptotically exact but slow and hard to diagnose; variational methods are fast but their error is hard to know. Choose a simulation with a fixed seed when you test a system (reproducibility), and different seeds when you estimate a quantity (independence).
friction: The 1/√n rate is slow, and nothing beats it for plain sampling. Each digit costs 100 times more samples. Rare events are the worst case: relative error scales as 1/√(np), so very small p needs enormous n. Variance reduction needs knowledge of the problem, and a wrong proposal in importance sampling can give estimates that look stable but are dominated by a few huge weights; the effective sample size of the weights, (Σw)²/Σw², is a useful check.
Quality of randomness and reproducibility matter. A weak generator (or a bad seeding scheme in parallel code) gives correlated streams and quiet errors. Floating-point accumulation of many small terms loses accuracy, so use pairwise summation or compensated sums. MCMC adds its own problems: slow mixing in high dimensions and multi-modal targets, the need to tune step sizes, and the lack of a proof of convergence from finite output. Finally, the answer is only as good as the model of the random process: a precise estimate from a wrong simulation is still wrong, and the error bar covers only sampling noise, not model error.
practice: Always report the standard error or an interval along with the estimate, and compute it from the samples. Start with plain Monte Carlo as a baseline, check that the error is acceptable, and only then add variance reduction. Use a good, seeded generator, one independent stream per worker (for example numpy's SeedSequence.spawn or JAX keys), and log the seed.
In data work, you meet Monte Carlo in sampling (TABLESAMPLE in Spark SQL, approximate counts), in the bootstrap (resample your data to get error bars for any statistic), in simulations of budget pacing and auction outcomes, and in Bayesian tools such as Stan, PyMC and NumPyro. In reliability work you meet it in randomised fault-injection and deterministic simulation testing, where the seed is stored so that a failing schedule can be replayed.
For rare events, check the number of hits: if you see fewer than about 100 hits, the relative error is above 10% and the interval from the normal approximation is unreliable. For MCMC, run at least four chains, require R-hat close to 1, look at the effective sample size for the quantity you care about, and run a simulation-based check where the true parameters are known. To learn the subject, read Owen's free online book "Monte Carlo theory, methods and examples", then Robert and Casella.

value: A Markov decision process (MDP) is the standard mathematical model for a decision maker who acts again and again in a world where outcomes are partly random. At each step the agent sees the current state, picks an action, receives a reward, and the world moves to a new state with some probability. The goal is not the best single step. It is the best long-run total reward. A chess move, a retry policy for a failing service, the bid for an ad impression with a limited budget, and a robot's next motion are all of this form.
The Bellman equation is the idea that makes the model solvable. It says that the value of a state (the total future reward you can expect from it under the best behaviour) equals the best immediate reward plus the discounted value of the state you land in. This turns a problem about long sequences into a one-step relation between neighbouring states. It lets us compute values by repeated updates (dynamic programming) when we know the model, and learn them from experience (reinforcement learning) when we do not.
For the layer above, Markov chains (L4) provide the "Markov property": the next state depends only on the current state and action, so the present summarises the past, and the whole problem fits in a table of transition probabilities. Probability (L4) provides the expectation over random outcomes. Optimization (L4) provides the objective, to maximise the expected discounted return, and the fixed-point reasoning that shows a best policy exists. Reinforcement learning (L1), Q-learning and DQN (L2), policy gradients and PPO (L2), dynamic programming (L3) and even the dopamine reward-prediction theory (L5) are all built on this one model.
terms: State (s) — a description of the situation that contains everything needed to predict what happens next.
Action (a) — a choice available to the agent in a state.
Transition probability P(s′ | s, a) — the chance that taking action a in state s leads to state s′.
Reward R(s, a) — the immediate numeric payoff for taking action a in state s.
Discount factor (γ) — a number in [0, 1) that makes a reward t steps away worth γᵗ of its face value; it keeps total reward finite and expresses a preference for sooner rewards.
Policy (π) — a rule that gives the action to take in each state, either fixed or random.
Value function V^π(s) — the expected total discounted reward when starting in s and following policy π; V*(s) is the value under the best policy.
Q-function Q(s, a) — the expected return of taking action a in state s and then acting well; the policy follows from Q by picking the action with the highest value.
history: Richard Bellman created the field at the RAND Corporation in the early 1950s. He needed to plan decisions over many stages under uncertainty, such as inventory and routing, and he noticed that an optimal plan has the property that whatever the first decision was, the remaining decisions must be optimal for the situation that results. He called this the principle of optimality and the method dynamic programming ("programming" here means planning, not writing code). His book "Dynamic Programming" (1957) and the paper "A Markovian Decision Process" (1957) set out the equations. He also named the "curse of dimensionality": the number of states grows exponentially with the number of variables.
Ronald Howard's "Dynamic Programming and Markov Processes" (1960) introduced policy iteration. Lloyd Shapley (1953) had studied the multi-player version, stochastic games. Martin Puterman's textbook "Markov Decision Processes" (1994) became the standard reference. For the learning case, Richard Sutton (1988) introduced temporal-difference learning, Chris Watkins (1989) introduced Q-learning, and Gerald Tesauro's TD-Gammon (1992) reached expert-level backgammon. Bertsekas and Tsitsiklis (1996) analysed neuro-dynamic programming with function approximation. Kaelbling, Littman and Cassandra (1998) brought the partially observed case (POMDP) into AI.
Deep networks changed the scale. Mnih et al. (DQN, 2013 and 2015) learned to play Atari games from pixels, using a neural network to approximate Q. AlphaGo (2016) combined policy and value networks with tree search. PPO (Schulman et al., 2017) became a default policy-gradient method, and the same formal setting, with a language model as the policy and a learned reward, underlies reinforcement learning from human feedback for chat models. Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997) showed that dopamine neurons in monkeys signal a temporal-difference error, which connected this mathematics to biology.
how: An MDP is a tuple (S, A, P, R, γ). The Markov property says P(s′ | s, a) does not depend on earlier history. A policy π(a | s) gives the action choice. The agent's objective is to maximise the expected discounted return G = r₀ + γ r₁ + γ² r₂ + …. Because 0 ≤ γ < 1 and rewards are bounded by some R_max, G is at most R_max/(1 − γ). The factor 1/(1 − γ) is the effective horizon: with γ = 0.9 the agent looks about 10 steps ahead, with γ = 0.99 about 100.
Take the value of a state under a fixed policy π. Separate the first reward from the rest: V^π(s) = Σₐ π(a | s) [ R(s, a) + γ Σₛ′ P(s′ | s, a) V^π(s′) ]. This is the Bellman expectation equation. It uses the Markov property in an essential way: the future after s′ is the same problem again, whatever happened before. For a fixed policy it is a linear system of |S| equations, V = R_π + γ P_π V, so V = (I − γ P_π)⁻¹ R_π. The matrix is invertible because the eigenvalues of γP_π have magnitude at most γ < 1.
For the best policy we replace the average over actions with a maximum: V*(s) = maxₐ [ R(s, a) + γ Σₛ′ P(s′ | s, a) V*(s′) ]. This is the Bellman optimality equation. It is non-linear because of the max, so we do not invert it; we iterate. Define the operator T that maps a value function V to the right-hand side. Then T is a contraction in the max norm: ‖TV − TW‖∞ ≤ γ ‖V − W‖∞. By Banach's fixed-point theorem, T has exactly one fixed point V*, and repeated application V ← TV from any start converges to it, with error shrinking by a factor γ each sweep. This is value iteration. To gain one more correct decimal digit you need about ln 10 / ln(1/γ) sweeps: 22 sweeps for γ = 0.9, and 229 for γ = 0.99.
The optimisation viewpoint explains why this works and what is being optimised. The existence of an optimal stationary deterministic policy follows from the fixed-point argument: once V* is known, the policy "pick the action that achieves the max" is optimal, and no history-dependent or random policy can beat it. Policy iteration uses this in a loop. Evaluate the current policy by solving the linear system, then improve it by acting greedily with respect to the resulting values; each round gives a policy at least as good as before, and since there are finitely many deterministic policies the loop stops at an optimal one, usually in a handful of rounds. A cost to know: one sweep of value iteration touches every state, every action and every successor, which is O(|S|² |A|) for a dense model.
When the model P and R are unknown, learn from sampled transitions (s, a, r, s′). The Bellman equation gives the target. Temporal-difference learning updates V(s) ← V(s) + α [ r + γ V(s′) − V(s) ], and the bracket is the TD error, the same quantity that dopamine neurons appear to report. Q-learning updates Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α [ r + γ maxₐ′ Q(s′, a′) − Q(s, a) ], a stochastic version of value iteration that converges to Q* if every state-action pair is visited enough and α decreases suitably. Policy-gradient methods skip values and move the policy parameters in the direction that raises expected return, using value estimates to reduce variance. With large state spaces, a neural network approximates V or Q; then the contraction guarantee is lost, and tricks such as target networks and replay buffers are needed to keep training stable.

example: Take a machine with two states, Healthy (H) and Degraded (D), and discount γ = 0.9. In H, action Run pays 10 and the machine stays healthy with probability 0.9 or becomes degraded with probability 0.1. In H, action Service pays 5 (some downtime) and keeps the machine healthy. In D, action Run pays 4 and the machine stays degraded. In D, action Service pays 0 and returns the machine to H.
Value iteration from V₀ = (0, 0). Sweep 1: for H, Run gives 10 and Service gives 5, so V₁(H) = 10; for D, Run gives 4 and Service gives 0, so V₁(D) = 4. Sweep 2: for H, Run gives 10 + 0.9 (0.9 × 10 + 0.1 × 4) = 10 + 0.9 × 9.4 = 18.46, Service gives 5 + 0.9 × 10 = 14, so V₂(H) = 18.46. For D, Run gives 4 + 0.9 × 4 = 7.6 and Service gives 0 + 0.9 × 10 = 9, so V₂(D) = 9. Already the greedy action in D has changed from Run to Service. Sweep 3: H, Run gives 10 + 0.9 (0.9 × 18.46 + 0.1 × 9) = 25.76; D, Service gives 0.9 × 18.46 = 16.61, against Run 4 + 0.9 × 9 = 12.1. So V₃ = (25.76, 16.61). The values keep growing but the policy, Run in H and Service in D, no longer changes. This is typical: the policy settles long before the values do.
Now solve exactly for that policy, using the linear equations. H = 10 + 0.9 (0.9 H + 0.1 D) and D = 0.9 H. Substituting: H = 10 + 0.81 H + 0.09 × 0.9 H = 10 + 0.891 H, so H = 10/0.109 = 91.74 and D = 82.57. Check that no action improves: in H, Service gives 5 + 0.9 × 91.74 = 87.57 < 91.74; in D, Run gives 4 + 0.9 × 82.57 = 78.31 < 82.57. Both checks pass, so the policy is optimal and V* = (91.74, 82.57). Interpretation: a degraded machine should pay the short-term cost of servicing (reward 0 now) because the discounted value of being healthy again is much higher.
Note what changes if γ falls to 0.3. The agent now cares little about the future. In D, Run gives 4 + 0.3 D, while Service gives 0.3 H, and with these values Run is better, so the machine is never serviced. The same environment with a different discount gives a different optimal behaviour. Choosing γ, like choosing the reward, is a modelling decision.
intuition: Plan a journey by asking only "which next step leaves me with the best remaining journey?", because the best remaining journey depends only on where you are.
The principle is natural because of the Markov property: all that matters about the past is the present state. So the future is a smaller copy of the same problem, and the best plan from here is the best first step followed by the best plan from wherever you land. Each state's value can then be written in terms of the values of its neighbours, which is a set of consistent equations, and consistent equations can be solved by repeated correction. The discount makes far-away rewards matter less, so errors in the distant future are shrunk at each step, which is why the iteration converges.
mistakes: People often think the Bellman equation finds the best action by looking ahead along every future path. In fact it never enumerates paths. It only compares one step ahead using already computed values of the next states, and the earlier computation is reused. That reuse is dynamic programming.
People often think a reward is the same as a goal. In fact the reward is a signal that the designer writes, and the agent maximises exactly that signal, including its loopholes. A reward for "time on page" can produce a policy that traps users, and a reward for clicks can produce clickbait. Reward design is where many practical failures start.
People often think the discount factor is only a technical device. In fact it changes behaviour: it sets the horizon of 1/(1 − γ) steps and the tradeoff between short-term and long-term gains. Setting γ = 1 on a task that never ends makes the return infinite, and the equations no longer have a unique solution.
People often think the Markov assumption is a fact about the world. In fact it is a property of how you define the state. If the state omits something that matters (the previous frame in a video game, a user's recent history), the process is not Markov in that state, and you need to add it (frame stacking, a recurrent network) or use a partially observed model (POMDP).
People often think Q-learning and policy gradients solve different problems. In fact they solve the same MDP; Q-learning learns values and acts greedily, policy gradients adjust the policy directly. Actor-critic methods combine them.
tradeoffs: Choose exact methods (value iteration, policy iteration, linear programming) when the state space is small enough to enumerate, up to a few million states, and the model is known. You get exact optimal policies and guarantees, and you pay O(|S|²|A|) per sweep. Choose function approximation (deep RL) when the state space is huge or continuous; you gain generality and give up convergence guarantees, and you need many samples, often millions to billions of environment steps.
Choose policy iteration when evaluation by a linear solve is cheap (a few thousand states): it converges in very few rounds. Choose value iteration when |S| is large and each sweep is cheap but a linear solve is not; it is simpler and each sweep has a clear error bound γᵏ. Choose model-based methods (learn P and R, then plan) when real interaction is expensive, such as in robotics or on a live ad platform, and a good simulator can be learned; choose model-free methods (Q-learning, PPO) when the dynamics are too complex to model, but expect to need far more samples.
Choose value-based methods (DQN) for small discrete action sets and off-policy data reuse; choose policy-gradient or actor-critic methods (PPO, SAC) for continuous actions or when a stochastic policy is needed. Choose offline or batch RL, or a simple contextual bandit (an MDP with horizon one), when you cannot explore freely, since exploration in a production system costs real money and can harm users. Whichever you choose, you give up the clarity of a hand-written rule in exchange for adaptivity.
friction: The curse of dimensionality is the first limit. A state described by 20 binary variables has about a million states; with 100 variables, 10³⁰. Tables are impossible, so function approximation is required, and it breaks the guarantees that make the tabular case clean. Backgammon has about 10²⁰ positions and Go about 10¹⁷⁰, so neural networks and search are used.
Learning needs exploration, and exploration costs. An agent must try actions it believes are bad to find out whether it is wrong, and in production that means showing worse ads or sending requests to a risky path. Rewards can be sparse (a win only at the end of a game), delayed (a purchase days after an ad) or badly specified. Credit assignment, working out which of many past actions caused a reward, gets harder as delays lengthen.
States are often only partly observed. A TV viewer's intent, the competition in an auction or a user's patience cannot be seen directly, so the true problem is a POMDP, where the agent must track a belief about the state; exact solutions are intractable except for tiny cases. Non-stationarity is another issue: if other agents learn at the same time, the environment changes under you and the stationary MDP assumption fails. Finally, safety and evaluation are hard: a policy that maximises expected return can have occasional catastrophic outcomes, and evaluating a new policy offline from logged data of an old policy needs careful off-policy estimators.
practice: Write down the MDP before choosing an algorithm: what is the state (is it Markov?), what are the actions, what is the reward and over what horizon, and what is the discount. Often this exercise shows that a simpler tool is enough. If there is no real long-term effect of actions on future states, you have a bandit or even a supervised prediction problem. If the state space is small, solve it exactly with value iteration in a few lines of NumPy and compare it with your intuition.
In systems work, you meet MDPs in retry and backoff policies, autoscaling and admission control, cache and storage tiering, query optimisation, budget pacing and bidding with a constraint over a day, and recommendation with long-term engagement. For pacing, a state of (time left, budget left) and an action of bid multiplier already gives a useful dynamic program when the arrival and win-rate models are known.
When you use reinforcement learning, start from a simulator or logged data, evaluate against a strong hand-written baseline, and watch the reward for loopholes. Log the state, action, the action probabilities and the reward so that you can evaluate new policies offline with importance weighting. Keep a safe fallback policy and limits on how far the learned policy can move from it. To study, read Sutton and Barto chapters 3 and 4 (free online), implement value iteration and Q-learning on a small gridworld, then take the Stanford CS234 lectures.
quiz: State the Bellman optimality equation in words. || The value of a state under the best policy equals the maximum over actions of the immediate reward plus the discount times the expected value of the next state. In symbols, V*(s) = maxₐ [R(s, a) + γ Σ P(s′ | s, a) V*(s′)].
Why does value iteration converge, and how fast? || The Bellman optimality operator is a contraction in the max norm with factor γ, so by the Banach fixed-point theorem it has a unique fixed point and each sweep shrinks the error by at least a factor γ. The error after k sweeps is at most γᵏ times the initial error.
What does the Markov property buy us, and what happens if the state leaves out important information? || It lets the value of a state depend only on that state, so the future is a smaller copy of the same problem, and dynamic programming applies. If the state omits relevant history, the process is not Markov in that state; add the history (frame stacking, a recurrent state) or use a POMDP.
With γ = 0.99, roughly how many value-iteration sweeps are needed to reduce the error by a factor of 1,000? || Need γᵏ ≤ 10⁻³, so k ≥ ln 1000 / ln(1/0.99) = 6.91/0.01005 ≈ 687 sweeps. The effective horizon of 100 steps explains why high discounts are slow.
You must choose between model-based planning and Q-learning for a bidding policy on a live platform where each experiment costs money. What do you do? || Prefer a model-based or offline approach first: fit a model of win rates and arrival from logs, plan with dynamic programming, and test in simulation. Use model-free exploration only in a small, guarded part of traffic with a safe fallback, because it needs many samples.

@@ concentration
value: A concentration inequality is a guarantee, true for every sample size, that a sum or average of many independent random quantities is very unlikely to be far from its expected value. The key word is "very". For the average of n independent values in [0, 1], the chance of missing the mean by more than t is at most 2·exp(−2nt²). That is not a 1-in-20 chance. With n = 10,000 and t = 0.02 it is at most 2·e⁻⁸ ≈ 0.0007, and it drops exponentially as n grows.
This is the tool that turns "randomised and usually right" into "randomised and provably right with failure probability δ you choose". Hash tables with random hashing keep their longest chain short. Sketches such as count-min and HyperLogLog meet their error bounds. A randomised algorithm can be repeated until its failure chance is 2⁻⁶⁰, below the chance of a hardware fault. A model trained on n examples has a test error close to its training error. In each case a proof that is only about the average would not be enough, because we need the bad cases to be rare.
For the layer above, probability (L4) supplies the basic objects, the expected value and variance, and the elementary inequalities of Markov and Chebyshev that these bounds are built from. The central limit theorem (L4) gives the picture in the limit: sums are bell-shaped. Concentration inequalities are the finite-sample, exponential version of that picture, and they are rigorous for every n, including the far tails where the normal approximation is not trustworthy. Randomised algorithms (L3), streaming sketches (L3), Bloom filters (L2), random projections (L4) and learning theory (L4) all quote these bounds.
terms: Markov's inequality — for a non-negative variable X, P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a; it uses only the mean.
Chebyshev's inequality — P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k²; it uses the mean and the variance.
Chernoff bound — an exponential tail bound for sums of independent 0/1 variables, found by applying Markov's inequality to e^(sX) and choosing the best s.
Hoeffding's inequality — an exponential bound for averages of independent bounded variables: P(|X̄ − μ| ≥ t) ≤ 2·exp(−2nt²/(b − a)²) for values in [a, b].
Moment generating function — M(s) = E[e^(sX)]; for independent variables the function of a sum is the product of the functions, which is why exponential bounds work for sums.
Union bound — the probability that at least one of m bad events happens is at most the sum of their probabilities; it is how a bound for one quantity becomes a bound for many.
With high probability (w.h.p.) — with failure probability at most 1/nᶜ for some constant c, where n is the problem size.
Sub-Gaussian — a variable whose tails are at least as thin as those of a normal distribution; bounded variables are sub-Gaussian, and Hoeffding-type bounds apply.
history: The oldest bounds are polynomial. Chebyshev proved his inequality in 1867 (Markov, his student, later gave the short proof taught today), and it let Bernoulli's law of large numbers be proved in a few lines. The inequality is general but weak: it only says that the chance of a deviation of k standard deviations is at most 1/k², far larger than the true chance for sums.
Exponential bounds appeared in the 20th century. Sergei Bernstein derived them in the 1920s and 1930s, using the moment generating function, for sums of bounded or light-tailed variables. Herman Chernoff's 1952 paper in the Annals of Mathematical Statistics used the same technique to bound the error of hypothesis tests based on sums. Wassily Hoeffding's 1963 paper "Probability inequalities for sums of bounded random variables" gave the clean form used today. Azuma (1967) and McDiarmid (1989) extended the idea from sums to martingales and to any function that changes little when one input changes (the bounded differences inequality). Talagrand (1995) developed the general theory of concentration of measure.
Computer science adopted them in the 1980s and 1990s as randomised algorithms spread. Raghavan and Thompson's randomised rounding (1987) used Chernoff bounds for routing. Hashing and load-balancing analyses followed, and Mitzenmacher's thesis on the "power of two choices" (1996), based on Azar, Broder, Karlin and Upfal (1994), used them for balls-and-bins results. Vapnik and Chervonenkis (1971) and Valiant (1984) used Hoeffding-type bounds for learning theory. The Johnson–Lindenstrauss proof, count-min sketch (Cormode and Muthukrishnan, 2005) and many streaming results later used the same few tools.
how: Everything starts from Markov's inequality: for X ≥ 0 and a > 0, P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]/a. The reason is that E[X] ≥ a·P(X ≥ a), because the outcomes at or above a contribute at least a each. It needs no assumption but non-negativity, and it is weak: a variable with mean 1 exceeds 100 with chance at most 1%, which may be far from the truth.
Apply Markov to the square of the deviation and you get Chebyshev: P(|X − μ| ≥ t) = P((X − μ)² ≥ t²) ≤ σ²/t². For an average of n independent samples, σ² becomes σ²/n, so P(|X̄ − μ| ≥ t) ≤ σ²/(nt²). The chance falls like 1/n. This is what the weak law of large numbers needs, but it is too slow if you want failure probabilities like 10⁻⁹.
The big step is to apply Markov to an exponential. For any s > 0, P(X ≥ a) = P(e^(sX) ≥ e^(sa)) ≤ e^(−sa) E[e^(sX)]. If X = X₁ + … + Xₙ with independent terms, then E[e^(sX)] = Π E[e^(sXᵢ)], because independence turns an expectation of a product into a product of expectations. Now each factor is a small, easy-to-bound number. Then choose the s that makes the bound smallest. For the sum X of independent 0/1 variables with mean μ = E[X], this gives the Chernoff bounds: P(X ≥ (1 + δ)μ) ≤ exp(−δ²μ/(2 + δ)), and P(X ≤ (1 − δ)μ) ≤ exp(−δ²μ/2) for 0 < δ < 1. The probability of a relative deviation δ falls exponentially in μ, the expected count, so a bin with expected load 10,000 deviates above its mean by more than 5% with probability at most exp(−0.05² × 10,000/2.05) = e^(−12.2) ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁶.
For bounded variables that are not 0/1, Hoeffding's lemma bounds E[e^(s(Y − E Y))] ≤ exp(s²(b − a)²/8) for Y in [a, b], and the same steps give P(X̄ − μ ≥ t) ≤ exp(−2nt²/(b − a)²), and twice that for the two-sided version. The exponent has t² and n together: one extra decimal digit of confidence costs only a constant factor more samples. To reach failure probability δ with error t, you need n ≥ (b − a)² ln(2/δ) / (2t²). The dependence on δ is logarithmic, which is the practical heart of the matter: going from δ = 10⁻² to 10⁻¹² needs only about 5.3 times more samples (ln(2 × 10¹²)/ln 200 = 28.3/5.3). Chebyshev, by contrast, needs 10¹⁰ times more.
Next comes the union bound, which converts a statement about one quantity into a statement about many. If one estimate fails with probability δ, then m estimates all succeed with probability at least 1 − mδ. Combined with the logarithmic dependence, this gives a very useful fact: you can make 10⁶ estimates simultaneously correct by paying only ln(10⁶) ≈ 14 extra units in the log term. This is the standard way to prove statements about all keys, all pairs, all hypotheses or all queries at once.
The same ideas extend beyond sums. McDiarmid's inequality says: if f(X₁ … Xₙ) changes by at most cᵢ when only Xᵢ changes, then P(|f − E f| ≥ t) ≤ 2·exp(−2t²/Σcᵢ²). It covers the number of occupied bins, the number of set bits in a Bloom filter, the size of a maximum matching in a random graph, and the training error of a learning algorithm that is stable under replacing one example. Bernstein's inequality refines Hoeffding for variables with small variance, giving an exponent involving σ² and the range, which is much tighter for rare events. Chernoff-type bounds fail when the variables are heavy-tailed (no moment generating function) or strongly dependent; then one uses truncation, median-of-means, or martingale methods.

example: Let us size a poll, a sketch and a hash table with the same few formulas.
Poll. We want to estimate the fraction of viewers who watched a show, within ±1 percentage point (t = 0.01), with failure probability δ = 0.05. Each viewer gives a value in [0, 1]. Hoeffding says n ≥ ln(2/δ)/(2t²) = ln(40)/(2 × 0.0001) = 3.689/0.0002 ≈ 18,400 viewers. Chebyshev, using the worst-case variance 1/4, gives n ≥ 1/(4 t² δ) = 1/(4 × 0.0001 × 0.05) = 50,000. The exponential bound needs 2.7 times fewer samples at δ = 0.05, and the gap widens fast: for δ = 10⁻⁶ Hoeffding needs ln(2 × 10⁶)/0.0002 ≈ 72,600, while Chebyshev needs 2.5 × 10⁹.
Many estimates at once. Suppose we need 1,000,000 such rates (one per app, say) to be within ±1 point at the same time, with total failure probability 0.05. Use the union bound: each must fail with probability at most 0.05/10⁶ = 5 × 10⁻⁸. Then n ≥ ln(2/(5 × 10⁻⁸))/0.0002 = ln(4 × 10⁷)/0.0002 = 17.5/0.0002 ≈ 87,600 viewers per rate. A million-fold increase in the number of questions raised the sample size by less than 5 times. (Chebyshev would need a million times more.)
Balls and bins. Throw n = 10⁶ items into 10⁶ buckets at random. The load of one bucket is Binomial(n, 1/n) with mean 1. The Chernoff bound gives P(load ≥ k) ≤ (e/k)^k. For k = 12 that is (0.2265)^12 ≈ 2 × 10⁻⁸. A union bound over 10⁶ buckets gives probability at most 0.02 that any bucket has load 12 or more. So, with probability 98%, the fullest bucket holds at most 11 items, although the average load is 1. (The typical maximum is about 9.) In general the maximum load is about ln n/ln ln n, so the longest chain in a random hash table grows very slowly. With two random choices per item (the power of two choices), it falls to about ln ln n/ln 2 plus a small constant, around 4 or 5 for this n.
Coin flips. Table in the diagram: flipping 1,000 fair coins, the chance of getting 600 or more heads or 400 or fewer is at most 4.1 × 10⁻⁹ by Hoeffding: 2·exp(−2 × 1000 × 0.1²) = 2·e⁻²⁰. Chebyshev only gives 0.025, and the true value is about 3 × 10⁻¹⁰. The exponential bound is looser than the truth by a factor of about 13, but it is a guarantee, and it is 6 million times tighter than Chebyshev.
intuition: A thousand people each toss a coin; the chance that 900 of them get heads is not just small, it is so small that you would see it once in the age of the universe.
The reason is that for the total to be extreme, nearly all the independent pieces must lean the same way at once. Each additional piece multiplies the probability of such an alignment by a factor below 1, so the chance falls exponentially in the number of pieces. This is a different thing from the fact that one variable is large. A single piece can be extreme with a fair chance; a sum can be extreme only if many pieces conspire, and independence means they cannot coordinate. That is why a logarithmic number of extra samples buys a huge gain in confidence.
mistakes: People often think a concentration bound is the same as the CLT. In fact the CLT is a statement about the limit and the typical range around the mean (a few standard errors), while the Chernoff and Hoeffding bounds hold at every n and for rare events far into the tail. The CLT may be inaccurate 6 standard errors out; the bound is still correct there.
People often think the bounds are tight. In fact they are upper bounds and are often loose by a constant factor in the exponent (Hoeffding ignores the variance, so it is much worse than Bernstein or the CLT for rates near 0 or 1). Use them for guarantees and sample-size planning, not for exact error bars.
People often think they only apply to coin flips. In fact Hoeffding applies to any independent bounded variables, McDiarmid to any function with bounded differences, and Bernstein to variables with small variance. What they need is independence (or limited dependence) and bounded or light tails.
People often think "with high probability" means "probably". In fact it has a precise meaning, a failure probability that shrinks polynomially in the input size, such as 1/n². For a service handling 10⁹ operations a day, a failure probability of 10⁻³ per operation is not "high probability", and 10⁻¹⁵ might be.
People often think independence is a minor technical detail. In fact the product step E[e^(sX)] = ΠE[e^(sXᵢ)] is exactly where it is used. If events are positively correlated (users who share a household, requests that share a cache line, hash functions that are only pairwise independent), the exponential bound can fail, and only weaker ones remain.
tradeoffs: Choose Chebyshev when all you know is the variance and you need a quick, distribution-free statement, for example the variance of a pairwise-independent hash. You pay with a bound that falls only like 1/n. Choose Chernoff or Hoeffding when you have full independence and bounded values, and need a tiny failure probability; you pay by needing stronger assumptions on the randomness (for hashing, more independence than pairwise, such as 4-wise or fully random functions).
Choose Bernstein or the multiplicative Chernoff form when the quantity is a rare event or a rate near 0 (the variance is much smaller than the range squared). Hoeffding there is too pessimistic, by a factor of 1/p in the exponent, and you would collect far more samples than needed. Choose Hoeffding when you know only the range and want the simplest formula.
Choose a guarantee-style bound (a union bound over hypotheses with Hoeffding) when you need a statement valid for every data set with a stated failure probability, as in a safety margin or a published sample-size justification. Choose the CLT interval, a bootstrap or an empirical estimate when you want a sharp estimate and can accept an approximation. The rigorous bound may ask for about twice the samples at a rate of 50% (18,400 against 9,600 for ±1 point at 95%) and many times more for small rates; the approximate one is cheaper and might be wrong in the far tail.
Choose median-of-means or truncation when the data are heavy-tailed. Split the samples into groups, average each, and take the median; this gets the exponential tail from a finite variance only. You pay a constant factor in efficiency, and you give up the clean mean estimate in favour of a robust one.
friction: The first limit is the independence assumption. Real events are correlated: a viral item affects many users, a failing rack takes out many replicas, a bad deploy affects many requests. Bounds that assume independence then understate the risk, and the effective number of independent samples is smaller than n. For hash-based structures, using a cheaper family of functions (pairwise independent) weakens the bound back to Chebyshev, so implementations that claim an exponential guarantee must use hash functions that really behave like random functions.
The bounds also hide constants. A proof may show that error ε needs n = O(log(1/δ)/ε²) samples, but the constant matters when you choose a real buffer size, and sometimes the proven constant is 10 times larger than needed. For small n the bounds can be vacuous (a probability bound above 1 says nothing). They give no help with heavy-tailed data, where moment generating functions do not exist, or for the maximum of dependent variables without extra tools.
Finally, they bound deviations, but do not locate them: they say the failure chance is at most δ, not what happens in the failure. For a safety-critical system you still need to know what the failure costs, and the union bound can be loose when failures are correlated in ways that make them clump together.
practice: When you read a randomised algorithm or sketch, find the sentence "with probability at least 1 − δ" and ask which inequality gives it. Most often it is Markov (one row of a count-min sketch), Chebyshev (pairwise-independent hashing) or Chernoff and Hoeffding (repetition and amplification). Repeating a constant-success algorithm k times independently and taking the median or majority drives the failure probability down as exp(−ck), which is how count-min uses depth ln(1/δ) and how randomised algorithms reach failure rates like 2⁻⁶⁴.
To size a test or a sample, use n ≥ ln(2/δ)/(2t²) for a rate in [0, 1], and add ln(m) inside the logarithm for m simultaneous estimates. For rare rates, use the multiplicative Chernoff form and expect n of order ln(1/δ)/(ε²p). Check the independence assumption in the real setting: sample by user, not by event; use a seeded, well-mixed hash.
When reading load-balancing numbers, remember that the maximum matters, not the average. With n items in n buckets the fullest bucket holds about ln n/ln ln n, and with two choices only about ln ln n/ln 2; capacity plans should use the maximum. Sensible study order: Markov, Chebyshev, Chernoff (derive it yourself with the exponential trick), Hoeffding, the union bound, then balls and bins and the Johnson–Lindenstrauss proof in Mitzenmacher and Upfal, chapters 3 to 5.
quiz: How are Chebyshev and Chernoff bounds derived from Markov's inequality? || Chebyshev applies Markov to (X − μ)², giving P(|X − μ| ≥ t) ≤ σ²/t². Chernoff applies Markov to e^(sX), then uses independence to factor E[e^(sX)] into a product and optimises s, which gives a bound that falls exponentially.
Why does a union bound combine well with Hoeffding's inequality? || Hoeffding's failure probability depends on δ only through ln(2/δ). Dividing δ by m for m simultaneous estimates therefore adds only ln m to the sample size, so a million simultaneous guarantees cost a few times more samples, not a million times.

@@ control
value: Control theory is the mathematics of making a system reach and hold a target when the world pushes it away. The idea is feedback: measure the output, compare it with the target (the setpoint), and use the difference, called the error, to decide the next action. A thermostat measures room temperature and switches the heater. A cruise control measures speed and adjusts the throttle. An autoscaler measures CPU use and changes the number of servers. Because the action depends on the measured result, the system corrects for disturbances and for errors in its own model, and you do not have to predict them.
The theory matters for software because so many systems are feedback loops, often without being designed as one. TCP congestion control, adaptive bitrate video, budget pacing for ads, Kubernetes reconciliation, circuit breakers, rate limiters and retry-with-backoff all measure something, compare it with a target and adjust. Control theory tells you what such loops can and cannot do: what accuracy to expect, how fast the response can be, and above all when the loop becomes unstable, so that it oscillates or runs away. The most common instability in computer systems, delay between action and measurement, is a central topic of the theory.
For the layer above, homeostasis (L5) is the original example: living bodies hold temperature, blood sugar and pH near setpoints with negative feedback, long before engineers built thermostats. Calculus (L4) gives the language: a system is a differential equation, the integral of the error is accumulated error, the derivative is the rate of change, and the stability of a loop is decided by the roots of an equation derived from them. Reconciliation loops (L2), autoscaling (L2), TCP and congestion control (L3), circuit breakers (L2), adaptive bitrate (L2) and budget pacing (L2) are all controllers built on this base.
terms: Setpoint (reference) — the target value the output should reach and hold.
Error — setpoint minus measured output; the signal that drives the correction.
Plant — the thing being controlled (a server pool, a network path, a room), described by how its output responds to inputs.
Negative feedback — correction that opposes the error, so it shrinks; positive feedback amplifies it and leads to runaway.
PID controller — a controller whose output is Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt + Kd·de/dt: a proportional, an integral and a derivative term on the error e.
Steady-state error — the error that remains after transients die; a pure proportional controller usually leaves some.
Stability — the property that the loop returns to the setpoint after a disturbance instead of oscillating with growing amplitude or diverging.
Loop gain and delay — how strongly the controller reacts to error, and how long it takes for an action to show in the measurement; high gain with long delay causes oscillation.
history: Feedback is old. Ktesibios built a float-valve water clock in Alexandria around 250 BCE, and James Watt's centrifugal governor (adopted for steam engines in 1788) held engine speed. Governors sometimes began to oscillate ("hunt") as they were improved, and nobody knew why. In 1868 James Clerk Maxwell wrote "On Governors", the first mathematical analysis: he linearised the equations of motion and showed that the loop is stable only if all roots of its characteristic polynomial have negative real parts. Edward Routh (1877) and Adolf Hurwitz (1895) found tests for this without solving the polynomial.
The 20th century brought a toolbox. Nicolas Minorsky designed a controller for ship steering in 1922 that is the first analysed PID controller. At Bell Labs, Harry Nyquist (1932) and Hendrik Bode gave frequency-domain tests of stability for amplifiers with feedback. Ziegler and Nichols (1942) published practical rules for tuning PID gains from a simple experiment, which are still in use. Norbert Wiener's "Cybernetics" (1948) named the field and linked machines to animals. Rudolf Kalman (1960) introduced state-space methods, the Kalman filter for estimating a hidden state from noisy measurements, and the linear-quadratic regulator for optimal control.
Computing adopted the ideas late. Van Jacobson's 1988 congestion control saved the Internet from collapse by making each sender react to loss, and Chiu and Jain (1989) analysed the additive-increase, multiplicative-decrease rule as a feedback system that converges to fair shares. Hellerstein, Diao, Parekh and Tilbury's "Feedback Control of Computing Systems" (2004) applied control theory to servers, queues and databases. Today Kubernetes controllers, autoscalers and model-based congestion control such as BBR (2016) are standard applications.
how: Every feedback loop has the same parts: a sensor measures the output y, a comparator forms the error e = r − y with the setpoint r, a controller turns e into an action u, and an actuator applies u to the plant, which produces y. The disturbance d acts on the plant from outside. The loop closes because y feeds back into e. The design question is how the controller should map e to u.
The simplest rule is proportional: u = Kp·e. Take a plant that responds to the input like a tank filling and leaking: dy/dt = −a·y + b·u. With u = Kp·(r − y), the output settles where −a·y + b·Kp·(r − y) = 0, so y = b·Kp·r/(a + b·Kp). The steady error is r·a/(a + b·Kp). A higher gain Kp shrinks it, but never to zero. This is why proportional control alone leaves a permanent offset when the plant loses something constantly (a leak, a base load). The integral term fixes it: u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt. While any error remains, the integral keeps growing and pushes u until the error is exactly zero. This uses calculus directly: the integral is the accumulated error, and it is the memory that removes offset.
The derivative term, Kd·de/dt, reacts to how fast the error changes. If the error is falling quickly, the derivative reduces the action to avoid overshooting. It acts as damping, but it amplifies measurement noise, so real controllers filter it or omit it. Most industrial loops are PI. Implementations need extra care: anti-windup (stop the integral from growing while the actuator is at its limit, for example when the autoscaler has reached the maximum number of replicas), rate limits on the action, and dead bands (no action for small errors) to avoid chattering.
Stability is where the maths matters most. Linearise the loop around the setpoint and describe it by a characteristic equation; the loop is stable when all roots lie in the left half of the complex plane (for continuous time) or inside the unit circle (for discrete time). A clean discrete example shows the effect of delay. Let the plant be an integrator, y_{t+1} = y_t + k·u_t, and the controller u_t = Kp·(r − y_{t−d}), where d is the number of steps by which the measurement is late. With g = k·Kp and e = y − r, the error obeys e_{t+1} = e_t − g·e_{t−d}. The loop is stable if and only if g < 2·sin(π/(2(2d + 1))). For d = 0 that is g < 2; for d = 1, g < 1; for d = 2, g < 0.62; for d = 3, g < 0.45. Each step of delay cuts the gain you can afford. Above the limit the oscillation grows without bound. This is the central lesson: the stable gain is set by the delay, and a controller tuned without knowing the delay will oscillate.
From this follows the main rule of design for computing systems: make the loop slower than the plant's delay, or give it a model of the delay. An autoscaler that adds servers based on current CPU, when servers take two minutes to start, will add again and again while the first batch is still booting. Kubernetes' Horizontal Pod Autoscaler computes desired replicas = ceil(current replicas × current metric/target metric), ignores errors within a 10% tolerance, and applies a stabilisation window (5 minutes by default) before scaling down, all of which are dead-band and damping tricks. Budget pacing does the same: compare the spend so far with the planned spend curve, and raise or lower the bid or participation rate gradually, with smoothing so that noisy hourly traffic does not cause swings.
Control theory also explains saturation and non-linearity. A queue near full utilisation is highly non-linear: a small extra load makes latency explode, so the gain of the plant varies by orders of magnitude. A linear controller tuned at 50% load may oscillate at 95%. Remedies are gain scheduling (different gains in different regimes), conservative gains, or a feed-forward term that uses a model or a known upcoming change (a scheduled campaign) so that the feedback only corrects the leftover error. Measurement and estimation come first, as Kalman showed: a smoothed or filtered estimate of the state beats a raw noisy reading.

example: Take the discrete loop e(t+1) = e(t) − g·e(t − d), the error of an autoscaler-like controller. Let the error start at e = 10 (for example 10 percentage points of CPU above target), with the earlier values also equal to 10, and let the measurement be one step late (d = 1).
Case 1, gain g = 0.5, which is below the limit of 1. Step by step: e₁ = 10 − 0.5 × 10 = 5; e₂ = 5 − 0.5 × 10 = 0; e₃ = 0 − 0.5 × 5 = −2.5; e₄ = −2.5 − 0.5 × 0 = −2.5; e₅ = −2.5 − 0.5 × (−2.5) = −1.25; e₆ = −1.25 − 0.5 × (−2.5) = 0; e₇ = 0 − 0.5 × (−1.25) = 0.625. The error crosses zero, overshoots to −2.5, and then the swings shrink: 10, 5, 0, −2.5, −2.5, −1.25, 0, 0.6. The loop is stable but rings, because g is above 0.25 (the limit for a smooth, non-oscillating decay at this delay).
Case 2, gain g = 1.2, above the limit of 1. e₁ = 10 − 12 = −2; e₂ = −2 − 12 = −14; e₃ = −14 + 2.4 = −11.6; e₄ = −11.6 + 16.8 = 5.2; e₅ = 5.2 + 13.9 = 19.1; e₆ = 19.1 − 6.2 = 12.9; e₇ = 12.9 − 22.9 = −10.1. The swings grow: from 10 to 14 to 19 and onwards. The controller is correct in direction but too strong for the delay, and it keeps pushing when the effect of its earlier push has not yet appeared. A real autoscaler would add far too many servers and then remove too many, forever.
Now the offset example. A pacing controller has a plant with leak: spend rate y responds as dy/dt = −0.5·y + 1.0·u, and the target is r = 100 (units per minute). With proportional gain Kp = 4: y = b·Kp·r/(a + b·Kp) = 1 × 4 × 100/(0.5 + 4) = 88.9. The steady error is 11.1 units, about 11%. With Kp = 20, y = 2000/20.5 = 97.6, and the error is 2.4%. Adding an integral term removes the offset: at the steady state the integral term supplies the missing input u = a·r/b = 50, with zero error. The price of high Kp is a faster reaction and less stability margin once delay or noise is present.
Finally the HPA rule. With 10 replicas at 90% CPU and a target of 60%, desired = ceil(10 × 90/60) = 15. If those five new pods need two minutes to become ready, the controller must not compute again from the current average alone, or it would ask for more again. HPA handles this by treating pods that are not ready conservatively, by a short cooldown, and by the tolerance band. These details are what keep the effective gain below the stability limit created by the two-minute delay.
intuition: Steering a car by looking at how far you are from the lane centre: small, steady corrections work, but a driver who reacts late and hard swings from side to side.
The method works because the error is the one thing you can always measure, and it contains everything you need to know about how wrong you are. By always acting against the error, you do not need an accurate model of the world, only the sign of the effect of your action. The trouble comes from time. Every real loop has a delay between acting and seeing the result, and a correction applied for an error that is already being fixed is an overcorrection. The theory is a precise statement of how much correction you can afford given the delay.
mistakes: People often think feedback needs an accurate model of the system. In fact a simple controller works without one, since the error signal corrects for model mistakes; this is the main benefit of feedback. A model helps with speed and with predicting delays, but it is not required for correctness.
People often think a bigger gain always gives a better, faster controller. In fact beyond a limit set by delays and lags the loop oscillates and diverges. The fastest stable response is usually found at a moderate gain, and the stability margin is a design target.
People often think a proportional controller reaches the setpoint. In fact it leaves a steady-state error whenever the plant needs a nonzero input to hold the setpoint. Integral action is what removes it, and it brings the risk of windup that you must handle.
People often think oscillation means the system is noisy or broken. In fact it is usually a sign of excessive gain or delay in a working loop. Adding more randomness or retries makes it worse; reducing gain, adding damping or slowing the loop fixes it.
People often think that more frequent control is always better. In fact running the loop faster than the sensor updates or than the plant can respond adds noise and chatter without information. The sampling period should be matched to the plant's time constant, commonly around one tenth of it.
tradeoffs: Choose proportional-only control when a small steady error is acceptable and the loop is simple (a limit on queue length, a coarse throttle). It is the most robust choice. Choose PI when you need zero offset on a slowly changing target (a daily spend curve, a utilisation target), and add anti-windup; you give up some stability margin and accept slower recovery from large disturbances. Choose to add D only if the signal is smooth or well filtered, as it amplifies noise.
Choose a slow, damped loop when the actuator has a long delay (new servers, caches warming up, a model retrain) or when overshoot is costly (overspending a budget, overloading a downstream service). You accept slower convergence to the target. Choose a fast, aggressive loop only when the delay is short compared with the loop time constant and the system is well modelled, such as a rate limiter that reacts within the same request.
Choose feed-forward plus feedback when you know about disturbances in advance (a scheduled launch, a daily traffic curve): feed-forward does the bulk of the work immediately and feedback removes the leftover error. You pay with the need for a model and its maintenance; a wrong model can make things worse, which is why the feedback part stays. Choose model-predictive control (MPC) when there are constraints and known delays, such as capacity limits and slow start-up; it solves an optimisation each step, which costs computation and needs a good model.
Choose AIMD (additive increase, multiplicative decrease) for distributed senders with no coordination, since it converges to fairness and uses only a one-bit signal (loss). You give up smooth throughput. Choose a model-based controller such as BBR where loss is not a reliable signal, at the price of complexity and coexistence problems with other flows.
friction: Delay, noise and non-linearity make real loops difficult. Measurements arrive late and are averaged over windows, which adds more delay: a 5-minute average used by a controller that acts every 30 seconds is a source of oscillation. Metrics are noisy, so naive derivative control amplifies noise, and thresholds near the noise level cause flapping. Plants change over time (a code change doubles CPU per request), so gains tuned once become wrong.
Several loops interact. If an autoscaler, a load balancer and a retry policy all react to latency, their combined behaviour can be unstable even when each loop is stable alone; metastable failures, in which a retry storm keeps a system overloaded after the original trigger has gone, are positive feedback that no single controller intended. Limits and saturation add trouble: actuators have maximums, minimums and rate limits, and when the actuator saturates the feedback is broken, which causes integral windup.
Finally, the theory covers linear and low-dimensional cases well, and gives much less for large, non-linear, multi-agent systems, such as many bidders adjusting their bids on the same exchange. The setpoint can also be the wrong thing: a loop that perfectly holds a bad metric (average latency, instead of the tail) achieves the wrong goal perfectly.
practice: Before writing a controller, write down: the signal measured, the setpoint, the action, the delay from action to measurement (including averaging windows), and the limits of the actuator. Estimate the time constant of the plant and set the control period to about a tenth of it. Start with a low gain, test with a step disturbance in a staging environment, and increase until the response is quick without sustained oscillation, leaving a margin of at least a factor of two to the gain at which it oscillates.
Use PI with anti-windup for targets such as utilisation or spend; add a dead band or hysteresis (for example scale up at 70% and down at 40%, not both at 60%) and a cooldown after each action; smooth noisy inputs with an exponentially weighted moving average, remembering that the smoothing adds delay. Rate-limit the actuator. Log the setpoint, measurement, error and action at every step, because tuning without those traces is guesswork.
In Kubernetes, read the HPA algorithm documentation and set the behaviour fields for stabilisation windows and scaling policies. In pacing, compare the realised spend curve to the target and use a PI loop on the throttle probability. For safety, add a circuit breaker or a hard bound outside the loop, and plan how the system behaves if the sensor fails. To study, read Åström and Murray's free book, then Janert's "Feedback Control for Computer Systems" for the engineering view.

value: Game theory is the mathematics of decisions that affect each other. In an ordinary optimisation problem you choose the best action in a fixed world. In a game, the world contains other decision makers, each choosing to serve their own interest, and your best action depends on what they do, which depends on what they think you will do. The theory gives precise models of this (players, strategies, payoffs) and a way to predict the stable outcomes: equilibria, where nobody gains by changing their own choice alone.
This is practical for a software engineer because many systems are games whether or not the designers think so. An ad exchange runs an auction, and each advertiser chooses a bid. A blockchain pays miners or validators and must make honesty more profitable than cheating. A congested road, a shared network link or a shared database is used by selfish clients, and the total result may be worse than what any coordinated plan would give. A security system faces an adversary that adapts. A generative adversarial network trains two models against each other.
The second half of the theory, mechanism design, reverses the question: instead of analysing a game, design the rules so that self-interested players produce a good outcome. Second-price auctions are the standard example, because they make truthful bidding the best strategy. For the layer above, probability (L4) provides mixed strategies (random choices with chosen odds) and expected payoffs, and evolution (L5) inspired evolutionary game theory, where strategies spread in a population according to their payoff. Ad auctions (L2), proof of work and proof of stake (L2), distributed systems theory (L3) and GANs (L2) all depend on these ideas.
terms: Game — a model with players, a set of strategies for each player, and a payoff to each player for every combination of strategies.
Strategy — a complete plan of action for a player; a pure strategy is one fixed choice, and a mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure strategies.
Dominant strategy — a strategy that is at least as good as every alternative whatever the other players do.
Nash equilibrium — a combination of strategies where no player can improve their payoff by changing only their own strategy.
Zero-sum game — a game where one player's gain is exactly the other's loss; the minimax value is then well defined.
Mechanism — a set of rules (who wins, what each pays) designed so that the players' own choices produce a desired outcome.
Incentive compatible (truthful) — a mechanism where reporting your true value or type is a best strategy for each player.
Price of anarchy — the ratio between the cost of the worst equilibrium and the cost of the best possible coordinated outcome.
history: John von Neumann proved the minimax theorem for two-player zero-sum games in 1928: with mixed strategies, each player has a strategy that guarantees the same value. In 1944, with the economist Oskar Morgenstern, he published "Theory of Games and Economic Behavior", which founded the field and gave a formal theory of utility. John Nash's papers of 1950 and 1951 proved that every finite game has an equilibrium in mixed strategies, for any number of players and without the zero-sum assumption. The proof used Brouwer's fixed-point theorem. The same period gave the prisoner's dilemma (Flood and Dresher at RAND, 1950, named by Albert Tucker), which showed that individually rational choices can give a collectively bad result.
Mechanism design grew from the work of Leonid Hurwicz in the 1960s and 1970s. William Vickrey (1961) analysed auctions and showed that the second-price sealed-bid auction makes truthful bidding a dominant strategy. Clarke (1971) and Groves (1973) generalised this to the VCG mechanism for many items. Roger Myerson (1981) derived the revenue-maximising auction and the revelation principle. Hurwicz, Maskin and Myerson received the 2007 Nobel prize in economics for this; Nash, Harsanyi and Selten had received it in 1994, and Vickrey in 1996.
Computer science joined in the 1990s and 2000s. Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou (1999) defined the price of anarchy; Roughgarden and Tardos (2002) showed that selfish routing in a network costs at most 4/3 times the optimum for linear costs. Daskalakis, Goldberg and Papadimitriou (2006) proved that computing a Nash equilibrium is PPAD-complete, so there may be no efficient algorithm. Sponsored search made auctions an industry: Google's generalised second-price auction was analysed by Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz and by Varian in 2007. Since about 2017 much of programmatic display moved to first-price auctions, which raised the importance of bid shading. In 2014 Goodfellow et al. described GANs as a two-player minimax game, and the counterfactual regret minimisation algorithm (Zinkevich et al., 2007) led to poker programs that beat professionals (Libratus 2017, Pluribus 2019).
how: A game in normal form is a table. Take the prisoner's dilemma with years in prison as negative payoffs. If both stay silent (cooperate, C), each gets −1. If both confess (defect, D), each gets −2. If one defects while the other is silent, the defector gets 0 and the silent one gets −3. For player 1, D gives 0 against C and −2 against D, while C gives −1 and −3; D is better in both cases, so D is a dominant strategy. The same holds for player 2. The only Nash equilibrium is (D, D), with payoffs (−2, −2), although (C, C) would give both −1. Individually rational choices lead to a result that both dislike.
Not every game has a pure equilibrium. In matching pennies, one player wants the coins to match and the other wants them to differ. Any fixed choice can be exploited. The equilibrium is to mix: each plays heads with probability 1/2, so the opponent is indifferent between their options and cannot gain. This is where probability enters. In general a mixed equilibrium is found by making each player indifferent among the strategies they use: choose your probabilities so that the other player's expected payoff is the same for all their options. Nash's theorem guarantees that at least one such equilibrium always exists for a finite game.
Finding it is another matter. For two-player zero-sum games, equilibria are found by linear programming (von Neumann's minimax is equivalent to LP duality), or by simple iterative methods such as multiplicative weights, where each player repeatedly shifts weight towards strategies that did well, and the average play converges to equilibrium. For general games, computing a Nash equilibrium is PPAD-complete, so we use heuristics (Lemke–Howson) or settle for approximate or correlated equilibria, which can be found efficiently by no-regret learning dynamics.
Mechanism design works the other way round. Take a single item and n bidders with private values v_i. In a second-price (Vickrey) auction the highest bidder wins and pays the second-highest bid. Bidding your true value is a dominant strategy. Suppose the highest competing bid is b. If you bid your value v and v > b, you win and pay b, getting v − b > 0. If v < b you lose and get 0. Could you do better by bidding differently? Bidding above v changes the result only when b is between v and your bid, where you would win and pay b > v, a loss. Bidding below v changes the result only when b is between your bid and v, where you lose a profitable win. So truthful bidding is optimal whatever the others do, with no need to guess their bids. This is why the auction is called incentive compatible.
First-price auctions do not have this property; the winner pays their own bid, so each bidder shades the bid below the value. With n bidders whose values are independent and uniform on [0, 1], the symmetric equilibrium is to bid ((n − 1)/n) × v. The revenue equivalence theorem (Vickrey, Myerson) says that under standard conditions all auctions that give the item to the highest-value bidder produce the same expected revenue, which for this case is (n − 1)/(n + 1). The practical difference is not revenue but strategy: in a first-price auction a bidder needs a model of the competition, and so the market has bid-shading algorithms.
Games are also repeated and evolve. In a one-shot prisoner's dilemma defection is the only equilibrium. If the game repeats with no known end, cooperation can be an equilibrium supported by threats of punishment (the folk theorem); Axelrod's 1980s tournaments found that the simple strategy tit-for-tat, cooperate first and then copy the opponent, did well. In evolutionary game theory (Maynard Smith and Price, 1973), strategies spread through a population in proportion to payoff, and a strategy that cannot be invaded by a rare mutant is an evolutionarily stable strategy. The replicator dynamics are differential equations, and they connect game theory with learning algorithms and with biology.

example: Take a first-price sealed-bid auction with two bidders. Each bidder's value is uniform on [0, 1] and private. The equilibrium bid is half of the value (n = 2, so (n − 1)/n = 1/2). Let us verify that bid = v/2 is a best response for a bidder with value v = 0.8 when the rival follows b = v/2.
If we bid 0.4 (the equilibrium), we win when the rival's bid is below 0.4, which means the rival's value is below 0.8, which has probability 0.8. If we win we gain 0.8 − 0.4 = 0.4. Expected gain: 0.8 × 0.4 = 0.32.
If we bid 0.5, we win when the rival's value is below 1.0, which is certain (probability 1.0). Gain when we win: 0.8 − 0.5 = 0.3. Expected gain: 0.30, which is less than 0.32. If we bid 0.35, we win when the rival's value is below 0.7 (probability 0.7), gain 0.45, and the expected gain is 0.315, again less than 0.32. So 0.4 beats both nearby choices; it is the best response, and so (v/2, v/2) is an equilibrium. The bidder gives up half of the value in exchange for a lower price, and this shading is the central problem of bidding in first-price markets.
Revenue. The seller's expected revenue in the first-price auction is the expected highest bid = ½ × the expected highest value = ½ × (2/3) = 1/3, since the highest of two uniform values has mean 2/3. In a second-price auction with truthful bids the seller gets the expected second-highest value, which is 1/3 as well. Both give 1/3, as revenue equivalence says. A reserve price (floor) can raise revenue: with a reserve of 0.5, the optimal for uniform values, the expected revenue rises to 5/12 ≈ 0.417. The seller trades the risk of not selling (a probability 1/4 that both values are below 0.5) for higher prices.
Price of anarchy. Pigou's example: one unit of traffic chooses between two roads. Road A always takes 1 hour; road B takes x hours where x is the fraction of traffic using it. Each driver compares: B is never worse than A, since x ≤ 1, so everyone chooses B, and the equilibrium cost per driver is 1. A planner would send half to each: average cost = 0.5 × 1 + 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.75. The ratio 1/0.75 = 4/3 is the price of anarchy for this network, and 4/3 is the worst case for any network with linear costs.
intuition: Playing chess, you choose a move by asking what your opponent will do in reply, and what you would do after that.
The idea of an equilibrium is a resting point of that reasoning: a pair of plans such that each is the best reply to the other. If such a point exists and players are rational, nobody has a reason to move. The unease in the theory is that stable does not mean good. Equilibria are stable because each player is stuck, and a collectively better outcome may exist that no single player can reach by acting alone. This is why mechanism design exists: change the payoffs, by prices, penalties or rules, so that the stable point and the good point coincide.
mistakes: People often think a Nash equilibrium is the best outcome for the players. In fact it is only a stable one. In the prisoner's dilemma the equilibrium is worse for both players than cooperation, and the price of anarchy measures how much worse it can be.
People often think game theory assumes people are perfectly rational and selfish. In fact the standard model does, and experiments show real people differ (ultimatum games, altruism). But the theory still matters for software: bidding algorithms, automated agents and protocols are written to maximise a payoff, and the assumption is much closer to true for them.
People often think truthful bidding in a second-price auction means the seller loses revenue. In fact the seller receives the second-highest bid, but in a first-price auction bidders shade their bids, and revenue equivalence says that the expected revenue is the same under standard assumptions. The difference appears when bidders are not symmetric or risk-neutral, or when collusion is possible.
People often think a unique mixed equilibrium means players really randomise. In fact the equilibrium only says that the opponent must be indifferent. Players may randomise, or a population may contain a mix of pure strategies. Also many games have several equilibria, and the theory does not say which will be played.
People often think a truthful mechanism is safe from manipulation. In fact incentive compatibility holds for one agent acting alone, under the stated model. Groups can collude, one agent can create fake identities (sybil attacks), and the second-price auction is not collusion-proof. Real protocols need extra defences.
tradeoffs: Choose a second-price (or VCG) mechanism when you want bidders to report true values without modelling rivals, the number of items is small, and you can trust the auctioneer; the price is lower revenue in some settings, weak resistance to collusion, and the need for the seller to be seen as honest (a seller who can see the bids may inflate the second price). Choose a first-price auction when transparency matters, and the buyer sees exactly what they pay; the price is that bidders need bid-shading models, and small buyers who cannot model the market are disadvantaged.
Choose a reserve price or floor when competition is thin: with few bidders, a floor raises expected revenue but loses sales when values fall below it. Raise the floor only as far as the number of bidders and the value distribution justify; with many bidders a floor adds little and risks empty slots.
Choose pure analysis of equilibrium when the players are few, the rules are fixed and payoffs are known. Choose simulation, learning agents or evolutionary dynamics when there are many players, repeated play and unknown payoffs; this gives results for realistic cases but without proofs. Choose to design for the worst equilibrium (price of anarchy) when the system's safety depends on it; choose to design for the typical one when failures are tolerable.
Choose to model an attacker as a rational adversary (minimax) when defences must hold against the best attack, and accept conservative defences. Choose a probabilistic or statistical model of attackers when they are many and unsophisticated, and accept that a clever attacker may exceed the model.
friction: Computing is hard: finding a Nash equilibrium is PPAD-complete in general, and equilibrium selection is unresolved, so theory cannot say which of many equilibria will occur. Models need payoffs, but real payoffs are unknown, vary between players and may include things that are not money. Information is incomplete: players do not know each other's values (Bayesian games), and the assumption of a known prior distribution of values is a strong one.
Real markets break assumptions. Ad auctions are repeated millions of times a day, with budgets, return-on-spend constraints and automated bidders that learn; the single-item model gives only the first idea, and the behaviour of many learning bidders against each other can cycle or settle in unexpected places. Collusion, sybil identities, and information leaks (a seller or exchange that sees the bids) create incentives the model excluded. In blockchains, incentive arguments assume the cost and reward model holds; selfish mining shows that a miner with a share of hash power well below 50% can earn more than the fair share by withholding blocks.
Finally, designing the rules changes the game: people respond to a metric by gaming it (Goodhart's law), so a mechanism that is optimal for yesterday's behaviour may be worse tomorrow. The theory is better at saying what can go wrong than at predicting what will.
practice: When you design or analyse a system with several self-interested parties, list the players, what each can do, what each wants, and what each knows. Ask what the best response of each is, and what happens if every party does that. This simple check finds many design flaws: rate limits that clients answer by opening more connections, quotas that make teams over-request, retries that add load in an overload, rewards that can be farmed.
For auctions, understand your format. In second-price auctions bid your value (adjusted for constraints). In first-price auctions use bid shading, based on an estimate of the distribution of the winning price, and monitor win rate and the gap between bid and clearing price. Floors, budgets and pacing change the picture, so test in simulation and with small live experiments. For protocols, write the attack as a deviation: what does a rational participant gain by deviating, and what does it cost?
For repeated interactions, use reputation or simple conditional strategies, as in rate limiting that is relaxed for well-behaved clients. For learning agents, no-regret algorithms such as multiplicative weights are simple and give convergence to coarse equilibria in repeated games. To study, read Roughgarden's "Twenty Lectures on Algorithmic Game Theory" and his lecture notes on auctions; the book by Nisan et al. is the standard reference, and Shoham and Leyton-Brown's "Multiagent Systems" is free online.
quiz: What is a Nash equilibrium, and does it have to be good for the players? || It is a combination of strategies in which no player can gain by changing only their own strategy. It is stable but not necessarily efficient; in the prisoner's dilemma the equilibrium (defect, defect) is worse for both than (cooperate, cooperate).
Why is truthful bidding a dominant strategy in a second-price auction? || The price you pay does not depend on your own bid, only on the highest rival bid b. Bidding your value v wins exactly when v > b, which is when winning is profitable. Bidding higher can win at a price above v, and bidding lower can lose a profitable win.
In a first-price auction with two bidders and values uniform on [0, 1], what is the equilibrium bid, and what is the expected revenue? || Bid half the value (bid = v/2). The expected revenue is 1/3, the same as in a second-price auction, by revenue equivalence.
Pigou's example has a road with a fixed cost 1 and a road with cost equal to the traffic fraction x. What are the equilibrium and optimal average costs? || In equilibrium everyone uses the variable road, so the average cost is 1. The optimum splits traffic half and half, giving 0.5 × 1 + 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.75. The price of anarchy is 4/3.
You are designing a reward system for a distributed network where nodes can lie about their work. What would you analyse before launch? || Treat each node as a rational player and find its best deviation: what it gains from lying, withholding or colluding, and what it risks. Check whether honesty is a dominant strategy or an equilibrium, whether coalitions or fake identities break it, and what the worst equilibrium (price of anarchy) looks like.

@@ randomgraphs
value: Random graph theory asks what a network looks like when its links are made by chance, and what follows from only a few simple rules. The answers are surprising. If every node has just a few random links, the graph is almost certainly connected into one giant piece, and any two nodes are only a handful of steps apart. A million nodes with an average of 10 links each have a typical distance of about 6 hops. Adding a few random long-range links to an ordered, local network, such as a ring of neighbours, collapses its distances from hundreds of hops to a handful while keeping local clusters intact. This is the small-world effect.
This explains why "six degrees of separation" is plausible, why a rumour or a failure notice spread by gossip reaches a whole cluster in about log n rounds, and why a search over a graph of nearest-neighbour links, as in HNSW vector indexes, can reach the right region in a few dozen steps out of millions of nodes. It also explains the opposite risks. Networks with power-law degrees have hub nodes that are hotspots and single points of failure, and diseases, outages and bad data spread along the same short paths that make search fast.
For the layer above, probability (L4) supplies the model: each possible edge appears independently with probability p, and counting arguments (expected degree, branching processes, tail bounds) give exact thresholds. Graph theory (L4) supplies the language: connected components, paths, diameter, degree distribution and clustering. Gossip protocols (L2) use random peer choice to spread information in O(log n) rounds, and approximate nearest-neighbour search such as HNSW (L2) builds a graph with the small-world property so that greedy search is fast. Datacenter fabrics, peer-to-peer overlays and social-network analysis use the same results.
terms: Erdős–Rényi graph G(n, p) — a graph on n nodes in which each pair is joined by an edge independently with probability p.
Average degree (c) — the mean number of neighbours per node; in G(n, p) it is about np.
Giant component — a connected piece that contains a constant fraction of all nodes.
Phase transition — a sudden change in the structure of the graph as a parameter crosses a threshold, such as the appearance of the giant component at c = 1.
Diameter and average path length — the longest, and the mean, shortest-path distance between pairs of nodes.
Clustering coefficient — the chance that two neighbours of a node are also neighbours of each other; high in social networks and in lattices, low in random graphs.
Small-world network — a network with high clustering and a short average path length, of the order of log n.
Scale-free network — a network whose degree distribution follows a power law, P(k) ∝ k^(−γ), so a few hubs have very high degree.
history: Paul Erdős and Alfréd Rényi created the theory in 1959 and 1960 with "On Random Graphs I" and "On the evolution of random graphs". They noticed that properties of random graphs appear suddenly: when the average degree passes 1, a giant component emerges, and when np passes ln n, the graph becomes connected. Edgar Gilbert had defined the same model in 1959. For decades this was pure mathematics, with the surprise that the properties were sharp.
The social side started with Stanley Milgram's 1967 letter-forwarding experiment: of 296 volunteers asked to get a letter to a stranger in Boston through acquaintances, 64 chains reached the target, with about 5 intermediaries on average, that is roughly six links. This gave the phrase "six degrees". Mark Granovetter's "The Strength of Weak Ties" (1973) argued that distant acquaintances are what connect communities. In 1998 Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz gave the mathematical explanation in Nature: start with a ring lattice, rewire a small fraction of edges at random, and path lengths collapse while clustering stays high. The following year, Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert (Science, 1999) showed that the Web and other networks have power-law degrees and explained them by growth with preferential attachment. Jon Kleinberg (2000) asked the algorithmic question: not whether short paths exist, but whether a node with only local knowledge can find them, and showed that this depends on how the long links are distributed.
In computing, the results were adopted gradually. Demers et al. (1987) proposed epidemic (gossip) protocols for replicated databases at Xerox. Peer-to-peer systems and distributed hash tables in the early 2000s (Chord, Symphony) used small-world ideas. Malkov and colleagues (2014, 2016) used navigable small-world graphs for nearest-neighbour search, culminating in HNSW, which is now the default index in many vector databases. Singla et al. (2012) proposed Jellyfish, a datacenter network wired as a random regular graph, which supports more servers than a fat-tree built with the same switches.
how: Take G(n, p) and let c = np be the average degree. Explore it by a branching process: start at a node, count its neighbours (about c on average), then their new neighbours, and so on. If each node produces on average c new nodes, the explored set grows like c^d after d steps while c > 1, and dies out if c < 1. This simple argument, combined with probability's results on branching processes, gives the phase transition. For c < 1, all components are small, with the largest of order log n. For c > 1, one giant component of size S·n exists, where S is the positive solution of S = 1 − e^(−cS). The equation says: a node is not in the giant component only if none of its neighbours is, which has probability e^(−cS) (the Poisson chance that all neighbours miss). For c = 2, S ≈ 0.80; for c = 3, S ≈ 0.94. The whole graph is connected, with high probability, when c exceeds ln n.
The path length is also easy. With branching factor c, the number of nodes within d hops is about c^d. It reaches n when d ≈ ln n/ln c. So the typical distance in a random graph is ln n/ln c, which grows only logarithmically in the number of nodes. For n = 10⁶ and c = 10, that is 13.8/2.3 = 6. Doubling the network adds a fraction of a hop. The clustering coefficient, in contrast, is only about c/n, almost zero. Real social networks have high clustering, because friends of friends are friends, and still have short paths. Neither the regular lattice (high clustering, long paths) nor the random graph (short paths, no clustering) fits.
Watts and Strogatz resolve this. Take n nodes in a ring, each joined to its k nearest neighbours (k/2 on each side). The path length is about n/(2k) and clustering is about 3/4. Now rewire each edge with probability p to a random endpoint. Each shortcut jumps across the ring and shortens many paths at once. A simulation with n = 1,000 and k = 10 gives these numbers: with p = 0 the average path length is 50 and clustering 0.67; with p = 0.01 (about 50 shortcuts) the path length is 9.6 and clustering 0.65; with p = 0.1 the path length is 4.4 and clustering 0.49; with p = 1 (fully random) 3.3 and 0.01. A thin layer of randomness is enough to reach short paths, and it barely harms clustering. Short paths are a property of the whole graph that a few edges can change, while clustering is a local property that those edges barely touch.
Having short paths does not mean they can be found. Kleinberg's model places nodes on a 2-D grid, links each node to its neighbours, and adds one long-range link per node, to a node at distance r with probability proportional to r^(−α). A message is passed by greedy routing: always hand it to the neighbour closest to the target. He proved that the delivery time is polylogarithmic in n only for α = 2 (the dimension of the grid): long links must be spread evenly over all scales, with about as many links at each distance scale. For other exponents, greedy routing takes a power of n steps even though short paths exist. This is the principle behind navigable small-world graphs, and HNSW builds it explicitly: each node gets links at several scales.
HNSW makes this concrete. Each inserted vector is assigned a top layer at random, with probability 1/M of reaching each layer above the previous one (M is the number of links per node; for M = 16 and 10⁶ vectors, layer sizes are roughly 10⁶, 62,000, 3,900, 244, 15, 1). A query starts at the top layer, where there are few nodes with long hops, moves greedily to the closest node, then drops one layer and repeats, ending in the dense bottom layer, where a best-first search with a candidate list of size ef returns the approximate neighbours. The layers act as the "all scales" of Kleinberg's analysis: coarse long jumps first, then finer ones. Cost per query is about O(log n) hops times M distance computations each.
For gossip, the random choice of peers makes the communication graph look like a random graph. In push gossip, each informed node sends the rumour to one random node per round. The number of informed nodes roughly doubles per round at the start, then the last nodes are reached more slowly, giving a total of about log₂ n + ln n rounds. For n = 10⁶ that is about 20 + 14 = 34 rounds, with no coordinator and no knowledge of the full membership. Scale-free graphs from preferential attachment have P(k) ∝ k^(−3); the largest hub has degree about m√n, so with m = 3 and n = 10⁶ it has around 3,000 links while the average degree is 6. Such graphs stay connected when random nodes fail, because most nodes are low-degree, but they split quickly if the hubs are removed.

example: Take a random graph with n = 1,000 nodes and average degree c = 3, so p = 3/999 ≈ 0.003. Each node has about 3 neighbours on average.
Size of the giant component. Solve S = 1 − e^(−3S). Try S = 0.9: 1 − e^(−2.7) = 1 − 0.067 = 0.933. Try 0.94: 1 − e^(−2.82) = 1 − 0.0596 = 0.9404. This is stable, so S ≈ 0.94 and about 940 of the 1,000 nodes are in the giant component. A simulation of exactly this graph gave 934. The remaining nodes are mostly isolated or in tiny pieces: the chance that a node has no neighbours at all is e^(−3) ≈ 5%, so about 50 isolated nodes are expected.
Distance. From a node, 3 hops reach about 3 + 9 + 27 = 39 nodes, 6 hops about 3 + 9 + 27 + 81 + 243 + 729 ≈ 1,090 (more than the 1,000 that exist, so the process saturates). The estimate ln 1000/ln 3 = 6.9/1.1 = 6.3 hops agrees with the simulation, which gave an average distance of 6.26 inside the giant component.
Below the threshold. Set c = 0.5. The branching process shrinks by half each generation, so a node's component is small: each node reaches on average 1/(1 − c) = 2 nodes, including itself. A run with n = 1,000 and c = 0.5 gave a largest component of only 10 nodes. Raising the average degree from 0.5 to 3 changed the graph from dust to one connected piece with 94% of the nodes. This sudden change is the phase transition.
Gossip cost. A cluster of 1,000 nodes uses push gossip with one message per node per round. Rounds needed ≈ log₂ 1000 + ln 1000 = 10 + 6.9 ≈ 17. Each round sends at most 1,000 messages (one per informed node), so the total is under 17,000 messages, about 17 per node, and it reaches all nodes with high probability. Doubling to 2,000 nodes adds only about 2 rounds. Compare a full broadcast from one coordinator, which needs 999 messages from one machine and fails if that machine fails.
Hubs. Build a preferential attachment network with n = 1,000,000 and m = 3 links per new node. Average degree is 2m = 6, but the biggest hub has about m√n = 3,000 links. If this is a service-dependency graph, one node is a dependency of 3,000 others. Removing 5% of random nodes barely changes connectivity, while removing the best-connected few percent of nodes damages it far more.
intuition: In a large party where everyone knows a few people, a message passed to one random acquaintance at a time reaches everyone after only a few passes, because each pass multiplies the reach.
The short paths come from growth by multiplication: each step outward multiplies the number of reachable nodes by the branching factor, and multiplying reaches a million nodes in a few steps. Locality cannot do this, as a lattice only adds a ring of nodes per step. Randomness creates shortcuts that jump across the graph, and a few shortcuts are enough because each one connects two whole neighbourhoods. The same arithmetic is behind gossip's log n rounds and the few layers of an HNSW index.
mistakes: People often think a random graph has no structure. In fact it has sharp, predictable properties: the giant component appears at c = 1, connectivity at c = ln n, and path lengths are ln n/ln c. Randomness makes the global structure more predictable, not less.
People often think a small-world network is the same as a random network. In fact a small-world network keeps the high clustering of a lattice. A random graph with the same average degree has clustering near zero. The interest of the Watts–Strogatz model is that both properties exist together.
People often think short paths mean you can find them easily. In fact finding a short path with local information needs the right distribution of long links, as Kleinberg showed: with the wrong distribution greedy routing takes a power of n steps. This is why HNSW builds links at all scales on purpose.
People often think real networks are Erdős–Rényi graphs. In fact real networks have heavy-tailed degrees, clustering and community structure. The Poisson degree distribution of G(n, p) has no hubs. Preferential attachment fits some networks, but Broido and Clauset (2019) found that strictly scale-free structure is rare. Use the models as baselines to compare against, not as descriptions.
People often think hubs make networks more robust. In fact hubs make a network robust against random failures and fragile against targeted attacks or hub failures. In a service graph, the hub is the dependency that every incident passes through.
tradeoffs: Choose random peer selection (gossip over a random graph) when membership is large and changing, nodes can fail, and you need robust spreading without central control. You pay with probabilistic delivery (the last nodes are reached slowly), redundant messages (each node gets the rumour several times), and latency of log n rounds. Choose a structured overlay or a spanning tree when you need exactly-once delivery with minimum messages or a defined order; the price is the cost of repair when nodes fail.
Choose a larger M and efConstruction for an HNSW index when recall matters and memory is available; memory per vector grows with M (roughly M × 2 × 4 bytes for links on the bottom layer, on top of the vector), build time rises, and query time rises with ef. Choose smaller M or a quantised index (IVF-PQ, DiskANN-style) when the dataset is too large for RAM. HNSW gives high recall and low latency, but it is memory-hungry and costly to update or delete.
Choose a random regular graph for a network or overlay when you want high bandwidth and short paths with simple wiring; you give up the predictable routing and the easy cabling of a regular pattern like a fat-tree. Choose a hierarchical topology when routing and fault isolation are more important than average path length.
Choose to design out hubs (limit fan-in, shard hot nodes, add replicas) when failure of a single node is unacceptable. Accept hubs when they are cheap to protect, such as a well-replicated metadata service, since a hub is also an efficient place to concentrate monitoring and caching.
friction: The models are idealised. Real graphs have degree correlations, communities, geography and time. A fitted random-graph model often predicts the path length well and the clustering badly, and tells nothing about communities. Properties such as "the diameter is log n" are asymptotic; for a given real network, path length may be dominated by a few remote parts.
In systems, the same short paths cause trouble. Failures, bad data and malware spread quickly through a small world. Gossip's reliability is probabilistic and its message overhead is high; membership information becomes stale; partitions break the random-graph assumption (two data centres with a slow link between them are not one well-mixed graph). In HNSW, the graph is built incrementally, so the result depends on insertion order, deletions leave holes that hurt recall until the index is rebuilt, and high-dimensional data with hubness (some points are near-neighbours of very many others) makes some regions hard to reach. Greedy search can stop at a local minimum, which is why the candidate list size ef matters.
Finally, measuring is hard: complete graph data may be unavailable, and sampled subgraphs distort degree distributions, so estimates of exponent and clustering from a sample can be biased.
practice: When you meet a graph in your system (service dependencies, lineage, a social graph, a peer network), compute the basic numbers first: node count, mean and maximum degree, the degree distribution on log-log axes, the size of the largest component, the average path length from a sample of sources, and the clustering coefficient. Compare with a random graph of the same size and degree to see what structure you really have.
For gossip, use random peer selection with a fanout of 2 to 3, push-pull exchange for faster convergence, and a limit on message age; expect about log₂ n + ln n rounds, and set the period from your convergence target. For vector search, tune HNSW with M between 16 and 48, efConstruction at 100 to 400 and ef at query time against a recall target measured on your own data; rebuild the index after many deletions. For dependency graphs, find the hubs and test their failure: they decide the blast radius of an incident.
To experiment, use NetworkX in Python: generate G(n, p), Watts–Strogatz and Barabási–Albert graphs, compute path lengths and clustering, remove random nodes versus top-degree nodes, and watch the giant component. To study, read Barabási's free "Network Science" book (chapters 3 to 5 for random, scale-free and small-world models), Newman's "Networks", and Kleinberg's 2000 paper.

@@ amdahl
value: Amdahl's law answers a question every engineer asks when a job is slow: if I add more machines, how much faster will it get? The answer is limited by the part of the work that cannot be split. If 5% of a job must run in sequence (reading a config, planning a query, merging final results, waiting for the slowest task), then even with a million processors the job cannot finish more than 20 times faster than with one, because that 5% still takes the same time. The formula is speedup = 1 / ((1 − p) + p/n), where p is the parallel fraction and n the number of processors. As n grows, it approaches 1/(1 − p).
The law is a warning against buying capacity before finding the serial part. Doubling a Spark cluster does nothing for the driver, for one skewed reducer, for a single-writer database or for a nightly job that waits on an upstream team. The law also applies to any speed-up of any part: making 80% of the runtime 10 times faster gives only 1/(0.2 + 0.08) = 3.6 times overall. The bottleneck moves to what you did not improve.
Gustafson's law (1988) adds the other half. People rarely use extra machines to run the same job faster; they use them to run a bigger job in the same time (more data, finer resolution, more users). If the serial part stays fixed while the parallel part grows with the machine, the speedup can be nearly linear in n. For the layer above, asymptotic analysis (L4) gives the habit of asking what happens as n grows without bound, and shows that the bound 1/(1 − p) does not depend on n at all. Data, tensor and pipeline parallelism (L2), batch processing (L1), collective communication (L1) and GPU computing (L1) are all judged by these two laws.
terms: Speedup — the time on one processor divided by the time on n processors, S(n) = T(1)/T(n).
Efficiency — speedup divided by the number of processors, S(n)/n; 100% means perfect scaling.
Serial fraction — the share of the original single-processor run time that cannot be parallelised; 1 − p in Amdahl's formula.
Strong scaling — keeping the problem size fixed while adding processors; this is the case of Amdahl's law.
Weak scaling — growing the problem size in proportion to the processors; this is the case of Gustafson's law.
Scaled speedup — the speedup measured when the problem grows with n; Gustafson gives S = s + (1 − s)·n for serial fraction s of the scaled run.
Work and span — total operations W, and the length D of the longest chain of dependent operations; run time on p processors is at least max(W/p, D).
Contention and coherency — the costs of waiting for shared resources (locks, queues) and of keeping copies consistent; they grow with n and are the extra terms in the Universal Scalability Law.
history: Gene Amdahl, the chief architect of the IBM System/360, presented the argument at the 1967 AFIPS Spring Joint Computer Conference in a three-page paper, "Validity of the Single Processor Approach to Achieving Large Scale Computing Capabilities". It was a reply to those who believed that many slow processors would soon replace one fast processor. He argued that the serial parts of real programs (bookkeeping, irregular data flow, input and output) were too large for that to pay. The formula in its modern form was written by others later; the paper itself contains the argument in words and examples.
In 1988 John Gustafson, at Sandia National Laboratories, published "Reevaluating Amdahl's Law" in the Communications of the ACM. His group had run real applications on a 1,024-processor nCUBE and obtained speedups of more than 1,000, which seemed impossible under Amdahl's law with a serial fraction of even 1%. His point was that the problem size was not fixed: users scaled the problem to the machine, and the serial part (setup, I/O) stayed almost constant in absolute time. The reformulation is also called the Gustafson–Barsis law, after Edwin Barsis, a colleague at Sandia.
Later work added realism. Karp and Flatt (1990) proposed measuring the effective serial fraction from observed speedups, so that a rising value signals overhead. Neil Gunther's Universal Scalability Law (1993, popularised in "Guerrilla Capacity Planning", 2007) added a coherency term, so that throughput can fall when too many nodes are added. Hill and Marty (2008) applied Amdahl's law to multicore chip design, asking how to spend a fixed transistor budget on a few large cores versus many small ones. The same reasoning appears in the roofline model and in cluster-scale training, where communication plays the part of the serial fraction.
how: Start with the model. A job takes time T on one processor. A fraction p of that time can be spread perfectly over n processors, and the remaining fraction 1 − p runs on one processor in any case. The time on n processors is T·((1 − p) + p/n). Dividing, the speedup is S(n) = 1/((1 − p) + p/n). Three facts follow. First, S(1) = 1. Second, S increases with n but with diminishing returns, because the p/n term shrinks while 1 − p stays. Third, as n goes to infinity, S approaches 1/(1 − p). This limit is where asymptotic analysis (L4) enters: we do not need the exact curve to know the ceiling, since the limit exists and is set by one number.
It is useful to read the formula as "speedup of a part". If a fraction f of the run time is made s times faster, and the rest is unchanged, the overall speedup is 1/((1 − f) + f/s). Parallelism is the case where s = n. This reading covers all optimisation: a faster disk, a cache, a GPU kernel, a better index. A kernel that is 50 times faster but takes 40% of the run time gives 1/(0.6 + 0.008) = 1.64 times. Before optimising, measure what fraction of the time each part takes. The law says: work on the biggest fraction first, and the gains are bounded by what you leave alone.
Efficiency makes the cost visible. Efficiency E(n) = S(n)/n. With p = 0.95, S(10) = 1/(0.05 + 0.095) = 6.9 and E = 69%; S(100) = 1/(0.05 + 0.0095) = 16.8 and E = 17%; S(1,000) = 19.6 and E = 2%. You pay for 1,000 machines and use the equivalent of 20. This is why strong scaling is limited in practice, long before the ceiling, by cost. The point at which you stop adding machines is decided by the efficiency you can accept (often 50% or more), not by the ceiling.
Gustafson's view changes the question. Suppose that on n processors the run takes time 1 (normalised), of which s is serial and 1 − s parallel. The same scaled problem on one processor would take s + (1 − s)·n. So the scaled speedup is S = s + (1 − s)·n, which grows linearly in n with slope 1 − s. With s = 0.05 and n = 1,000, S = 0.05 + 0.95 × 1,000 = 950. The two laws are not in conflict; they answer different questions. Amdahl: how much faster is this fixed job? Gustafson: how much more work can I do in the same time? A related tool is isoefficiency: how fast must the problem size grow with n to keep efficiency constant? Summing N numbers on p processors takes N/p + log₂ p steps, so efficiency is about N/(N + p log₂ p), and N must grow as p log p to hold efficiency near 1.
Real systems add overheads that grow with n. Communication, synchronisation, load imbalance and coordination (shuffles, barriers, locks, leader nodes) make the "parallel" part cost more as n increases. Gunther's Universal Scalability Law captures this with two parameters: α for contention (waiting on a shared resource, like Amdahl's serial fraction) and β for coherency (the cost of pairwise agreement, which grows like n²). Relative capacity is C(n) = n/(1 + α(n − 1) + β·n(n − 1)). With β > 0 the curve reaches a maximum at n* = √((1 − α)/β) and then decreases: adding nodes lowers throughput. This explains why a database cluster, a lock-heavy service or an all-to-all shuffle can get slower with more nodes.
The work-span view gives a program-side form. Count the total work W and the span D (the longest chain of dependent steps). With a good scheduler, time on p processors is at most W/p + D (Brent's theorem) and at least max(W/p, D). The span plays the role of the serial part: parallelism W/D is the most processors you can use well. For Spark, the stages and shuffles define D, and a single huge task is a long chain that no number of executors shortens. To measure the effective serial fraction from experiments, use Karp–Flatt: e = (1/S − 1/n)/(1 − 1/n). If e is constant as n grows, you have a plain Amdahl limit; if it grows, there are overheads that increase with n.

example: Take a Spark job that processes 10 TB of impressions in 10 hours on a single node-equivalent. Profiling shows that 10% of the time is serial: query planning and file listing on the driver, one final aggregation done on one task, and writing a manifest. So p = 0.9.
Amdahl. With 10 nodes, S = 1/(0.1 + 0.09) = 5.26, so the job runs in 1.9 hours and efficiency is 53%. With 100 nodes, S = 1/(0.1 + 0.009) = 9.17, giving 1.09 hours and an efficiency of 9%. With 1,000 nodes, S = 9.91: 1.01 hours. The ceiling is 10, a run time of 1 hour, which is the serial part. Going from 100 to 1,000 nodes buys about 5 minutes for ten times the cost. The better investment is to attack the serial 10%: if the final aggregation is half of it (5% of the run time) and a tree aggregation makes it parallel, p rises to 0.95 and the ceiling doubles to 20.
Karp–Flatt check. Suppose the job was measured: 16 nodes give a speedup of 6.0, and 32 nodes give 8.0. Then e(16) = (1/6 − 1/16)/(1 − 1/16) = (0.1667 − 0.0625)/0.9375 = 0.111, and e(32) = (0.125 − 0.03125)/0.96875 = 0.0968. Both are close to 0.1, so the job behaves as Amdahl predicts with about a 10% serial part. If e had risen from 0.05 to 0.15, shuffle or coordination overhead would be growing with the cluster.
Gustafson. Next quarter the data grows 100 times, and the team keeps the 10-hour window by using 100 nodes. The serial part stays at about 1 hour of absolute time (planning and manifests do not grow with data), so the serial fraction of the scaled run is s = 1/10 = 0.1. Scaled speedup S = 0.1 + 0.9 × 100 = 90.1: the 100-node cluster finishes in 10 hours a job that one node would need about 900 hours for, with efficiency 90%. The same hardware that gave only 9.17 for a fixed job gives 90 for a growing job.
USL. A service with contention α = 0.05 and coherency β = 0.001 has C(8) = 8/(1 + 0.35 + 0.056) = 5.7, C(16) = 16/(1 + 0.75 + 0.24) = 8.0, C(32) = 32/(1 + 1.55 + 0.992) = 9.0, and C(64) = 64/(1 + 3.15 + 4.03) = 7.8. The peak is at n* = √(0.95/0.001) ≈ 31, with C ≈ 9.0. Beyond 31 nodes the system gets slower. The decision is clear: scale up to about 30, and then fix contention and coherency, not add nodes.
intuition: Ten people can paint a fence ten times faster, but they cannot make the paint dry faster, and if they all queue for one brush they get in each other's way.
The law is natural because time adds up. The total time is the sum of the serial time and the parallel time, and only the second part shrinks when you add workers. Whatever remains at the limit is the serial time, and it is a fixed floor. Gustafson's view is just as natural: if the fence gets ten times longer when you have ten times more painters, the drying time is the same relative to the job, so the painters stay busy. Which law applies depends on whether the job stays fixed or grows.
mistakes: People often think Amdahl's law says parallel computing does not scale. In fact it says that for a fixed job it scales only until the serial part dominates. For jobs that grow with the machine (more data, more users), scaling can be nearly linear, as Gustafson showed, and modern big-data workloads are mostly of this kind.
People often think the serial fraction is just the code that is not multithreaded. In fact it includes everything that does not speed up with more workers: waiting on a lock, the slowest straggler task, a shuffle bottleneck, a single-threaded write to a database, a leader node, network latency, start-up time and human steps such as review or approval.
People often think speedup stops at the Amdahl ceiling and cannot go beyond. In fact the ceiling holds for the model in which the work is fixed and one machine has the same speed per processor. Superlinear speedup is possible when more machines also bring more cache or memory (the data now fit in RAM), and algorithmic changes can lower the serial part.
People often think adding nodes never hurts. In fact with coordination costs that grow with n (all-to-all shuffles, lock contention, consensus), throughput can fall beyond an optimum, as the Universal Scalability Law shows. More nodes can also raise the chance of a straggler, which sets the finish time of a stage.
People often think Amdahl's law is only about CPUs. In fact it is a statement about any improvement of a part of a process: a faster network, caching, a quicker build, a faster review. The overall gain is limited by the share of time spent in the part you did not change.
tradeoffs: Choose scale-out (more machines) when the parallel fraction is above about 95%, the work is partitionable with little communication, and the problem is growing, so Gustafson's view applies. Choose scale-up (faster single cores, more memory per node) or algorithmic work when the serial fraction is 10% or more, or when coordination dominates; a faster core helps every part of the run time, including the serial part. You pay more per unit of compute, and single machines have limits.
Choose to attack the serial part before adding nodes when efficiency at the current size is below roughly 50%. Techniques: tree aggregation instead of a single reducer, key salting for skew, speculative execution for stragglers, asynchronous I/O, moving work from the driver to executors, and pipelining stages. You give up engineering time and some simplicity; you gain a higher ceiling that applies at any cluster size.
Choose strong scaling for deadlines (finish tonight's report in under an hour) and weak scaling for capacity (process next year's data in the same window). Strong scaling needs high efficiency at every added node, so costs rise quickly; weak scaling is cheaper per unit of work but needs the serial part to stay constant as the data grows.
Choose heterogeneous designs (a few fast cores for the serial part, many small ones for the parallel part) when you control the hardware, as Hill and Marty analysed: the serial fraction runs fastest on a big core. You trade flexibility and cost for a higher ceiling. In organisations, the same choice applies: add reviewers or invest in the one release step that everything queues behind.
friction: The model is simple, and real systems are not. The serial fraction is hard to measure: it changes with input size, data skew and cluster configuration, and part of it only shows up at scale (a shuffle that was cheap with 10 nodes costs more with 1,000 because the number of connections grows as n²). The model assumes the parallel part divides perfectly and costs nothing to divide, which is never true: load imbalance, stragglers and communication add terms that grow with n.
Fixed-size and scaled views are often mixed up, leading to wrong predictions in both directions. Speedups quoted on small problems with many cores do not predict behaviour on large ones; and weak-scaling results hide the fact that a single job is not faster. Memory and bandwidth limits form another ceiling that Amdahl's law does not include (the roofline model addresses it), and in power-limited hardware, adding cores can reduce clock speed.
Finally, in distributed systems the cost of reliability grows with scale. With more machines, some machine is almost always failing or slow, retries add work, and the tail latency of a stage is the maximum over its tasks, which rises with the number of tasks. So the realistic ceiling is lower than the fraction-based estimate, and the shape of the curve is closer to the USL than to a smooth Amdahl curve.
practice: Measure before you scale. Run the job at three or four sizes (for example 8, 16, 32, 64 cores), record the run time, and compute speedup, efficiency and the Karp–Flatt serial fraction e. If e is flat, find the serial part with a profile or the Spark UI: look for a long driver phase, a stage with few tasks, one task that runs much longer than the median, or a long gap between stages. If e grows, look at shuffle volume, lock contention and network use.
Plan capacity with efficiency targets. Decide the lowest efficiency you will pay for (for batch jobs 50 to 70% is common), find the n where the measured curve reaches it, and stop there. Beyond that point use the money to fix the serial part. For data pipelines, a useful habit is to list the stages that run on a single task or a single node (writing a single output file, a global sort, a final dedupe) and to remove or distribute them.
For growth, think in Gustafson terms: how does each stage's cost change when the data grows 10 times? Parts that grow linearly parallelise with the data; parts that stay constant (planning) or grow with the cluster (coordination) set the limit. Use the USL to fit throughput measurements from load tests with a few node counts, and read off the optimum n*. To study, read Amdahl's three pages, Gustafson's two pages, then the Hill and Marty paper and Gunther's book, and run a small experiment with Python's multiprocessing on a task with a known serial part.
quiz: State Amdahl's law and its limit. || Speedup S(n) = 1/((1 − p) + p/n), where p is the parallel fraction and n the number of processors. As n grows, S approaches 1/(1 − p), so a 5% serial part limits the speedup to 20 times.
What question does Gustafson's law answer differently from Amdahl's? || Amdahl asks how much faster a fixed job gets with more processors. Gustafson asks how much larger a job can be run in a fixed time, if the serial part stays constant; the scaled speedup is S = s + (1 − s)n, nearly linear in n when s is small.
A job has 10% serial time. What speedup and efficiency do 100 processors give, and what would you do instead of adding more? || S = 1/(0.1 + 0.9/100) = 9.17, efficiency 9.2%. Reduce the serial part (tree aggregation, move work off the driver, fix skew), because the ceiling is 10 and more nodes buy almost nothing.
Speedups of 6.0 on 16 cores and 8.0 on 32 cores are measured. What does Karp–Flatt say? || e(16) = (1/6 − 1/16)/(1 − 1/16) ≈ 0.111 and e(32) ≈ 0.097. They are roughly constant near 10%, so the job is Amdahl-limited by a serial part of about 10%, and overhead is not growing with the cluster.
A service's throughput falls when going from 40 to 64 nodes. Which model explains it, and what do you do? || The Universal Scalability Law: a coherency term β·n(n − 1) makes capacity peak at n* = √((1 − α)/β) and then fall. Stay near the optimum and reduce contention (α) and coordination (β), for example by sharding state, using fewer cross-node round trips, or batching.
