@@ raytracing
value: Ray tracing computes an image by following rays of light. For each pixel, the renderer sends a ray from the camera into the scene, finds the nearest surface the ray hits, and asks what light reaches that point. To answer, it sends more rays: toward each light to check for shadows, along the mirror direction for reflections, and in random directions to collect light bounced from other surfaces. The core operation is always the same: given a ray, find the first object it hits. A bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) is the data structure that makes this question cheap.
Without a BVH, a ray must be tested against every triangle. A film scene can have 100 million triangles, and an image of 1920 × 1080 pixels has about 2 million pixels, each needing tens or hundreds of rays. A BVH wraps groups of triangles in boxes and groups of boxes in bigger boxes, forming a tree. A ray that misses a box also misses everything inside it, so whole branches are skipped. A scene with 1 million triangles needs roughly 20 levels of descent, plus a few dozen box tests, instead of 1 million triangle tests. The cost per ray grows with the logarithm of the scene size, not linearly.
Offline renderers such as Pixar RenderMan, Blender Cycles and Disney Hyperion use ray tracing for almost every film frame. Since 2018, NVIDIA RTX GPUs have had fixed-function ray tracing cores, and AMD, Apple and Intel GPUs followed, so games combine rasterization with traced shadows, reflections and global illumination. The layers below supply the pieces: trees (L3) give the BVH its structure, spatial indexes (L2) give the idea of partitioning space to prune searches, coordinate transforms (L4) let one object be placed many times, and Monte Carlo methods (L4) turn the hard integral of "all light arriving at a point" into an average of random rays.
terms: Ray — a half-line with an origin and a direction; points on it are \( \mathbf{o} + t\,\mathbf{d} \) for \( t \ge 0 \).
Primary ray — the ray from the camera through a pixel; secondary rays are shadow, reflection and bounce rays.
Bounding volume hierarchy (BVH) — a tree where each node holds a box that contains all the geometry below it; leaves hold a few triangles.
Axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) — a box whose sides are parallel to the coordinate axes; it needs only six numbers and is very cheap to test.
Slab test — the ray–box test: intersect the ray with the pair of planes on each axis and check that the three intervals overlap.
Surface area heuristic (SAH) — a rule for building a good BVH: choose the split that minimizes the expected cost of traversal, with the chance of a ray entering a box taken as proportional to its surface area.
Path tracing — ray tracing that follows one random light path per sample and averages many samples per pixel; it is an unbiased Monte Carlo estimate of the rendering equation.
Acceleration structure — the general name for a BVH, k-d tree or grid built to speed up ray queries; in DirectX and Vulkan this is the object you build before tracing.
Denoising — a filter, often a neural network, that cleans the noisy image produced by a low number of samples per pixel.
history: The idea of tracing rays from the eye is old. Arthur Appel (IBM, 1968) described "ray casting" to decide which surface is visible and whether a point is in shadow. The breakthrough for realistic images came with Turner Whitted's 1980 paper "An improved illumination model for shaded display". He recursively spawned reflection and refraction rays from every hit, and showed glass spheres and mirrors that rasterization of that time could not produce. It was also very slow, which started the search for faster intersection.
In the same year Steven Rubin and Whitted proposed hierarchies of bounding volumes. Kay and Kajiya (1986) made the box test cheap with slabs. In 1984, Cook, Porter and Carpenter introduced "distributed ray tracing": many random rays per pixel gave soft shadows, blur and anti-aliasing. In 1986 James Kajiya wrote the rendering equation and described path tracing, the Monte Carlo method that is the basis of modern film rendering. Goldsmith and Salmon (1987) and MacDonald and Booth (1990) developed the surface area heuristic.
For three decades, ray tracing was a luxury. Films used rasterization (Pixar's REYES) because it was faster. This changed around 2013 to 2016: render farms became big enough, Intel's Embree made fast CPU traversal available, and studios found that path tracing was simpler for artists, because lighting just worked. Disney's Hyperion was used for Big Hero 6 (2014), and Pixar moved RenderMan to path tracing. In 2018 NVIDIA released the Turing GPU with RT cores, which test rays against boxes and triangles in hardware, and Microsoft released DirectX Raytracing (DXR). Vulkan ray tracing extensions followed in 2020. Since then, the research focus has been on making a few samples per pixel look good: denoising (SVGF, then neural denoisers) and reusing samples between pixels and frames (ReSTIR, 2020).
how: Step 1 is to generate a primary ray. The camera is a point, and each pixel has a position on an imaginary image plane. The ray origin is the camera position and the direction points through the pixel. Objects are usually stored in their own local coordinate frame and placed in the world by a 4 × 4 matrix. Homogeneous coordinates (the transforms3d concept) make this simple: rather than moving every vertex of an instanced object, the renderer multiplies the ray by the inverse matrix, traces it in the object's frame, and moves the hit result back. A forest of one million trees can then share a few tree models and one BVH per model, with one small instance record per tree.
Step 2 is to find the nearest hit, and this is where the BVH works. Traversal starts at the root box. The renderer tests the ray against the box with the slab method: on each axis, the ray enters the pair of planes at \( t_1 = (b_{min} - o)/d \) and leaves at \( t_2 = (b_{max} - o)/d \) (swap them if the direction is negative). The ray hits the box if the largest entry time is smaller than the smallest exit time and the exit time is positive. That is six subtractions, six multiplications (using a precomputed 1/d), and a few min and max operations, with no branches that depend on the data. If the ray misses the box, the whole subtree is skipped. If it hits, the traversal visits the two children, nearest first. When it reaches a leaf, it tests the few triangles inside, using the Möller–Trumbore algorithm, which solves for the hit point in barycentric coordinates (u, v) with one cross-product-based computation. Whenever a triangle is hit at distance t, the ray's maximum distance is shortened to t, so any box farther than t is rejected at once. This "closest hit so far" bound is why visiting the near child first matters: it prunes the far child more often. The traversal keeps a small stack of nodes still to visit, in registers or on-chip memory on a GPU.
Step 3 is to build the tree well. A BVH is a binary tree (the trees concept), but unlike a search tree its shape depends on geometry, and a bad shape can be 10 times slower. The best-known method is top-down with the surface area heuristic. For a node with N triangles, try many candidate splits along an axis. For each candidate, estimate the cost as \( C = C_{trav} + \frac{A_L}{A}N_L C_{isect} + \frac{A_R}{A}N_R C_{isect} \), where A is the surface area of the box and the fractions are the probability that a random ray hitting the parent also hits the child. The idea comes from geometry: for random rays, the chance of hitting a convex box is proportional to its surface area. The split with the lowest cost wins, and splitting stops when keeping a leaf is cheaper. Real builders bin the triangles into, say, 16 to 32 buckets per axis to make this fast. Dynamic scenes cannot afford a full rebuild every frame, so they use a cheaper builder (LBVH, which sorts triangles along a Morton space-filling curve) or keep the tree shape and only refit the boxes. As in any spatial index (the spatialidx concept), there is a choice: a quadtree or k-d tree cuts space, while a BVH groups objects, so no object is split or duplicated, but sibling boxes may overlap.
Step 4 is shading and more rays. At the hit point, the renderer needs the incoming light. A Whitted-style tracer sends one shadow ray to each light and one ray along the mirror direction. A path tracer picks one random direction according to the material, follows it to the next hit, and continues for several bounces. Each complete path gives one noisy sample of the pixel's color. The Monte Carlo concept supplies the mathematics: the average of N independent random estimates converges to the true integral, with error that falls as \( 1/\sqrt{N} \). To halve the noise you need four times as many samples. Better sampling (sending shadow rays toward lights directly, importance sampling the material, and Russian roulette to end long paths without bias) lowers the constant, but the \( 1/\sqrt{N} \) law remains.
Step 5 is to run it at scale. Wide BVHs with 4 or 8 children per node let one SIMD instruction test all child boxes at once. On GPUs, the RT core performs the box and triangle tests and the traversal loop in hardware, while shader cores run the material code. The most expensive part is memory: a ray visits scattered nodes, so each step may wait on a cache miss. Compact nodes matter as much as fewer tests.

example: Take a scene of 1,000,000 triangles and a BVH with 2 triangles per leaf. That gives about 500,000 leaves and a tree depth near 19. A typical ray follows one or two root-to-leaf paths and tests perhaps 30 to 60 boxes and 4 to 10 triangles, so about 50 to 70 tests, compared with 1,000,000 for brute force. That is a speed-up of around 15,000 times for one ray.
Now a slab test with numbers, in 2D to keep it short. The box covers x from 2 to 4 and y from 1 to 3. The ray starts at the origin and goes in direction (2, 1). On the x axis the ray enters at \( t = 2/2 = 1 \) and leaves at \( t = 4/2 = 2 \). On the y axis it enters at \( t = 1/1 = 1 \) and leaves at \( t = 3/1 = 3 \). The largest entry is 1 and the smallest exit is 2. Since 1 ≤ 2 and the exit is positive, the ray hits the box, entering at t = 1, at the point (2, 1), a corner. Now change the direction to (1, 2): the x interval is 2 to 4 and the y interval is 0.5 to 1.5. The largest entry is 2 and the smallest exit is 1.5, so entry is after exit and the ray misses. Both answers needed only a few arithmetic steps.
Now the cost of an image. A 1080p frame has about 2.07 million pixels. With 1 sample per pixel and one primary ray, a shadow ray and one bounce with its own shadow ray, that is about 4 rays per pixel, roughly 8 million rays per frame. At 60 frames per second that is 500 million rays per second, which a modern GPU with ray cores can trace; NVIDIA quoted about 10 billion rays per second for its 2018 flagship. But one sample per pixel is extremely noisy. A film frame may use 500 to 4,000 samples per pixel with 5 to 10 bounces each. At 1,000 samples and 6 bounces, one frame needs about 2 million × 1,000 × 6 × 2 ≈ 25 billion rays, which is minutes of GPU time or hours of CPU time per frame. A 2-hour film at 24 frames per second has 172,800 frames, so the render farm budget is large, and this is why denoising (cutting samples by 10 times or more) is so valuable.
intuition: Ray tracing is the camera run backwards: instead of asking every light ray where it goes, you ask each pixel where its light came from.
Only a tiny part of the light in a room ever reaches the camera, so following rays forward from lights would waste almost everything. Tracing backward from the camera follows only rays that matter. The BVH makes the same idea work for search: a ray is a question about a huge pile of triangles, and wrapping the pile in nested boxes lets you throw away most of it with one cheap test. The two ideas fit together because both turn a linear scan into a logarithmic descent. The random sampling then handles the part that has no closed form: the light arriving from all directions is an integral, and averaging random samples is the simplest tool that always works.
mistakes: People often think ray tracing means shooting rays from the lights. In fact almost all renderers trace from the camera, because most light never reaches the eye. Bidirectional methods trace from both ends for hard cases such as light seen through glass, but the default is camera-first.
People often think a BVH is a spatial partition like a grid. In fact a BVH partitions the objects, not the space. Each triangle appears in exactly one leaf, and sibling boxes may overlap. A k-d tree or grid partitions space and can store one triangle in several cells. This makes BVH memory predictable and its update (refit) easy, but overlapping boxes can make a ray visit both children.
People often think ray tracing is slow because of the ray count alone. In fact the cost is dominated by memory access and divergence. Neighbouring rays diverge after one bounce, so they touch different memory and different shader code, and GPUs lose efficiency. This is why sorting rays and special hardware matter more than raw arithmetic speed.
People often think real-time "ray tracing" in games is full path tracing. In fact most games use rasterization for primary visibility and trace only a few rays per pixel for selected effects (shadows, reflections, ambient light), then denoise. Full path tracing in real time is possible for some scenes only with heavy denoising and upscaling.
friction: Noise is the central problem of path tracing. The error falls only as \( 1/\sqrt{N} \), and difficult lighting (a small bright lamp seen through glass, light entering a room through a narrow slit) produces rare, very bright samples called fireflies. Sampling strategies such as multiple importance sampling and bidirectional paths help but never remove the problem; denoisers hide it at the cost of blur and artefacts, such as flicker over time or lost fine detail.
Building and storing the acceleration structure has a cost. A BVH needs roughly 30 to 60 bytes per triangle, so a 100-million-triangle scene needs several GB just for the tree. Dynamic scenes need rebuilds or refits every frame, and refitting degrades quality when objects move a lot, so engines rebuild when the tree becomes poor. GPUs cannot easily share one huge scene beyond VRAM, and out-of-core rendering is slow.
The hardware is still a black box in places. RT cores accelerate fixed box and triangle tests; custom primitives, curves for hair and deforming surfaces need extra work in shaders or in additional hardware features. Rays are also hard to schedule: coherent primary rays are cheap, incoherent bounce rays are not. There is also a precision problem: a hit point computed in floating point may lie slightly inside the surface, so a new ray started there can hit the same triangle again (self-intersection, "shadow acne"). Renderers offset the ray origin carefully, and this still causes bugs.
tradeoffs: Choose rasterization with ray-traced effects when you must hold a frame time of about 8 to 16 ms on consumer hardware: rasterization gives primary visibility for almost free, and you spend only 1 to 2 rays per pixel on shadows or reflections. Choose full path tracing when visual fidelity and artist simplicity matter more than speed, as in film, product visualization and architecture, where minutes per frame are fine. You give up frame rate with the second and physical correctness with the first.
Choose a high-quality BVH build (SAH, full rebuild) for static geometry that is traced millions of times, because a 20% to 50% faster traversal repays a build that takes seconds or minutes. Choose a fast build (LBVH) or refit for animated or streamed geometry that changes every frame, because build time is then part of the frame budget. The price of the fast build is a tree that is 10% to 30% slower to traverse.
Choose more samples per pixel when noise is the main artefact and time is available, since the quality gain is predictable (noise ∝ \( 1/\sqrt{N} \)). Choose a denoiser when time is short, accepting that it can blur detail or flicker, and keep the sample count above the level where the denoiser starts to invent structure (often 1 to 4 samples per pixel for real-time, 64 or more for film).
interview: Why does a BVH make ray queries faster, and what is the cost per ray? || A ray that misses a box skips everything inside it, so the search descends a tree instead of scanning all primitives. The expected cost is about O(log N) box tests plus a few triangle tests, if the boxes overlap little. Cost grows when sibling boxes overlap a lot, because the ray must visit both.
How does the surface area heuristic choose a split? || For random rays, the probability that a ray hitting a parent box also hits a child is proportional to the child's surface area divided by the parent's. SAH estimates the cost as traversal cost plus the sum over children of this probability times the number of triangles times the intersection cost, and picks the split with the smallest estimate. It is a model of the traversal, not just a balance of triangle counts.
Your path tracer is correct but images are too noisy. What are your options? || Noise falls as 1/√N, so four times the samples halves it, which is expensive. Reduce variance instead: importance sampling of materials and lights, next-event estimation (shadow rays to lights), multiple importance sampling, low-discrepancy sequences, and clamping fireflies at the cost of some bias. Then apply a denoiser using albedo and normal buffers.
How would you handle a scene where objects move every frame? || Do not rebuild the whole BVH. Keep a bottom-level structure per object (rebuild or refit only the objects that deform), and a top-level structure over the instances with their transforms, rebuilt each frame because it is small. Use refit for small motion and rebuild when quality drops. This two-level design is what DXR and Vulkan expose.
Why can a GPU trace many rays and still run slowly? || Rays diverge: neighbouring rays take different paths in the tree and then execute different shader code, so threads in a warp wait for each other, and memory access is random. Mitigations are ray sorting, shader execution reordering, compact wide nodes, and keeping the working set in cache.
practice: If you work with graphics, start from an existing library rather than writing traversal: Embree (CPU), OptiX or the hardware ray tracing in DXR and Vulkan (GPU), and Blender Cycles as a readable production example. Use the two-level model: one bottom-level acceleration structure per mesh and one top-level over instances, so a mesh used 10,000 times is stored once. Set the build flags by use: "prefer fast trace" for static meshes, "prefer fast build" or "allow update" for animated ones.
When learning, write a small CPU ray tracer first, following "Ray Tracing in One Weekend" (Peter Shirley) and then the book "Physically Based Rendering" (Pharr, Jakob, Humphreys), which is free online and contains a complete BVH and path tracer. Count intersection tests per ray with and without a BVH; seeing a drop from millions to dozens makes the idea concrete. Render the same scene at 4, 16, 64 and 256 samples per pixel and verify the \( 1/\sqrt{N} \) behaviour by measuring the standard deviation of a flat region.
In production, profile tree build time, nodes visited per ray, and the share of time in shading versus traversal. Leaves of 2 to 8 triangles are typical. Avoid self-intersection by offsetting ray origins along the surface normal, and keep scene scale sensible, because floating-point precision gets worse far from the origin.
quiz: Why does a ray that misses a bounding box skip all the geometry inside it? || Every triangle in that subtree lies inside the box, so a ray that does not intersect the box cannot intersect any triangle in it. One cheap test removes the whole subtree.
Why does the surface area of a box appear in the surface area heuristic? || For randomly directed rays, the probability that a ray which hits a convex box also hits a convex box inside it is the ratio of their surface areas. So surface area estimates how often traversal will enter each child.
A path tracer takes 100 samples per pixel and the noise is too high. How many samples reduce the noise by 3 times, and what happens to render time? || Noise falls as 1/√N, so reducing it 3 times needs 9 times the samples, about 900 per pixel, and roughly 9 times the render time.
A ray enters a box at t = 2 on the x slab, at t = 0.5 on y, and leaves at t = 1.5 on x, t = 3 on y. Does it hit? || The largest entry is 2 and the smallest exit is 1.5. Entry is after exit, so the intervals do not overlap and the ray misses the box.
Your game has an animated character and static buildings. How should you structure the acceleration structures? || Build the buildings' structure once with the best quality, and give the character its own bottom-level structure that is refit or rebuilt each frame. A small top-level structure over instances is rebuilt each frame. This balances build cost and trace speed.

@@ shading
value: Shading answers one question for every visible point: what color of light leaves this point toward the eye? The physical answer is the rendering equation: the light leaving a point is the light it emits plus the light arriving from every direction, each part multiplied by how much the material sends that incoming direction toward the viewer. The function that describes the material is the BRDF (bidirectional reflectance distribution function). Physically based rendering (PBR) means using BRDFs that obey physics, such as conserving energy, together with measured or physically meaningful material parameters, so that a material looks right under any light.
Before PBR, artists tuned ad hoc numbers (ambient, diffuse, specular, shininess) for each scene, and a material that looked good under one light looked wrong under another. PBR separates the material (base color, how metallic it is, how rough it is) from the lighting. The same material works in a game level, a film and a product preview. The industry settled on a small shared model: Disney's principled BRDF (2012), Unreal Engine 4's version (2013), and the glTF 2.0 metallic-roughness material, which is used by the web, Blender, and most real-time engines.
For the layer above, shading is the code that runs on the GPU for every pixel: a shader program written in HLSL, GLSL, WGSL or Metal Shading Language and compiled by a graphics API. The layers below supply the physics and the maths. Optics (L5) gives reflection, refraction and Fresnel effects. Calculus (L4) gives the integral over directions. Monte Carlo (L4) gives the way to estimate it. Color science (L3) says in which space to add light. Human vision (L5) says how to display the result so it looks natural.
terms: Radiance — the amount of light travelling along a ray, per unit area and per unit solid angle; it is what a camera pixel measures.
Irradiance — the light power arriving at a surface per unit area, from all directions.
BRDF — a function \( f_r(\omega_i, \omega_o) \) giving how much light arriving from direction \( \omega_i \) leaves toward direction \( \omega_o \), per unit solid angle.
Albedo — the fraction of incoming light a surface reflects diffusely, between 0 and 1; "base color" in PBR.
Microfacet — a tiny mirror-like facet of a rough surface; the surface is modelled as a statistical mix of facets with different normals.
Fresnel effect — the reflectance of any surface rises toward 100% as the viewing angle approaches grazing.
Roughness — a 0-to-1 parameter for how spread out the microfacet normals are; low roughness gives a sharp highlight.
Energy conservation — a surface never reflects more light than it receives.
Tone mapping — the step that compresses high-range light values into the range a display can show.
history: Early shading was empirical. Henri Gouraud (1971) interpolated colors across triangles, and Bui Tuong Phong (1975) introduced the specular highlight model that bears his name and interpolated normals. Jim Blinn (1977) made the highlight cheaper with the half vector. These models were fast and looked acceptable, but their constants had no physical meaning.
Physics entered with Robert Cook and Kenneth Torrance (1982), who used a microfacet model with a Fresnel term, from earlier work by Torrance and Sparrow in physics. In 1986, James Kajiya wrote down the rendering equation and gave a Monte Carlo way to solve it, which defined what "correct" meant. For about 25 years, film and games went different ways: film studios used raytracers with simple but tweaked materials, games used cheap hacks.
The shift to PBR in practice came around 2010 to 2014. Walter, Marschner, Li and Torrance (2007) introduced the GGX distribution, which has the long, soft highlight tails seen in real materials. Brent Burley at Walt Disney Animation published "Physically Based Shading at Disney" (2012), a single BRDF with a few artist-friendly sliders, derived from measured materials. Brian Karis at Epic Games adapted it for real time in "Real Shading in Unreal Engine 4" (2013), choosing which terms were cheap enough. Frostbite, Unity, and later Khronos's glTF 2.0 (2017) followed. Since then, work has gone into more layers (clear coat, sheen, subsurface scattering), measured materials, and, at the high end, evaluating shading with ray tracing. In 2024 Khronos added the PBR Neutral tone mapper so that product colors look the same across viewers.
how: Start with the rendering equation for the light leaving point p toward the viewer direction \( \omega_o \):
\[ L_o(p,\omega_o) = L_e(p,\omega_o) + \int_{\Omega} f_r(p,\omega_i,\omega_o)\, L_i(p,\omega_i)\, (n\cdot\omega_i)\, d\omega_i \]
Read it piece by piece. \( L_e \) is light the surface emits itself (zero for most surfaces). The integral runs over the hemisphere \( \Omega \) above the surface (the calculus concept: a sum over a continuous set of directions). For each incoming direction \( \omega_i \), \( L_i \) is the radiance arriving, \( f_r \) is the BRDF, and \( n\cdot\omega_i \) is the cosine of the angle to the surface normal. The cosine appears because light that arrives at a shallow angle spreads over a larger area. Light is physical, so everything must be added in linear units: twice the photons means twice the number. This is why shaders work in linear color and convert to the display's gamma curve only at the end (the colorsci concept).
The BRDF is the heart of the material. PBR splits it into a diffuse part and a specular part. The diffuse part models light that enters the surface, scatters, and leaves in all directions equally. The Lambertian model is \( f_d = \text{albedo}/\pi \). The division by \( \pi \) makes the surface conserve energy: integrating the cosine over the hemisphere gives \( \pi \). The specular part models mirror-like reflection from the surface itself. Its standard form, the Cook–Torrance microfacet BRDF, is
\[ f_s = \frac{D(h)\,F(v,h)\,G(l,v)}{4\,(n\cdot l)(n\cdot v)} \]
Here \( l \) is the direction to the light, \( v \) is the direction to the viewer, and \( h \) is the half vector between them. Only facets whose normal equals \( h \) can reflect light from \( l \) to \( v \), so three questions matter. D is the normal distribution: what fraction of facets point along \( h \)? With GGX and roughness \( r \), set \( \alpha = r^2 \) and \( D(h) = \alpha^2 / (\pi((n\cdot h)^2(\alpha^2-1)+1)^2) \). A small \( \alpha \) gives a tall narrow spike (sharp highlight) and a large \( \alpha \) gives a low wide hill. F is the Fresnel term: how much of the light is reflected at this angle, commonly the Schlick approximation \( F = F_0 + (1-F_0)(1-\cos\theta)^5 \). G is the geometry term: facets shadow and mask each other, so some light is lost at grazing angles (the Smith model). The factor 4 \( (n\cdot l)(n\cdot v) \) converts from the facet's frame to the surface's frame.
Metals and non-metals differ mainly through \( F_0 \), the reflectance at normal incidence. Non-metals (plastic, wood, skin) have a low, gray \( F_0 \) of about 0.04 (4%) and a coloured diffuse part. Metals have a high, coloured \( F_0 \) (gold is about (1.0, 0.77, 0.34)) and no diffuse part, since free electrons absorb the light that enters. The "metallic" slider blends between these two cases. This is why one pair of parameters, metallic and roughness, plus a base color describes most real materials.
Lights are treated by the same equation. For a point light with intensity \( I \) at distance \( d \), the integral collapses to one term, because light arrives from a single direction: irradiance is \( I\cos\theta/d^2 \) and the output is \( f_r \times \) irradiance. For environment lighting (the sky), the integral is real, and real-time engines pre-compute it: a blurred "irradiance map" for the diffuse part, and a set of prefiltered environment maps plus a small lookup table for the specular part (the split-sum approximation in UE4). Offline renderers estimate the integral with Monte Carlo: choose random directions, with more probability where \( f_r \times L_i \) is large (importance sampling), and average \( f_r L_i \cos\theta / \text{pdf} \) (the montecarlo concept). Film renderers do this with ray tracing; games approximate it with the precomputed maps and screen-space tricks.
Finally the shader outputs radiance values that may exceed 1.0 (a sun-lit wall may be 100 times brighter than a shadow). A display shows only 0 to 1. Tone mapping compresses the range with a smooth curve (Reinhard, ACES, or the Khronos PBR Neutral operator) that is modelled on how film and the eye respond, and then the sRGB curve encodes the result for the screen. Human vision responds roughly to ratios, not differences, so this encoding spends bits where eyes can distinguish steps (the perception concept).

example: Take a white-ish plastic surface with albedo 0.8, F0 = 0.04, lit by one point light of intensity 100 (arbitrary units per steradian) at distance 2 m. The light direction makes an angle of 60° with the normal, so cos θ = 0.5. The irradiance is 100 × 0.5 / 2² = 12.5. The diffuse output is albedo/π × irradiance = 0.8 / 3.1416 × 12.5 ≈ 3.18. Now move the light directly overhead (cos θ = 1): irradiance is 100 / 4 = 25 and the diffuse output doubles to 6.37. Double the distance to 4 m and it drops by a factor of 4, as the inverse-square law says.
Next the specular highlight, in the simplest geometry: light and viewer are both along the normal, so \( h = n \), \( n\cdot h = 1 \), and \( D = 1/(\pi\alpha^2) \). With roughness 0.5, \( \alpha = 0.25 \) and D = 1 / (3.1416 × 0.0625) ≈ 5.09. With roughness 0.2, \( \alpha = 0.04 \) and D = 1 / (3.1416 × 0.0016) ≈ 199. Take F = F0 = 0.04 and G ≈ 1. The specular BRDF value is D × F × G / 4: about 0.051 for roughness 0.5, and about 1.99 for roughness 0.2. The diffuse BRDF value is 0.8/π ≈ 0.255. So at the highlight peak a smooth surface (roughness 0.2) reflects about 8 times more than the diffuse part, and a rough one only 0.2 times as much. This is the numerical reason a smooth plastic ball has a bright, small dot, and a rough one has a faint, wide glow. The peak is about 39 times higher for the smooth surface, since the ratio is \( (0.25/0.04)^2 \), but the highlight is correspondingly smaller in area, so the total reflected energy is similar.
Fresnel at a grazing angle: for the same plastic seen at 60° from the normal (cos θ = 0.5), Schlick gives \( F = 0.04 + 0.96 \times 0.5^5 = 0.04 + 0.03 = 0.07 \). At 85° (cos θ ≈ 0.087), \( F = 0.04 + 0.96 \times 0.913^5 \approx 0.04 + 0.96 \times 0.634 \approx 0.65 \). A lake or a table top turns into a mirror at a low angle, and the model captures it.
Last, display. A linear value of 0.18 (middle gray) is encoded by the sRGB curve as about 0.46, so it is stored as roughly 118 out of 255. If a shader skipped the conversion, the result would look much too dark, and if it did blending in gamma space, a 50% mix of black and white would come out as 0.5 encoded (linear 0.21) instead of the linear 0.5 which is encoded as 0.735.
intuition: A material is a filter on light: it decides, for each pair of incoming and outgoing directions, what fraction passes.
The rendering equation is a bookkeeping statement: light out equals light in times the fraction the surface passes along. The BRDF is that fraction, which depends on only two directions and the material. Everything else in the model is bookkeeping about angles: cosines because of how light spreads, a Fresnel curve because of how waves meet a boundary, and statistics of facets because real surfaces are rough at scales smaller than a pixel. This is why PBR is stable: each term has a physical reason, so the parameters mean something and combine without surprises. An artist changes roughness, and the highlight changes in the way physical surfaces change.
mistakes: People often think PBR means "realistic" in an absolute sense. In fact it only means the shading model respects physical constraints (energy conservation, Fresnel, reciprocity) and uses meaningful parameters. Stylized art can be physically based, and a physically based pipeline with bad textures looks wrong.
People often think shading and lighting are the same thing. In fact lighting is the light that arrives (\( L_i \)), shading is how the surface responds (\( f_r \)) and the integration of the two. Global illumination is the part of \( L_i \) that comes from other surfaces, and many real-time engines still approximate it separately from the material model.
People often think specular is shiny and diffuse is dull, so a rough surface has no specular. In fact every non-metal has specular reflection at all roughness levels (at least about 4% at normal incidence, much more at grazing angles). Roughness spreads that energy over a wider cone and lowers its peak. Metals have no diffuse part at all.
People often think the colors in a shader can be mixed as they are in an image file. In fact image files store gamma-encoded values, and averaging or lighting these gives wrong results (too dark blends). Textures that hold colors must be decoded to linear before lighting, while data textures (normals, roughness) must not be decoded.
People often think a bigger shader with more terms is always more accurate. In fact a mismatched model, for example a rough-surface formula applied to a clear-coat car paint, can be wrong in a way no parameter can fix. This is why modern materials have layers (base plus clear coat, sheen, transmission).
friction: The rendering equation is recursive: \( L_i \) at one point is \( L_o \) at another. An exact solution needs ray tracing, many samples, and minutes per frame. Real-time shading therefore uses approximations, and each one has a failure mode: shadow maps have resolution artifacts, screen-space reflections cannot show what is off screen, and baked light probes do not react to moving objects. The gap between a real-time and an offline result is shrinking, but is still visible in reflections and indirect light.
Materials are hard to author and measure. Textures for base color, roughness and metallic maps are painted by hand or scanned, and a small mistake (a metal with 0.5 metallic, a roughness map in the wrong color space) gives results that look wrong yet are hard to diagnose. Real materials have effects the basic model leaves out: layered coatings, anisotropy (brushed metal), subsurface scattering (skin, marble, wax), iridescence, fabric sheen. Each needs extra terms and more texture channels.
There is also a cost issue. A pixel shader runs millions of times per frame, so every instruction counts. A full GGX evaluation with a Smith term costs on the order of 50 to 100 arithmetic operations per light, and with several lights, shadows and texture reads, a heavy material can make a game drop frames. Engines use deferred or clustered shading, level-of-detail materials, and simplified approximations (for instance, a cheap approximation of the Smith term) to stay inside a budget of a few milliseconds. Finally, there is no single standard: engine, renderer and glTF models differ in small ways, so the same asset looks slightly different in each tool.
tradeoffs: Choose the metallic-roughness workflow (glTF, UE, Unity) when you need a portable, simple authoring model with three or four parameters. Choose a richer model (Disney principled with clear coat, sheen, transmission, subsurface) when materials include car paint, cloth, skin or glass, and you can afford the extra textures and shader time. You give up simplicity with the second and some material types with the first.
Choose baked lighting (lightmaps, probes) when the scene is static and the target hardware is weak, such as mobile: it costs almost nothing per frame and gives soft indirect light. Choose dynamic global illumination (ray tracing, voxel or probe-based) when lights or objects move, or when authoring bake times of hours are a bottleneck. You pay with GPU time (1 to 5 ms or more per frame) in exchange for iteration speed and correct moving shadows.
Choose a forward renderer when you have few lights, need MSAA and transparent objects, or target mobile. Choose deferred shading when you have hundreds of lights: it shades each pixel once, but needs a wide G-buffer (memory bandwidth) and handles transparency badly.
Choose a cheap analytic model (Lambert plus Blinn–Phong) for distant, small or low-importance objects, or for tiny devices, since the difference is invisible at a few pixels. Choose full GGX with Smith and Fresnel for hero objects close to the camera, where highlights and edges reveal the difference. The deciding factor is how many pixels the object covers, and what each pixel's shader costs.
interview: Walk me through the terms of the Cook–Torrance BRDF and what each one models. || D is the distribution of microfacet normals (how many point along the half vector), F is Fresnel reflectance at the angle between view and half vector, G is the geometric masking and shadowing between facets. The denominator \( 4(n\cdot l)(n\cdot v) \) converts from facet space to surface space. Diffuse is added as albedo/π, reduced by the energy that went to specular.
Why does a diffuse BRDF divide by π? || The cosine-weighted integral of a constant BRDF over the hemisphere is π times that constant. Dividing albedo by π makes the total reflected light equal albedo times the incoming irradiance, so the surface reflects at most the fraction albedo and conserves energy.
Why do shaders compute in linear space, and where do mistakes happen? || Light adds linearly, and lighting equations are linear in radiance. Display values are gamma-encoded, so averaging or multiplying them gives wrong brightness. Typical mistakes are sampling color textures as raw values, blending in gamma space, or applying the sRGB curve twice. Use sRGB texture formats for color data and linear for normal, roughness and metallic maps.
How does a real-time engine light a scene with an HDR sky without integrating over the hemisphere per pixel? || It prefilters. The sky is converted into an irradiance map (a low-order spherical harmonics or small cubemap) for diffuse light, and a mip chain of cubemaps blurred by roughness for specular, plus a 2D lookup table for the BRDF's scale and bias (the split-sum approximation). A pixel then does two or three texture lookups.
Why does a path tracer give noise, and how does importance sampling help? || It estimates the integral from random directions, and the error shrinks as 1/√N. Importance sampling draws directions in proportion to the BRDF or the light, so samples land where the integrand is large, which lowers variance without bias if each sample is divided by its probability.
practice: When you author or debug materials, keep three rules. Use linear workflow end to end: mark color textures as sRGB and everything else as linear. Keep base color values physically plausible: nonmetal albedo between about 0.04 (charcoal) and 0.9 (fresh snow), never pure 0 or 1. And set metallic to 0 or 1 for most pixels, with intermediate values only at edges of dirt or rust, since real materials are almost never half metal.
For real-time work, learn the shader entry points: the vertex stage that transforms geometry, the fragment (pixel) stage that evaluates the BRDF, and the data it needs (normals, tangents, texture coordinates, light lists). Read the UE4 and Filament shading models; Google's Filament documentation is an open, detailed explanation that includes the formulas and code. For physical correctness, check energy: render a white furnace test, a sphere of albedo 1 inside a uniform white environment, and verify that it disappears. Check that doubling light intensity doubles pixel values, and compare against a reference path tracer (Blender Cycles or Mitsuba) for the same asset. For learning, the online book "Physically Based Rendering" explains the equation and sampling in depth, and Naty Hoffman's SIGGRAPH course notes on physically based shading explain the theory behind real-time practice.

value: A Kalman filter estimates something you cannot observe directly from measurements that are noisy. A phone knows where it is only through GPS fixes that jump by several metres, accelerometer readings that drift, and a map. A car tracker in video sees a box that flickers from frame to frame. The true position and velocity are hidden; the data are partial and wrong in small random ways. The filter keeps a belief about the hidden state: a best guess and a number for how uncertain that guess is. Each step has two moves. Predict: use a model of how the state moves to push the belief forward, which makes it more uncertain. Update: when a measurement arrives, move the guess toward it by an amount that depends on how much you trust the measurement compared with the prediction.
That weighting is the whole idea. If the sensor is precise and the prediction is vague, the estimate follows the sensor. If the sensor is noisy and the model is good, the estimate follows the model. The weight, the Kalman gain, is recomputed at every step from the two uncertainties. For linear systems with Gaussian noise, the result is optimal: no other method, however complex, produces a lower expected squared error. It needs only the previous estimate and its uncertainty, not the whole history, so the cost per step is small and fixed.
The layers below give it its form. The Markov property (L4) lets the state summarize the past. Bayes' rule (L4) says how to combine a prior belief with new evidence; for Gaussians this has a closed form. Linear algebra (L4) packs positions, velocities and uncertainties into vectors and matrices. Least squares (L4) shows that the update is the same as a weighted fit that is updated one measurement at a time. Above it, robots, drones, aircraft, phones and video trackers use state estimation to know where they are and what is moving. Where the model is not linear or the belief is not Gaussian, the particle filter replaces the formulas with many random samples.
terms: State — the vector of quantities you want to know, for example position and velocity.
Process model — the equation that predicts the next state from the current state; it includes a small random disturbance.
Measurement model — the equation that links the state to what sensors report, \( z = Hx + v \).
Covariance — a matrix P that holds the variance of each state variable and the correlations between them; it is the filter's measure of uncertainty.
Process noise Q — the uncertainty added at each prediction because the real world does not follow the model exactly.
Measurement noise R — the covariance of the sensor errors.
Innovation — the difference between the measurement and what the prediction expected; the "surprise".
Kalman gain K — the matrix that decides how far the estimate moves toward the innovation.
Particle filter — a method that represents the belief by many weighted random samples instead of a Gaussian.
history: Before the Kalman filter, Norbert Wiener (1940s) and Andrey Kolmogorov solved filtering for signals that never change their statistics, using frequency methods. These solutions were hard to use for moving objects or for computers working one sample at a time. Rudolf Kálmán, a mathematician and engineer, published "A New Approach to Linear Filtering and Prediction Problems" in 1960. He used a state-space description and a recursive formula, which fit a digital computer: store the estimate and covariance, update them when a measurement arrives. Peter Swerling had published a similar recursion in 1958, and Ruslan Stratonovich in the Soviet Union worked on related ideas; the method is often named for Kalman because his paper was general and widely read.
The first big use came quickly. In 1960 and 1961, Kalman met Stanley Schmidt at NASA Ames, and Schmidt's team applied the filter to the trajectory of a spacecraft to the Moon. Because spacecraft dynamics are not linear, Schmidt developed the extended Kalman filter, which linearizes the model around the current estimate at each step. This idea flew in the Apollo navigation computer. Later came the GPS and inertial navigation systems in aircraft and missiles, with the Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoother (1965) to improve past estimates using later data, and the continuous-time Kalman–Bucy filter (1961).
The extended filter can fail badly for strongly non-linear systems. Julier and Uhlmann proposed the unscented Kalman filter in 1997, which passes a few carefully chosen sample points through the non-linear function instead of using derivatives. Gordon, Salmond and Smith (1993) introduced the bootstrap particle filter, which drops the Gaussian assumption altogether and became popular in robotics through Monte Carlo localization. Evensen's ensemble Kalman filter (1994) made the idea work in weather models with millions of variables. Today the Kalman family is inside every smartphone's sensor fusion, in visual-inertial SLAM for AR headsets and drones, and in multi-object trackers for video and radar.
how: The model has two equations. The state evolves as \( x_k = F x_{k-1} + B u_k + w_k \), where F is the transition matrix, u is a known control input (optional) and w is process noise with covariance Q. A measurement is \( z_k = H x_k + v_k \), where H picks out the measured quantities from the state and v is measurement noise with covariance R. Both noises are assumed to be zero-mean Gaussian, independent over time. The state is a Markov process: given \( x_{k-1} \), the past carries no extra information. That is what lets the filter keep only the latest estimate (the markov concept).
The belief is a Gaussian, which is fully described by two things: the mean \( \hat{x} \) and the covariance P. Step 1 is prediction. Push the mean through the model and let the uncertainty grow:
\[ \hat{x}^-_k = F\hat{x}_{k-1} + Bu_k, \qquad P^-_k = F P_{k-1} F^\top + Q \]
The term \( F P F^\top \) says how the uncertainty is carried by the dynamics (for a car, an unknown velocity turns into growing uncertainty about position). Q adds more, because the model is never perfect. Without measurements, P only grows.
Step 2 is the update. Compute what the model expected to see, \( H\hat{x}^-_k \), and the innovation \( y_k = z_k - H\hat{x}^-_k \). Then compute the gain:
\[ K_k = P^-_k H^\top \left(H P^-_k H^\top + R\right)^{-1} \]
\[ \hat{x}_k = \hat{x}^-_k + K_k y_k, \qquad P_k = (I - K_k H) P^-_k \]
Look at the gain in one dimension with H = 1: \( K = P^-/(P^- + R) \). If R is huge (a bad sensor) K is near 0 and the measurement is nearly ignored. If R is near 0, K is near 1 and the estimate jumps to the measurement. In between it is the share of the total uncertainty that belongs to the prediction. After the update, P is smaller than both P⁻ and R: combining two independent pieces of information always reduces uncertainty. In matrix form, the same weighting happens along every direction of the state, including directions that are never measured. If you measure only position, the correlation in P between position and velocity, built by the prediction step, lets a position error also correct the velocity estimate. This is how a tracker infers speed from positions alone (the linalg concept).
Why is this the right formula? There are two equivalent views. The Bayesian view (the bayes concept): the prediction is a Gaussian prior over the state, the measurement defines a Gaussian likelihood, and the product of two Gaussians is again Gaussian, with a mean between the two and a variance smaller than both. The Kalman update is exactly that product, written for vectors. The least-squares view (the leastsquares concept): the estimate minimizes the sum of squared errors, each weighted by the inverse of its variance: the distance from the prediction weighted by \( P^{-1} \) plus the distance from the measurement weighted by \( R^{-1} \). The Kalman filter is recursive least squares with a state that moves: it updates the solution one measurement at a time without refitting from scratch. Both views give the same gain. Under the linear-Gaussian assumptions the filter is the exact Bayesian answer, and even when noise is not Gaussian, it remains the best linear estimator (lowest variance among linear methods).
Real systems are rarely linear. The extended Kalman filter replaces F and H with the Jacobians of the non-linear functions at the current estimate, which works if the model is nearly linear over the size of the uncertainty. The unscented filter picks about 2n + 1 sigma points around the mean, passes each through the true function, and recomputes the mean and covariance from the results. When the belief is clearly non-Gaussian (two possible positions after a robot passes two similar doors), use a particle filter: keep N samples, each with a weight. Predict by moving each particle with the model plus random noise. Update by multiplying each weight by the likelihood of the measurement given that particle. Then resample: draw N new particles in proportion to the weights, so that particles with tiny weights disappear. The number of particles needed grows quickly with the dimension of the state, which limits this method to small state spaces (below about 10 to 20 dimensions) unless the structure is exploited.

example: Track a car on a straight road, position only (one dimension). The estimate is 100 m with variance P = 4 (standard deviation 2 m). The car moves at a known 10 m/s, the step is 1 s, and the process noise is Q = 1. The sensor gives position with variance R = 5.
Prediction: the mean moves to 100 + 10 = 110 m. The variance grows to P⁻ = 4 + 1 = 5. A measurement arrives: z = 113 m. The innovation is 113 − 110 = 3 m. The gain is K = 5 / (5 + 5) = 0.5, since the prediction and the sensor are equally uncertain. The new estimate is 110 + 0.5 × 3 = 111.5 m, exactly halfway. The new variance is (1 − 0.5) × 5 = 2.5, which is smaller than both 5 (prediction) and 5 (sensor). Two equally bad sources made one better source.
Next step. The prediction is 111.5 + 10 = 121.5 m, and the variance is 2.5 + 1 = 3.5. The sensor reads z = 120 m. The innovation is −1.5 m. The gain is K = 3.5 / (3.5 + 5) ≈ 0.412: the filter is now more certain than the sensor, so it moves less than halfway. The estimate is 121.5 + 0.412 × (−1.5) ≈ 120.88 m. The variance is (1 − 0.412) × 3.5 ≈ 2.06. Over time P settles at a steady value where the growth from Q balances the shrinking from each measurement, and the gain becomes a constant (here around 0.4). In this case a fixed-gain filter would work just as well; this is the form of the "alpha filter" used in cheap controllers.
Here is the same filter for two states, position and velocity, with only position measured. It is the standard constant-velocity tracker used on bounding boxes in video:
```python
import numpy as np

dt = 1.0
F = np.array([[1, dt], [0, 1]])      # position += velocity * dt
H = np.array([[1.0, 0.0]])           # we measure position only
Q = np.diag([0.01, 0.1])             # process noise
R = np.array([[5.0]])                # measurement noise

def step(x, P, z):
    x = F @ x                        # predict mean
    P = F @ P @ F.T + Q              # predict covariance
    y = z - H @ x                    # innovation
    S = H @ P @ H.T + R              # innovation covariance
    K = P @ H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)   # Kalman gain
    x = x + K @ y                    # update mean
    P = (np.eye(2) - K @ H) @ P      # update covariance
    return x, P
```
After a few steps with a car moving at 10 m/s, the velocity entry of x converges close to 10 although speed was never measured, because the filter links position errors to velocity through the off-diagonal terms of P.
intuition: The filter is a running weighted average whose weights are set by how sure each source is, and which also knows that the world moves between measurements.
If you average two thermometers, you should trust the more accurate one more, in proportion to the inverse of its variance. The Kalman filter does exactly this between "what I predicted" and "what I just measured", and it keeps track of its own confidence so it knows the weights next time. Prediction makes the belief wider, because time passes and things drift; measurement makes it narrower, because evidence arrives. The two forces balance at a level that depends on the ratio of Q to R. So the filter is not a trick: it is the only sensible way to combine an old belief with new evidence when both are uncertain, and Bayes' rule proves it for Gaussians.
mistakes: People often think the Kalman filter smooths data like a moving average. In fact it combines a physical model with data. A moving average has no model and always lags, while a Kalman filter predicts ahead using velocity, so it can follow a steady motion with no lag, and it widens its uncertainty when measurements stop arriving.
People often think it works only with Gaussian noise. In fact it is optimal among all linear estimators for any noise with the given mean and covariance, and exact for Gaussian noise. Heavy-tailed noise (outliers from GPS reflections) still hurts, so real systems add an outlier test on the innovation (a gate), or robust variants.
People often think tuning Q and R is a detail. In fact it is where the filter succeeds or fails. If Q is too small, the filter trusts its model, becomes overconfident and ignores real changes (it "diverges" while reporting small P). If Q is too large, the output is as noisy as the sensor. Check the innovations: they should be zero-mean, uncorrelated, and have a size matching S (the normalized innovation squared test).
People often think a particle filter is simply a better Kalman filter. In fact it handles non-linear and multimodal beliefs, but at a cost that grows quickly with the state dimension, and it has its own failure modes (particle deprivation, when no particle is near the truth). If the problem is nearly linear and Gaussian, the Kalman filter is far cheaper and just as good.
friction: The assumptions are strong. The model must be roughly right, the noise statistics known, and the noise independent over time. In practice Q and R are guessed and tuned, the sensor error is correlated over time (a GPS error that drifts slowly is not white noise), and the measurements may arrive late or out of order. Each problem has a fix, such as state augmentation to model a bias, smoothers for delayed data, or adaptive noise estimation, but each adds complexity.
Nonlinearity is the main source of failure. The extended filter linearizes around an estimate that may be wrong; if the uncertainty is large (at start up, or after a long gap in measurements), the linearization is poor and the filter can diverge or become inconsistent, meaning that P claims more accuracy than the filter has. Rotations in 3D, as in attitude estimation, need special care because angles wrap around and the Gaussian is defined on a curved space; modern systems use error-state formulations or work on manifolds. Numerical issues arise too: P must remain symmetric and positive definite, but rounding can break this. Use the Joseph form of the covariance update or a square-root filter to stay stable.
Data association is a separate hard problem. A tracker with many objects must decide which detection belongs to which track, using gating, the Hungarian algorithm, or probabilistic data association. A wrong assignment poisons the filter. And the Gaussian belief cannot represent several hypotheses; when the world is ambiguous you need a multiple-hypothesis tracker or a particle filter, with more computation.
tradeoffs: Choose a Kalman filter when the dynamics are close to linear, the noise is roughly Gaussian, and you need a result at every step with a few microseconds of computation and a guarantee of optimality. Choose an extended or unscented filter when the model is mildly non-linear: the extended filter is cheaper and needs Jacobians; the unscented one needs no derivatives and handles stronger curvature at about 2n + 1 function evaluations per step. Choose a particle filter when the belief is multimodal or the non-linearity is severe and the state has fewer than about 10 to 20 dimensions; you pay with thousands of particles per step.
Choose a filter (causal, uses past data only) for real-time control, where a decision is needed now. Choose a smoother (uses past and future data, for example the RTS smoother) for offline analysis, mapping or when a delay of a few steps is allowed: it gives lower error but needs the data to be buffered.
Choose a simple fixed-gain filter (complementary filter, exponential moving average) when the compute budget is a few instructions, the sensors are well understood, and the noise is stable, as on a small microcontroller. Choose the full covariance filter when noise levels change (GPS quality varies with the sky view), when several sensors with different rates must be fused, or when you need the uncertainty itself to make decisions, for instance to widen a safety margin.
Choose a learned estimator (a neural network that outputs state) when the dynamics are too complex to write down and you have a lot of data; keep a Kalman layer on top when you need calibrated uncertainty and a graceful response to missing data.

Equivalently it minimizes a weighted sum of squared errors, with weights from the inverse covariances, and updates the solution recursively without storing the data.

value: A PID controller keeps a measured quantity at a target by looking at the error, the gap between the target and the measurement, and computing a correction from three views of that error. The proportional part reacts to how big the error is now. The integral part reacts to how long and how large the error has been, which removes a steady offset. The derivative part reacts to how fast the error is changing, which damps overshoot. The output is the sum, weighted by three gains: Kp, Ki and Kd. The controller needs no model of the system; it needs only a measurement, an actuator, and good gains.
That is why PID is everywhere. Thermostats, cruise control, drone attitude control, the temperature of a chemical reactor, the speed of a hard-disk motor, and a large majority of industrial control loops use it. In software, the same loop appears in a Kubernetes autoscaler that adds replicas when CPU is above target, and in ad budget pacing, which raises or lowers the bid rate so that a campaign spends its daily budget evenly instead of in the first hour.
The layers below give PID its meaning. Control theory (L4) provides the feedback loop idea: measure, compare, act, and the conditions under which the loop is stable. Calculus (L4) provides the two operations in the name: the integral of the error over time and its derivative. Differential equations (L4) describe the controlled system, such as a tank that warms or a server pool that fills, and tell you what the loop does when closed: it settles smoothly, overshoots, or oscillates forever. Above it, robotics drives motors with it, autoscaling uses its proportional form, and pacing uses all three terms.
terms: Setpoint — the target value of the measured quantity.
Error — setpoint minus measurement, \( e(t) = r(t) - y(t) \); positive when the measurement is too low.
Plant — the thing being controlled (room, motor, server pool, ad campaign), which turns the control output into a measured value.
Gain — a multiplier; Kp, Ki and Kd set how strongly each of the three terms acts.
Steady-state error — the error that remains after the system settles; a proportional-only controller usually leaves some.
Overshoot — how far the measurement goes beyond the setpoint before returning.
Integral windup — the integral term growing very large while the actuator is saturated, which causes a large overshoot later.
Dead time — the delay between a change of the output and the first effect on the measurement.
Saturation — the actuator reaching its limit (valve fully open, bid multiplier at its cap).
history: Feedback control is older than the name. James Watt's centrifugal governor (1780s) regulated steam engines, and James Clerk Maxwell's 1868 paper "On Governors" gave the first mathematical analysis of why some governors hunted (oscillated). The PID form came from ships. Nicolas Minorsky, a Russian engineer working in the US, studied how a helmsman steers and in 1922 published an analysis of automatic ship steering with the three terms, and tested it on the US Navy battleship New Mexico. The helmsman reacts to the heading error (proportional), to the drift that persists (integral) and to how fast the ship is turning (derivative).
In the 1930s, industry needed to control temperature and pressure in factories and pneumatic controllers with proportional and integral action appeared, followed by derivative action. In 1942, John Ziegler and Nathaniel Nichols of Taylor Instrument published "Optimum Settings for Automatic Controllers", with two simple experimental recipes for choosing gains without a model. The Ziegler–Nichols rules are still taught, and still start many tuning sessions, even though their results are usually aggressive.
Digital control began in the 1950s and 1960s, and PID became code: a discrete loop run at a fixed period. Karl Åström and Tore Hägglund (the relay autotuner, 1984) made controllers tune themselves by making the loop oscillate under a relay and measuring the oscillation. Their later work and survey papers document that PID is used in the large majority of industrial loops. Today tuning has tools (MATLAB, Python control library), model-based rules (IMC, lambda tuning), and, in cases with constraints or many inputs, model predictive control, which has not replaced PID but sits above it, setting its targets.
how: The control law in continuous time is
\[ u(t) = K_p\, e(t) + K_i \int_0^t e(\tau)\, d\tau + K_d\, \frac{de(t)}{dt} \]
where u is the output sent to the actuator. Take the terms one at a time (the calculus concept). The proportional term is a spring: a correction proportional to the error, so a big error gives a big push. It acts immediately, but it needs an error to work. With only P, the controller must keep a non-zero error to keep a non-zero output. For a simple room heater, if 20% output is needed to hold the temperature, P-only control settles at the error that makes \( K_p e = 20\% \). The integral term accumulates the error over time. As long as any error remains, the integral keeps growing and keeps pushing, so the system cannot rest until the error is zero. This is the term that removes steady-state error. Its cost is memory: the integral carries the past, so it reacts slowly and can overshoot. The derivative term measures the slope of the error. If the error is shrinking fast, the derivative gives a negative contribution, which reduces the push before the target is reached. It is a brake that acts on the trend, like looking ahead. It amplifies measurement noise, because a noisy signal has a steep random slope.
Why does the closed loop behave as it does? Here the differential equations concept helps. Model the plant as a first-order lag, \( \tau\, \dot{y} = -y + u \): the output moves toward the input with time constant \( \tau \). With P-only control, \( u = K_p (r - y) \), so \( \tau \dot{y} = -(1 + K_p) y + K_p r \). The solution settles at \( y = \frac{K_p}{1 + K_p} r \), so a gain of 4 leaves a 20% error and the loop responds faster, with time constant \( \tau/(1+K_p) \). Larger gain gives less error and a faster response. But real plants have delay or extra lags, and a delayed correction can arrive when the situation has changed. Then the closed-loop equation has complex roots: high gain makes the loop oscillate, and past a limit the oscillation grows. Stability is exactly the question of whether all roots have negative real parts. This is why more gain is not always better, and why dead time is the enemy of a feedback loop.
In software the controller runs at a fixed period \( \Delta t \), and the integral is a running sum and the derivative a difference:
```python
class PID:
    def __init__(self, kp, ki, kd, umin, umax):
        self.kp, self.ki, self.kd = kp, ki, kd
        self.umin, self.umax = umin, umax
        self.i = 0.0
        self.prev_y = None

    def update(self, setpoint, y, dt):
        e = setpoint - y
        d = 0.0 if self.prev_y is None else -(y - self.prev_y) / dt
        self.prev_y = y
        u_raw = self.kp * e + self.ki * self.i + self.kd * d
        u = max(self.umin, min(self.umax, u_raw))
        # anti-windup: do not integrate further into saturation
        if u == u_raw or (u_raw - u) * e < 0:
            self.i += e * dt
        return u
```
Three details separate a working controller from a textbook one. First, anti-windup: when the output is clamped at its limit, the integral must stop growing; otherwise it stores a huge value and keeps the output saturated long after the error has changed sign. The code above integrates only when the output is within limits, or when the error pulls it back from the limit. Second, derivative on measurement: differentiating the measurement instead of the error avoids a large spike (a "derivative kick") when the setpoint is changed in a step. Third, filtering the derivative with a low-pass filter, because noise at high frequency would otherwise dominate the output.
Gains are chosen by tuning. The manual rule: raise Kp until the response is fast but starts to overshoot, add Kd to damp the overshoot, then add a little Ki to remove the remaining offset. The Ziegler–Nichols closed-loop method: with Ki and Kd at zero, raise Kp until the output oscillates steadily; call that gain \( K_u \) and the oscillation period \( T_u \). Then \( K_p = 0.6 K_u \), \( K_i = K_p / (T_u/2) \), \( K_d = K_p \, T_u/8 \). The result is fast but often with 25% or more overshoot, so people soften it. Modern practice often fits a small model from a step test and calculates gains, or uses relay autotuning.

example: Take a heater with time constant 10 s and a 2 s delay before it starts to respond (a typical lag from a slow sensor and a thick wall). The target is 1.0 (say, 1 unit above the starting temperature) and the controller runs 10 times per second. Simulating this loop gives these results.
With P only and Kp = 4: the temperature rises to about 1.06 once, then settles at 0.80. The formula predicts \( 4/(1+4) = 0.8 \). There is a permanent 20% error because the heater is only on when the error is positive: at 0.8 the error is 0.2 and \( 4 \times 0.2 = 0.8 \), exactly the output that holds 0.8.
With P only and Kp = 8: the loop overshoots to about 1.8 and keeps ringing for more than a minute (between 0.4 and 1.3 even 60 to 90 s later). The delay makes the correction arrive late, and the high gain amplifies it. A little more gain and the swings would grow; Ziegler–Nichols deliberately finds that edge to measure \( K_u \).
With PI, Kp = 2 and Ki = 0.2: the temperature reaches 1.00 and holds it, with no overshoot, about 10 s after the start (within 2%). The integral keeps adding output while an error remains, so the final output is exactly the level that holds the target, and the error is zero. Lower gains (Kp = 1, Ki = 0.1) also work but take about 33 s. With PID and a derivative of Kd = 2 added to the PI, the response overshoots slightly to about 1.03 and takes longer (about 28 s) to settle in this simulation, because with a 2 s dead time the derivative reacts to a measurement that is already old and to noise. Not every term helps every plant.
Now the pacing version. A campaign has a daily budget of 24,000 and should spend 1,000 per hour. The controller output is a multiplier on the bid. After 3 hours the target spend is 3,000 but the actual spend is 3,600, so the error is −600 (overspending by 20%). A P-controller with Kp = 0.5 changes the multiplier by 0.5 × (−600 / 3,000) = −0.10, so bids drop by 10%. If spend stays above target for hours, the integral term grows and reduces bids further until the cumulative overspend is repaid. If there is a morning rush of traffic, the derivative reacts to the rising spend rate before the total is far off. The actuator limits (multiplier between 0 and, say, 2) matter here: when the campaign cannot spend more because it is limited by auction wins, the multiplier saturates at its cap, and without anti-windup the integral would be huge and cause overspending when the traffic returns.
intuition: Driving a car toward a lane position: you steer in proportion to how far off you are, you keep adjusting if you stay off to one side, and you ease off when you see yourself turning back quickly.
Each term fixes a weakness of the previous one. P is quick but leaves a gap, because it needs an error to produce a push. I removes the gap by remembering the past, but remembering makes it slow and prone to overshoot. D anticipates the future by looking at the trend, and calms the overshoot, but it is sensitive to noise. Together they use present, past and predicted future of one number, which is nearly everything you can know from a single measurement without a model. That is why the controller is so general: it does not need to know whether it controls a furnace or a pool of servers.
mistakes: People often think that a higher gain always gives better control. In fact gain trades speed against stability. Every real loop has delay and lag, and above a critical gain the corrections arrive out of phase and amplify the error instead of removing it. The right gain is the one that leaves a safety margin below that limit.
People often think the integral term is for speed. In fact it is for accuracy: it removes the steady-state error that P leaves, and it makes the response slower and more oscillatory. If a loop is already accurate enough, a pure P or PD controller can be better.
People often think the derivative term is always useful. In fact it amplifies noise and, on plants with a long dead time, it acts on stale information. Many industrial loops use PI only. When D is used, it is filtered and applied to the measurement, not to the setpoint step.
People often think PID is a model of the plant. In fact it is a model-free rule that works because most plants respond smoothly and monotonically. It has no knowledge of limits, delays or multi-variable interactions, so for a system with strong coupling (many inputs and outputs) or hard constraints, model predictive control or a state-space design is needed.
People often think autoscaling and pacing loops are "just if-statements". In fact they are feedback loops with delay (instances take minutes to start), and the same stability rules apply. A scaler that reacts to the current metric with no damping will oscillate: add too many instances, see low load, remove them, and repeat.
friction: Tuning is the main cost. Gains depend on the plant, and the plant changes: a heater with a full tank behaves differently from an empty one, a campaign in the morning differs from the evening, a motor loses grip as it wears. A tuning that is good in one operating point may be sluggish or unstable at another. Gain scheduling (different gains for different regions) and adaptive control handle this, but add complexity.
The structural limits are dead time, saturation and noise. With a long dead time, a feedback loop cannot be fast: the controller must wait to see the effect of its action, so loops are limited to responses much slower than the delay, and a Smith predictor or model-based control is needed to do better. Saturation breaks the linear analysis, which is why anti-windup is needed. Noise in the measurement is amplified by the derivative, and quantization of sensors (a thermometer with 0.5 degree steps) produces limit cycles, a small, permanent oscillation around the target.
Nonlinear or time-varying plants violate the assumptions behind the formulas. A system that is much faster in one direction than the other (heating is fast, cooling slow), a valve with friction (stiction), or a service whose latency grows sharply at high utilization will not respond to a linear law the same way across its range. Finally, the control loop sits in a larger system: an autoscaler loop and a request-rate loop and a load balancer loop may interact and oscillate together, even if each is stable alone. Observability of the loop itself (logging error, each term, and output) is often missing, which makes problems slow to diagnose.
tradeoffs: Choose P-only control when a steady offset is acceptable and robustness matters most, for example an autoscaler that targets 60% CPU and tolerates 55% to 65%. Choose PI when the target must be met exactly over time, such as a budget that must be spent to the cent by the end of the day, and accept a slower response and the need for anti-windup. Choose PID only when the plant responds quickly enough, with little dead time, and the measurement is clean enough for the derivative to help; otherwise it adds noise for little gain.
Choose manual or rule-based tuning (Ziegler–Nichols, lambda) when you have access to a step test and the loop is slow and cheap to test. Choose automatic tuning (relay feedback, or optimization in simulation) when you have many loops, or the plant changes. Choose model predictive control when the system has several coupled inputs, hard constraints (limits on rate or budget) and a good model, and you can afford the compute (a small optimization at every step). You give up simplicity and a short setup time.
Choose a slow loop with a long interval (for example evaluate pacing every 5 minutes, scaling every minute) when the actuator is slow or the measurement noisy: slower loops are more stable and less jittery. Choose a fast loop when response time matters, such as attitude control in a drone at 500 Hz to 1 kHz, but then you must keep delays small. A loop that runs faster than its actuator can respond only adds noise.
interview: What does each term of a PID controller do, and what goes wrong with each if it is too large? || P gives a correction proportional to the current error: too high causes overshoot and oscillation. I accumulates error to remove steady offset: too high causes slow, oscillating response and windup. D reacts to the rate of change to damp overshoot: too high amplifies noise and causes jitter. The art is to balance them for the plant's lag and delay.
What is integral windup and how do you prevent it? || When the actuator saturates, the error stays non-zero and the integral keeps growing, storing a large value. When the error finally reverses, the output stays saturated until the integral unwinds, causing a large overshoot. Prevent it by clamping the integral, stopping integration when saturated, or back-calculation that feeds the saturation difference back into the integral.
Your autoscaler oscillates between 5 and 20 instances. practice: Start every loop with a design on paper: what is measured, how often, what the actuator is and what its limits are, and how long it takes for an action to show in the measurement. value: A hidden Markov model (HMM) describes a system that you cannot see directly but can observe through its effects. The system is always in one of a few hidden states, and the state changes over time by simple probabilistic rules. At each step the current state produces an observation, again by a probabilistic rule. Speech is the classic case: the hidden states are the sounds a person is making, and the observations are short slices of audio. A tagger for text is another: the hidden states are parts of speech, the observations are the words.
Two algorithms make the model usable, and both are dynamic programming. The forward algorithm computes how likely a whole observation sequence is under the model, summing over every possible hidden path, in time proportional to the sequence length instead of exponential. The Viterbi algorithm finds the single most probable hidden path with the same cost. A third procedure, Baum–Welch, learns the probabilities from data when the hidden states are not labelled.
The layers below give the model its parts. A Markov chain (L4) is the hidden layer: the next state depends only on the current one, which is what keeps the computation small. Bayes' rule (L4) turns "how likely is this observation given the state" into "how likely is the state given the observation". Dynamic programming (L3) is the reason a search over exponentially many paths takes a few multiplications per step. Above, speech recognizers and part-of-speech taggers were built from HMMs for decades, graphical models generalize the idea to arbitrary dependency graphs, and the same hidden-state view reappears in Kalman filters, which are HMMs with continuous Gaussian states.
terms: Hidden state — the variable you want to infer; it follows a Markov chain and is never observed directly.
Observation (emission) — the visible output produced at each step, drawn from a distribution that depends on the current hidden state.
Transition matrix A — the probabilities \( a_{ij} \) of moving from state i to state j.
Emission probabilities B — the probabilities \( b_j(o) \) of seeing observation o while in state j.
Initial distribution π — the probabilities of each state at time 1.
Forward probability — \( \alpha_t(j) \), the probability of the first t observations and of being in state j at time t.
Viterbi path — the single hidden-state sequence with the highest joint probability with the observations.
Posterior (smoothing) — the probability of being in state j at time t given all observations, past and future.
Baum–Welch — an expectation–maximization algorithm that estimates A, B and π from unlabelled sequences.
history: The Markov chain (Andrey Markov, 1906–1913) is old, but the hidden version appeared in the 1960s. Leonard Baum and Ted Petrie, working at the Institute for Defense Analyses, published "Statistical inference for probabilistic functions of finite state Markov chains" in 1966, with the learning method that was completed in 1970 by Baum, Petrie, Soules and Weiss (now called Baum–Welch, an early example of expectation maximization). Separately, Andrew Viterbi (1967) published the algorithm that bears his name to decode convolutional error-correcting codes, the same dynamic program on a different problem; it was later used in every cell phone.
Speech recognition adopted the model in the 1970s. James Baker at Carnegie Mellon (the Dragon system, 1975) and Fred Jelinek's group at IBM treated recognition as finding the most probable word sequence given the audio, using HMMs for sounds. Over the next decades, systems used one small HMM per phone, combined into words, with Gaussian mixtures for the emission probabilities. Lawrence Rabiner's 1989 tutorial in the Proceedings of the IEEE made the method known across engineering. The method also spread to biology (gene finding with GENSCAN, 1997; profile HMMs for protein families) and to language (part-of-speech tagging, named entity recognition).
In the 2000s, discriminative models such as the conditional random field (Lafferty, McCallum and Pereira, 2001) replaced HMMs for tagging, since they can use richer features. In speech, around 2011 and 2012, deep neural networks replaced the Gaussian mixtures as emission models (the "hybrid" DNN–HMM), and then end-to-end systems (CTC, RNN-T, attention models) removed the explicit HMM. HMMs are no longer the state of the art for these tasks, but the forward and Viterbi dynamic programs live on inside CTC loss, beam search decoders, and many sequence models.
how: An HMM is defined by three things: the initial distribution π over N states, the N × N transition matrix A, and the emission probabilities B. It makes two assumptions, both from the Markov chain concept: the next state depends only on the current state, and each observation depends only on the current state. Together they say that the joint probability of a state sequence \( q_1 \dots q_T \) and an observation sequence \( o_1 \dots o_T \) factorizes:
\[ P(q, o) = \pi_{q_1}\, b_{q_1}(o_1) \prod_{t=2}^{T} a_{q_{t-1} q_t}\, b_{q_t}(o_t) \]
Three questions follow. Evaluation: how likely is the observation sequence under the model? Decoding: which hidden sequence explains it best? Learning: which parameters fit the data? For evaluation, a direct sum over all state sequences has \( N^T \) terms. Dynamic programming (the dp concept) removes the explosion, because the probability of reaching state j at time t depends on the past only through the state at time t − 1. The forward recursion is
\[ \alpha_1(j) = \pi_j\, b_j(o_1), \qquad \alpha_t(j) = \Big(\sum_{i} \alpha_{t-1}(i)\, a_{ij}\Big)\, b_j(o_t) \]
and the likelihood is \( \sum_j \alpha_T(j) \). Each step does about \( N^2 \) multiplications, so the total is \( O(TN^2) \). For a 1,000-step sequence and 50 states, that is about 2.5 million operations, compared with \( 50^{1000} \) paths.
Decoding replaces the sum with a maximum. Viterbi keeps \( \delta_t(j) \), the probability of the best path that ends in state j at time t, and a back-pointer to the previous state that achieved it:
\[ \delta_t(j) = \max_i \big(\delta_{t-1}(i)\, a_{ij}\big)\, b_j(o_t) \]
At the end pick the best final state and follow the pointers backwards. The reason a local max is enough is the Markov property: the best path to (j, t) must pass through the best path to some state at t − 1, since what happens after depends only on the state, not on how it was reached. Viterbi finds the single most likely sequence, which is not the same as picking the most likely state at each time. The second answer comes from the forward–backward algorithm: combine \( \alpha_t(j) \) with a backward probability \( \beta_t(j) \) (the probability of the future observations given state j at t) to get \( P(q_t = j \mid o_{1:T}) \propto \alpha_t(j)\beta_t(j) \). This is Bayes' rule applied to the whole chain (the bayes concept): the prior comes from the past, the likelihood from the future.
Learning uses expectation maximization. Start with guessed A, B, π. E-step: with forward–backward compute the expected number of times each transition and emission was used (soft counts). M-step: re-estimate the probabilities as normalized soft counts. Each round never decreases the likelihood, but it converges to a local optimum, so initialization matters. If you have labelled sequences (words tagged with parts of speech), you simply count and divide, with smoothing for unseen events.
In speech the construction is layered. Each phone gets a small left-to-right HMM, usually three states for the start, middle and end of the sound, with self-loops so that a phone can last several 10 ms frames. The phone models are chained into words using a pronunciation dictionary, and words are chained into sentences with a language model giving the transition probabilities between words. The result is one huge HMM, and decoding the audio means running Viterbi (with a beam) over it. The emission model, which scores one 10 ms frame of audio features against one state, was a Gaussian mixture until about 2012, and a neural network afterwards. This shows the general pattern: the HMM supplies the time structure and exact search, and the emission model can be replaced with anything that scores observations. In graphical-model terms (the pgm concept), an HMM is a chain-shaped Bayesian network with hidden nodes in one row and observed nodes in another, and forward–backward is belief propagation on that chain.
Numbers shrink fast. A product of 1,000 probabilities underflows a double. Real code works in log space (Viterbi: sums of logs, no problem; forward: log-sum-exp) or rescales \( \alpha_t \) at every step. For large state spaces, as in speech with thousands of states and many time steps, beam search prunes states whose score is far below the best.

example: The classic weather example, with every number to hand. Two hidden states: Rainy and Sunny. Initial probabilities: Rainy 0.6, Sunny 0.4. Transitions: Rainy→Rainy 0.7, Rainy→Sunny 0.3, Sunny→Rainy 0.4, Sunny→Sunny 0.6. Emissions (what a friend does each day): in Rainy, walk 0.1, shop 0.4, clean 0.5; in Sunny, walk 0.6, shop 0.3, clean 0.1. You see three days: walk, shop, clean. What was the weather?
Forward pass. Day 1 (walk): α(Rainy) = 0.6 × 0.1 = 0.06; α(Sunny) = 0.4 × 0.6 = 0.24. Day 2 (shop): α(Rainy) = (0.06 × 0.7 + 0.24 × 0.4) × 0.4 = (0.042 + 0.096) × 0.4 = 0.0552; α(Sunny) = (0.06 × 0.3 + 0.24 × 0.6) × 0.3 = (0.018 + 0.144) × 0.3 = 0.0486. Day 3 (clean): α(Rainy) = (0.0552 × 0.7 + 0.0486 × 0.4) × 0.5 = 0.02904; α(Sunny) = (0.0552 × 0.3 + 0.0486 × 0.6) × 0.1 = 0.004572. The likelihood of the three observations is 0.02904 + 0.004572 = 0.033612, about 3.4%. A brute-force sum over the 8 possible weather sequences gives the same value.
Viterbi pass. Day 1 is as above: 0.06 and 0.24. Day 2, state Rainy: the best predecessor is Sunny (0.24 × 0.4 = 0.096, against 0.06 × 0.7 = 0.042), so δ = 0.096 × 0.4 = 0.0384, pointer Sunny. State Sunny: predecessors give 0.06 × 0.3 = 0.018 and 0.24 × 0.6 = 0.144, so δ = 0.144 × 0.3 = 0.0432, pointer Sunny. Day 3, Rainy: 0.0384 × 0.7 = 0.02688 beats 0.0432 × 0.4 = 0.01728, so δ = 0.02688 × 0.5 = 0.01344, pointer Rainy. Sunny: 0.0432 × 0.6 = 0.02592 beats 0.0384 × 0.3 = 0.01152, so δ = 0.02592 × 0.1 = 0.002592. The best final state is Rainy with 0.01344. Backtracking: Rainy on day 3, pointer Rainy for day 2, pointer Sunny for day 1. The most likely weather is Sunny, Rainy, Rainy, with joint probability 0.01344, which is 40% of the total likelihood.
Posterior check. With forward–backward, the probability of Rainy is 23% on day 1, 62% on day 2 and 86% on day 3. These agree with the Viterbi path here, but they need not: Viterbi maximizes the probability of the whole sequence, the posterior gives the best guess day by day, and the two can disagree. The posterior also tells you how sure you are: day 2 is only 62% Rainy, a weak call.
Cost: with 2 states and 3 days the work was about 12 multiplications. For 50 states and 1,000 steps it is \( 1000 \times 2500 \approx 2.5 \times 10^6 \) multiply-adds, which takes a few milliseconds.
intuition: Follow the best route through a map of time and states, remembering only the best way to reach each place, because the future does not care how you got there.
The hidden chain forgets its history, so everything that matters about the past is summarized by "which state am I in now". That is why you can fold an exponential number of paths into N numbers per step. Observations then add evidence about which state is the true one, in the same way a detective updates a belief with each clue. The difference between the forward sum and the Viterbi max is only whether you want the total probability of all explanations or the single best one. In both cases, the shared structure of paths (they share prefixes) is what the table reuses.
mistakes: People often think Viterbi gives the most likely state at each time. In fact it gives the most likely whole sequence. The individually most probable states (from forward–backward) can form a sequence that is impossible, for example a transition with probability 0. Use Viterbi when the sequence must be consistent, and posteriors when you want per-step confidence.
People often think the hidden states have a meaning given by the model. In fact states learned by Baum–Welch are whatever helps explain the data; they may not match the categories you hoped for. Labels come only from supervised training or from interpreting the result.
People often think an HMM needs the observations to be discrete. In fact the emission can be any distribution: Gaussian mixtures for audio features, or a neural network that outputs a score for each state. Only the hidden state space is discrete.
People often think HMMs are obsolete. In fact they are rarely the best model for accuracy now, but they are cheap, interpretable, need little data, and run in milliseconds. Their algorithms are inside modern decoders, and the idea of a latent state chain is the base of state-space models.
People often think the Markov assumption means the model has no memory beyond one step. In fact the hidden state can encode a lot of memory if there are enough states, and higher-order dependencies can be turned into first-order ones by enlarging the state space, at the cost of more states.
friction: The two independence assumptions are strong. Real observations are correlated beyond what the hidden state explains: adjacent audio frames overlap, and words depend on distant words. The model cannot use arbitrary overlapping features of the input (capitalization, suffixes, neighbouring words), which is the reason discriminative sequence models (CRFs, then neural networks) took over from HMMs in tagging. An HMM is also generative: it models P(observations and states) and spends capacity on modelling the observations, while for tagging you only need P(states given observations).
State duration is modelled badly. In a plain HMM the time spent in a state is geometric: staying probability \( a_{ii} \) gives a decaying distribution with the highest mass at one step, which does not match phone or event durations. Hidden semi-Markov models fix this at higher cost. Learning has local optima, and the number of states must be chosen by hand or by validation. Sparse data needs smoothing, since an emission never seen in training would get probability 0, and one zero kills a whole path.
At scale, the cost \( O(TN^2) \) is fine for 50 states but not for 100,000 (a word-level language model in a decoder). Systems use sparse transitions, beam search, and compiled graphs (weighted finite-state transducers), which are fast but complex. Fully probabilistic output is also hard to calibrate: the scores are products of many small terms, dominated by a few of them, and not reliable as confidence values without care.
tradeoffs: Choose an HMM when the data is a sequence with a small number of discrete regimes, labelled data is scarce, you need exact inference in milliseconds, and you want to read the parameters (regime detection, simple activity recognition, alignment of a known script to audio). Choose a CRF or a neural sequence model when you have labelled data, need rich input features, and accuracy matters more than interpretability; you pay with training cost and opacity.
Choose Viterbi decoding when you need one consistent output sequence, as in a tagger or an aligner. Choose forward–backward posteriors when you need calibrated per-step probabilities, expected counts for training, or soft decisions passed to another component. Viterbi is cheaper in memory only if you keep back-pointers (T × N); posteriors need a full forward and backward pass.
Choose supervised counting when you have tagged sequences: it is a closed form and exact. Choose Baum–Welch when states are hidden by design and you can afford several restarts; run it from multiple random starts and keep the best likelihood.
Choose a Kalman filter when the hidden state is a continuous vector with linear dynamics and Gaussian noise. Choose an HMM when the hidden variable is a category, and a particle filter when it is continuous but non-linear.
interview: Explain the forward algorithm and why it is polynomial. || It computes α_t(j), the probability of the first t observations ending in state j, from the α_{t−1} values: sum over previous states of α × transition, times the emission. Each of the T steps does N² work, so O(TN²), instead of enumerating N^T paths. It works because all paths reaching state j share the same future, so their probabilities can be pooled.
What is the difference between Viterbi and forward–backward decoding? || Viterbi replaces the sum with a max and keeps back-pointers, giving the single most probable state sequence. Forward–backward gives the marginal probability of each state at each time. The best per-step states may not form a valid or most probable sequence.
You implement Viterbi and get all-zero scores on long sequences. Why and what do you do? || Products of many probabilities underflow doubles. Work in log space, where products become sums (for forward use log-sum-exp), or rescale each step. Also smooth probabilities so no emission is exactly zero.
How do you train an HMM without labelled states? || Baum–Welch, which is EM: run forward–backward with the current parameters to get expected counts of transitions and emissions, then renormalize them to be the new parameters. It increases likelihood each round but only finds a local optimum, so use several initializations and watch for states that collapse.
Why were HMMs replaced in sequence tagging, and what did the replacements keep? || HMMs assume each observation depends only on its own state, so they cannot use overlapping features, and they model the wrong distribution for the task. CRFs and neural networks model the conditional distribution with rich features. They keep the same dynamic programs (Viterbi decoding, forward–backward for the normalizer).
practice: To use an HMM, first write down the hidden states and what each emits, and check that the Markov assumptions are plausible. Count transitions and emissions from labelled data if you have it, with add-one or better smoothing. Otherwise run Baum–Welch from multiple starts, and choose the number of states by held-out likelihood, not by the training score. Use a tested library: hmmlearn in Python, or pomegranate; for speech, Kaldi (which uses HMM-based decoding with neural emissions) and its finite-state-transducer decoder.
A compact Viterbi in log space for N states:
```python
import numpy as np

def viterbi(logpi, logA, logB, obs):
    T, N = len(obs), len(logpi)
    delta = np.full((T, N), -np.inf)
    back = np.zeros((T, N), dtype=int)
    delta[0] = logpi + logB[:, obs[0]]
    for t in range(1, T):
        scores = delta[t - 1][:, None] + logA      # N x N
        back[t] = scores.argmax(axis=0)
        delta[t] = scores.max(axis=0) + logB[:, obs[t]]
    path = [int(delta[-1].argmax())]
    for t in range(T - 1, 0, -1):
        path.append(int(back[t][path[-1]]))
    return path[::-1]
```
Test it on the weather example above: it should return Sunny, Rainy, Rainy. In production, always check that sequences are long enough that the Markov structure matters, and compare against a baseline that ignores the order. If you work with event streams, for example sessions of viewing events, an HMM with a few states can separate modes such as browsing and watching from the pattern of switches, which is a cheap first model before anything heavier. quiz: What two independence assumptions define an HMM? || The next hidden state depends only on the current hidden state (Markov), and each observation depends only on the current hidden state.

value: Bayesian inference answers "what do I believe about the unknowns after seeing the data?" with a posterior distribution. The posterior is exact in a few simple models and intractable in almost all useful ones: its normalizing constant is an integral over all values of the unknowns, which grows exponentially with their number. The traditional answer was Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC): draw samples from the posterior by a long random walk. It is accurate in the limit but slow, and hard to run on millions of data points.
Variational inference (VI) turns the integration problem into an optimization problem. Pick a family of simple distributions q (for example independent Gaussians), and find the member of that family closest to the true posterior. "Closest" means smallest KL divergence. Because the posterior is unknown, you cannot compute that KL directly, but you can maximize an equivalent quantity that you can compute: the evidence lower bound (ELBO). Then ordinary gradient-based optimization does the work, and you can use mini-batches of data, GPUs and automatic differentiation.
The layers below make this work. Bayes' rule (L4) defines the target posterior. Information theory (L4) supplies the KL divergence as the measure of closeness. Optimization (L4) turns the search for the best q into maximizing a function. Stochastic gradient descent (L2) scales that maximization to large data. VI is the engine of the variational autoencoder, where a neural network produces the q for each data point, and the training loss of diffusion models is a variational bound. It is also used for topic models, Bayesian neural networks and probabilistic programming systems (Stan's ADVI, Pyro, NumPyro, PyMC).
terms: Posterior — the distribution of the unknown variables z after seeing data x, \( p(z \mid x) \).
Evidence — the probability of the data, \( p(x) = \int p(x,z)\,dz \), the hard integral that makes the posterior intractable.
Variational family — the set of simple distributions \( q_\phi(z) \) (parameters \( \phi \)) that we search over.
KL divergence — a measure of how different q is from p, \( \mathrm{KL}(q\|p) = E_q[\log q - \log p] \); it is zero only when they are equal and never negative.
ELBO — evidence lower bound, \( E_q[\log p(x,z)] - E_q[\log q(z)] \); maximizing it minimizes the KL to the posterior.
Mean-field — the family where q factorizes into independent parts, \( q(z) = \prod_i q_i(z_i) \).
Reparameterization trick — writing a sample from q as a deterministic function of its parameters and fixed noise, so gradients can flow through the sampling.
Amortized inference — using one neural network (an encoder) to output the parameters of q for any data point, instead of optimizing q separately for each.
Black-box VI — VI that needs only the ability to evaluate \( \log p(x,z) \) and sample from q, not model-specific derivations.
history: Variational methods come from physics and calculus of variations: approximate a hard distribution by optimizing over a simpler family. Mean-field theory in statistical physics is the ancestor. Hinton and van Camp (1993) used a variational bound to fit neural networks with noisy weights, and Neal and Hinton (1998) showed that the EM algorithm is a coordinate ascent on this kind of bound. Michael Jordan, Zoubin Ghahramani, Tommi Jaakkola and Lawrence Saul published "An Introduction to Variational Methods for Graphical Models" in 1999, which made VI the standard tool for large graphical models. Blei, Ng and Jordan (2003) used it for the topic model LDA.
Early VI needed pages of algebra for each new model, because the update formulas had to be derived by hand. Two changes in the 2010s removed that. First, stochastic variational inference (Hoffman, Blei, Wang and Paisley, 2013) used mini-batches of data and natural gradients, so VI could run on millions of documents. Second, black-box methods (Ranganath, Gerrish and Blei 2014, and the automatic differentiation variational inference, ADVI, of Kucukelbir et al. 2017) used generic gradient estimators and automatic differentiation, so a user only writes the model.
In 2013 and 2014, Kingma and Welling, and Rezende, Mohamed and Wierstra, introduced the reparameterization trick, a low-variance gradient estimator, and amortized inference with a neural network. Together these gave the variational autoencoder. Rezende and Mohamed (2015) added normalizing flows to make q much richer than a Gaussian. Diffusion models (Sohl-Dickstein 2015; Ho, Jain and Abbeel 2020) are trained with a variational bound on the data likelihood. In the 2020s, VI is often hidden inside deep generative models, while for small Bayesian models in statistics, MCMC (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo in Stan) remains the accuracy reference.
how: Let x be the data, z the unknowns, and \( p(x,z) = p(z)\,p(x \mid z) \) the model: a prior and a likelihood, both easy to evaluate. The posterior is \( p(z \mid x) = p(x,z)/p(x) \), and the evidence \( p(x) \) is the hard part. Choose a family \( q_\phi(z) \) and look for the \( \phi \) that minimizes \( \mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z) \,\|\, p(z \mid x)) \). This still involves \( p(x) \). But a short derivation (the infotheory concept supplies the KL) rearranges it:
\[ \log p(x) = \underbrace{E_q[\log p(x,z)] - E_q[\log q(z)]}_{\text{ELBO}} + \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z)\,\|\,p(z \mid x)\big) \]
The left side does not depend on q. The KL term is never negative, so the ELBO is always at most \( \log p(x) \): a lower bound. Raising the ELBO lowers the KL by exactly the same amount, and the ELBO uses only \( p(x,z) \), which we can evaluate. The ELBO has a useful reading: \( E_q[\log p(x \mid z)] \) rewards q for explaining the data, and \( -\mathrm{KL}(q \,\|\, p(z)) \) penalizes q for moving far from the prior. This is the same trade-off as in a regularized loss, which is why the ELBO is also the loss of a VAE (reconstruction plus regularization).
The optimization (the optimization and gd concepts): compute the gradient of the ELBO with respect to \( \phi \) and ascend it. The difficulty is that the ELBO is an expectation over q, which itself depends on \( \phi \). Two estimators exist. The score-function estimator works for any q (even for discrete z) but has high variance. The reparameterization estimator writes \( z = g_\phi(\epsilon) \) with noise \( \epsilon \) from a fixed distribution, for a Gaussian \( z = \mu + \sigma\epsilon \) with \( \epsilon \sim N(0,1) \). Then the gradient passes through g by the chain rule, and one sample per step often suffices. Automatic differentiation computes it. With a data set of N points and mini-batches of size B, the likelihood term is rescaled by N/B, which gives an unbiased estimate of the full ELBO and lets the method scale linearly in the data.
A classic special case is coordinate ascent VI (CAVI) with mean-field q: update one factor at a time, \( \log q_j(z_j) = E_{q_{-j}}[\log p(x,z)] + \text{const} \), which has closed forms in conjugate models. Every update increases the ELBO, and the algorithm converges to a local optimum.
Two choices of family decide the quality. The mean-field family is cheap (the number of parameters grows linearly) but ignores correlations between unknowns. Full-covariance Gaussians capture them at quadratic cost, and normalizing flows or mixtures capture non-Gaussian shapes. The second choice is the direction of the KL. VI minimizes \( \mathrm{KL}(q\|p) \), the reverse KL, which punishes q for putting mass where p has little, and does not mind q missing some of p. The result is mode-seeking and tends to underestimate uncertainty: q is too narrow, and if p has two peaks, q settles on one. The forward KL \( \mathrm{KL}(p\|q) \), used by expectation propagation, covers all of p but is harder to optimize.
For a VAE the two ELBO terms have concrete forms. With a Gaussian encoder \( q(z \mid x) = N(\mu, \sigma^2) \) and a standard normal prior, the KL term has a closed form per latent dimension, \( \tfrac12(\mu^2 + \sigma^2 - 1 - \ln\sigma^2) \). It is zero when \( \mu = 0 \) and \( \sigma = 1 \), and grows as the encoder commits to a specific code for x. The reconstruction term \( E_q[\log p(x \mid z)] \) is estimated with one reparameterized sample and, for a Gaussian decoder, is a squared error. Training is then a standard neural network loop: sample a mini-batch, encode, sample \( \epsilon \), decode, compute the sum of the two terms, and backpropagate. The same recipe covers Bayesian neural networks, where z is the weights and q gives each weight a mean and a standard deviation.
In amortized VI (a VAE), q is not optimized separately for each data point. An encoder network maps x to the parameters of \( q_\phi(z \mid x) \), and the weights of that network are trained on the ELBO averaged over data. New points then get their q from one forward pass. The price is an amortization gap: the encoder's q is not as good as an individually optimized one.

example: A tiny model first, so every number can be checked. A binary unknown z has prior P(z=1) = 0.5. One observation x has likelihood P(x | z=1) = 0.8 and P(x | z=0) = 0.2. The joint values are p(x, z=1) = 0.4 and p(x, z=0) = 0.1, so the evidence is p(x) = 0.5, with log p(x) = −0.693 (natural logarithm). The exact posterior is P(z=1 | x) = 0.4/0.5 = 0.8.
Choose q with q(z=1) = 0.5. The ELBO is \( 0.5(\ln 0.4 - \ln 0.5) + 0.5(\ln 0.1 - \ln 0.5) = 0.5(-0.223) + 0.5(-1.609) = -0.916 \). The KL from q to the posterior is \( 0.5\ln(0.5/0.8) + 0.5\ln(0.5/0.2) = 0.5(-0.470) + 0.5(0.916) = 0.223 \). Check: −0.916 + 0.223 = −0.693 = log p(x). Now choose q(z=1) = 0.8, the true posterior: ELBO = \( 0.8(\ln 0.4 - \ln 0.8) + 0.2(\ln 0.1 - \ln 0.2) = -0.693 \), the KL is 0, and the bound is tight. Maximizing the ELBO found the posterior without ever computing the evidence 0.5, which in this tiny case we could, but in a large model we cannot.
Now a case where the family is too simple. Suppose the true posterior is a two-dimensional Gaussian with mean 0, unit variances and correlation ρ = 0.9. Fit a mean-field q, two independent Gaussians. The optimal factor for each variable has variance 1/Λ_ii, where Λ is the precision matrix of the posterior. Here \( \Lambda = \frac{1}{1-\rho^2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & -\rho \\ -\rho & 1\end{pmatrix} \), so Λ_ii = 1/0.19 ≈ 5.26 and the mean-field variance is 0.19, a standard deviation of 0.44, against a true marginal standard deviation of 1. The approximation is more than two times too narrow. The means are right: CAVI alternates \( m_1 = \rho\, m_2 \) and \( m_2 = \rho\, m_1 \). Starting from \( m_2 = 1 \), it gives \( m_1 = 0.9 \), then \( m_2 = 0.81 \), and so on, shrinking by a factor ρ² = 0.81 per sweep toward the true mean 0. So VI gets the center right and the spread wrong, which is typical.
Finally a practical sketch in NumPyro, fitting the mean and spread of some data with a Gaussian guide:
```python
import numpyro, numpyro.distributions as dist
from jax import random
from numpyro.infer import SVI, Trace_ELBO
from numpyro.infer.autoguide import AutoNormal

def model(x):
    mu = numpyro.sample("mu", dist.Normal(0.0, 10.0))
    sigma = numpyro.sample("sigma", dist.HalfNormal(5.0))
    with numpyro.plate("data", x.shape[0]):
        numpyro.sample("obs", dist.Normal(mu, sigma), obs=x)

guide = AutoNormal(model)
svi = SVI(model, guide, numpyro.optim.Adam(0.01), Trace_ELBO())
result = svi.run(random.PRNGKey(0), 5000, x)   # x: array of data
```
intuition: Instead of exploring a landscape of beliefs point by point, fit the best simple shape over it and measure how well it fits with a bound you can compute.
The posterior is a function you can evaluate up to a constant but cannot integrate. A sampler tries to visit it fairly; VI instead asks for the best cheap model of it. The ELBO is the trick that makes "best" computable: the missing constant cancels in the comparison, because it is the same for every q. Once the problem is "maximize a smooth function of parameters", the whole toolkit of deep learning applies. The bound also has a clean meaning: it is the log evidence minus the distance to the posterior, so a higher number is always a better approximation, and the gap is the error.
mistakes: People often think VI finds the true posterior. In fact it finds the best member of the chosen family. If the family is too simple (mean-field on correlated variables) the answer is biased, and in particular the variance is usually too small. A high ELBO does not tell you how far you are from the posterior, because you do not know log p(x).
People often think the ELBO is a loss that you want to be near zero. In fact it is a lower bound on a log-probability and is usually a large negative number. Only changes in it matter: compare models or fits by the ELBO values and watch it increase.
People often think VI is deterministic and so gives repeatable results. In fact stochastic gradients and random starts give different local optima. The ELBO is non-convex, so run multiple seeds and check that the results agree.
People often think VI is simply faster MCMC. In fact it trades accuracy for speed in a different way: MCMC is asymptotically exact but slow; VI is fast and biased, with no guarantee of convergence to the truth. For small models where accuracy matters, run MCMC; for large data or latent-variable deep models, use VI.
People often think amortized VI (a VAE encoder) gives the best q for each point. In fact the encoder shares parameters across points, so q is only as good as the network can learn. This amortization gap is separate from the family gap and can be reduced with extra per-point optimization steps.
friction: The bias of VI is its core limitation. Reverse-KL minimization makes q avoid regions where the posterior is low, so q is under-dispersed and credible intervals are too narrow, which is dangerous when the uncertainty is the point. Multimodal posteriors are collapsed onto one mode (or onto "posterior collapse" in a VAE, where the model ignores the latent variable). Few diagnostics exist: the ELBO tells you about progress, not correctness. Pareto-smoothed importance sampling (the k̂ diagnostic) and simulation-based calibration check the fit, but they are not run by default in most tools.
Optimization is also hard. The ELBO is non-convex; gradient variance can be large, especially with discrete latent variables; and the learning rate, the initialization and the number of Monte Carlo samples matter. Constrained variables (positive scales, probabilities) need transformations, which change the geometry. Model-specific derivations are replaced by automation, but automatic guides can be a poor fit to a model's structure.
Finally there is a cost in expressiveness versus speed. Richer families (full covariance, flows) are slower and have more parameters to fit, and mean-field remains the default because it scales. For very high-dimensional models, such as large Bayesian neural networks, even mean-field posteriors can be hard to train and are often no better than much cheaper ensembles in predictive accuracy.
tradeoffs: Choose VI when the data set is large (millions of points), the model has many latent variables, or you need fast, repeated inference such as for each new data point (amortized) and approximate uncertainty is acceptable. Choose MCMC (HMC/NUTS) when the model has up to a few thousand parameters, the data is moderate, and calibrated uncertainty is the product. You pay in time with MCMC (minutes to hours) and in bias with VI.
Choose mean-field when you need speed and only the means matter, and full-rank Gaussians or flows when correlations drive the answer, accepting quadratic or higher cost in the number of parameters. Choose the reparameterization gradient for continuous latents (low variance); choose score-function gradients with control variates for discrete latents, or relax them (Gumbel-softmax).
Choose amortized inference (an encoder) when you must infer on many new inputs fast, as in a VAE at serving time. Choose per-data-point optimization (non-amortized) when you have few points and want the best q for each, or use the encoder as a start and refine it.
Choose Laplace approximation, a Gaussian at the posterior mode with curvature from the Hessian, when the posterior is close to a single Gaussian, and you can afford the Hessian; it is cheaper than VI but local. Choose expectation propagation when forward-KL coverage matters and the model has a tractable structure.
interview: Derive the ELBO and say why maximizing it minimizes the KL to the posterior. || Start from log p(x) = log ∫ p(x,z) dz, multiply and divide by q(z), apply Jensen's inequality to get log p(x) ≥ E_q[log p(x,z) − log q(z)]. Equivalently, log p(x) = ELBO + KL(q‖p(z|x)). The left side is fixed, so raising the ELBO lowers the KL by the same amount.
Why does mean-field VI underestimate the variance? || It minimizes the reverse KL, KL(q‖p), which is infinite if q puts mass where p has none, so q stays inside p. A factorized q cannot represent correlation, and the best factorized fit has variance 1/Λ_ii, which is smaller than the true marginal variance. With correlation 0.9, the standard deviation is 0.44 instead of 1.
What is the reparameterization trick and why does it help? || It writes z = μ + σ·ε with ε from a fixed distribution, so a sample is a differentiable function of the parameters. The gradient of the expectation then passes through the sample by the chain rule, with much lower variance than the score-function estimator, which needs only log q's gradient but has high variance.
Compare VI and MCMC for a Bayesian model with 10 million data points. || MCMC needs the likelihood over all data per step, or tall-data tricks, and is slow. VI uses mini-batches and stochastic gradients, so it scales, with a biased, under-dispersed posterior. I would use VI, then check against MCMC on a subsample or with posterior predictive checks and calibration tests.
What is the amortization gap and posterior collapse in a VAE? || The amortization gap is the loss from using one encoder network for all data points instead of optimizing q per point. Posterior collapse is when the decoder is strong enough to ignore z and the encoder outputs the prior, so the KL term goes to zero and the latent carries no information; mitigate with KL annealing, weaker decoders or free bits.
practice: Begin with the model, not the algorithm: write the joint \( p(x,z) \) in a probabilistic programming system (Stan, PyMC, NumPyro, Pyro, TensorFlow Probability), and try an automatic guide first. Watch the ELBO curve over steps: it should rise and flatten with noise; use a decaying learning rate, several seeds and enough Monte Carlo samples (1 to 10 per step) to keep it stable.
Check the result, since the ELBO cannot. Use posterior predictive checks (simulate data from the fitted model and compare it with the real data), compare with MCMC on a small version of the problem, and look at calibration: do 90% intervals contain the truth about 90% of the time? For production use, freeze the encoder and serve it as an ordinary neural network.

value: An architectural style is a rule about which parts of a system may depend on which, and how they talk to each other. The rule is chosen once and then holds across the whole code base. In a layered style, each layer may call only the layer below. In hexagonal style, the business logic sits in the middle and knows nothing about databases, web frameworks or message queues; the outside world connects to it through small interfaces. In pipes-and-filters, independent steps each read a stream and write a stream, and any sequence of steps can be joined. In event-driven style, components do not call each other but publish facts that others may react to.
Why bother? A system of 200,000 lines with no rule about dependencies slowly becomes a graph where everything may call everything, and a change in any place may break any other. A style limits the allowed edges of the dependency graph. That gives a team three things: the ability to change one part (replace the database, add a new input channel) without touching the rest, the ability to test the core logic quickly without infrastructure, and a shared map that a new engineer can learn in a day.
The layers below supply the ideas. Software engineering principles (L3) explain why dependencies must be managed: coupling and cohesion decide how expensive change is. Design patterns and SOLID (L2) provide the Adapter and the dependency inversion principle, which hexagonal architecture applies at the scale of a whole system. Inter-process communication (L3) provides the Unix pipe, which pipes-and-filters turns into a design rule. Above it, microservices choose a style for service boundaries, event sourcing builds on the event-driven style, web applications are layered, and domain-driven design is usually combined with hexagonal structure.
terms: Layer — a group of modules at one level of abstraction that may use only the layer(s) below it.
Dependency rule — the rule that source-code dependencies point in one direction only (downward in layers, inward in hexagonal).
Port — an interface owned by the core, which describes what the core needs from the outside world or offers to it.
Adapter — code that connects a port to a concrete technology (a SQL database, an HTTP controller, a queue).
Domain (core) — the business rules, written in plain code with no framework or I/O.
Filter — a component that reads an input stream, transforms it and writes an output stream, knowing nothing about its neighbours.
Pipe — the channel that carries the output of one filter to the input of the next.
Event — an immutable record that something happened (OrderPlaced), published for any interested consumer.
Broker — the infrastructure (Kafka, a message queue) that stores events and delivers them to subscribers.
history: The idea that structure should limit dependencies is old. Edsger Dijkstra's THE multiprogramming system (1968) was built as strict layers, each using only the one below, so that each layer could be tested and proved correct in turn. David Parnas wrote in the 1970s about information hiding and the "uses" relation between modules, which is the formal basis for all dependency rules. In 1964 Doug McIlroy wrote a memo suggesting that programs be connected "like garden hose" by passing data from one to another; Ken Thompson added pipes to Unix in 1973, and the shell syntax with the vertical bar made pipes-and-filters an everyday tool.
Styles became a named field with Perry and Wolf's "Foundations for the Study of Software Architecture" (1992) and the book by Mary Shaw and David Garlan, "Software Architecture: Perspectives on an Emerging Discipline" (1996), which catalogued pipes and filters, layers, repositories, event systems and others. Roy Fielding's 2000 doctoral thesis defined REST as a style, a set of constraints on a network architecture, and showed how constraints give properties such as scalability. Martin Fowler's "Patterns of Enterprise Application Architecture" (2002) described layering for business software, and Hohpe and Woolf's "Enterprise Integration Patterns" (2003) did the same for messaging.
Alistair Cockburn described hexagonal architecture (ports and adapters) in the mid-1990s and published it in 2005; its goal was to let an application be driven equally by users, tests, batch programs or other systems. Jeffrey Palermo's onion architecture (2008) and Robert C. Martin's clean architecture (blog 2012, book 2017) are close variants: concentric rings, with source dependencies pointing inward. In the 2010s, event-driven designs grew with Kafka, microservices and cloud messaging. Fowler's 2017 note "What do you mean by Event-Driven?" separated four different things that share the name: event notification, event-carried state transfer, event sourcing and CQRS.
how: All styles work by restricting a graph. Take the modules of a system as nodes and "uses" relations as edges. Without a style, any edge is allowed. A style says which edges are legal, and a build tool or a test can check the rule automatically.
Layered style (the swe concept: the module and dependency view). Put modules in a stack, for example presentation, application, domain, persistence. A module may call only the next layer down (strict layering) or any lower layer (relaxed). Upward communication goes through callbacks or events, never direct calls. What this buys: each layer can be understood by what is below it, and a layer can be replaced if its interface stays the same. What it costs: a request must pass through every layer, even when a layer adds nothing ("pass-through" code), and the persistence layer at the bottom becomes the centre of the design. The business rules, which change for business reasons, end up depending on database details, which change for technical reasons.
Hexagonal style reverses that dependency. The core contains the domain rules and defines ports, interfaces written in the language of the core: "find campaign by id", "publish bid decision". Adapters sit outside and implement ports: a PostgreSQL adapter implements the repository port, a REST controller calls the core's inbound port, a Kafka consumer calls the same port. Dependencies point inward: adapters know the core, the core never knows adapters. This is the dependency inversion principle (the designpatterns concept) at system scale, and each adapter is the Adapter pattern: it converts one interface (SQL rows, HTTP JSON) to the shape the core expects. The main benefit is testing: you can run the entire core in memory with fake adapters, in milliseconds, without a database. A second benefit is replaceability: change from PostgreSQL to DynamoDB by writing one adapter. The cost is extra interfaces and mapping code between the core's objects and the outside representation, which is waste in a simple CRUD application.
```python
from typing import Protocol

class BudgetRepo(Protocol):          # port, owned by the core
    def left(self, campaign_id: str) -> float: ...

def should_bid(repo: BudgetRepo, campaign_id: str,
               price: float) -> bool:
    return repo.left(campaign_id) >= price   # pure business rule

class FakeRepo:                      # adapter used in tests
    def __init__(self, data): self.data = data
    def left(self, campaign_id): return self.data[campaign_id]

assert should_bid(FakeRepo({"c1": 5.0}), "c1", 3.0)
```
The production adapter would implement `left` with a SQL query or a cache lookup; `should_bid` never changes.
Pipes-and-filters (the ipc concept). Each filter reads a stream, transforms it and writes a stream. Filters share no state and know nothing about each other; the only contract is the data format on the stream. Pipes connect them, and a pipe has a buffer: if the buffer is full, the writer blocks, so a slow filter automatically slows the fast ones (back-pressure) and memory stays bounded. This is why the Unix pipeline `grep | sort | uniq -c` can process a file much larger than memory, and the filters run in parallel as separate processes. The same shape appears in compilers (lexer, parser, optimizer, code generator), image-processing chains, ETL jobs, and in stream processors such as Kafka Streams and Flink, where each operator is a filter and each topic or shuffle is a pipe.
Event-driven style. A producer publishes an event to a broker, and zero or more consumers react. The producer does not know who listens, so adding a consumer does not change the producer. The three common shapes are: event notification (the event says "order 17 was placed" and consumers call back for details), event-carried state transfer (the event contains the data, so consumers keep their own copy and need no call back), and event sourcing (the events are the source of truth for state). The effects of this style: components are decoupled in time (a consumer may be down and catch up later), the flow of control is no longer visible in the code, and consistency becomes eventual. The style needs infrastructure with durable, ordered logs, which is what Kafka offers.
The styles combine. A typical service has a hexagonal core, whose inbound adapters are an HTTP controller and a Kafka consumer, whose outbound adapters are a database and an event publisher, and whose internal structure is layered in a loose way. What the styles share is one move: decide which direction information may flow and enforce it.

example: Pipes-and-filters first, since you can try it now. To find the five client addresses with the most server errors in a web log:
```bash
cat access.log | grep ' 500 ' | cut -d' ' -f1 \
  | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -5
```
There are six filters: `grep` keeps error lines, `cut` extracts the address, `sort` groups equal addresses, `uniq -c` counts them, a second `sort` ranks them, `head` takes the top five. None knows the others exist. To change the question (for status 404 instead) you change one argument. To count unique addresses per hour, you insert another filter. On a 50 GB log, the first three filters stream and use a small constant memory; only `sort` needs temporary files, and the processes run in parallel on different cores.
Now an event-driven change. A checkout service must, after an order, charge the card, reserve stock, send an email, and update analytics. With direct calls, checkout depends on four services. If each has 99.9% availability and checkout calls them synchronously, the chance that all four are up is about 0.999⁴ ≈ 0.996, so roughly 35 hours of failed checkouts a year instead of 8.8 for one service. Adding a fifth action means changing and redeploying checkout. With events, checkout writes `OrderPlaced` to a durable log and returns. Each of the four consumers reads at its own pace. If the email service is down for an hour, the events wait, and it catches up. A fifth consumer is added without touching checkout. The cost is that the customer may see "order received" before the email is sent, and an error in a consumer is found by monitoring, not by an exception in the checkout call.
Finally, hexagonal in a concrete refactoring. A bid service has a function that reads budgets with SQL inside the decision logic. Testing a decision takes 2 seconds, since it needs a database container. After extracting the `BudgetRepo` port, the 40 decision tests run with the in-memory fake in 30 milliseconds in total, and a production Redis cache adapter can be added without touching the rule. The only new code is the interface (3 lines) and one adapter class.
intuition: Decide where the doors are, and keep the rooms behind them free to change.
A large system fails when changes ripple. The style puts walls where ripples should stop and doors where information should pass. Layers say "you may look downward only". Hexagonal says "the business never looks outward; the world plugs into sockets". Pipes say "talk only through the stream". Events say "announce, do not ask". In each case the rule is cheap to follow and easy to check, and it keeps the number of things one part must know about small. That is the same logic as in a country with clear borders: you can change the rules inside without every neighbour being involved.
mistakes: People often think layers mean one deployable per layer. In fact layering is about code dependencies inside a program or service. Splitting layers across the network (a separate "data service" for every table) adds latency and failure modes without adding structure.
People often think hexagonal or clean architecture means lots of folders and interfaces for everything. In fact the benefit is isolation of the part that changes for business reasons. For a small CRUD service with little logic, an interface in front of every class is waste. Apply it where the core has real rules.
People often think event-driven means asynchronous and therefore faster. In fact it decouples components in time and knowledge, but the end-to-end latency can be longer, and a request-response question ("is this card valid?") still needs a synchronous answer. Using events where you need an answer makes code harder to follow.
People often think a style is chosen once for the whole company. In fact styles are chosen per system or per boundary: a service can be hexagonal inside, event-driven between services, and have a batch pipeline for analytics. Mixing is normal; what matters is that each boundary has a clear rule.
People often think the dependency rule is a convention that people will follow. In fact rules decay without enforcement. Teams add automated checks (ArchUnit in Java, import-linter in Python, dependency-cruiser in JavaScript, or package visibility) so that a forbidden import fails the build.
friction: Every style has a price in indirection. Layers give pass-through code and can force a change in all layers for one new field. Hexagonal needs mapping between internal and external objects, and the number of interfaces grows. Pipes-and-filters needs a common data format, and filters cannot easily share state or do multi-stream joins; error handling in a pipeline (what to do with a bad record) is hard to put in the filter model. Event-driven systems lose the explicit call graph, so to answer "what happens when an order is placed?" you must search for subscribers, and the answer is spread over many services.
Event-driven designs also bring distributed-systems issues: ordering (only within a partition), duplicates (consumers must be idempotent), schema evolution (old events live forever), poison messages, and eventual consistency visible to users. Debugging needs tracing with correlation ids. The infrastructure itself becomes a critical dependency that needs operation and capacity planning.
Finally, styles are not a substitute for fit. A wrong style can force a system into a shape that does not match its problem: a latency-critical path inside strict layers, or a simple script wrapped in ports and adapters. And styles drift: after two years, a layered system usually has shortcuts across layers that nobody remembers allowing.
tradeoffs: Choose layered style when the application is mostly data in, data out with thin logic, the team is small, and speed of building matters. Choose hexagonal when the domain has real rules that change often, when the system has several input channels (HTTP, queue, batch) or you expect to replace infrastructure, and when fast tests matter. You give up some directness and write more interfaces.
Choose synchronous request-response between services when the caller needs the answer now and a failure should be seen at once (payment authorization, price lookups). Choose events when several consumers need the same fact, when consumers can lag, or when you want to add consumers without changing the producer. You pay with eventual consistency, harder debugging and an operated broker; with synchronous calls you pay with coupled availability (the product of the availabilities) and latency (the sum).
Choose pipes-and-filters for data transformations that are naturally a sequence and stream-oriented (ETL, log processing, compilers). Choose a different style when steps need shared mutable state, random access to earlier data, or interactive control flow.
Choose a modular monolith (one deployable, strict module boundaries enforced by tooling) as the starting point for a team of fewer than about 30 engineers; choose microservices when independent deployment and scaling per team is the actual bottleneck. The style (module boundaries) is the same; the deployment unit is the choice, and moving from the first to the second is easy only if the boundaries were kept clean.

value: Every system has two kinds of requirements. Functional requirements say what the system does: "accept a bid request and return a bid". Quality attributes say how well it does it: how fast, how reliable, how secure, how cheap to change, how much it costs to run. Functional requirements can usually be met by many different designs. Quality attributes decide which design is acceptable, and they are the reason architecture exists as a separate activity. The same feature list built as a single process, as a replicated cluster, or as event-driven microservices gives very different latency, availability and cost.
The difficulty is that goals like "fast", "scalable" and "reliable" are empty words until they are made measurable, and that quality attributes pull against each other. Caching makes reads faster and makes data staler. Replication improves availability and makes consistency harder. Encryption adds security and latency. Every tactic buys one quality by spending another. The discipline of quality attributes is to write each goal as a testable scenario with a number, choose tactics that meet it, and say out loud what each tactic costs.
The layers below supply the tools. Software engineering principles (L3) say that architecture is judged on qualities such as modifiability, not only on features. Queueing theory and Little's law (L4) turn a performance goal into arithmetic: how many requests are in flight, and why latency explodes as utilization approaches 100%. The CAP theorem (L2) is the best-known single trade-off: during a network partition, a system must choose between consistency and availability. Above, microservices are a bet that independent deployment and scaling are worth added complexity, and multi-tenancy is a collection of trade-offs between isolation, cost and noisy neighbours.
terms: Quality attribute — a measurable property of how a system behaves, such as latency, availability, security, modifiability or cost (also called non-functional requirement).
Quality attribute scenario — a short, testable statement with six parts: source, stimulus, artifact, environment, response and response measure.
Tactic — a design decision that improves one quality attribute, for example a cache for performance or a heartbeat for availability.
Trade-off point — a design decision that affects two or more quality attributes in opposite directions.
Sensitivity point — a design decision where a small change has a large effect on one quality attribute.
Utility tree — a tree that breaks "utility" into attributes, then refinements, then concrete scenarios, ranked by importance and difficulty.
ATAM — Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method, a structured review that checks an architecture against ranked scenarios.
SLO — service level objective, a numeric target for a service measure, such as 99.9% of requests under 200 ms in 30 days.
Architecture decision record (ADR) — a short document that records one decision, its context, the options and the consequences.
history: Software architecture became a field in the 1990s (Perry and Wolf 1992, Shaw and Garlan 1996), and quality attributes were part of it from the start: the architect's job was to meet the requirements that features do not show. At the Software Engineering Institute (SEI) of Carnegie Mellon, Len Bass, Paul Clements and Rick Kazman wrote "Software Architecture in Practice" (first edition 1998; fourth edition 2021). They organized the knowledge as attributes, scenarios and tactics: for each attribute (availability, performance, modifiability, security, testability, usability and later others) a catalogue of design moves.
The same group created the Architecture Tradeoff Analysis Method (ATAM) around 1998 to 2000 (Kazman, Klein and Clements, SEI technical report 2000). It was a response to reviews that were either vague ("looks fine") or only about code. ATAM brought together the stakeholders and the architect for a one- to two-day workshop that ends with a list of risks and trade-off points.
Standards followed: ISO/IEC 9126 (1991) and its replacement ISO/IEC 25010 (2011) define a product quality model with characteristics such as performance efficiency, reliability, security and maintainability; a 2023 revision updated the list, adding safety. In the 2010s, the practice moved into operations and agile teams. Google's SRE book (2016) made service level objectives and error budgets the daily form of quality targets. Michael Nygard's 2011 post proposed lightweight architecture decision records, and "Building Evolutionary Architectures" (Ford, Parsons and Kua, 2017) proposed fitness functions, automated tests of quality attributes that run in the delivery pipeline.
how: The method has four moves: state the quality goals as scenarios, rank them, choose tactics, and analyze the trade-offs.
Step 1 is to write scenarios. A scenario has six parts. Source: who or what causes the event (a user, a failing disk). Stimulus: the event (a burst of 200,000 requests per second). Artifact: the part of the system affected (the bidder). Environment: the state at that time (normal operation, peak hour, degraded mode). Response: what the system does. Response measure: the number that decides success. For example: "During the evening peak, when the primary database node fails (stimulus), the bidder (artifact) continues to serve bids from replicas (response) with fewer than 0.1% errors and p99 latency under 80 ms, recovering full redundancy within 10 minutes (measure)." The response measure is the key: it turns "highly available" into something a test or a monitor can check. Without a number, two engineers will build for different goals and both claim success.
Step 2 is to rank. You cannot maximize everything, so a utility tree lists the attributes as roots, each divided into specific concerns, ending with scenarios. Each leaf scenario gets two ratings, such as high, medium or low: how important it is to the business, and how hard it is to achieve. The scenarios that are high in both are the ones that drive the architecture. A system usually has three to five of them, and the rest are met by ordinary good practice. Ranking is where stakeholders disagree, and it is better for that to happen before the design than after launch.
Step 3 is to choose tactics. For each driving scenario, pick from the known tactics of the attribute. For availability: detect faults (heartbeats and timeouts, as in the failure detection concept), recover (redundant replicas, failover, retry with backoff, rollback), and prevent (graceful degradation, removing single points of failure). For performance: control demand (rate limits, shedding load, caching) and manage resources (more servers, concurrency, queues, partitioning). For modifiability: reduce coupling (interfaces, hexagonal boundaries, events), increase cohesion, and defer binding (configuration, plugins). For security: authenticate, authorize, encrypt, audit, limit exposure. Each tactic has side effects, and this is where the trade-offs live.
Step 4 is to analyze. Queueing theory supplies quick checks for performance scenarios (the queueing concept). Little's law says that the average number in a system equals the arrival rate times the average time in the system, \( L = \lambda W \). With 50,000 requests per second at 40 ms, 2,000 requests are in flight, which sets the number of threads, connections and memory. The second result is about utilization. For a simple single-server queue with random arrivals (the M/M/1 model), the mean time in system is \( W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda} = \frac{S}{1-\rho} \), where S is the service time and \( \rho \) is utilization. If a request takes 10 ms of service, then at 50% utilization W = 20 ms, at 90% it is 100 ms, at 95% 200 ms and at 99% 1,000 ms. Latency does not rise gently; it rises like \( 1/(1-\rho) \). So a performance scenario with a tail latency measure forces a utilization ceiling, often 60% to 70% for latency-critical services, and that ceiling is a cost decision: more headroom means more servers.
For availability, arithmetic is also simple. Components in series multiply: three services in a request path each with 99.9% availability give \( 0.999^3 \approx 99.7\% \). Redundant components in parallel multiply the failure probabilities: two independent 99.9% replicas fail together with probability \( 0.001^2 = 10^{-6} \), i.e. 99.9999%. The independence assumption is the weak point: shared deployments, shared configuration and shared dependencies fail together, which is why real systems rarely reach the theoretical number. CAP is the same reasoning in a distributed setting: if a partition separates replicas, you either refuse some requests (consistency) or serve possibly stale data (availability).
Finally record the outcome. ATAM names four kinds of finding: risks (decisions that may cause a scenario to fail), non-risks (decisions that are safe), sensitivity points (decisions that strongly affect one attribute) and trade-off points (decisions that affect several attributes in opposite directions). Write the decisions as ADRs and the scenarios as automated checks (load tests, chaos experiments, dependency-rule tests) so that the architecture can be re-evaluated when the context changes.

example: Take a bidding service in an ad platform. The business says: "It must be fast, never overspend a budget, and always be up." We turn it into three scenarios.
Scenario P (performance): at peak, 200,000 bid requests per second arrive; 99% of responses must leave within 80 ms. Scenario C (correctness): the spend of a campaign must never exceed its daily budget by more than 0.1%. Scenario A (availability): a single node or zone failure must cause fewer than 0.1% failed bids and no manual action.
Check P with arithmetic. Each request needs about 5 ms of CPU. One 16-core server then handles 16 / 0.005 = 3,200 requests per second at 100% utilization. For tail latency we plan for 60% utilization, so 1,920 per second per server. For 200,000 requests per second we need 200,000 / 1,920 ≈ 105 servers. Little's law gives 200,000 × 0.04 s ≈ 8,000 requests in flight at an average of 40 ms, so about 76 per server, which sets the thread and connection limits. If we ran the servers at 90% utilization the fleet would be 70 servers, but the queueing factor 1/(1−ρ) says the mean wait grows from 2.5 times the service time to 10 times, putting the 80 ms target at risk. Cheaper servers, worse tail.
Now the trade-off between P and C. To meet P we cannot read the budget from the database on every bid (about 2 ms and one hot row), so we apply the caching tactic: each bidder keeps a local copy of the remaining budget and refreshes it every second. The cost is staleness. During one refresh interval, bidders cannot see each other's spending. If a campaign could win up to 500 per second at full speed, a stale budget lets it overshoot by up to 500 × 1 s = 500 in the worst case, which breaks C for a campaign with a budget of 10,000 (5%). So the design changes: each of the 100 bidders receives an allowance of 1/100 of the remaining budget and may spend only that between refreshes. Total spend can no longer exceed the remaining budget, and the price is under-delivery: a bidder that sees more traffic than its share stalls early, and a small campaign (a budget of 1,000 split 100 ways is 10 per bidder) may under-spend. This is a trade-off point: the refresh interval and the slice size move performance and correctness in opposite directions. An ADR records the context, the two scenarios, the choice (1 s, 1/100), and the consequence "small campaigns under-deliver", so a later engineer knows why.
Check A. Run three zones, each sized for 50% of the peak, so that losing one zone leaves two zones with 100% of the peak capacity. A load balancer health check every 5 s that needs two failures means up to 10 s of failed requests to the dead zone, which holds one third of the traffic. At 200,000 requests per second that is about 200,000 / 3 × 10 ≈ 670,000 failed bids. If the platform handled 200,000 bids per second all day (17 billion bids), that is about 0.004% of the day's traffic, well inside the 0.1% target. In a real day the failure may fall inside a busy minute, so the sharper check is the share of failures in the affected hour: 670,000 of 720 million bids in that hour is 0.09%, which just meets the target. The numbers show that the health-check interval is a sensitivity point, and that a 10 s detection time is the largest value the scenario can accept.
intuition: A quality attribute is a promise with a number; a trade-off is the price of that promise in some other currency.
Architecture is the art of spending a limited budget of complexity, money and time. Each tactic is a purchase: it buys a quality and pays with another. Making the promise measurable is what allows you to compare purchases, and making the price explicit is what keeps the decision honest. The reason a structured method works is not magic; it forces the conversation that otherwise happens at the first incident.
mistakes: People often think quality attributes are "non-functional" extras added at the end. In fact they decide the structure: a system that must handle 200,000 requests per second, or be correct across regions, cannot be reached from a simple design by adding code later. Changing the architecture after launch costs far more than choosing it before.
People often think scenarios like "the system should be scalable" are enough. In fact an adjective is not a requirement. Scalable to what load, in what time, at what cost? Without a response measure, you cannot design to it or test it.
People often think more of every quality is always possible with good engineering. In fact many qualities conflict by nature: low latency and strong consistency across regions are limited by the speed of light; strong security adds friction; maximum flexibility costs performance. A design that claims to maximize all of them has not made its choices visible.
People often think availability of 99.99% is just a bit better than 99.9%. In fact it is ten times less downtime: 52.6 minutes per year against 8.76 hours. Each extra nine usually costs an order of magnitude more effort (redundancy across zones, automated failover, no manual deploys), so choose the nines the business needs.
People often think the architecture review is a one-time gate. In fact quality attributes change with load, team and business. Scenarios should be checked repeatedly, with fitness functions in the pipeline, load tests and SLO monitors.
friction: The first problem is getting honest numbers. Stakeholders ask for "five nines" without knowing its cost, and engineers do not know the real peak or growth. Scenarios based on guesses give false confidence, so mark each number as measured or assumed, and revisit it after launch. The second problem is that qualities interact in ways that tactics lists do not show. Retries improve availability and create retry storms; caching reduces load and can cause stampedes; microservices improve modifiability and add network latency and a larger failure surface.
Measurement is hard. Some qualities (latency, throughput) are easy to measure; others (modifiability, usability, "operability") are not, and teams substitute proxies such as lead time or the number of files touched per change. Many methods, ATAM included, need senior people for days and are skipped under schedule pressure. Lighter forms (a one-hour scenario workshop, ADRs, an SLO review) keep most of the value.
There is also a human issue. Quality attributes belong to different people: operations cares about availability, security teams about risk, product about speed of change, finance about cost. A trade-off is political as well as technical, and the architect must make the costs visible to those who decide. Finally, an architecture that satisfies today's scenarios may fail tomorrow's. Keeping the option to change (modular boundaries, reversible decisions) has its own cost, and it is a judgment about which future is likely.
tradeoffs: Choose to design to explicit scenarios and SLOs when the system is large, shared across teams, or customer-facing, because the numbers prevent argument and drive monitoring. Choose a lightweight approach (a short list of five scenarios and a few ADRs) for a small team and a young product. You give up some precision with the second and some speed with the first.
Choose consistency over availability (CP) when wrong answers are costly: payments, budgets, inventory. Choose availability over consistency (AP) when stale answers are cheap and downtime is expensive: feeds, recommendations, caches, analytics. Whichever you choose, you give up something that a part of the business wanted, so name it.
Choose more headroom (60% to 70% utilization) for latency-critical paths, and higher utilization (80% to 90%) for batch or background work where the queue delay does not matter. The price of headroom is hardware cost, roughly 1.5 to 2 times; the price of tight packing is tail latency and no room for failures or traffic bursts.
Choose to build for the next ten times of scale when growth is very likely and re-architecture would be expensive; choose to build for the next two to three times and plan the next step when growth is uncertain. Over-building for scale is a common waste, and under-building means a rewrite, so look at what is hard to change later (data model, public API, partitioning scheme) and spend there first.
interview: How do you turn "the system must be highly available" into something you can design and test? || Write a scenario: source, stimulus (zone failure at peak), artifact, environment, response (failover to remaining zones without operator action) and measure (error rate under 0.1% for under 30 seconds). Compute the capacity needed to survive the failure, define the SLO and error budget, and verify the scenario with a failure injection test.
Give an example of a trade-off point and how you would record it. || Caching budget data in the bidder: improves latency, reduces database load, but allows overspend by the staleness window times the spend rate. I would record it in an ADR with the two scenarios it affects, the options considered, the chosen refresh interval and slice size, the quantified risk and the monitoring that detects a violation.
Why does latency rise sharply near full utilization? || In a queue with random arrivals, the waiting time scales as 1/(1−ρ). At 50% utilization the time in the system is twice the service time; at 90% it is ten times; at 99% it is a hundred times. Small bursts create queues that take a long time to drain, so plan for 60% to 70% on latency-sensitive services.
Three services in a request path each offer 99.95%. What do you promise for the whole path and how can you improve it? || About 0.9995³ ≈ 99.85% if failures are independent. Improve by removing a service from the synchronous path (async, cache, fallback with degraded results), adding redundancy at each hop, and bounding retries and timeouts so that failures do not spread. Promise a bit less than the arithmetic, given shared causes.
How do you keep architecture decisions alive after the review? || Capture them in ADRs with context and consequences, turn scenarios into automated checks (load tests, fitness functions, SLO alerts), and revisit the ranked scenarios on a schedule or when a trigger occurs (growth beyond a threshold, new compliance rule, new region).
practice: Start every design discussion by writing three to five scenarios with numbers, ranked by importance and difficulty. Ask for each number's source: measured, contractual or guessed. Do the napkin math before choosing tactics: requests per second, in-flight requests by Little's law, utilization ceiling, availability by series and parallel combination, and cost per unit. Many designs can be rejected in ten minutes by arithmetic.
When you pick a tactic, write its price next to it. Keep a table of decisions with columns: scenario, tactic, quality improved, quality reduced, how we will know if it fails. Use ADRs of half a page each, stored in the repository next to the code, with status (proposed, accepted, superseded). Revisit decisions that are marked as trade-off points when load or business priorities change.
Put the qualities into the delivery process. Define SLOs and error budgets for availability and latency; run load tests for the performance scenario before each release; add architecture tests for modifiability rules; schedule failure injection for availability scenarios. For a review, use a lightweight ATAM: a half-day with the architect, a product owner, an operations engineer and a security engineer, producing the ranked scenarios and a list of risks. To learn more, read "Software Architecture in Practice" for the catalogue of tactics, the Google SRE book for SLOs, and "Designing Data-Intensive Applications" for the reliability and consistency trade-offs in data systems.

value: Accessibility means that people can use a product whatever their abilities and devices. Some people cannot see the screen and listen to a screen reader. Some cannot use a mouse and navigate with a keyboard, a switch or their voice. Some are colour blind, have low vision, are deaf, have tremors, or have a cognitive condition that makes cluttered or time-limited interfaces hard. Others are temporarily limited: a broken arm, a bright sunny street, a noisy train. Roughly one in six people worldwide lives with a significant disability (World Health Organization estimate, 2022), so this is not a rare case.
The technical idea is simple. Assistive technology cannot "look at pixels" the way a person does. It needs a structured description of the interface: what each element is (button, link, heading, text field), what it is called, what state it is in (checked, expanded, disabled), and how elements relate. The browser or the operating system builds this description as the accessibility tree and exposes it through platform APIs. If the developer uses the right native elements, the tree is correct for free. If the developer builds controls out of generic boxes, the tree is empty or wrong, and the interface is invisible to a screen reader even though it looks perfect.
The layers below set the targets and the data structure. Human vision and hearing (L5) fix the contrast ratios, text sizes and caption rules, which come from the measured limits of eyes and ears. Working memory and attention (L5) explain rules about time limits, consistent layout and avoiding repeated data entry. Trees (L3) are the structure assistive technology reads. The layers above depend on it: the browser builds the tree from HTML and ARIA, and frameworks such as React must keep roles, names and focus correct when the page changes. Law now reinforces it: the European Accessibility Act has applied since 28 June 2025, and US rules and lawsuits refer to WCAG.
terms: Accessibility tree — a tree of objects, derived from the page, that exposes each element's role, name, state and relations to assistive technology.
Role — what kind of thing an element is (button, link, heading, checkbox, dialog), which tells the user what actions are possible.
Accessible name — the text a screen reader announces for an element, taken from its label, content or an ARIA attribute.
Screen reader — software that reads the interface aloud or to a braille display (NVDA, JAWS, VoiceOver, TalkBack, Narrator).
WCAG — Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, the W3C standard with testable success criteria at levels A, AA and AAA; AA is the usual legal target.
ARIA — Accessible Rich Internet Applications, attributes that add roles, names and states to elements where HTML cannot.
Focus — the element that currently receives keyboard input; the focus order is the sequence in which Tab visits elements.
Contrast ratio — a number from 1 to 21 comparing the luminance of foreground and background colours.
Landmark — a region role (main, navigation, search) that lets screen reader users jump between page areas.
history: Accessibility on the web began with the Web Accessibility Initiative (WAI) of the W3C, started in 1997 with Tim Berners-Lee's statement that "the power of the Web is in its universality". WCAG 1.0 was published in 1999. WCAG 2.0 followed in 2008 with four principles, often remembered as POUR: content must be Perceivable, Operable, Understandable and Robust. WCAG 2.1 (2018) added rules for mobile, low vision and cognitive needs. WCAG 2.2 became a W3C Recommendation on 5 October 2023, adding criteria such as minimum target size and accessible authentication. A new major version, WCAG 3, is in draft.
In parallel, the problem of dynamic pages was addressed by WAI-ARIA, a W3C specification (1.0 in 2014, 1.2 in 2023) that lets a developer say, for a custom widget made of generic elements, "this is a tab list, this tab is selected". Platform APIs matured too: Microsoft Active Accessibility (1997), UI Automation (introduced with Windows Vista), Apple's NSAccessibility and later UIAccessibility on iOS, Android's AccessibilityService, and AT-SPI on Linux. Browsers map the same DOM to each of them.
Law and policy followed. In the US, Section 508 (revised 2017 to reference WCAG 2.0) and the ADA, applied by courts to websites, are the main drivers; in 2024 the Department of Justice adopted WCAG 2.1 AA as the standard for state and local government web content. In Europe, the standard EN 301 549 refers to WCAG, and the European Accessibility Act (Directive 2019/882) requires accessible e-commerce, banking, e-books and similar products and services from 28 June 2025. Despite this, the WebAIM Million survey of the top one million home pages found in 2026 that 95.9% had at least one detectable WCAG failure, up from 94.8% in 2025, with an average of 56 detected errors per page.
how: Start with how the tree is made. A browser parses HTML into the DOM, combines it with CSS, and for each element that matters computes an accessibility object. Four properties form the core. Role comes first from the HTML element (<button> gives role button, <a href> gives link, <h2> gives heading level 2, <input type=checkbox> gives checkbox) or from an explicit ARIA role. Name is computed by an algorithm (the Accessible Name and Description specification): for a form control it uses an associated <label>; for a button its text content; for an image its alt attribute; an aria-labelledby or aria-label can override these. State and properties come from native attributes (disabled, checked, required) or ARIA (aria-expanded, aria-selected, aria-invalid). Relations such as "this label belongs to this input" or "this text describes that field" link nodes. The tree is also pruned: elements hidden with display:none or aria-hidden are removed; purely decorative ones can be marked as presentational.
This tree is exposed through the platform accessibility API. A screen reader (NVDA on Windows) subscribes to events, reads the tree, and speaks "Submit order, button" when focus moves. Voice control uses the same names: if you say "click Submit order", the software looks for an element whose accessible name matches the words. Switch devices move focus through the tree one element at a time. So the same data supports blind, motor-impaired and voice users, and it also supports automated tests and search crawlers. This is the leverage of the design: describe the interface once, semantically, and many tools can work with it.
The practical rule follows: use native HTML elements, because they come with role, name, state, keyboard behaviour and focus handling already implemented. A <button> is focusable, responds to Enter and Space, appears in the tree as a button, and works with voice control. A <div onclick> has none of these. ARIA only changes the description, not the behaviour: if you write role="button" on a div, you must still add tabindex="0", key handlers for Enter and Space, and a visible focus style. The first rule of ARIA from the W3C is exactly this: if a native element does the job, use it, and "no ARIA is better than bad ARIA". The WebAIM data fits the warning: pages using ARIA averaged 59 detected errors against 42 for pages without it (partly because they are more complex).
Next is keyboard operation, which also stands behind most other assistive input. Every function must be reachable and operable by keyboard (WCAG 2.1.1), without traps (2.1.2). The focus order must follow the visual and logical order (2.4.3), and the focused element must be clearly visible (2.4.7); in WCAG 2.2 it must also not be completely hidden by sticky headers (2.4.11). When content changes dynamically, move focus deliberately: a modal dialog should take focus on open, keep it inside (a focus trap, the one allowed trap), and return it to the opening button on close. Live changes that are not caused by focus, such as a toast or a form error, are announced with live regions (aria-live="polite" or role="status"), which tell the screen reader to speak the new text.
Visual rules come from the limits of human vision (the perception concept). Contrast is computed from relative luminance, \( L = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B \), where R, G and B are the colour channels converted from sRGB to linear light, and the ratio is
\[ \text{contrast} = \frac{L_1 + 0.05}{L_2 + 0.05} \]
with \( L_1 \) the lighter colour. Level AA requires 4.5:1 for normal text, 3:1 for large text (18 pt or 14 pt bold) and 3:1 for user-interface parts and graphics (1.4.11). Information must not be conveyed by colour alone (1.4.1): an error needs an icon or text, not just a red border. Text must be resizable to 200% without loss, and the page must reflow at 320 CSS pixels width, equal to 400% zoom on a 1280 pixel screen (1.4.10). Touch targets should be at least 24 × 24 CSS pixels (2.5.8, new in 2.2). Media needs captions for speech (1.2.2) and text alternatives for images that carry meaning (1.1.1).
Cognitive rules come from limits of working memory and attention (the workingmem concept). Do not rely on remembering information from an earlier step: WCAG 2.2 adds "redundant entry" (3.3.7), saying that data the user has entered before must be auto-filled or selectable, and "accessible authentication" (3.3.8), which forbids requiring a cognitive test such as remembering a password or solving a puzzle without an alternative such as paste support or a password manager. Keep navigation and help in consistent places (3.2.3, 3.2.6), give enough time or let users extend limits (2.2.1), and write clear error messages that identify the field and say how to fix it (3.3.1, 3.3.3).

Version A looks the same on screen. In the accessibility tree it is a generic container with the text "Save", with no role and no focus stop. Tab skips it, so a keyboard user cannot reach it. A screen reader reading by line says "Save" as plain text, not as something to activate. Voice control "click Save" may or may not find it. Version B is a button, appears in the tab order, activates with Enter or Space, and is announced as "Save, button". The fix cost one tag name. To make Version A work you would have to add role="button", tabindex="0", a keydown handler for Enter and Space, a focus style and disabled-state handling, which is five changes to re-create what the browser gives for free.
Now a contrast calculation. Light grey text, #777777, on a white background. Convert each 8-bit channel to 0-1: 0x77 = 119, 119/255 = 0.4667. Linearize: \( ((0.4667 + 0.055)/1.055)^{2.4} \approx 0.1845 \). Since R = G = B, luminance is 0.1845. White has luminance 1. The ratio is (1 + 0.05) / (0.1845 + 0.05) = 1.05 / 0.2345 ≈ 4.48. That is below 4.5, so it fails AA by a hair. The slightly darker #767676 gives luminance 0.1812 and a ratio of 1.05 / 0.2312 ≈ 4.54, which passes. Common "light grey" placeholder colours such as #999999 give 2.85:1 and fail clearly. A one-digit change in a colour value moves a page from non-compliant to compliant, so check by tool and not by eye.
Finally a check of scale from the WebAIM Million 2026: low-contrast text appeared on 83.9% of home pages, missing image alternatives on 53.1%, missing form labels on 51%, empty links on 46.3% and empty buttons on 30.6%. These six kinds of failure account for 96% of all detected errors. All of them are simple to fix, and a linter in the build catches most. But automated tools find only part of the problem: a UK Government Digital Service test in 2017 found that the best tool detected about 40% of the planted issues. Whether an alt text is meaningful, or a focus order is sensible, needs a human with a keyboard and a screen reader.
intuition: A building has ramps, signs and elevator buttons so that every door can be used; the accessibility tree is the sign system for software.
Visual layout is only one way to present an interface. Underneath, the interface is a structure of things with meanings and actions. If that structure is described explicitly, then any renderer can show it: pixels for the eyes, speech for the blind, a scan sequence for a switch user. Accessibility is what happens when you treat the structure as the real interface and the pixels as one of its views. This is also why the work pays back: the same semantics help search engines, automated tests, translation tools, voice assistants and users in a noisy train station.
mistakes: People often think accessibility is only for blind users. In fact the majority of the criteria serve other groups: contrast and zoom for low vision and ageing eyes, captions for deaf and for people in noisy places, keyboard operation for motor impairments and power users, clear language and consistency for cognitive differences. Many are used by anyone at some time.
People often think ARIA makes a page accessible. In fact ARIA adds descriptions only and no behaviour; wrong ARIA misleads users (a button announced as a link, a menu that cannot be operated). Use native elements first, and add ARIA only for patterns HTML cannot express, following the ARIA Authoring Practices.
People often think passing an automated scan means accessible. In fact tools check things that can be decided by rules (missing alt, low contrast, missing labels) and miss the rest: whether the labels make sense, whether a task can be completed with the keyboard, whether focus goes to the right place after a dialog opens. Test by hand with the keyboard and with a screen reader.
People often think accessibility is an expensive extra at the end. In fact fixing it after launch costs several times more than building it in, because it touches components and design systems. If built into the component library once (a correct button, dialog, tabs, form field), every product that uses it inherits the behaviour.
People often think a text-only or "accessible version" of the site is a good solution. In fact separate versions get less maintenance, fall out of date and treat users as second class. One site, built to the standard, is better for everyone.
friction: Standards leave room for judgement. WCAG success criteria are testable, but many require human decisions (is this alt text equivalent? is this error message helpful?), and two auditors can differ. WCAG conformance is also defined per page and per process, so a large product with changing content can never be "done". WCAG 2.x does not cover everything: it is weaker on cognitive accessibility and on new interaction types such as AR, VR and voice assistants, which is one reason WCAG 3 is being drafted with a different scoring model.
Implementation is fragile. Screen readers, browsers and operating systems differ in how they interpret ARIA; a pattern that works in NVDA with Firefox may fail in VoiceOver with Safari. Custom components such as date pickers, data grids, rich text editors and canvas-based apps (maps, charts, games, apps drawn with WebGL or Flutter canvas) are especially hard because the DOM gives no semantics, and they must rebuild a parallel accessible structure. Single-page applications must manage focus, titles and announcements when the route changes, because the browser no longer does it on page load. Sticky headers, cookie banners, chat widgets and ads from third parties often introduce failures that the product team does not control.
There is also an organisational problem. Accessibility is a quality attribute that belongs to design, engineering, content and QA, so it falls between teams. Without a named owner, tests in CI and a design system with accessible components, regressions return with every release. And automated overlays that promise one-line fixes ("accessibility widgets") have a poor record: they do not repair the underlying markup, can interfere with users' own assistive technology, and have been the subject of complaints and legal action.
tradeoffs: Choose native HTML elements and platform controls whenever one exists; choose a custom component with ARIA only when the pattern is missing in HTML (tabs, comboboxes, tree views), and then follow the ARIA Authoring Practices keyboard model exactly. You give up some visual freedom with native controls (styling a select is limited), and you pay engineering effort and testing in every browser and screen reader combination with custom ones.
Choose to target WCAG 2.2 AA as the baseline for any public product, since it is the level named by law and procurement in most places. Choose AAA criteria selectively (enhanced contrast of 7:1 for body text, plain-language summaries) where your audience needs them, since full AAA is not possible for all content. You give up some design choices (very light grey text, tiny controls) for AA, and more for AAA.
Choose automated checks in the pipeline (axe-core, Lighthouse, ESLint jsx-a11y, Storybook checks) for fast, broad, cheap coverage of the roughly 30% to 40% that tools can find. Choose manual tests (keyboard-only pass, screen reader pass with at least NVDA plus Chrome or Firefox and VoiceOver plus Safari, zoom and reflow, user testing with disabled people) for key flows before release. Automation finds regressions, humans find real barriers; you need both, and the manual passes cost days per major flow.
Choose to build accessibility into a shared design system when you have several products, so each fix reaches all of them; choose per-page remediation when you have one small product and no component library. The first has a higher setup cost and a much lower cost per feature.

value: A connection pool keeps a small set of already-open connections to a database (or any server) and lends one to each piece of work that needs it. The work borrows a connection, runs its queries, and gives it back. Nobody pays to open a new connection for each request, and the database never sees more connections than the pool allows.
Opening a connection is expensive in three ways. In time: a TCP handshake (one round trip), a TLS handshake (one or two round trips), then the database's own start-up and authentication (more round trips), which together take milliseconds inside one data centre and tens of milliseconds across regions, often more than the query itself. In server resources: a PostgreSQL connection is a separate operating system process, with its own memory (a few megabytes at least, and more when it sorts or caches), and the server must schedule hundreds of them. In limits: servers refuse connections beyond a maximum (PostgreSQL's default `max_connections` is 100). A web tier with 40 application instances, each wanting 20 connections, asks for 800.
The less obvious value is that a pool is also a limiter. A database does the most work per second when a modest number of queries run at once; too many in parallel lead to context switching, lock contention and cache thrashing, and throughput falls while latency climbs. A pool turns excess concurrency into waiting in an orderly queue at the client, which is cheaper than waiting inside the database. The layers below explain each part: TCP and TLS (L3) are the setup costs, processes (L3) explain why PostgreSQL connections are heavy, and queueing theory with Little's law (L4) tells you how big the pool should be. PostgreSQL deployments rely on PgBouncer for this, and serverless functions need a proxy pool such as RDS Proxy.
terms: Connection — a long-lived, authenticated session between a client and a database server, with its own state (settings, transaction, temporary tables).
Pool — a collection of open connections managed by the client library or by a proxy, from which requests borrow and to which they return connections.
Borrow and release — taking a connection from the pool for the duration of a unit of work, and returning it afterwards; also called checkout and checkin.
Pool size — the maximum number of connections the pool may hold open at once.
Connection timeout — how long a request waits for a free connection before it fails.
Pooler (proxy) — a separate server process, such as PgBouncer, that accepts many client connections and multiplexes them onto few real database connections.
Session, transaction and statement pooling — the three PgBouncer modes: a server connection is held for the whole client session, for one transaction, or for one statement.
Little's law — in a stable system, the average number of requests inside equals the arrival rate times the average time each one spends there.
Max lifetime — the age after which a pooled connection is closed and replaced, to avoid stale or leaked state.
history: Early web applications opened a database connection for each page request, in the style of CGI scripts: connect, query, disconnect. This was acceptable when sites were small. As traffic grew in the late 1990s, connection setup dominated, and application servers added pools: the Java world standardised on JDBC data sources (with pooling from the JDBC 2.0 optional package, around 1999), and Apache Commons DBCP, c3p0 and Tomcat's pool spread the pattern. Later pools such as HikariCP (2013, by Brett Wooldridge) focused on a tiny, fast code path, and became the default in Spring Boot 2.
For PostgreSQL the issue was sharper because of its process-per-connection design, inherited from the original architecture of the 1980s and 1990s, which favoured robustness over cheap connections. Marko Kreen wrote PgBouncer at Skype around 2007 as a lightweight pooler in front of PostgreSQL, and it became a standard component. Others followed: Pgpool-II, Odyssey (Yandex), PgCat, and Supavisor. PostgreSQL itself has improved: version 14 (2021) reduced the cost of having many idle connections in transaction snapshots. But the process model remains, so the advice to pool has stayed.
Cloud and serverless changed the shape of the problem again. A function-as-a-service platform can start hundreds of instances within a second, and each opens its own connection, which can exhaust the database in moments. In 2020 AWS released RDS Proxy as a managed pool for this case. At the same time, an influential performance argument spread through the HikariCP documentation: a database performs best with a small number of active connections, a point demonstrated by Oracle's Real-World Performance group, which cut a pool by about twenty times and saw response times fall sharply. Pool size became a topic of capacity planning, not a default value.
how: Begin with what a pool does at run time. At start-up it opens a minimum number of connections (or none, lazily). When code asks for a connection, the pool returns an idle one if available, marks it busy, and starts timing. If none is idle and the pool is below its maximum, it opens a new one (paying the setup cost once). If the pool is at its maximum, the request waits in a queue until a connection is released or the connection timeout expires, when it fails with an error. When the code releases the connection, the pool resets its state (rolls back an open transaction, clears session settings), and either hands it to the next waiter or marks it idle. Background tasks close connections that are idle too long or older than a maximum lifetime, and test connections to remove dead ones (a database restart or a firewall dropping idle flows leaves broken connections in the pool; a validation query or a lightweight check detects them).
Why is this a win? The setup cost (the tcp and tls concepts): a new connection needs a TCP three-way handshake (one round trip), a TLS 1.3 handshake (one more round trip; TLS 1.2 needs two), then the PostgreSQL start-up message, authentication (SCRAM-SHA-256 takes extra round trips and hashing work) and the creation of a server process (the process concept). With 0.5 ms round trips inside one zone, setup costs a few milliseconds; across regions at 70 ms it is several hundred milliseconds. A pooled connection is already warm: it has finished all of this, and the server process has its caches filled. A query that takes 1 ms on a warm connection can take 10 ms or more on a new one.
The server resources: a PostgreSQL backend process uses memory for its own structures, and each sort or hash can use up to `work_mem` (default 4 MB) per node. Thousands of connections therefore take gigabytes and cause heavy context switching. MySQL uses a thread per connection, which is lighter but still limited. Oracle and SQL Server have their own models. In all of them, there is an optimal number of concurrently executing queries, related to the number of CPU cores and the disks. Beyond it, extra concurrency adds contention, not throughput.
How big should the pool be? Use Little's law (the queueing concept). The number of connections busy at once equals the query arrival rate times the average time a connection is held: \( L = \lambda W \). A service running 2,000 queries per second with 5 ms per query holds on average 2,000 × 0.005 = 10 connections busy. A transaction that holds the connection for 20 ms while the application also calls an external API from inside it raises the figure to 40. Then add headroom because arrivals are bursty: with random arrivals, waiting appears long before utilization reaches 100%. The M/M/c queue model gives the numbers. A pool of 10 connections where each serves 200 queries per second (5 ms) can serve at most 2,000 per second. At 1,600 per second (80% busy) the mean wait for a connection is about 1 ms and 41% of requests wait at all. At 1,900 per second (95%) the mean wait is about 8 ms and 83% of requests wait; the tail is much worse. A rule of thumb: aim for 60% to 70% average utilization of the pool.
A second rule gives an upper bound from the database side. The PostgreSQL community and the HikariCP guide suggest a starting point of about \( 2 \times \text{cores} + \text{number of disks} \) active connections for the whole database, then adjust by measurement. A database with 16 cores should have, across all clients together, in the range of 30 to 50 active connections, not 500. All application instances share this budget: with 20 instances, the pool per instance is about 2, not 20. When the sum of client pools exceeds the budget, add a pooler.
A pooler such as PgBouncer sits between many clients and the database. Clients connect to PgBouncer (cheap: it is a single event-loop process that handles thousands of idle connections with little memory), and PgBouncer opens a limited number of real connections to PostgreSQL. In session mode, a server connection is bound to a client for its whole session; this saves setup cost but does not reduce the number of busy connections. In transaction mode (the one most used for scale), the server connection is bound only between BEGIN and COMMIT, then returned to the pool. 800 clients that are each in a transaction 5% of the time need only about 40 server connections. In statement mode, the connection is released after every statement, which prohibits multi-statement transactions. The price of transaction mode is that session state does not follow the client: `SET` commands, session-level advisory locks, `LISTEN`, temporary tables and, historically, named prepared statements break because the next transaction may run on a different server connection. PgBouncer 1.21 (October 2023) added support for protocol-level prepared statements in transaction mode through the `max_prepared_statements` setting.
Here is a typical application-side configuration, in Python with SQLAlchemy:
```python
from sqlalchemy import create_engine

engine = create_engine(
    "postgresql+psycopg://app@db.internal/orders",
    pool_size=8,          # connections kept open per process
    max_overflow=0,       # never exceed pool_size
    pool_timeout=2,       # seconds to wait before failing
    pool_recycle=1800,    # replace connections after 30 minutes
    pool_pre_ping=True,   # test a connection before lending it
)
```
Short timeouts and no overflow matter: when the pool is exhausted, you want a fast, visible failure for the excess requests, not a growing queue of waiting threads.

example: Take a service in front of PostgreSQL. It has 40 application pods, each with a pool of 20 connections, and the database allows 500 connections on a 16-core machine. That is 800 connections requested against 500 allowed, so the 26th pod fails to start with "too many clients". Raising `max_connections` to 1,000 would let them in, but would use about 1,000 × 10 MB = 10 GB for backends alone, and the database would spend its time switching between processes.
Look at the real load instead. The service receives 4,000 requests per second. Each request makes one transaction that holds a connection for about 6 ms. By Little's law, the average number of busy connections is 4,000 × 0.006 = 24. At peak, with a 2 times burst, about 48. That is right in the range of 2 × 16 + a few disks, about 35 to 50. So the database needs about 50 server connections, not 800. The 800 client connections are idle most of the time: on average 24 of them are inside a transaction (3%).
The fix: place PgBouncer in transaction mode with `default_pool_size = 50` and `max_client_conn = 2000`. Pods keep their pool of 20 (they are cheap now), but real server connections stay at 50. When 60 transactions are in flight at the same moment, the extra 10 wait in PgBouncer's queue for a few milliseconds instead of competing for CPU in PostgreSQL. Estimated queue effect: at 48 busy of 50, utilization is 96%, so waiting is noticeable at the burst peak, which is why you size to about 60 to 70 average, or set the pool to 60 for burst. Always check with a load test.
Finally a failure case. A downstream slowdown increases each transaction from 6 ms to 60 ms. Now the same 4,000 per second needs 240 busy connections, but the pool has 50. Requests queue in the pooler and in each application's pool, latency grows, timeouts occur, clients retry, and retries add more load: a classic retry storm. The defence is to fail fast (a short pool timeout), cap retries, and limit the work admitted, so the system returns errors early instead of collapsing.
intuition: A pool is a set of taxis waiting at a stand: the engine is already running, there are only so many cars, and the queue forms at the stand and not in the middle of the road.
The connection is a scarce resource with a high start-up cost. When something is expensive to create and cheap to reuse, you keep a stock of them. When something is limited, you put the queue where waiting is cheapest. The cheapest place to wait is outside the database, in the client or in a light proxy, where a waiting request costs a few bytes, not a process and a CPU slot. The limit also protects the server: a machine that is asked to do 500 things at once does each one slowly, and one that does 40 at a time and queues the rest finishes the same work sooner.
mistakes: People often think a bigger pool means more throughput. In fact beyond roughly the number the database can run efficiently in parallel (a small multiple of its cores), more connections lower throughput and raise latency, because of contention for CPU, locks and memory bandwidth. Start small and increase only if the database is not saturated and requests wait for connections.
People often think a pool size is a per-application setting. In fact the limit belongs to the database, and all clients share it. Total pool size = pool per instance × instances × services. Autoscaling the application tier from 10 to 100 instances multiplies the connections by ten without anyone changing a setting.
People often think connection pooling solves slow queries. In fact it removes setup cost and limits concurrency, but a slow query holds its connection longer, so it reduces pool capacity. Fix the query, add timeouts, and keep transactions short.
People often think a transaction-mode pooler is a drop-in. In fact it breaks session-based features: `SET`, session advisory locks, `LISTEN/NOTIFY`, temporary tables, and some uses of prepared statements. Check what your framework relies on before switching, and use session mode for those clients.
friction: The pool hides but does not remove the limit. When demand exceeds capacity, requests wait, and waiting becomes failure through timeouts, and failures often become retries. Pool exhaustion is one of the most frequent causes of production incidents: one slow query, a leaked connection (code that borrows and does not release on an error path), or a long transaction can take the whole service down. Most pools offer leak detection (HikariCP's `leakDetectionThreshold`), but it must be switched on and monitored.
Pools also keep state that can be stale. After a database failover, or after a firewall silently drops idle connections, the pool holds dead connections, and the first requests fail until they are detected. Validation on borrow adds a round trip; max lifetime must be shorter than any network idle timeout; DNS changes during failover need pools to re-resolve. Prepared statements, session variables and temporary state need care when connections are reused, and in transaction-mode poolers they can silently move to another session.
tradeoffs: Choose an in-process pool (HikariCP, SQLAlchemy, pgx pool) when you have a few long-lived application instances and the total pool across instances stays under the database budget. Choose an external pooler (PgBouncer, RDS Proxy) when the number of clients is large or changes quickly (autoscaling, serverless, many microservices), or when you need to protect the database from connection storms. You give up simplicity and a little latency with the proxy; you gain a single place to cap connections.
Choose transaction pooling when your clients use plain transactions and you need the best multiplexing, since 800 clients can share 40 server connections. Choose session pooling when clients depend on session state (temporary tables, advisory locks, `LISTEN`), accepting that you save setup cost only and that the number of server connections equals the number of busy sessions. Statement mode is rarely right outside simple autocommit workloads.
Choose a small pool with a short timeout (say 2 seconds) when you prefer fast failure to protect latency and the database; callers can degrade or retry later. Choose a larger pool with a longer wait only for background jobs that can tolerate delay. The price of short timeouts is more visible errors during bursts, and the price of long waits is thread pile-up and slow recovery.
Choose to size pools by measurement and Little's law, starting from throughput × connection hold time, with headroom for 60% to 70% utilization. Choose a formula such as 2 × cores + disks only as a first guess for the database total. The deciding data is the database's CPU and wait profile under load, since adding connections stops helping when the database saturates.

To learn more, read the HikariCP "About Pool Sizing" page, the PgBouncer documentation on pool modes and the feature limits of each, and the PostgreSQL wiki page on handling many connections.
quiz: Why does a PostgreSQL connection cost more than a MySQL connection? || PostgreSQL forks a separate operating system process per connection, with its own memory and scheduling cost, while MySQL uses a lighter thread per connection.
A service runs 3,000 queries per second and each holds a connection for 4 ms. How many connections are busy on average? || By Little's law, 3,000 × 0.004 = 12 connections.
Why can reducing a pool from 200 to 40 connections speed a service up? || With too many active queries, the database spends time on contention and context switching, so throughput falls and latency rises. A smaller pool limits concurrency to what the database handles efficiently, and the extra requests wait cheaply at the client.
40 pods each have a pool of 25 connections. The database allows 600. What happens, and what is a fix? || The pods ask for 1,000 connections, which is more than 600, so some connections fail. value: Chaos engineering is the practice of causing failures on purpose, in a controlled way, to find out whether a system survives them. You kill a server, add 500 ms of delay to a dependency, cut a network link, or fill a disk, and you watch whether the user-facing behaviour stays within its target. If it does, you have evidence that the failover, timeout or retry works. If it does not, you have found a weakness during a planned experiment instead of during a 3 a.m. outage.
The reason is that distributed systems fail in ways nobody can fully predict by reading code. At scale, failure is routine: if each of 1,000 instances has a 0.1% chance to fail on a given day, the chance that at least one fails today is \( 1 - 0.999^{1000} \approx 63\% \). Every redundancy mechanism (replicas, retries, circuit breakers, failover scripts) exists to handle these failures, and every one of them is untested code that runs only when something is already wrong. Untested recovery paths often do not work: the standby has a stale configuration, the timeout is 30 seconds instead of 300 ms, the retries make the overload worse. Chaos engineering makes the recovery paths run regularly, while people are watching and ready.
It is different from ordinary testing in two ways. It is based on a hypothesis about steady-state behaviour ("checkout success stays above 99.9%"), so it tests the whole running system, including people and alerts, not one function. And it is run with a limited blast radius, which is the share of users or traffic that can be harmed, and with automatic abort conditions. The layers below give the vocabulary: distributed systems theory (L3) defines the failure models (crash, omission, partition, slowness); probability (L4) says how often failures happen and how to choose random targets; failure detection (L2) is the heartbeat and timeout machinery that experiments test; deterministic simulation testing (L2) is the same idea inside a simulator, where every failure can be replayed. The pipeline above it, CI/CD, uses fault-injection tests and scheduled game days.
terms: Steady state — a measurable description of normal behaviour from the user's point of view, such as orders per minute or the success rate of requests.
Hypothesis — the prediction that the steady state will hold during the injected fault ("the system survives losing one zone").
Fault injection — deliberately causing a failure: killing a process, adding latency, dropping packets, returning errors, or filling a disk.
Blast radius — the amount of users, traffic or infrastructure that an experiment can affect; kept small and increased step by step.
Abort condition — a metric threshold that stops the experiment automatically (for example the error rate above 1%).
Game day — a scheduled exercise where a team runs a failure scenario together, including the human response.
Resilience — the ability to keep serving users acceptably, perhaps in a degraded way, while parts fail.
Graceful degradation — dropping a non-essential feature (recommendations, personalization) to keep the essential function (checkout) working.
Control group — traffic or instances that receive no fault, compared with the experiment group to see the effect of the fault.
history: Practices of this kind are older than the name. Jesse Robbins, who held the title "Master of Disaster" at Amazon, ran GameDay exercises in the mid-2000s that took down parts of the infrastructure to test recovery. Google began its DiRT (Disaster Recovery Testing) exercises in 2006. In 2008 a database corruption stopped Netflix's DVD shipping for several days, and the company decided to move from its own data centres to Amazon Web Services and to design for failure of any single instance.
In 2010, Netflix engineers built Chaos Monkey, a service that randomly terminates virtual machines in production during working hours, so that every team builds services that tolerate the loss of an instance. The 2010 and 2011 Netflix technology blog posts described it, and in 2011 the "Simian Army" extended the idea: Latency Monkey for slow calls, Chaos Gorilla to take out a whole availability zone, and later Chaos Kong for a whole region. Chaos Monkey was released as open source in 2012. Netflix then wrote down a method: the "Principles of Chaos Engineering" (published at principlesofchaos.org around 2015) and an IEEE Software article by Basiri and colleagues (2016), which describe steady state, hypotheses, real-world events, production experiments, automation and small blast radius. In 2017 Netflix presented ChAP, the Chaos Automation Platform, which runs experiments on a small slice of production traffic against a control group.
Others followed. Gremlin, a company founded in 2016, offered fault injection as a service. Open-source tools such as Chaos Mesh (PingCAP) and LitmusChaos became projects of the Cloud Native Computing Foundation. Cloud providers added managed services: AWS Fault Injection Service and Azure Chaos Studio. Casey Rosenthal and Nora Jones wrote the book "Chaos Engineering" (O'Reilly, 2020). A parallel line of work tested databases and consensus systems under network partitions: Kyle Kingsbury's Jepsen analyses, from 2013, found correctness bugs in many well-known databases. Today chaos experiments are common in large technology firms and in regulated sectors such as banking, where resilience testing is also required by rules such as the EU's DORA regulation.
how: An experiment follows a fixed protocol, and the discipline is in the protocol, not in the tooling.
Step 1 is to define steady state. Pick one to three metrics that show whether users are served well, measured at the edge and not inside one service: request success rate, p99 latency, orders per minute, stream starts per second (Netflix's choice). Know their normal range and daily rhythm, since "the error rate rose 0.2%" means little without the baseline. Without a trustworthy steady state there is no experiment, only breakage.
Step 2 is to form a hypothesis from a known recovery mechanism, and name it. "If one of the three cache nodes dies, checkout latency stays under 300 ms at p99 because the client falls back to the database." "If the recommendation service takes 500 ms per call, checkout still completes, because the client times out at 100 ms and shows a default list." The hypothesis comes from the failure model of the distributed systems concept: crash of a process, loss of a node, slow responses (the hardest in practice, since a slow dependency holds resources), dropped or delayed messages, network partition, clock skew, resource exhaustion. Choosing which fault to try is a probability question: start with the failures that happen most often (instance loss, deploys, dependency slowness), then the rare and expensive ones (zone loss, region loss).
Step 3 is to set the blast radius and abort conditions. Start with the smallest scope that can show an effect: one instance, 1% of requests, one test account, or a staging environment. Define a rule that stops the experiment automatically if the steady-state metric crosses a threshold, and a manual stop button that works within seconds, since the point is to learn, not to cause an incident. Tell the people who may be paged. Many platforms (ChAP, Gremlin, Chaos Mesh with a guard job) implement the abort by watching metrics.
Step 4 is to inject the fault. Faults are injected at different levels. At the infrastructure level: terminate a virtual machine or container, drain a node, fail a zone. At the network level: Linux `tc netem` adds delay, loss and reordering, `iptables` drops packets to create a partition, and a fault-injecting proxy (Toxiproxy, Envoy or a service mesh fault filter) delays or aborts requests between two services. At the application level: return HTTP 500 for a share of calls, throw exceptions, exhaust a thread pool. At the resource level: stress CPU, fill memory or disk, change the clock. Kubernetes operators such as Chaos Mesh express these as declarative objects with a selector, an action and a duration.
Step 5 is to observe and compare. Watch the steady-state metric in the experiment group against the control group, together with the internal signals: queue depths, retry rates, circuit-breaker states, thread pools. Did the alert fire? Did it fire in time? Did the on-call person know what to do, and did the runbook match? These human results are often more valuable than the technical ones. If the hypothesis holds, increase the blast radius or the severity and repeat. If it fails, stop, fix the weakness (a timeout, a missing fallback, a wrong retry policy), and run the same experiment again to prove the fix.
Step 6 is to automate and repeat. A system changes with every deploy, so a pass last quarter proves little about today. Mature teams run a core set of experiments continuously (instance termination every day, as Chaos Monkey does) and put selected fault-injection tests into the delivery pipeline, so a change that removes a timeout fails the build. This is where chaos meets deterministic simulation testing: a simulator such as the one used by FoundationDB runs the whole system in a single process with a virtual clock and a seeded random generator, injects thousands of faults per simulated hour, and can replay any failing run exactly from its seed. Chaos experiments in a real environment find configuration and operational problems that a simulator does not model, but a failure may not be reproducible. The two approaches complement each other.

example: A checkout service calls a recommendations service on every request to show "you may also like". Checkout handles 1,000 requests per second, has a thread pool of 200 threads, and calls recommendations with the HTTP client's default timeout of 30 seconds, no fallback. Normally recommendations answer in 5 ms.
Hypothesis: "if recommendations become slow, checkout still works, because recommendations are optional". Before the experiment we can check this with Little's law (from the queueing concept): the number of requests in flight inside checkout is arrival rate times time in system. Normally 1,000 × 0.005 = 5 threads wait on recommendations. If every call takes 500 ms, then 1,000 × 0.5 = 500 threads would be needed, but there are only 200. The thread pool fills in about a fifth of a second of full-speed traffic (the 200 threads are used up as 1,000 requests per second arrive, each stuck for 0.5 s), and all checkout requests queue or fail. The hypothesis is probably false, and an experiment will show it.
Run it safely. Use a Chaos Mesh object that delays traffic on 5% of the recommendation pods, for 10 minutes, in a namespace with abort rules:
```yaml
apiVersion: chaos-mesh.org/v1alpha1
kind: NetworkChaos
metadata:
  name: recs-delay-500ms
  namespace: chaos-testing
spec:
  action: delay
  mode: fixed-percent
  value: "5"
  selector:
    namespaces: ["shop"]
    labelSelectors:
      app: recommendations
  delay:
    latency: "500ms"
    jitter: "50ms"
    correlation: "100"
  duration: "10m"
```
With 5% of calls slow, the average hold time is 0.95 × 5 ms + 0.05 × 500 ms ≈ 30 ms, so 30 threads are busy, still safe. The steady-state metric (checkout success 99.95%, p99 120 ms) stays inside its band, so the experiment is safe, but it shows only a mild effect. Raise the share to 20%: average hold time is 0.8 × 5 + 0.2 × 500 = 104 ms, so 104 busy threads, and p99 of checkout rises by roughly 500 ms because one in five requests waits that long. At 50%, the average is 252 ms, so about 250 threads are needed against 200 available: the pool saturates and the abort rule (error rate above 1%) ends the run. The experiment has found the cliff: checkout falls over when more than about 40% of recommendation calls are slow.
The fix is a design change that the experiment motivates: a client timeout of 100 ms, a fallback to a default list, and a circuit breaker that stops calling a dependency that is failing. Now even if every call fails by timeout, each holds a thread for at most 0.1 s: 1,000 × 0.1 = 100 threads, below 200. Re-running the experiment at 100% delay confirms that checkout success stays at 99.95%, the p99 grows by about 100 ms and the breaker opens after a few seconds. The weakness is now closed, and the experiment is added to the weekly schedule as a regression test.
intuition: A fire drill: you do not wait for a real fire to find out that the door is locked.
Confidence in a safety mechanism should come from watching it work. Reading code and diagrams tells you what the designers intended, but not what the running system does with its real settings, real load and real people. A small deliberate failure, with a hypothesis and a stop button, is the cheapest way to get evidence. Over time, repeated small failures make large ones boring: teams build services that expect failure, the on-call knows the runbooks, and the surprises left are the ones nobody imagined, which no drill can find but a habit of experiments makes easier to handle.
mistakes: People often think chaos engineering means breaking things at random in production. In fact it is a controlled experiment: a hypothesis, a small and bounded blast radius, monitoring, an abort rule and a plan for the fix. Random termination is one tool, not the method.
People often think you must run it in production. In fact you should start in a staging or test environment to remove obvious weaknesses and learn the tooling. Production is where the real traffic, data and configuration are, so mature teams go there carefully, but a team without observability, runbooks or redundancy should not start there.
People often think it is for finding bugs in code. In fact it tests the system as a whole, including configuration (timeouts, pool sizes, retry limits), capacity, monitoring, alerts and the people on call. The most common finds are missing timeouts, retries without limits, alerts that never fire and runbooks that are out of date.
People often think chaos engineering replaces testing, or that passing proves resilience. In fact it complements tests. A passed experiment means that one fault, at one scale, at one time, was handled. Systems change daily, so the evidence expires, and the faults you did not think of are not covered.
People often think more severe faults are better. In fact a severity that exceeds what the system is designed for just proves it fails. Start with failures the system claims to survive, and increase step by step. A failed hypothesis at a small scale teaches more than an outage at a large scale.
friction: The first limit is readiness. Chaos engineering needs good observability (to see steady state and the effect), a clear on-call process, and some redundancy to fail over to. Teams that lack these learn little and risk harm; the right first investment may be monitoring and runbooks, not injection tools. A second limit is culture: running a fault in production requires management support, careful communication and a blameless review of results. Teams worry about customer impact, and a single experiment that causes a real incident can end the programme.
There are technical limits. Real faults are messy: partial failures, slowness, correlated failures across a deployment, cascades through shared dependencies. Injected faults are cleaner than real ones and may miss subtle behaviour (gray failures, where a node is half-working and passes health checks). Reproducibility is poor: a failure found in production may not reappear, and there is no seed to replay, which is the reason deterministic simulation exists. Safe blast radius control needs traffic-routing capabilities (request tagging, a service mesh, feature flags), and stateful systems (databases, queues) are harder to experiment on than stateless ones because failure may corrupt or lose data.
tradeoffs: Choose experiments in a pre-production environment first when your observability or fallbacks are immature, the tooling is new, or the service is critical and has no way to limit blast radius; choose production experiments, with a small slice of traffic and automatic abort, once staging passes, because only production has real traffic, caches, data sizes and configuration. You give up realism with the first and accept a small risk to customers with the second.
Choose random, continuous experiments (such as daily instance termination) to build a habit of resilience in every team and to catch regressions; choose planned game days with a specific scenario (lose a zone, lose the primary database) for complex or rare failures that need people in the loop and a rehearsed runbook. Continuous automation costs low effort per run and finds only what it tests; game days cost days of preparation and find coordination, communication and procedure gaps.
Choose real-environment fault injection to test configuration, deployment and human response. Choose deterministic simulation testing when you build a core system (a database, a consensus protocol, a scheduler) where replaying a failure exactly from a seed and running a million fault schedules overnight are worth the investment of designing the system around a simulator. The simulator gives reproducibility and volume; the real environment gives fidelity.
Choose to inject at the infrastructure level (kill nodes) when you test redundancy; choose network or application level (latency, errors) when you test timeouts, retries and degradation, because slow dependencies are the more common source of cascades than clean crashes. Choose to stop the programme and fix known weaknesses first when the list of known failures is long: do not run an experiment to rediscover a problem you already know about.
interview: What is chaos engineering and how is it different from a normal failure test? || It is experimentation on the running system to build confidence it survives turbulent conditions. It starts from a steady-state metric and a hypothesis, injects realistic faults with a limited blast radius, compares experiment and control groups, and aborts on defined thresholds. A normal failure test checks one component against expected output; chaos tests the whole system, including configuration and humans.
How do you run a first production experiment safely? || Prepare observability and a steady-state metric, pick a fault the system claims to tolerate, scope it to a tiny slice (one instance or 1% of traffic), define abort rules and a manual stop, notify on-call, run in working hours, and compare with a control group. Increase scope only after it passes. Have rollback ready.
Your service calls a dependency with 30 s timeout and 3 retries. What does an experiment with 500 ms delay reveal, and what do you change? || Threads or connections are held for the delay times retries, so Little's law shows exhaustion: in flight = rate × hold time. Retries multiply load on an already slow dependency. Set a timeout close to the dependency's p99 (for example 100 to 200 ms), limit retries with jitter and a budget, add a circuit breaker and a fallback, and bulkhead the pool.
How is chaos engineering related to deterministic simulation testing? || Both inject faults to find bugs in failure handling. Chaos does it on real systems, so it finds configuration and operational problems but cannot replay a failure. Simulation runs the system in a controlled virtual environment with a seeded random source, so every failure is reproducible and millions of schedules can run, but it covers only what the simulator models. Use both.
You find that failover works in tests but takes 4 minutes in the experiment. How do you investigate? || Break down the timeline: failure detection (heartbeat interval and timeout), leader election, DNS or load balancer propagation, connection pool recovery, cache warm-up, client retry behaviour. Compare each to the design. Common causes are long DNS TTLs, health check settings, stale connections in pools, and slow state transfer. Fix the largest contributor and re-run.
practice: Start with observability and a list of failure modes, not with a tool. For each critical service, write down its dependencies and for each one the intended behaviour when it is slow, wrong or down (timeout, fallback, retry policy). This list becomes your hypotheses. Then make the first experiments boring: terminate one instance of a stateless service in staging and check that traffic moves; add 200 ms of latency to one dependency and check the timeout and the alert.
Use the tools your platform gives you: Chaos Mesh or LitmusChaos on Kubernetes, AWS Fault Injection Service or Azure Chaos Studio on the cloud, Toxiproxy for local and integration tests, `tc netem` for Linux hosts, and a service mesh fault filter for request-level faults. Always tag experiments in your monitoring so people see them on dashboards, and keep a record: hypothesis, setup, result, fix and follow-up date. Make each failed hypothesis a ticket with an owner, and rerun the experiment to close it.
Run a game day each quarter for the failures that hurt most: loss of a zone or of the primary database, an expired certificate, a bad deploy, a dependency outage. Include the people and tools used in a real incident (paging, chat, status page), and review the timeline in a blameless way. Over time, move stable experiments into CI/CD or a scheduler, and keep humans for the exploratory ones. To learn more, read the Principles of Chaos Engineering, the book "Chaos Engineering" by Rosenthal and Jones, and the Netflix articles on ChAP; study the Jepsen reports for how to design experiments on stateful systems.
quiz: What are the five advanced principles of chaos engineering? || Build a hypothesis around steady-state behaviour, vary real-world events, run experiments in production, automate experiments to run continuously, and minimize the blast radius.
Why does a slow dependency often cause more damage than a dead one? || A dead dependency fails fast, and the caller can fall back at once. A slow one holds threads and connections for the whole delay, so by Little's law the resources in use grow with rate × delay and the caller runs out, spreading the failure.
A service handles 800 requests per second and every call to a dependency takes 250 ms. How many concurrent calls are in flight, and is a pool of 150 enough? || 800 × 0.25 = 200 concurrent calls, so a pool of 150 is not enough and requests will queue or fail.
You have 500 instances, each with a 0.2% chance of failing on a given day. What is the chance that at least one fails today? || 1 − 0.998^500 ≈ 1 − 0.367 = 63%. At this scale, instance failure is a daily event, which is the argument for continuous experiments.
When would you choose deterministic simulation instead of production fault injection? || When you are building a core stateful system where exact replay of failures and massive numbers of fault schedules matter more than fidelity to the production environment, as FoundationDB and TigerBeetle do.
