value: Back-of-the-envelope estimation is the habit of answering "will this design work?" with arithmetic before you build anything. You take a few inputs (users, actions per user, bytes per action), multiply them into queries per second, storage per year and network bandwidth, and then check each number against a short list of known costs: reading from a CPU cache takes about 1 nanosecond, reading from main memory about 100 nanoseconds, a random read from an NVMe SSD about 100 microseconds, a round trip inside one data centre about 0.5 milliseconds, and a round trip between continents about 100 milliseconds or more. Those last numbers are the "latency numbers": they span eight orders of magnitude, and a design that ignores them is wrong by a factor of a million.
The skill is valuable because most design mistakes are not subtle. A service that makes 40 sequential cross-region calls cannot answer in 200 ms. A 10 TB cache will not fit on a 256 GB machine. You can find each of these in two minutes with a pen, or after the system is built, in production.
This is the discipline behind caching (justified by the gap between RAM and disk or network), CDNs (justified by the 100 ms cost of crossing a continent), multi-region design and capacity planning. The layers below supply the numbers: the memory hierarchy and storage devices (L3) explain why each medium has its cost, the speed of light (L5) sets a floor under network latency, queueing theory (L4) says how much worse real latency is than the average, Amdahl's law (L4) limits what parallelism can buy, and binary arithmetic (L3) gives the powers of two that make the multiplication easy.
terms: Latency — the time one operation takes from start to finish, for example 100 µs for one SSD read.
Throughput — the number of operations or bytes per second a system completes; it is not the same as 1 / latency when work runs in parallel.
QPS — queries (requests) per second; the basic unit of load. Average QPS is total requests divided by 86,400 seconds in a day.
Peak factor — the ratio of peak load to average load, commonly 2 to 5 for consumer traffic; you size for the peak.
Order of magnitude — a factor of ten. Estimation aims to be right within one order of magnitude, not exactly right.
Round trip time (RTT) — the time for a message to reach a server and its reply to come back; at least twice the one-way distance divided by the signal speed.
Little's law — in a stable system, the average number of requests in flight equals the arrival rate times the average time each request spends inside (\( L = \lambda W \)).
Working set — the data a workload touches repeatedly; whether it fits in cache or memory decides which row of the latency table applies.
history: The best-known list is Jeff Dean's "Numbers Everyone Should Know", from a 2009 talk on building large distributed systems at Google (the LADIS workshop keynote). His point was practical: before choosing a design, know roughly what a cache hit, a disk seek and a datacentre round trip cost. Peter Norvig had published a similar table earlier in "Teach Yourself Programming in Ten Years".
The numbers themselves keep moving. In the 2009 list a main-memory reference cost 100 ns, a datacentre round trip 500 µs and a disk seek 10 ms. A decade later main memory is still about 100 ns, because DRAM latency has barely improved, but the SSD arrived and put a new rung between memory and disk at roughly 100 µs, and datacentre networks improved to 100 Gbit/s links with round trips well under a millisecond. The "Latency Numbers Every Programmer Should Know" page by Colin Scott at Berkeley shows these values by year, and it is a good reminder that bandwidth grew much faster than latency. Hardware gets more bytes per second every year; it does not get the first byte faster.
Estimation itself is older: Enrico Fermi taught students to chain rough guesses, trusting that errors partly cancel. Google's "Site Reliability Engineering" book (2016) and system-design interviews made "do the capacity estimate first" a standard opening. Little's law (John Little, 1961) supplies the one formula nearly every estimate needs.
how: Estimation has two parts. The first is a chain of multiplications that turns a product idea into load. The second is a lookup against known costs that tells you whether that load is cheap, expensive or impossible. Do them in that order, write each step down, and keep one significant digit.
Step 1: turn users into requests. Start with the number of daily actions: users times actions per user per day. Divide by 86,400 seconds in a day to get average QPS. A useful shortcut is that a day is about 10⁵ seconds, so 1 million events per day is roughly 10 QPS, and 1 billion per day is roughly 10,000 QPS. Multiply by a peak factor of 2 to 5 for the peak. Then multiply by the size of each request or record to get bandwidth (bytes per second) and storage (bytes per day, then per year). Replication multiplies storage by the replica count, and compression divides it, typically by 3 to 10 for columnar files.
Step 2: turn requests into machines. Estimate the cost of one request in CPU time, for example 1 ms for a typical parse, lookup and response. One core then serves about 1,000 requests per second at full load, and a 16-core server about 10,000. Plan for 50% utilisation or less at peak, because queueing theory shows that response time grows sharply as utilisation approaches 100%: for a simple single-server queue, the average time is the service time divided by \( (1 - \rho) \), so at 90% utilisation a request takes 10 times its bare service time, and the slow tail is worse. Little's law gives the concurrency you must support: in-flight requests are \( L = \lambda W \), the arrival rate times the time each takes. At 100,000 QPS and 20 ms per request, 2,000 requests are in flight at once, and that sets thread, connection and memory needs.
Step 3: check each step against the latency ladder. The memory hierarchy (L3) explains the first rungs: an L1 cache hit costs about 1 ns, L2 about 4 ns, L3 about 10 to 40 ns, and a random DRAM access about 80 to 100 ns, because the data must travel from a separate chip. A 64-byte cache line is fetched at a time, so scanning an array sequentially is far faster than chasing pointers. Reading memory sequentially runs at roughly 10 GB/s per core or more, so 1 MB takes on the order of 100 µs. Storage devices (L3) give the next rungs: a NVMe SSD serves a random 4 KB read in about 50 to 100 µs and streams several GB/s, while a hard disk needs a head movement and half a rotation, about 5 to 10 ms for a random read, and streams only about 150 to 250 MB/s. Network-attached cloud disks add a network hop and usually cost around 0.5 to 1 ms per read.
Step 4: respect the floor set by physics. Light in optical fibre travels at about two thirds of its speed in vacuum, about 200,000 km per second, which is 5 µs per kilometre one way. A round trip therefore costs at least 1 ms per 100 km. New York to London is about 5,570 km, so the minimum round trip is about 56 ms, and real networks, with routing and equipment, give about 70 ms. No software change can lower this. The speed of light (L5) is why a CDN puts bytes near the user and why a user in Bengaluru calling a server in Virginia will see 200 ms or more per round trip, however fast the server is. Setting up a new TCP connection with TLS adds one to three more round trips before the first byte of data, which is why connection reuse matters.
Step 5: add the sequential and parallel structure. Latencies add when calls are sequential and combine by maximum when calls run in parallel. A request that makes 10 dependent calls at 2 ms each takes 20 ms; the same 10 calls made in parallel take about 2 ms plus the slowest outlier. Amdahl's law (L4) puts a ceiling on this: if a fraction \( s \) of the work is serial, the speedup from any amount of parallelism is at most \( 1/s \), so a request that is 20% serial can never be more than 5 times faster. Finally, compare against the budget: if the product needs a response in 100 ms, subtract the network round trips first, then see what is left for computation.
Step 6: use powers of two to keep the arithmetic easy. Binary arithmetic (L3) gives 2¹⁰ ≈ 10³, 2²⁰ ≈ 10⁶, 2³⁰ ≈ 10⁹, and 2⁴⁰ ≈ 10¹². So 1 KB, 1 MB, 1 GB and 1 TB are each about a thousand times the previous one, and an ID of 64 bits is 8 bytes. With these you can compute 5 billion rows × 40 bytes = 200 GB in your head.

example: Estimate an impression-logging service for a CTV ad platform. Assumptions: 3 billion impressions per day, each logged as a 500-byte event, with a peak factor of 3.
Traffic. 3 × 10⁹ / 10⁵ s ≈ 30,000 events per second on average. Using 86,400 exactly gives 34,700, so 35,000 is fine. At a peak factor of 3, plan for about 100,000 events per second. Bandwidth at peak is 100,000 × 500 B = 50 MB/s, which is 400 Mbit/s. One server with a 10 Gbit/s network card carries this easily, so bandwidth is not the constraint.
Storage. 3 × 10⁹ × 500 B = 1.5 TB per day raw. Per year that is about 550 TB. Stored as compressed Parquet at about 5 times compression, it is about 110 TB per year. The message queue in front keeps, say, 3 days at 3 replicas: 1.5 TB × 3 × 3 = 13.5 TB of broker disk, which fits on a handful of brokers.
Servers. Suppose each event costs 1 ms of CPU (parse, validate, serialise, send to the queue). One core handles about 1,000 events per second, so 100,000 per second needs 100 cores at full load. At 50% target utilisation, 200 cores, or about 13 servers of 16 cores. Add one more zone's worth for failure, and plan roughly 18 to 20.
Now the check that finds the trap. Each event must be enriched with a campaign record. If the service looks up the campaign in a remote database over the network at 1 ms per lookup, Little's law gives \( L = 100{,}000 \times 0.001 = 100 \) lookups in flight at the peak. That is fine for a database with a connection pool of a few hundred, but the database must serve 100,000 reads per second, which usually needs a cache. Put the campaign table in each server's memory instead (say 2 GB, which fits), and the lookup drops to about 100 ns, ten thousand times cheaper. Compare the worst design: the lookup reads from a hard disk at 10 ms. Then \( L = 100{,}000 \times 0.01 = 1{,}000 \) seeks in flight, but one disk does only about 100 seeks per second, so you would need 1,000 disks. The arithmetic rejected this design in one line, before any code.
Last, the latency budget. If the ad server must answer in 100 ms, a synchronous cross-region call to this logger (70 ms round trip) blows it. The estimate shows the logging call must be asynchronous or local to the region.
intuition: Estimation is like checking a bridge design with a ruler and a table of material strengths before ordering steel.
The design is natural because systems are built from a small number of physical operations, and each operation has a cost set by physics: electrons crossing a chip, a head moving over a platter, light crossing an ocean. A big system is a sum of those costs multiplied by how often they happen. If you know the few costs and can count the occurrences, you can predict the total within a factor of two or three, which is exactly the accuracy needed to choose between designs that differ by a factor of ten or a hundred.
It works despite rough inputs because errors in a chain of multiplications tend to partly cancel (some guesses are too high and some too low), and because design decisions are usually not close. Whether a workload needs 4 servers or 400 changes the whole architecture; whether it needs 4 or 5 does not.
mistakes: People often think that estimation needs accurate data first. In fact the point is to decide with rough data; the answer is useful within a factor of 10, and real numbers arrive only after you build. Write the assumption down and change it when data comes.
People often think average latency describes what users see. In fact the tail matters: with queueing, the 99th percentile can be several times the mean, and a request that touches many servers sees the slowest of them. Use the latency numbers as typical values and then add a tail margin; the neighbouring concept of tail latency explains the maths.
People often think faster hardware makes the ladder irrelevant. In fact only bandwidth has grown quickly. DRAM latency has stayed near 100 ns for fifteen years and the speed of light has not changed. A modern server streams memory at tens of GB/s but still waits about 100 ns for each random pointer.
People often think that throughput is simply 1 divided by latency. In fact that is true for one thread doing one thing at a time. A disk with 10 ms latency can serve thousands of requests per second if requests are queued and many run in parallel (an SSD has many internal channels), and a network link can carry a million requests per second at a 50 ms round trip if enough are in flight. Little's law links the two: throughput equals concurrency divided by latency.
friction: Estimation is only as good as its assumptions, and the most common errors are about what you forgot. People forget the peak factor and size for the average. They forget replication, indexes and compression ratios when counting storage, which can change the answer by 5 times. They forget overheads such as per-message headers (a 20-byte payload can become 200 bytes on the wire), TLS handshakes, serialisation and garbage collection pauses. They forget that a read often needs more than one device access: a B-tree lookup can need three to four page reads, and a cold LSM read can touch several files.
The ladder is also a simplification. Caches, prefetchers and the operating system page cache mean the same code can run 100 times faster or slower depending on whether the working set fits in cache, so you must decide which row applies. Cloud numbers vary: cross-zone round trips are 1 to 2 ms rather than 0.5 ms, network-attached disks have their own queues and throttles (IOPS and throughput limits), and burst credits can make a test look better than steady state. Latency also depends on load, so a number measured on an idle machine is optimistic.
Finally, estimation finds scale problems but not correctness problems: it cannot see a race condition or a bad consistency model. It also tempts people to over-engineer. A result of 300 QPS and 50 GB of data means one database server is enough, and sharding is then a cost, not a safeguard.
tradeoffs: Choose a rough estimate (one significant digit, powers of ten) at the start of a design, when the choice is between architectures that differ by 10 times or more. Choose a measured benchmark when the estimate lands within 2 to 3 times of a limit (a disk's IOPS, a network link, a budget), because then rough numbers cannot tell you which side you are on. You give up speed with measurement and precision with estimation.
Choose to keep data in memory when the working set fits in a machine you can afford (say up to a few hundred GB) and requests are latency sensitive, because DRAM is about 1,000 times faster than SSD per random access. Choose SSD when the data is terabytes and 100 µs per read is acceptable, since memory costs roughly 10 to 20 times more per GB. Choose object storage or hard disks for cold data read mostly in large sequential pieces, where throughput matters and latency of 10 to 100 ms per request does not.
Choose to put a cache in front of a remote store when the round trip (0.5 ms in a zone, more across regions) times your request rate dominates the budget and a good share of reads repeat. Choose not to when hit rates will be low, data must be strongly consistent, or the store is already fast enough, because a cache adds invalidation bugs and another failure mode.
Choose to spend extra work to avoid a cross-region round trip (replicate data, route users to the nearest region, make calls asynchronous) when the user-facing path would otherwise exceed its budget. Accept a single region when the audience is local and the extra 60 to 200 ms is below what users notice. The price of multi-region is consistency complexity and about double the cost.
interview: Estimate the storage and QPS for a service you choose (a URL shortener, say). What do you do first? || State assumptions aloud: users, actions per day, bytes per record, read to write ratio. Convert to average QPS with 10⁵ seconds per day, multiply by a peak factor, compute storage per year with replication, and round to one digit. Say which number would change the design if wrong.
Your service makes 12 downstream calls, each with p99 of 20 ms. What latency do you expect? || Sequential: about 12 times the typical latency plus tail effects. Parallel: about the maximum, and with 12 calls the chance that at least one is slow is 1 − 0.99¹² ≈ 11%. Restructure to parallel calls, cut calls, set per-call deadlines and consider hedging.
How do you use Little's law in capacity planning? || Concurrency equals arrival rate times time in the system: 50,000 requests per second at 40 ms means 2,000 in flight, which sets thread, pool and memory sizes. If latency doubles in an incident, concurrency doubles too, which can exhaust pools and cause a collapse.
A teammate says "SSDs are fast, so we can drop the cache". How do you check? || An SSD read is about 100 µs against 100 ns for memory, and one drive serves perhaps 100,000 to 1,000,000 random reads per second. Compute peak read rate and budget. At 50,000 reads per second and a 5 ms budget a cache may be unnecessary; at a 1 ms p99 with 20 reads per request it is not.
practice: Keep a one-page table of the ladder and a standard form for the estimate: users, actions per day, QPS (average and peak), bytes per record, storage per year with replication, cores, memory, network. Put your assumptions at the top and mark each as a guess or a measurement. When a result is within 3 times of a hardware limit, stop and benchmark that part.
Check every estimate in two ways. First do a reasonableness check against something you know: your own production system, or a known public figure (for example, 1 billion requests per day is about 12,000 QPS). Second, look at where the time goes: if one term is more than half the total, work on that term. Re-run the estimate when the design changes, as a design document should carry its numbers, and compare them with real metrics after launch so your intuition improves.
Learn the shape of the ladder, not each digit: nanoseconds for CPU, hundreds of nanoseconds for memory, hundreds of microseconds for flash and the local network, milliseconds for disks and cross-zone calls, tens to hundreds of milliseconds for wide-area calls. For your own platform, measure these rungs once on your real hardware and instance types (a few hours with a benchmark tool such as fio for disks and a ping-style test for networks) and keep the results in the team wiki. Many teams are surprised by their own cloud's numbers.

@@ hotkeys
value: A hot key is a key that receives a much larger share of traffic than the others. Partitioning assumes that load spreads evenly over keys: hash the key, pick a shard, and each of 100 shards gets about 1% of the work. That works only if no single key is large. If one key brings 7% of all requests, whichever shard owns it carries 7 times its fair share, and adding more shards does not help, because one key always lives on exactly one shard. Skew is the general name for this unevenness, and a hot key is its sharpest form.
Hot keys appear everywhere: a celebrity account in a social network, a viral product page, the default "unknown" value of a column in a join, the null device ID in an ad log, a global counter, a popular partition of a Kafka topic. The symptoms are the same. One node is at 100% CPU or hits its throughput limit while the rest are idle; one Spark task runs for an hour while the other 199 finish in a minute; one DynamoDB partition returns throttling errors although the table has spare capacity.
The fixes follow from what the key is doing. Reads can be served from copies: a cache in front, replicas, or a local cache in every client. Writes must be split: write to N sub-keys and combine them when reading. Joins and aggregations in batch engines can be salted: add a random suffix to spread one key's rows over N tasks, then merge the partial results. The layers below explain why the problem exists and bound what the fixes can do: power-law traffic (L4) says how skewed real workloads are, hashing (L3) spreads distinct keys but cannot split one key, partitioning (L2) fixes each key to one owner, and the shuffle (L2) sends all rows of one key to one task.
terms: Hot key — a key whose request rate or data volume is far above the average key, large enough to overload the shard that owns it.
Skew — uneven distribution of work or data across partitions, tasks or shards.
Straggler — a task that runs much longer than its peers; in a batch stage the whole stage waits for it.
Zipf distribution — a distribution where the r-th most popular item has frequency proportional to \( 1/r^s \), with s near 1 in many real workloads.
Salting — adding a random suffix (0 to N−1) to a key so that its work spreads across N sub-keys.
Sharded counter — one logical counter stored as N sub-counters; writes go to a random one and a read sums all of them.
Heavy hitter — one of the few keys that account for most of the traffic; detected with sketches such as Count-Min or Space-Saving.
Request coalescing — merging concurrent requests for the same key into one backend request whose result is shared (also called single-flight).
history: Skew was seen as soon as data was partitioned. Parallel database research in the 1980s and 1990s, such as DeWitt and colleagues on parallel hash joins, already named "data skew" as the main enemy of parallel speed-up: the slowest partition sets the finish time. George Zipf's 1949 observation that word frequencies follow a power law explains why this is normal and not rare. Web traffic, product popularity, social-network followers and search queries all show Zipf-like distributions, so a few keys are always large.
In 2000s web systems the same problem moved to caches. Consistent hashing (Karger et al., 1997) was designed to spread keys evenly over cache servers, and it does spread keys, but one popular URL still went to one server. Large sites answered by replicating hot items. Facebook's "Scaling Memcache at Facebook" (NSDI 2013) describes replicating very popular keys across several servers, and using leases so that a thundering herd of misses on one key does not hit the database. Mirrokni, Thorup and Zadimoghaddam (2016) later gave consistent hashing with bounded loads, which caps each server at a small multiple of the average load.
Managed stores made the limits explicit. Amazon's DynamoDB paper (USENIX ATC 2022) describes how fixed per-partition throughput limits caused throttling on hot partitions, and how the service added adaptive capacity and automatic splitting. In batch engines, Spark's adaptive query execution (Spark 3.0, 2020) added automatic skew-join handling, after years of users salting keys by hand.
how: Step 1: find the hot keys. You cannot fix what you cannot see. For streams, run a heavy-hitter sketch on the key: Count-Min Sketch (Cormode and Muthukrishnan, 2005) gives approximate counts in small memory, and Space-Saving (Metwally et al., 2005) keeps the top-k keys directly. For batch jobs, run `SELECT key, count(*) ... GROUP BY key ORDER BY 2 DESC LIMIT 20` on a sample. Per-shard metrics (CPU, queue length, throttle count) show which shard is hot, and the key frequencies show why.
Step 2: see why hashing cannot help. A hash function (L3) maps different keys to different shards roughly uniformly, so it fixes skew caused by many keys of unequal placement. It cannot fix skew caused by one key's volume, because the same key always gives the same hash. A Zipf distribution makes this concrete. With one million keys and exponent s = 1, the top key has \( 1/H_n \) of the traffic where \( H_n \approx \ln n + 0.577 \approx 14.4 \), so about 6.9%; the top 10 keys have about 20%. Across 100 shards, the shard holding the top key gets about 7.9% against a fair 1%, almost 8 times the average, and this does not improve with more shards: with 1,000 shards the top key's shard still gets 6.9%, now 69 times the average.
Step 3: for read-hot keys, add copies close to the readers. A local in-process cache with a short time to live (TTL) is the cheapest. If 2,000 application servers each cache a hot key for 1 second, the backend sees at most 2,000 reads per second for that key, however many users ask. Request coalescing prevents a stampede when the entry expires: the first miss fetches and the others wait for its result. A shared cache can replicate the key on several nodes with the client choosing one at random. Replicas of the database can serve the key too. The cost is staleness up to the TTL.
Step 4: for write-hot keys, split the key. A counter incremented 50,000 times a second cannot live in one row or one partition. Store it as N sub-counters, `views#0 ... views#N-1`. A write picks a random suffix, so each sub-key gets 1/N of the writes. A read fetches all N and adds them. This works because addition is associative and commutative, so partial results merge in any order. The same pattern holds for any mergeable aggregate: maximum, minimum, set union, HyperLogLog sketches. It does not work for operations whose result depends on a global order. A second option is write combining: buffer increments in memory for 100 ms and write one update with the sum.
Step 5: for skewed batch joins and aggregations, salt. The shuffle (L2) sends all rows of one key to one task, so a key with 300 million rows creates one huge task. For an aggregation, first aggregate by (key, salt) with salt in 0..N−1, which spreads the key over N tasks, then aggregate again by key alone to merge. For a join, add a random salt 0..N−1 to the large side's rows and replicate each row of the small side N times, once per salt value, so every salted row finds its match. This costs N times the small side, so apply it only to the few hot keys, handling the rest normally. Spark's adaptive query execution does this automatically for sort-merge joins: it flags a partition as skewed when it is larger than 5 times the median partition size and larger than 256 MB (`spark.sql.adaptive.skewJoin.skewedPartitionFactor` and `...skewedPartitionThresholdInBytes`), splits it into several tasks and duplicates the matching partition of the other side.
Step 6: for stores with fixed partition limits, isolate or spread. DynamoDB gives each partition up to 3,000 read units and 1,000 write units per second. Adaptive capacity shifts throughput toward hot partitions and can split them, but a single item still cannot exceed the limit of one partition, so the hot item needs a cache in front (DAX) or sharded keys. In Kafka, a hot key makes one partition (and one consumer) slow; a custom partitioner or a salted key spreads it, at the cost of losing per-key ordering.
Finally, consider the data itself. Many "hot keys" are artefacts: nulls, empty strings, default IDs such as an all-zero device ID. Filtering or handling them separately often removes the skew with no machinery at all.

example: A video platform logs 1 billion view events a day and wants the view count per video. One viral video gets 30% of today's events.
First, the problem. The job groups by `video_id` over 200 shuffle partitions. The 700 million events of other videos spread over 199 partitions, about 3.5 million each. The viral video's 300 million events all go to one partition. At 100 bytes each that is 30 GB for one task, against about 350 MB for the others. If a normal task takes 1 minute, this one takes about 85 minutes, and the whole stage waits.
Now salt. Stage 1 groups by (`video_id`, `salt`) where `salt` = `floor(rand() * 50)`. The viral video becomes 50 keys of about 6 million events each, which is the size of a normal task. Stage 2 groups by `video_id`, sums 50 partial counts, and finishes in seconds. Total time falls from about 85 minutes to about 2 minutes. In PySpark:
```python
from pyspark.sql import functions as F
N = 50
partial = (events
  .withColumn("salt", (F.rand() * N).cast("int"))
  .groupBy("video_id", "salt").agg(F.count("*").alias("c")))
views = partial.groupBy("video_id").agg(F.sum("c").alias("views"))
```

The serving side has the same shape. The live view counter for that video receives 300 million increments in a day, which is about 3,500 per second on average and perhaps 15,000 in a spike. A single Redis key or DynamoDB item handles that with difficulty (a DynamoDB item shares a 1,000 writes per second partition limit). Use 32 sub-counters: each takes about 470 writes per second in the spike. intuition: A hot key is like a single checkout lane with a long queue in a store that has twenty lanes: adding lanes does nothing until the queue itself is split.
Hashing balances many small things; it cannot balance one big thing. Every fix therefore turns one big key into many small ones (salting, sub-counters), or makes the single key cheaper to serve (copies, caches, coalescing). The first works when the operation can be merged afterwards, and the second when the data can be slightly stale. If the key is neither mergeable nor cacheable, you are left with giving it its own machine.
mistakes: People often think a better hash function or more shards fixes hot keys. In fact the same key always maps to the same shard, so adding shards only dilutes the other keys. The hot shard stays hot.
People often think consistent hashing solves skew. In fact it solves a different problem, moving few keys when nodes join or leave. It does not split a single key, although variants with bounded loads limit how much total work any one server takes.
People often think skew is a bug in the data that only appears in rare cases. In fact Zipf-like distributions are the normal case for user, product, device and word counts, so a design that assumes uniform keys is the unusual one. Test with realistic key distributions, not uniform random keys.
People often think salting is free. In fact it multiplies work somewhere: a salted join copies the small side N times, a sharded counter needs N reads, and a salted Kafka key loses per-key order. Salt only the keys that need it and keep N as small as works.
friction: Detecting hot keys is itself hard. Heavy-hitter sketches add cost and can miss keys that become hot suddenly (a flash crowd), so mitigation needs both automatic detection and a fast manual override. Hotness also moves: the key that is hot at noon is not hot at midnight, so static plans such as a fixed list of salted keys go stale. Spark's adaptive handling reacts to partition sizes it observes after the map stage, which is correct for that run but cannot help a streaming job whose skew changes by the minute.
Each remedy has a cost. Caches add staleness and invalidation bugs. Replicas make writes more expensive. Sub-counters make reads N times more expensive and make exact point-in-time totals harder. Salting joins moves the problem into the broadcasting of the small side. For ordered or transactional work, such as a hot account balance that must apply debits in order, none of the splitting tricks apply, and the options are serialising updates through one queue, batching them, or redesigning the data model (for example, an append-only ledger summed later).
Finally, skew hides in the tail of a fan-out: one slow shard delays an entire scatter-gather request, so a hot key turns into a tail-latency problem for every query that touches that shard, not just queries for that key.
tradeoffs: Choose caching or replicas when the hot key is read-heavy and some staleness is acceptable: a TTL of 1 second gives a hit rate near 100% for a key read thousands of times per second. Choose key splitting (sub-counters, salting) when the hot key is write-heavy and the operation can be merged, such as sums, counts, max or set union. You give up freshness with the first and read cost with the second.
Choose automatic skew handling (Spark AQE, DynamoDB adaptive capacity) as the default, since it needs no code and reacts to actual sizes. Choose explicit salting when you know the hot keys in advance, when the engine cannot split the operation (aggregations like distinct count, window functions, non-sort-merge joins), or when streaming state is the hot spot. Automatic handling reacts after the fact; explicit salting costs code and a list to maintain.
Choose a dedicated shard or node for a hot key when it is a single, long-lived and non-mergeable entity (one very large tenant, one global lock) and you can afford the machine. Choose splitting when many keys might turn hot, because dedicated placement needs a manual decision per key and does not scale to a changing set.
Choose to filter or special-case dummy keys (nulls, default IDs) when they carry no information. Choose to keep them, with salting, when the rows must be preserved for counts or compliance.

@@ taillatency
value: Tail latency is the latency of the slowest few percent of requests, written as p99 (the 99th percentile: 99% of requests are faster) or p99.9. It matters more than the average for two reasons. Users remember the slow page, not the average one. And in a system where one request fans out to many servers, the tail of the parts becomes the typical case for the whole. If one call in 100 is slow, a request that makes 100 such calls in parallel and waits for all of them is slow 1 − 0.99¹⁰⁰ ≈ 63% of the time. Dean and Barroso made this point in "The Tail at Scale" (Communications of the ACM, 2013), and it is the reason the slowest server dominates at warehouse scale.
Slow responses are not rare accidents with one cause. A garbage collection pause, a background compaction, a noisy neighbour on shared hardware, a cache miss that goes to disk, a lock queue, a network retransmission: each affects a small share of requests, and together they make a long tail. Because the causes are many and hard to remove, the practical answer is to build systems that tolerate variable parts and still give predictable whole responses. The main tools are hedged requests (send a second copy of a request if the first is slow), tied requests, tight timeouts and deadlines, load-aware routing, and "good enough" partial answers.
The layers below give the exact reasons. Queueing theory (L4) shows why latency distributions have long tails and why the tail explodes as utilisation rises. Probability (L4) gives the fan-out formula, and concentration inequalities (L4) show how predictable the number of slow parts is. Power laws (L4) explain why a few very slow requests dominate. Statistics (L4) says how to measure percentiles without fooling yourself. Speculative execution (L2) is the idea of doing work that may be wasted to reduce waiting, which hedging applies to requests, and scatter–gather (L2) is the fan-out pattern that creates the exposure.
terms: Percentile (pXX) — the value below which XX% of observations fall; p99 of 200 ms means 99% of requests finished within 200 ms.
Tail latency — the high-percentile end of the latency distribution, usually p99 and above.
Fan-out — one incoming request causing N calls to other servers, whose results are combined.
Hedged request — sending a second copy of a request to another replica when the first has not answered within a set delay, and using whichever answer comes first.
Tied request — sending the request to two replicas at once, each told about the other, so that when one starts it cancels the other.
Deadline propagation — passing the remaining time budget along with a request so every service knows when to give up.
Head-of-line blocking — a slow or large request at the front of a queue delaying all the requests behind it.
Load shedding — rejecting or dropping some requests when a server is overloaded, to keep the rest fast.
history: Latency averages were the norm in early performance work, and queueing theory had already shown that mean and tail diverge near saturation. The modern focus on tails began with web companies that fanned queries out to thousands of machines. Google's Jeff Dean gave talks on this around 2010–2012, and in 2013 Dean and Luiz André Barroso published "The Tail at Scale", which gave the field its vocabulary and techniques. They reported, for example, that a hedged request sent after a 10 ms delay cut the 99.9th-percentile latency of reading 1,000 keys from a BigTable benchmark from 1,800 ms to 74 ms with only 2% more requests.
Related ideas appeared in different places. Mitzenmacher's "power of two choices" (2001) showed that choosing the less loaded of two random servers reduces the worst queue dramatically, which became a standard load-balancing technique. Amazon's Dynamo paper (2007) reported its SLAs at the 99.9th percentile rather than the mean, and made tail latency a business metric. Gil Tene's talks on "coordinated omission" (from about 2013) showed that many benchmark tools under-report tails because they stop sending requests while waiting for a slow one.
Since then the techniques have moved into infrastructure. Finagle, Envoy, gRPC and cloud load balancers provide retry budgets, timeouts and, in some, hedging policies. Latency-aware sketches such as t-digest and DDSketch, and HdrHistogram, were built so that percentiles can be measured cheaply and merged across servers. Service-level objectives written as percentiles are now standard in site reliability engineering.
how: Step 1: understand why the tail exists. Queueing theory (L4) gives the first reason. For a simple single-server queue with random arrivals and service (M/M/1) at utilisation \( \rho \) (the fraction of time the server is busy), response time is exponentially distributed with mean \( S/(1-\rho) \), where S is the bare service time. The p-th percentile is \( -\ln(1-p) \times S/(1-\rho) \). So at \( \rho = 0.5 \) the mean is 2S and p99 is about 9.2S, and at \( \rho = 0.9 \) the mean is 10S and p99 is about 46S. Even a perfectly regular server gets a long tail from queueing alone, and the tail grows much faster than the mean as load rises. Real service times also have heavy tails (power laws, L4): a few requests are much more expensive, like a huge query or a GC pause, and they block those behind them.
Step 2: see how fan-out multiplies the problem. Suppose each call is slow with probability p, independently. A request that waits for N calls is slow with probability \( 1 - (1-p)^N \). For p = 1%: N = 10 gives 9.6%, N = 100 gives 63%, N = 1,000 gives 99.996%. Turn it around: to have 99% of fan-out-100 requests fast, each call must be fast with probability \( 0.99^{1/100} \approx 99.99\% \), so the leaf p99.99 must meet the target. The request's median is set by a very high leaf percentile: with 50 leaves, the request median is the leaf p98.6. The system's typical user sees the component's tail.
Step 3: choose where to attack. The paper separates techniques by time scale. Within a request (tens of milliseconds) you use hedged and tied requests. Across requests (seconds to minutes) you use micro-partitions (many small partitions per machine so load can move in small pieces), selective replication of hot items, and latency-induced probation (temporarily remove a slow server from rotation and keep probing it). In search-like systems you use good-enough responses (return when most shards answered) and canary requests (send a new query to a few servers first so a query that crashes servers does not hit thousands). At the component level you reduce variability: separate service classes, time-slice long requests to avoid head-of-line blocking, and avoid synchronised background work.
Step 4: hedged requests. Send the request to one replica. If no reply arrives within a delay d, send a copy to a different replica; take the first answer and cancel the other. Choose d near the p95 latency of the call, so only about 5% of calls are duplicated, which is the extra load. The effect is large: a call becomes slow only if both copies are slow, so with independent slowness the probability drops from p to about p². Starting from p = 1% this is 0.01%, and a fan-out of 100 is slow only \( 1-(1-10^{-4})^{100} \approx 1\% \) of the time instead of 63%. The independence assumption is the weak point. Slowness is often correlated (same overloaded rack, same bad query), so the second copy should go to a replica that is unlikely to share the cause.
Step 5: tied requests and load-aware routing. A tied request goes to two replicas at once, each with the identity of the other; when one starts executing it tells the other to cancel. This avoids the delay d, and the wasted work stays small because the loser is cancelled while still queued. Dean and Barroso measured, on a file-system read benchmark, about a 40% reduction in the 99.9th-percentile latency with under 1% extra disk use. Load-aware routing attacks the queue itself. Picking two random replicas and sending to the one with the shorter queue (the power of two choices) gives most of the benefit of knowing every queue, without the cost.
Step 6: bound the damage with deadlines, budgets and shedding. Pass a deadline with each request so downstream work is abandoned when the caller has given up. Limit retries and hedges with a budget (for example, extra requests at most 10% of the base rate) so a slow period does not become a retry storm. Shed load at the server when queues grow, because a rejected request costs less than a request that waits for seconds and then times out. Make hedged operations idempotent, so doing them twice is safe.
Step 7: measure correctly. Percentiles cannot be averaged across servers; the average of p99s is not the p99. Record histograms or mergeable sketches and combine them. To see p99.9 you need thousands of samples per window. Beware of coordinated omission: if a load generator waits for each response before sending the next, a stall hides the requests that should have been sent during it.

example: A search service fans each query out to 50 index shards. Each shard has p50 = 5 ms, p95 = 20 ms, p99 = 50 ms. The root waits for all shards.
Without hedging. The chance that at least one shard takes longer than 50 ms is 1 − 0.99⁵⁰ = 39.5%. So 4 queries in 10 are slower than the shard's p99. The median query needs all 50 shards to be fast, so it falls at the shard percentile where 0.5^(1/50) = 98.6%: about 40 ms in this distribution, eight times the shard's median.
With hedging at d = 20 ms. A shard call is hedged when it has not finished by 20 ms, which happens 5% of the time, so on average 2.5 of the 50 calls per query are duplicated and the cluster handles 5% more shard requests. A call stays slower than 50 ms only if the first copy is slower than 50 ms (1%) and the second copy, sent at 20 ms, needs more than 30 ms. Suppose that happens 3% of the time. The joint chance is 1% × 3% = 0.03%. Over 50 shards the query is slow with probability 1 − (1 − 0.0003)⁵⁰ ≈ 1.5%, down from 39.5%, for a 5% increase in load.
How reliable is the extra load? The number of hedged calls per query is binomial with mean 2.5 and standard deviation \( \sqrt{50 \times 0.05 \times 0.95} \approx 1.5 \). Concentration inequalities (L4) say that for many independent queries this share stays close to 5%, so you can plan capacity for 5 to 6% extra, not a wide range. That predictability is what makes hedging a budgetable technique.
When it breaks. Suppose the system is already near 90% utilisation. A 5% increase in load moves utilisation to 94.5%, and by the M/M/1 formula the mean time goes from 10S to about 18S. The hedges that were meant to cut the tail raise it for everyone. This is why hedging must be capped by a budget and paired with load shedding, and why it works best on systems with spare capacity.
intuition: Waiting for many parts is like a group of friends who cannot start dinner until the last one arrives: the more friends, the more likely someone is late, so the group's start time is the latest arrival, not the average.
Hedging is natural because it turns one risky bet into two independent bets. If one try is slow with probability 1% and you can cheaply try again elsewhere after you notice a delay, both fail only about 0.01% of the time. You pay a small, known amount of extra work (only the slowest 5% get a second copy) for a large drop in the worst case. This works only when a second attempt is cheap and independent, which is why it suits reads and idempotent operations.
mistakes: People often think the average or median latency is the number that matters. In fact users and fan-out structures experience the tail. A service with a 20 ms median and a 2 s p99 feels broken to 1 in 100 users, and for a request that fans out to 100 services, that same tail hits most requests.
People often think tail latency comes from a few bad servers that can be found and removed. In fact it is spread across every server and has many transient causes (GC, compaction, a noisy neighbour, a retransmission). Removing one cause moves the problem to the next, which is why the paper stresses tolerating variability instead of removing it.
People often think hedging simply doubles the load. In fact hedging after the p95 delay duplicates only about 5% of calls, and the loser is cancelled. Hedging at a delay near the median would double the load, so the delay choice is the point.
People often think retries and hedges are the same. In fact a retry follows a failure or timeout and replaces the first attempt, while a hedge runs in parallel with a slow first attempt before it has failed. Both multiply load under overload, so both need budgets.
People often think a p99 can be computed by averaging p99 values from each server. In fact percentiles do not average; merge histograms or sketches and compute the percentile from the merged data.
friction: Hedging needs spare capacity and independence. If slowness comes from overload, extra copies add to the overload, and the system can enter a metastable failure where retries and hedges keep it overloaded after the original trigger has gone. If slowness is correlated (a shared dependency, a bad deployment, a slow zone), the second copy is slow too. Hedging also needs requests to be safe to run twice, so writes need idempotency keys, and it complicates accounting (billing, rate limits, logs show two requests for one user action).
Measurement is hard. Percentiles are expensive to track exactly at high volume, tail values are noisy (the p99.9 of a minute with 1,000 requests rests on one request), coordinated omission silently under-reports, and different tools define latency from different starting points (client, load balancer, server). Targets in service level objectives need a window long enough to be statistically meaningful.
Some tail causes cannot be hedged: a request that is intrinsically large, a lock held by a slow holder, or a stage that has only one replica. In a deep call graph, the tail compounds at every level, and a hedge at the top cannot recover time lost in several lower hops, so deadlines and per-hop budgets must be designed together. Finally, trimming the tail often costs money: spare capacity for headroom, extra replicas, and engineering time to remove GC and compaction pauses.
tradeoffs: Choose hedged requests when the call is idempotent and read-like, you have 5 to 10% spare capacity, and replicas fail independently. Choose tied requests when requests wait in long queues (disks, shared services) and cancellation is supported, because they remove the hedge delay. You give up some extra load and complexity in both; with neither, you accept the tail or buy capacity.
Choose a hedge delay near p95 (about 5% extra load) as a default; choose a later delay (p99) when the system is close to saturation; choose a shorter one only for tiny, cheap requests where a 20% load increase is affordable. A shorter delay buys a lower tail and costs more work.
Choose good-enough responses (return after most shards reply, mark the result partial) when answers are ranked or approximate, as in search or ad auctions where a deadline of about 100 ms is typical. Choose to wait for every shard when correctness requires completeness, such as a financial total, and then pay for it with replication, tight shard timeouts and over-provisioning.
Choose to reduce fan-out (fewer, larger shards, caching, pre-aggregation) when you can, because it attacks the cause: 10 shards instead of 100 changes 63% to 9.6%. Choose more shards when data size or throughput demands it, and then rely on hedging and partial results.
Choose load shedding and a retry budget before adding hedging to any system near saturation. Hedging helps when spare capacity exists; shedding protects when it does not.

value: An API (application programming interface) over a network is a contract: it says which requests a client may send, what the server will answer, and what each answer means. Its design matters more than most internal code because it is hard to change. Once other teams, mobile apps or customers depend on a field name or a status code, you cannot rename it without breaking someone. Good API design is therefore the art of making decisions you will not need to reverse: clear resources, predictable errors, safe retries, bounded lists, and a plan for change.
The common styles answer different needs. REST (representational state transfer) models the system as resources with URLs and uses the standard HTTP verbs, which gives caching, simple tooling and wide client support. GraphQL lets the client name the exact fields it wants in one request, which helps mobile and front-end teams that would otherwise make many calls or download unused data. RPC styles such as gRPC expose operations as typed function calls, which suit service-to-service traffic. Beneath all of them sit the same recurring problems: how to return a list of ten million items, how to make a payment request safe to repeat after a timeout, and how to add a field next year without breaking old clients.
The layers below supply the pieces. HTTP (L3) gives the verbs, status codes, headers, caching and conditional requests that REST reuses. Remote procedure calls and serialization (L3) give the alternative style and the typed message formats. Idempotence (L2) is the property that makes retries safe, and schema evolution (L2) tells you which changes old and new clients can survive. What the API layer adds is the human-facing discipline on top: naming, shapes, limits, and a contract that is documented and versioned.
terms: Resource — a thing the API exposes, identified by a URL, such as `/v1/orders/123`; the API's nouns.
Idempotent — an operation that has the same effect whether done once or many times, so repeating it after a timeout is safe.
Safe method — a method that does not change server state (GET, HEAD, OPTIONS).
Cursor (page token) — an opaque string returned with a page of results that tells the server where the next page starts.
Keyset pagination — paging by "rows after the last seen sort key" using an index, instead of skipping rows by count.
Idempotency key — a unique value the client attaches to a write so that the server can recognise a repeat and return the first result.
Breaking change — a change that makes a correct existing client fail, such as removing a field or changing its type.
N+1 problem — one query for a list followed by one extra query per item, a common cost when a GraphQL resolver loads related data item by item.
Tolerant reader — a client that ignores fields it does not know, which makes additive server changes safe.
history: Early web services used SOAP and WSDL (late 1990s), XML envelopes with heavy tooling. Roy Fielding's 2000 doctoral dissertation described REST as the architectural style that explains why the web scales: uniform interface, stateless requests, cacheable responses, and hypermedia links. Most "REST APIs" that followed use only part of this style: resources and verbs over JSON. Leonard Richardson's maturity model (2008, popularised by Martin Fowler in 2010) named the steps from one endpoint, to resources, to correct verbs, to hypermedia links.
Public APIs set the practices people copy. Stripe's API (2011 onward) popularised idempotency keys and date-based versions that each account is pinned to. Google published API design guidance and, from the late 2010s, the API Improvement Proposals (AIPs) that fix patterns such as `page_token` pagination and field masks. The OpenAPI Specification (from Swagger, 2011; donated 2015) became the standard machine-readable description of REST APIs, with code generation for clients and servers.
GraphQL was built at Facebook in 2012 to feed its mobile news feed, where many round trips and over-fetching hurt on slow networks, and it was open-sourced in 2015. gRPC (Google, 2015) brought Protocol Buffers and HTTP/2 to service-to-service calls. The HTTP standards were consolidated in RFC 9110 (2022), which restates the semantics of methods, status codes and idempotence, and RFC 9457 (2023) defines a standard JSON error body ("problem details"). An Idempotency-Key header is being standardised by the IETF httpapi working group; at the time of writing it is still an Internet-Draft.
how: Step 1: model resources, not actions. Choose the nouns your clients care about (`customers`, `orders`, `payments`), give each collection a plural URL (`/v1/orders`) and each item an ID (`/v1/orders/123`), and nest only when the child cannot exist without the parent (`/v1/orders/123/items`). HTTP (L3) supplies the verbs and their promises. GET reads and is safe and cacheable. PUT replaces a resource and is idempotent. DELETE is idempotent. POST creates or triggers work and is neither safe nor idempotent. PATCH changes part of a resource and is not guaranteed idempotent. Use the status code families honestly: 2xx success, 4xx the client was wrong (400 invalid, 401 unauthenticated, 403 forbidden, 404 not found, 409 conflict, 422 semantic error, 429 rate limited), 5xx the server failed. Returning 200 with an error in the body defeats clients, proxies and monitoring.
Step 2: make the shapes predictable. Use the same field names, date format (RFC 3339 timestamps in UTC), money representation (integer minor units plus currency) and ID type everywhere. Return a standard error body: RFC 9457 gives `type`, `title`, `status`, `detail` and `instance`, and you add a stable machine-readable `code` so clients do not parse messages. Use conditional requests from HTTP: `ETag` with `If-None-Match` returns 304 and saves bandwidth, and `If-Match` on updates rejects stale writes with 412, which gives optimistic concurrency without locks.
Step 3: consider GraphQL when clients differ. A GraphQL server exposes one endpoint and a typed schema. The client sends a query that names the exact fields and nested objects, and the server returns exactly that shape. This removes over-fetching and many round trips. The cost moves to the server: each field has a resolver, and a naive resolver for `orders { customer { name } }` runs one customer query per order (the N+1 problem). The fix is batching with a loader (DataLoader collects keys in one tick and issues one query). Because queries are arbitrary, you must bound cost: limit depth and complexity, use persisted queries (the client sends a hash of a pre-approved query), and rate-limit by computed cost. HTTP caching is harder since requests are POSTs to one URL.
Step 4: paginate every list. Never return an unbounded list. Offset pagination (`?offset=5000000&limit=50`) is simple but has two flaws: the database must read and discard all skipped rows, so cost grows with the offset, and if rows are inserted or deleted during paging, items are skipped or repeated. Keyset (cursor) pagination uses the sort key of the last row as the starting point: `WHERE (created_at, id) < (:t, :id) ORDER BY created_at DESC, id DESC LIMIT 50`. With an index on (created_at, id) this is one index seek plus 50 rows, whatever the depth, and it is stable under inserts. The response carries an opaque `next_page_token` that encodes the last key (and, signed, the filter), so clients cannot depend on its format and the server can change it. Cap the page size, and avoid a `total_count` unless you can compute it cheaply, because counting millions of rows on every page is costly.
Step 5: make writes safe to retry. Networks time out, so a client that did not get an answer does not know if the write happened. Idempotence (L2) is the answer. GET, PUT and DELETE are idempotent by definition, but POST (create a payment, send an email) is not. The idempotency key pattern fixes it: the client generates a unique key (a UUID) per logical operation and sends it in a header. The server, in the same transaction as the side effect, stores the key with a fingerprint of the request and the response. A retry with the same key and the same body returns the stored response without repeating the work. The same key with a different body is an error (422), and a retry that arrives while the first is still running gets a 409 or waits. Keys are kept for a limited time; Stripe, for example, allows them to be removed after 24 hours.
Step 6: plan how the API changes. Schema evolution (L2) gives the rules. Safe changes are additive: new endpoints, new optional request fields, new response fields (clients must be tolerant readers). Breaking changes include removing or renaming a field, changing a type or meaning, making an optional field required, tightening validation, and adding a value to an enum that clients switch on. Versions are the escape hatch: in the URL (`/v1/`), in a header, or by date (a client pinned to `2026-04-01` sees the behaviour of that day, and the server keeps compatibility shims). Announce removals with a deprecation period, the `Sunset` header (RFC 8594), usage metrics per client, and direct contact for big users. Version the whole API rarely, and prefer additive change.
Step 7: protect the service. Use authentication (bearer tokens, OAuth scopes), rate limits that answer 429 with `Retry-After`, request size limits, timeouts, and a documented retry policy (exponential backoff with jitter). Publish an OpenAPI description, generate SDKs from it, and run contract tests so the implementation cannot drift from the document.

example: A shop exposes an orders API with 40 million orders. A mobile app lists orders newest first and creates payments.
Listing. The first request is `GET /v1/orders?limit=50`. The server runs the query and returns 50 rows plus `next_page_token`, which encodes the last row's (created_at, id), for example base64 of `2026-10-20T09:14:03Z|8841207` with a signature. The next request sends that token and the server runs:
```sql
SELECT id, total, status, created_at
FROM orders
WHERE customer_id = :c
  AND (created_at, id) < (:last_t, :last_id)
ORDER BY created_at DESC, id DESC
LIMIT 50;
```
With an index on (customer_id, created_at, id) every page costs about one B-tree descent (3 to 4 page reads) and 50 rows, around a millisecond. The offset version, `OFFSET 5000000 LIMIT 50`, must walk 5 million index entries to skip them, which takes seconds and gets slower for each deeper page. It also breaks under writes: while a user reads page 1, 10 new orders arrive at the top, and the offset version shows the last 10 rows of page 1 again at the top of page 2. The keyset version does not.
Paying. The app sends the payment request with a fresh key:
```text
POST /v1/payments
Idempotency-Key: 6f1c2b9e-52a1-4c0a-9d1e-7e5b0c3d9a11
Content-Type: application/json

{"order_id": "8841207", "amount": 4999, "currency": "INR"}
```
The server begins a transaction, inserts a row (key, request hash, status "in progress"), calls the payment network, stores the 201 response with the key and commits. The phone loses signal before the reply arrives and the app retries with the same key. The server finds the key, sees the same request hash and returns the stored 201 with the same payment ID, and no second charge happens. If a bug made the app send the same key with a different amount, the hash differs and the server answers 422. Without the key the retry would have created a second payment, and the customer would be charged twice.
Evolving. Next year the team wants to split `name` into `first_name` and `last_name`. Removing `name` is breaking. The additive path is: add the two new fields, keep returning `name`, mark it deprecated in the OpenAPI file and the `Sunset` header, track which API keys still read it, and remove it in the next major version only when usage reaches zero.
intuition: An API is a plug standard: once millions of devices use the shape of the plug, you can add features to the wall socket but you cannot change the shape of the pins.
The design rules follow from one fact, that you do not control your clients. You cannot ship all of them at once, you do not know which fields they read, and their network drops half your replies. So you keep resources and fields stable (they are the pins), make repeated requests harmless (idempotency), bound every response (pagination), and change by adding rather than editing. Each rule removes a way for a distant client to break without any change on their side.
mistakes: People often think REST means JSON over HTTP with CRUD endpoints. In fact REST is an architectural style with constraints (uniform interface, statelessness, cacheability, hypermedia), and most "REST APIs" follow only part of it. What matters in practice is not the label but whether resources are consistent and HTTP semantics are used correctly.
People often think GraphQL replaces REST or is always faster. In fact it moves cost from the client to the server: one request can trigger hundreds of database queries, and arbitrary queries are harder to cache, rate-limit and secure. It pays off when many different clients need different slices of the data.
People often think HTTP retries are automatically safe. In fact only idempotent methods are safe to repeat by definition, and even then only if the server implements the semantics. A POST that creates a charge needs an idempotency key, and a PUT that increments a counter inside is not really idempotent.
People often think offset pagination is fine because it is simple. In fact it is slow for deep pages (cost proportional to the offset) and unstable when data changes. Use it only for small, mostly static lists.
People often think adding an enum value or a response field is always safe. In fact strict clients that switch exhaustively on an enum, or that deserialize into closed types, can fail. Document that clients must ignore unknown fields and handle unknown enum values.
friction: Versioning has no free option. URL versions are visible and easy to route but copy the whole API and tempt teams to maintain several versions for years. Header or date-based versions give finer control and push complexity into compatibility code on the server. Either way, the old behaviour has to be kept and tested. Hyrum's law says that with enough users, every observable behaviour (field order, error text, timing) becomes depended on, so even "harmless" changes break someone.
Idempotency storage has its own problems: the key table must be written atomically with the side effect, expiry rules must be chosen, and a request still in progress must be handled. For calls to other systems, the key must be passed on, or a crash between the external call and the commit can still double-execute. GraphQL adds operational cost: query complexity analysis, per-field authorisation, and monitoring by operation name instead of URL. REST's weakness is the opposite: clients make many round trips or over-fetch, and teams add ad hoc endpoints (`/orders-with-customer`) that slowly spoil the model.
Documentation and reality drift. An OpenAPI file that nobody tests becomes wrong, and generated SDKs then spread the error. Consistency across teams is another problem in large companies: one team uses `limit/offset`, another `page/size`, a third tokens. A shared style guide and linting in CI help more than any single technology choice. Finally, long-running work (a report that takes minutes) fits poorly in a request: it needs an asynchronous pattern, with 202 Accepted, an operation resource to poll, or a webhook, and each of those needs its own retry and security design.
tradeoffs: Choose REST with JSON when the API is public or consumed by many unknown clients, caching and simple tooling matter, and resources map well to the domain. Choose GraphQL when you have several client types (web, iOS, Android, partners) with different data needs, the backend is a federation of services, and you can afford query-cost controls. Choose gRPC for internal service-to-service calls where you control both ends, need streaming or low overhead, and can generate typed clients. You give up easy browser access and human-readable traffic with gRPC, and simple caching with GraphQL.
Choose keyset (cursor) pagination for any list that can grow beyond a few thousand rows or that changes while read; choose offset only when the user needs to jump to page N of a small, stable list. You give up random access to arbitrary pages with cursors. Offer a search or filter instead.
Choose URL-path major versions when you expect rare, large breaking changes and want clear routing. Choose date-based or header versions with compatibility shims when you ship breaking improvements often and have a few large customers you cannot force to migrate. In both cases choose additive changes first; every new version is a long-term cost.
Choose server-side idempotency keys for every non-idempotent write that moves money, sends messages or creates scarce resources. Choose natural idempotence (PUT with a client-chosen ID, upsert) when the client can name the resource itself, since it needs no key table. Skip both for cheap, harmless operations where a duplicate is acceptable.
Choose synchronous responses when work finishes in under a second or two, and an asynchronous operation resource (202 plus polling or a webhook) when it takes longer or depends on external systems.

practice: Write the contract first: an OpenAPI file or protobuf service reviewed before code. Pick one style guide (for example Google's AIPs or Microsoft's REST guidelines) and lint against it in CI. Decide on error format, pagination, filtering, field naming, time and money formats once, and use them everywhere.
For every list endpoint, ship pagination on day one, with a default and a maximum page size. For every non-idempotent write that matters, require an idempotency key. Return clear 4xx errors with machine-readable codes, and rate limit with 429 and `Retry-After`. Log a request ID and return it in a header so support can trace a call.
Treat compatibility as a test. Keep a suite of recorded old-client requests and run it against each release, and use consumer-driven contract tests with your biggest clients. Track API usage by client, version and field so deprecations are based on data. Release changes behind flags, and publish a changelog and a deprecation policy (for example, 12 months of notice) so users can plan.
quiz: Which HTTP methods are idempotent by definition? || GET, HEAD, OPTIONS, PUT and DELETE (and TRACE). POST and PATCH are not guaranteed to be.
Why is keyset pagination faster than offset pagination for deep pages? || Offset makes the database read and discard all skipped rows, so cost grows with the offset. Keyset uses an index to seek straight to the row after the last key, so cost is constant, about one B-tree descent plus the page.
Why must the idempotency record be written in the same transaction as the side effect? || If the key were saved separately, a crash between the two steps would either lose the key (a retry repeats the charge) or record a key for work that never happened (a retry returns a false success). One transaction makes both succeed or fail together.

What should the server do? || Reject it, normally with 422, because the key is already bound to a different request. Silently returning the first result would hide a client bug, and processing it would risk a double charge.
Your mobile team wants the order list, each customer's name and the last three items in one call, but the web team needs different fields. REST or GraphQL? || GraphQL fits if several clients need different slices and round trips are costly; add batching and cost limits. value: A spatial index answers questions such as "which stores are within 2 km of this phone?", "which delivery zone contains this address?" and "which two objects overlap?" without comparing the query with every object. The difficulty is that a position has two coordinates (latitude and longitude), and ordinary indexes sort by one key. A B-tree sorted by latitude finds a thin horizontal band around the world; the points you want are scattered inside it. Spatial indexes solve this by turning 2-D space into something a one-dimensional index can handle, or by building a tree whose nodes are regions of space.
There are two families. Grid and curve methods (geohash, S2, H3) cut the world into cells, give each cell an ID, and arrange the IDs so that nearby cells have nearby IDs or shared prefixes. A radius query then becomes "scan a few ID ranges, then check exact distances". Tree methods (quadtree, k-d tree, R-tree) divide space where the data is dense and keep bounding boxes in the nodes, so a query descends only into boxes that overlap the search area. Both reduce a search over millions of points to touching a handful of cells or nodes.
This idea is used everywhere above: Redis GEO commands store points as sorted-set entries, PostGIS uses an R-tree built on GiST, Elasticsearch uses a tree over geo points for `geo_point` queries, and physics engines and ray tracers use grids and bounding-volume hierarchies to find nearby objects. The layers below give the parts: binary arithmetic (L3) for bit interleaving, trees (L3) and B-trees (L2) for the search structures, Z-order clustering (L2) for the curve idea, computational geometry (L3) for boxes and point-in-polygon tests, and metric spaces (L4) for distances on a sphere.
terms: Cell — one region of a grid that divides the plane or the sphere; points inside the same cell get the same cell ID.
Geohash — a text code for a cell, made by repeatedly halving longitude and latitude ranges and interleaving the resulting bits, written in base 32.
Space-filling curve — a path through all cells of a grid that visits neighbours in order, such as the Z-order (Morton) and Hilbert curves.
Quadtree — a tree in which each node covers a square and splits into four child squares when it holds too many points.
R-tree — a balanced tree whose nodes store the bounding rectangle of everything below them; rectangles may overlap.
Bounding box (MBR) — the smallest axis-aligned rectangle that contains an object.
Haversine distance — the great-circle distance between two latitude/longitude points on a sphere.
Candidate set — the points returned by the cell lookup; they must be filtered by exact distance because cells are larger than the query circle.
history: The tree methods come from the 1970s and 1980s. Raphael Finkel and Jon Bentley described the quadtree for associative searching in 1974, and Bentley introduced the k-d tree in 1975. Antonin Guttman's 1984 paper "R-trees: A dynamic index structure for spatial searching" adapted the B-tree idea to rectangles, so that databases could index maps, building footprints and other objects with extent. The R-tree and its variants (R*-tree, 1990; bulk-loading methods) remain the default in spatial databases, and PostGIS exposes it through GiST (Generalized Search Trees).
The curve idea is older still. Guy Morton's 1966 report at IBM used bit interleaving to give grid cells a single number, as a file-ordering technique for a geodetic database. The Hilbert curve (1891) keeps nearby cells closer in order than Morton's curve, because it has no long jumps. In 2008 Gustavo Niemeyer published geohash as a free web service, with short, human-copyable codes, and it spread quickly because it reduced a spatial query to a string-prefix query that any key-value store or text index can do. Redis added GEO commands in 3.2 (2016) on top of this idea.
Larger systems wanted better cells. Google's S2 library projects the sphere onto the six faces of a cube and orders cells on each face with a Hilbert curve, giving 64-bit cell IDs at 30 levels; it underlies Google Maps data and MongoDB's 2dsphere index. Uber open-sourced H3 in 2018, a hexagonal grid with 16 resolutions, chosen because every hexagon has six neighbours at the same distance, which suits demand modelling and surge pricing. Both are now standard for large-scale location analytics.
how: Step 1: turn a position into a cell ID (grid and curve methods). Geohash halves the longitude range [−180, 180) and the latitude range [−90, 90) alternately, starting with longitude. Each halving gives one bit: 1 if the point is in the upper half, 0 if in the lower. Taking 5 bits at a time gives one base-32 character. For Bengaluru (latitude 12.9716, longitude 77.5946), the first bits are: longitude 77.59 is in the upper half of [−180, 180), so 1; latitude 12.97 is in the upper half of [−90, 90), so 1; longitude is in the lower half of [0, 180), so 0; latitude is in the lower half of [0, 90), so 0; longitude is in the upper half of [0, 90), so 1. The bits 11001 are 25, which is the character `t`. Continuing gives `tdr1v9` at 6 characters. Each added character multiplies the resolution by 32 and shrinks the cell. Binary arithmetic (L3) is what makes this cheap: interleaving is shifts and masks, and a Z-order (Morton) value is just the interleaved bits.
Step 2: use the cell ID as an ordinary key. Because the bits are interleaved from the most significant end, all points in the same cell share a prefix, and the cell's sub-cells are the strings that extend it. The Z-order clustering concept (L2) is the same idea: sort by the interleaved value and nearby points mostly land close together. So a B-tree (L2) over the geohash string, a sorted set in Redis, or a column in a Parquet file sorted by cell, all support "all points in cell `tdr1v`" as a single range scan. This is the central benefit: no new index structure, just a new key.
Step 3: answer a radius query. Pick the cell size so that its smaller side is at least the radius; then every point within the radius lies in the query point's cell or one of its 8 neighbours. Scan those nine ranges to get candidates, then keep only points whose exact distance is within the radius. Distance on a sphere uses the haversine formula (metric spaces, L4):
\[ d = 2R \arcsin\sqrt{\sin^2\frac{\Delta\varphi}{2} + \cos\varphi_1 \cos\varphi_2 \sin^2\frac{\Delta\lambda}{2}} \]
with R ≈ 6,371 km. Neighbours are needed because of the boundary problem: two points a metre apart can lie on opposite sides of a cell edge with completely different prefixes (at the equator or the prime meridian, they differ in the very first bit). Prefix sharing tells you points are close; it does not follow that close points share a prefix. A Z-order curve also jumps: consecutive cell IDs can be far apart in space. The Hilbert curve used by S2 has no such jumps, so a region covers fewer separate ID ranges.
Step 4: use the tree methods when data is uneven. A quadtree starts with one square for the area. When a node holds more than a set number of points (say 32), it splits into four quarter squares and passes the points down. Dense areas become deep; empty areas stay as large leaves. A rectangle query visits a node only if its square overlaps the query, so it prunes everything else. An R-tree keeps objects with extent. Leaf entries hold bounding rectangles of objects; each inner node holds the bounding rectangle of its children. Like a B-tree it is balanced and has 50 to a few hundred entries per node, so the tree is 3 to 5 levels deep for millions of objects. A query descends into every child whose rectangle overlaps. Because rectangles can overlap, several paths may be followed, and good insertion and splitting rules (R*-tree) and bulk loading by sorting keep overlap low. The bounding-box tests and point-in-polygon tests come from computational geometry (L3): the tree finds candidate polygons by box, and an exact test on the real shape finishes the job.
Step 5: handle regions and joins. S2 and H3 provide a region coverer: give it a polygon or circle and it returns the set of cells (at mixed levels in S2) that cover it. Index the regions by cell ID, then a point's cell ID lookup returns candidate regions; an exact test confirms. This turns "which of 200,000 store geofences contains this ping?" into a hash join on cell ID. H3 gives `grid_disk(cell, k)` for all cells within k steps, which makes neighbour search uniform because a hexagon has no corner neighbours. Hexagon children do not nest exactly inside their parent (H3 uses an approximate aperture-7 hierarchy), so H3 is not a pure prefix scheme the way geohash is.
Step 6: know the geometry traps. Longitude degrees shrink with latitude: one degree is about 111 km of latitude, but 111 × cos(latitude) km of longitude, about 108 km at 13° N and 55 km at 60° N. So geohash cells are narrower near the poles, and cell area varies by 2 to 3 times across the range of inhabited latitudes. Queries across the antimeridian (longitude ±180) and near the poles need special handling. S2 and H3 reduce distortion; none removes it completely.

example: Find all stores within 1 km of a user in Bengaluru, in a city of 1,000 km² with 2 million points of interest, about 2,000 per km².
Naive scan. Compute the haversine distance to all 2 million points. At roughly 50 ns each that is 100 ms of CPU for one query, too slow for thousands of queries per second.
Geohash with 3×3 neighbours. The query point is `tdr1v9...`. Cell size must be at least 1 km on the smaller side. Precision 6 cells are 1.2 km × 0.6 km, so 0.6 km is too small. Precision 5 cells are 4.8 km × 4.9 km, which works. The nine cells cover about 9 × 23.5 ≈ 212 km², so the scan returns about 212 × 2,000 = 424,000 candidates, though the circle holds only π × 1² × 2,000 ≈ 6,300. That is a 67-fold waste.
Finer cells. Use precision 6 and compute which cells actually touch the circle. The circle's bounding box is 2 km × 2 km, so it overlaps about (2 + 1.2) / 1.2 × (2 + 0.6) / 0.6 ≈ 2.7 × 4.3 ≈ 11 cells in the worst case. These cells cover about 11 × 0.73 ≈ 8 km², so the scan returns about 16,000 candidates, 2.5 times the true answer, and the exact distance filter on them costs about 1 ms. The price is 11 range scans instead of 9. Finer cells scan less data and need more ranges, and the best level is usually the one where the circle spans 4 to 16 cells.
In SQL on a table that stores the geohash as a string column with a B-tree index, the scan for one cell is:
```sql
SELECT id, lat, lon
FROM poi
WHERE geohash6 BETWEEN 'tdr1v9' AND 'tdr1v9~';
```
and the application repeats this for each covering cell, then filters by distance. The same query in PostGIS uses an R-tree and needs no cell logic:
```sql
CREATE INDEX poi_geog ON poi USING GIST (geog);
SELECT id FROM poi
WHERE ST_DWithin(geog, ST_MakePoint(77.5946, 12.9716)::geography, 1000);
```

For batch ad targeting, convert each of 200,000 store geofences to H3 cells at resolution 9 (cells of about 0.1 km², edge roughly 170 m), and each device ping to its resolution 9 cell, then join the two tables on cell ID and apply the exact test only to the matches.
intuition: A spatial index is like a city's postal grid: you do not search every house, you go to the right block and the blocks around it.
It works because space is local: points that are near each other in the world are, in most cases, near each other in the key. The curve methods spend a little precision (some neighbours get distant IDs) to get a one-dimensional order that every database already knows how to index. The tree methods spend structure instead: they let the data decide where boundaries go, so that a query touches a number of nodes proportional to its answer plus the tree height, not to the total number of points.
mistakes: People often think points that share a geohash prefix are always close, and close points always share a prefix. In fact the first is true (a shared prefix means the same cell), but the second is false: neighbours across a cell edge can share nothing. Always query the neighbouring cells too.
People often think geohash cells are squares of equal size. In fact each character alternates between longitude and latitude bits, so cells are rectangles with width about twice the height in some lengths, and longitude width shrinks with the cosine of latitude. Cell area at 60° N is half that at the equator.
People often think the index returns the exact answer. In fact the cell lookup returns candidates. Without a distance or polygon test after the lookup, you return points in the corners of the cells that are outside the radius.
People often think a quadtree and an R-tree are interchangeable. In fact a quadtree partitions space (each point is in exactly one leaf) and suits points; an R-tree groups objects and its boxes may overlap, which suits shapes with extent such as roads and polygons.
People often think more precision always helps. In fact very fine cells split a query into many ranges and make the index larger; choose the level so a query covers a modest number of cells.
friction: Boundary effects are the main nuisance. Queries near cell edges, the antimeridian and the poles need extra cells or special cases, and forgetting them gives missing results that appear only for some users. Cell shapes distort: geohash cells vary in area with latitude, which biases "count per cell" analytics unless you divide by area; H3 and S2 are more even but not exactly equal, and H3 has twelve pentagon cells per resolution.
Updates are another cost. Cell IDs are cheap to recompute when a point moves, but R-trees need rebalancing and can degrade after many updates; moving-object workloads (vehicles, phones) often use grids in memory instead, with periodic rebuilds. Hot cells are a skew problem: a stadium or a downtown cell can hold thousands of times more points than a rural one, so a fixed grid gives uneven partitions, and adaptive structures (quadtrees, or H3 at mixed resolutions) are needed.
Spatial indexes also do not capture road distance or travel time. A point 300 m away across a river may be a 20-minute drive. For "nearest by route" you use the index to get candidates and then a routing engine on the road graph to rank them. Finally, location data is personal: fine cells and device IDs together are identifying, and retention and privacy rules apply to the index as much as to the raw data.
tradeoffs: Choose geohash when you want something simple that works in any key-value store or text index, with human-readable prefixes and range scans, and modest accuracy is enough. Choose S2 when you need a spherical cell hierarchy with good locality (Hilbert order), exact coverings of regions, and a mature library (MongoDB, Google systems). Choose H3 when you aggregate and analyse (demand, density, joins between regions) and want uniform neighbours at each resolution. You give up prefix simplicity with H3 and some readability with S2.
Choose an R-tree (PostGIS GiST) when you need rich spatial queries on a database: intersects, contains, nearest neighbour, and objects with extent such as polygons and lines. Choose a cell-ID column plus a B-tree when you need to scale out across many nodes or files, because cells partition data cleanly and join well, while an R-tree is harder to distribute.
Choose a quadtree or k-d tree in memory for moving points and game or simulation workloads where rebuilds are cheap and data is mostly points. Choose a uniform grid when density is roughly even and updates are frequent; a grid update is O(1).
Choose coarser cells with more filtering when the exact test is cheap and cell lookups are expensive (remote key-value store), and finer cells with more ranges when the exact test is costly or candidates are many. Measure candidate count against the true answer; a ratio near 2 to 5 is typical.
interview: Design "find nearby drivers" for a ride-hailing app with 1 million active drivers updating every 4 seconds. || Updates are about 250,000 per second, so use an in-memory grid or sharded geohash/H3 index keyed by cell, updating a driver by removing from the old cell and adding to the new one only when the cell changes. Why can't you just use B-tree indexes on latitude and longitude separately? || A B-tree on latitude returns a whole band around the globe; one on longitude returns a vertical band. Each is selective in one dimension only, and the intersection of two large result sets is costly. A composite index sorts by latitude first, so the longitude condition only filters within the band. Spatial indexes keep two dimensions together.
What are the problems with geohash, and how do S2 or H3 address them? || Cells vary in size with latitude, Z-order has jumps, and neighbours across edges can have unrelated prefixes. S2 uses a cube projection with Hilbert ordering (less distortion, better locality). H3 uses hexagons with equal-distance neighbours, so rings of cells approximate circles better. Neither removes boundary handling.
How does a query on an R-tree work, and why does overlap hurt? || Start at the root and descend into every child whose bounding box intersects the query; at the leaves, test the actual objects. If sibling boxes overlap a lot, a point may lie in several, so the query follows several paths and the cost rises toward a full scan. Good split heuristics, R*-tree reinsertion and bulk loading reduce overlap.
How would you find the nearest k points, not points within a radius? || Search expanding rings of cells around the query point until you have at least k candidates and the k-th distance is smaller than the distance to the unexplored ring; or use a best-first search on an R-tree or k-d tree with a priority queue ordered by minimum distance from the query to each box. Then verify exactly.
practice: Pick the index by workload. For a few million static points in an existing database, use PostGIS with a GiST index and `geography` type (distances in metres). For large-scale analytics in Spark or Databricks, add an H3 cell column at one or two resolutions and join and group on it; choose the resolution so that cells hold hundreds to thousands of points. For a lookup service on key-value storage, use geohash or S2 cell IDs as keys.
Always do two steps, candidates then exact filter. Query the neighbouring cells too, test the edge cases (near the equator, the prime meridian, the antimeridian, the poles) and compare the answer with a brute-force scan on a sample in a unit test. Store coordinates as double-precision degrees (WGS84), and be clear about the order (latitude, longitude versus longitude, latitude), since mixing it up is one of the most common bugs; GeoJSON and PostGIS use x = longitude first.
Watch the candidate-to-answer ratio and the hottest cells. Partition big tables by a coarse cell and sort or cluster inside partitions by a finer cell, so data skipping works (the Z-order idea in Delta tables). Avoid storing raw high-precision device locations longer than needed, and round to coarser cells when exact points are not required.
quiz: Why does bit interleaving make nearby points share prefixes in a geohash? || Each bit halves the remaining range in one dimension, alternating between longitude and latitude, so the leading bits describe the large region and later bits describe positions inside it. Points in the same region agree on all the leading bits.
Why can two points 10 metres apart have completely different geohashes? || They may lie on opposite sides of a cell boundary that is also a boundary at a high level (such as the equator or prime meridian), where even the first bit differs. So queries must check neighbouring cells.
At 13° N, how large is a 6-character geohash cell, and what precision do you need for a 1 km radius with the 3×3 neighbour method? || About 1.2 km wide and 0.6 km high. @@ streamjoin
value: A stream join combines records from two sources that never stop arriving. In a batch join both inputs are complete, so the engine can sort or hash them and finish. In a stream, any record on one side may match a record on the other side that has not arrived yet. The engine therefore has to remember records, and the central question becomes: for how long? Remember forever and memory grows without limit; forget too early and you miss matches.
Stream joins come in three kinds. A stream–stream join matches two event streams within a time bound, for example an ad impression and the click that follows within 30 minutes. A stream–table join enriches each event with a row from a slowly changing table, for example adding the advertiser name to an impression. A table–table join keeps the result of joining two changing tables up to date. The first kind is the hard one, because it needs state and a rule for expiring it; the second raises a question of time: should the event see the table as it is now, or as it was when the event happened?
The pieces below supply the answers. A join is a relational operation (relational algebra, L3). The usual method is a hash join (join algorithms, L2) in which each side keeps a hash table (hash tables, L3) by join key and probes the other side's table. Windows (L2) say which records can match, event time and watermarks (L2) say when no more matches can arrive so state can be dropped, and stateful operators (L2) say how the buffered records are stored, checkpointed and restored. Together they give attribution (matching exposures to conversions), feature pipelines (point-in-time joins for training sets) and enrichment, in Flink, Kafka Streams and Spark Structured Streaming.
terms: Stream–stream join — joins two unbounded event streams on a key and a time condition, buffering records of both sides.
Stream–table join (enrichment) — joins each event with the matching row of a table, which may change over time.
Interval join — a stream–stream join whose condition is that one side's timestamp lies within a range around the other side's timestamp.
Window join — a join that matches only records of the same window (for example the same 10-minute tumbling window).
Temporal join — a join that uses the version of a table that was valid at the event's time, not the latest version.
Watermark — a marker in the stream saying that no events with an earlier event time are expected; it tells the engine when state can be dropped.
Co-partitioning — arranging that both inputs use the same key, the same partitioner and the same number of partitions, so matching records meet in one task.
Join state — the records of each input buffered by the engine so they can be matched with later arrivals.
history: Joins over streams were studied in the early 2000s in data stream management systems. The symmetric hash join, from Wilschut and Apers (1991) for parallel main-memory databases, was adapted to streams because it produces results as soon as either input delivers a record, with no blocking phase: each arriving record is inserted into its own side's hash table and probes the other side's. XJoin (Urhan and Franklin, 2000) extended it to spill to disk when memory runs out. Babcock, Babu, Datar, Motwani and Widom's survey "Models and Issues in Data Stream Systems" (2002) and the CQL language (Arasu, Babu, Widom, 2003–2006) introduced sliding windows as the way to bound join state.
The question of when a window is complete was answered later. Systems before 2010 used processing time, which gives different answers if the stream is replayed. The Dataflow Model (Akidau et al., VLDB 2015) separated event time from processing time and made watermarks the standard way to reason about completeness. Flink, Beam, Kafka Streams and Spark Structured Streaming then offered stream joins on that foundation. Spark added stream–stream joins in version 2.3 (2018); Flink added interval joins to its DataStream API around 2018 and temporal joins to SQL in later releases.
Kafka Streams brought a different model, based on tables: a KTable is a stream of updates viewed as the latest value per key (stream–table duality, described by Kleppmann and others), so a join between a KStream and a KTable is a lookup. Foreign-key table joins were added in Kafka 2.4 (2019). More recent work, such as versioned tables, makes stream–table joins respect event time instead of arrival order.
how: Step 1: the core algorithm, the symmetric hash join (join algorithms, L2; hash tables, L3). Keep two hash tables, one per input, keyed by the join key. When a record arrives on side A, insert it into table A and probe table B with its key. Every match is emitted at once. When a record arrives on side B, do the same in reverse. Each pair of matching records is emitted exactly once, when the later of the two arrives. This gives low latency, and relational algebra (L3) tells you the output equals an ordinary join of everything seen so far. In a distributed engine the inputs are shuffled by join key so matching records meet in one parallel task; Kafka Streams requires the topics to be co-partitioned, and repartitions them if not.
Step 2: bound the state with a time condition. Without a bound, the tables grow forever. A time condition makes old records useless. In an interval join such as `click.ts BETWEEN imp.ts AND imp.ts + 30 min`, an impression with timestamp x can only match clicks with timestamp up to x + 30 min. A window join matches only records in the same window. These conditions come from windowing (L2), and they decide which records can still matter.
Step 3: use watermarks to decide when to drop. The watermark W says that no record with event time below W is still expected (usually it is the largest event time seen, minus an allowed lateness). For the interval join above, an impression with timestamp x can be dropped when the click side's watermark passes x + 30 min, because every future click is later than that. A click with timestamp y can be dropped as soon as the impression side's watermark passes y, because any future impression has a timestamp above W and cannot be earlier than y. The retention is therefore asymmetric: impressions are kept for the interval plus lateness, while clicks are kept only for the lateness. Event time and watermarks (L2) provide this; stateful operators (L2) provide the keyed storage (heap or RocksDB), checkpoints and recovery that keep the buffered records safe when a node fails.
Step 4: decide when to emit non-matches. An inner join emits only when a match arrives. An outer join must also emit "impression with no click" (padded with NULLs), and it can do that only when it knows no click will arrive. That moment is when the watermark passes x + 30 min. So outer results are delayed by the interval plus the lateness, and they stall if the watermark stalls: Spark documents that if one input has no data for a while, outer output can be delayed. Late records, with event time below the watermark, are dropped from the join (or sent to a side output) because their state may be gone.
Step 5: stream–table joins and the question of time. The table is not a stream of events to buffer; it holds the latest row per key. In a lookup join the engine reads the table for each event, from a local copy built from a changelog (Kafka Streams KTable, Flink broadcast state), or from an external store (Flink lookup join with a cache). This is cheap, but it uses the table's state at processing time. If a campaign's name changes at 10:00, an event from 09:59 that is processed at 10:01 sees the new name; a replay a month later sees today's version. A temporal join fixes this: the table keeps its versions (a changelog with timestamps), and the join picks the version valid at the event's time (`FOR SYSTEM_TIME AS OF event_time` in Flink SQL). It is deterministic and replayable, but it needs the versioned state and a watermark on the table side to know the version is final. Feature stores need this same point-in-time rule so that a training row is joined with the feature values known at its time, not later ones.
Step 6: handle the practical constraints. Deduplicate sources first, since duplicates multiply matches. Keep the join key a single well-distributed field (an impression ID, not a campaign ID, or hot keys appear). Choose the retention to cover the real delay distribution, not the average. When the join window is days (attribution over 7 or 30 days), do not hold it as streaming state at all: write both streams to tables and join in batch or incrementally with a MERGE, or look up impressions from a key-value store keyed by ID when a conversion arrives.

example: An ad platform joins an impression stream (50,000 events per second, 200 bytes each) with a click stream (about 500 per second) on `imp_id`. A click counts if it happens within 30 minutes of the impression. The allowed lateness is 5 minutes.
In Flink SQL:
```sql
SELECT i.imp_id, i.campaign_id, c.click_ts
FROM impressions AS i
JOIN clicks AS c
  ON i.imp_id = c.imp_id
 AND c.click_ts BETWEEN i.imp_ts AND i.imp_ts + INTERVAL '30' MINUTE;
```
The equivalent Spark Structured Streaming code sets `withWatermark("imp_ts", "5 minutes")` on impressions and `withWatermark("click_ts", "5 minutes")` on clicks, and joins on `imp_id` with the condition `click_ts >= imp_ts AND click_ts <= imp_ts + interval 30 minutes`.
Sizing the state. Impressions must be kept for the interval plus lateness: 35 minutes = 2,100 s, so 50,000 × 2,100 = 105 million records at 200 bytes, about 21 GB of payload. A state store adds keys, indexes and overhead, so plan about 2 times: roughly 40 GB. Spread over 20 parallel tasks that is 2 GB each, which fits RocksDB on local SSD. Clicks are kept only for the lateness, 5 minutes = 300 s × 500 = 150,000 records, about 30 MB. If a product manager asks for a 24-hour click window, the impression state becomes 50,000 × 86,700 s ≈ 4.3 billion records, about 870 GB raw. That is the signal to change the design: write impressions to a Delta table partitioned by hour and join clicks in a micro-batch or hourly job.
Following a few events. I1 arrives with ts 10:00 and is stored. C1 arrives with ts 10:12 and key I1: the probe finds I1, 10:12 − 10:00 = 12 minutes is within 30, and (I1, C1) is emitted at once. I3 with ts 10:02 never receives a click. When events with ts 10:38 arrive, the watermark is 10:33, which is past 10:02 + 30 min = 10:32. A left outer join now emits (I3, NULL) and drops I3. Later a click for I3 with ts 10:20 shows up: its timestamp is below the watermark, so it is dropped as late, and this conversion is missed. The 5-minute lateness setting decides how often that happens, and the choice trades state size against lost matches.
Enrichment. Each impression also needs the advertiser name from a campaigns table that changes a few times a day. A temporal join (`JOIN campaigns FOR SYSTEM_TIME AS OF i.imp_ts AS d ON ...`) gives the advertiser name as of the impression time, so a replay next month reproduces the same output.
intuition: A stream join is like a dance floor where partners arrive at random times: each person waits for a limited time, and when the clock says no partner can still come, they leave.
The design is natural because a join needs both records in the same place at the same time. In a batch, "same time" is trivial. In a stream, the engine must keep each record until its possible partners have all arrived, and the only way to know that is a time bound plus a statement about lateness. The time bound is part of the meaning of the join, not a tuning option: "click within 30 minutes of the impression" is a business rule, and it also is the rule that limits memory.
mistakes: People often think a stream join is the same as a SQL join on two tables. In fact a table join sees all rows. A stream join sees rows as they arrive, only keeps a bounded part of the past, and may miss a match that is later than the bound or later than the watermark allows.
People often think the watermark delay is the same as the join window. In fact the window or interval (30 minutes) is a business condition on timestamps, while the watermark delay (5 minutes) is how much out-of-order arrival you tolerate. State is kept for roughly the sum of both.
People often think enriching events from a table gives the same result whenever you run it. In fact a lookup join reads the table at processing time, so a replay or a delay changes the result; only an event-time temporal join is reproducible.
People often think an outer join emits non-matches right away. In fact the engine can only say "no match" after the watermark passes the end of the window, so those rows come late, and a stalled input stalls them further.
People often think state is small because the window is short. In fact state is the arrival rate times the retention: 50,000 records per second for 35 minutes is 105 million records. Compute it before you deploy.
friction: State is the main cost. It consumes memory or SSD, enlarges checkpoints and slows recovery, since a restart must reload gigabytes. Growth in input rate, in lateness or in the window multiplies state directly. Skewed keys (see hot keys) put one key's records into one task. Many-to-many joins can blow up the output: a join key that appears 1,000 times on each side creates a million results.
Time is the second cost. Watermarks depend on the slowest input, so one idle partition or a source with a clock error holds the watermark back and delays outer results and state cleanup; engines offer idle-source detection for this. Choosing lateness is a guess about a distribution that shifts during outages: after a backlog, many events become late at once and are dropped from the join silently, so you must count late drops as a metric. Joining two streams from different systems also exposes clock differences between them.
Semantics are the third. Exactly-once output needs transactional sinks or idempotent writes. Replays and backfills with stream–table lookup joins give different answers from the original run. Changes to the join logic or window usually require starting from scratch with a new state, because old state cannot be reinterpreted. Finally, streaming joins are not always the right tool: if freshness of minutes is enough, a micro-batch or batch join over tables is simpler, cheaper and easy to correct afterwards.
tradeoffs: Choose an interval join when events pair up within a known time distance (impression to click, request to response) and you want exact bounds and tight state cleanup. Choose a window join when you want per-window results (counts per window), accepting that pairs straddling a window edge are missed. You give up edge-case matches with windows and a more complex condition with intervals.
Choose a lookup join with a local cache or broadcast state when the dimension is small (under a few GB), changes slowly, and approximate freshness is fine. Choose an event-time temporal join when results must be reproducible or correct at event time (billing, attribution, training data), and pay for versioned state and watermark handling. A lookup against an external store costs a network round trip (about 0.5 ms), so cache or batch lookups.
Choose streaming state when the bound is minutes to hours and latency of seconds matters (fraud, live dashboards, pacing). Choose a batch or micro-batch join over tables when the bound is days (7 or 30 day attribution), when the late tail is long, or when late corrections are normal; you accept minutes to an hour of delay and gain simplicity and cheaper reruns.
Choose a longer allowed lateness when missed matches are costly and state is cheap; choose a short one when state is the limit and a later correction job can fix the rest. A common design uses both: a short-lateness streaming join for fast numbers, and a daily batch recompute for final figures.
interview: How do you join impressions and clicks in streaming, and how do you bound state? || Key both by impression ID, use an interval join with a 30-minute bound and watermarks on both sides. Why are outer stream joins delayed, and how can you reduce the delay? || A non-match can be declared only once the watermark proves no partner can arrive, which is the interval plus lateness after the event. Reduce the delay by shortening the lateness, advancing the watermark on idle partitions, or emitting a provisional result and later a retraction or correction.
Explain the difference between a lookup join and a temporal join on a changing dimension. || A lookup join uses the dimension's current value when the event is processed, which depends on arrival delay and gives different results on replay. A temporal join uses the version valid at the event time, which requires the dimension's history and a watermark. Use temporal for reproducibility and for point-in-time correctness in feature building.
A join job has run for weeks and its checkpoint is 600 GB. What do you check? || Retention (window plus lateness) against real delays, skewed keys that keep large state, duplicate input records, a stalled watermark that prevents cleanup, a many-to-many key, and state TTL. Consider shortening the window, moving long windows to batch, or using RocksDB with incremental checkpoints.
How would you build 30-day attribution for ad conversions? || Do not keep 30 days of impressions as streaming state. Store impressions in a table (or key-value store) keyed by device or ID with timestamps, and when a conversion arrives, look up candidate exposures in that store (or run an incremental batch join), choose by the attribution rule, and keep a streaming path only for near-real-time estimates.
practice: Start from the business rule and the delay distribution. Measure how long after an impression clicks, conversions and logs actually arrive (the 50th, 99th and 99.9th percentile), then set the interval and lateness from that. Compute state as rate times retention times record size, with a factor of 2 for overhead, before choosing the cluster.
Prefer to join on a single high-cardinality ID. Deduplicate first, project only the columns needed into the join state, and keep records small. Use RocksDB with incremental checkpoints for large state. Monitor state size per task, watermark lag per input, late-record drops, and output delay; alert on a stalled watermark. In Kafka Streams, check co-partitioning, and use a global table for small dimensions to avoid repartitioning. In Spark Structured Streaming, set watermarks on both inputs and a time-range condition, or state will grow without limit and outer joins will not emit.
Keep a batch path that recomputes the join from the raw tables and compare it with the stream output on a sample. This catches late-data loss, wrong time zones and key mismatches, and gives you the corrected figures for reports. For dimensions that change, store effective-from and effective-to timestamps, so any engine can do a time-correct join.
quiz: In a symmetric hash join, when is a matching pair emitted? || When the later of the two records arrives: that record is inserted in its own table and probes the other side's table, which already holds the earlier record.
Why can a click record be dropped from state much sooner than an impression record in the 30-minute interval join? || Any future impression has a timestamp above the impression-side watermark, so a click with an earlier timestamp can no longer match it. An impression must wait 30 minutes after its timestamp, since its click can come up to then.
Why is a lookup join not reproducible on replay, while a temporal join is? || A lookup join reads the table's latest state at processing time, so replaying later sees newer values. A temporal join selects the table version valid at the event's own time, which does not depend on when the job runs.
Impressions arrive at 20,000 per second, 150 bytes each, retained for 40 minutes. How large is the join state? || 20,000 × 2,400 s = 48 million records × 150 B = 7.2 GB raw, about 14 GB with a 2× storage overhead.
When should an attribution join use batch tables instead of streaming state? || When the window is days (for example 7 or 30 days) so streaming state would be hundreds of GB, or when late data and corrections are common. A table-based join is cheaper and easy to rerun.

@@ fanout
value: A feed (a home timeline, a notification list, an activity stream) shows each reader the recent posts of the accounts they follow. The system has to decide when to do the work of connecting posts to readers. Fan-out on write (also called push) does it when a post is created: the system copies the post's ID into the inbox of every follower. Reading is then fast, because the inbox is already built. Fan-out on read (pull) does it when someone opens the feed: the system fetches recent posts from each account the reader follows and merges them. Posting is then cheap, but every read is expensive.
Neither is best for all accounts, because the cost depends on shape of the follower graph. An account with 200 followers is cheap to push. An account with 50 million followers turns one post into 50 million inbox writes, and the follower count of real social networks is very uneven. So production systems use a hybrid: push for ordinary accounts, pull for the few very large ones, and merge both at read time. Twitter described exactly this around 2012: home timelines were built by fan-out on write into Redis, but accounts with huge follower counts were merged into the timeline when it was read.
The choice reaches beyond social feeds. Publish–subscribe brokers are fan-out on write (a copy per subscriber queue), real-time systems fan out an event to many connections, and a notification service decides whether to precompute per-user lists. The layers below supply the reasoning: graph theory (L4) describes who follows whom and the degree of each node, power laws (L4) explain why a few accounts have enormous degree, queueing theory (L4) predicts the backlog when a burst of fan-out arrives, and batching (L2) is how the writes are made efficient.
terms: Fan-out — one event causing work for many recipients; the fan-out factor is the number of recipients.
Fan-out on write (push) — copying a new item to every recipient's precomputed inbox at write time.
Fan-out on read (pull) — assembling the feed when a reader asks, by fetching and merging recent items from each followed account.
Inbox (materialized timeline) — a per-reader list of post IDs, kept ready to serve.
Celebrity (hub) problem — an account whose follower count is so large that push fan-out for it is too slow or costly.
Hybrid feed — push for most accounts and pull for high-fan-out accounts, merged at read time.
Hydration — turning a list of post IDs into full post objects (text, author, counts) by looking them up in a cache or store.
Degree distribution — the count of accounts having each number of followers; in social graphs it is heavy-tailed.
history: Feeds appeared with social networks in the mid-2000s. Facebook launched the News Feed in 2006, and Twitter's timeline grew through 2008–2012, when the number of users and follow edges outgrew the first designs. Twitter's first timeline was computed at read time with database joins, and it was replaced by precomputed timelines in memory. Raffi Krikorian's 2012–2013 talk "Timelines at Scale" described the result: tweets were fanned out to the Redis timelines of followers, with about 800 tweet IDs kept per timeline. In that talk, as summarised by High Scalability, timeline reads ran at roughly 300,000 requests per second against about 6,000 tweet writes per second, so spending effort at write time paid off, and fan-out delivered about 300,000 entries per second.
The same talk showed the exception. Accounts with tens of millions of followers made push too slow, so these accounts were not fanned out; their tweets were merged into a follower's timeline at read time. The academic form of the idea is "Feeding Frenzy: Selectively Materializing Users' Event Feeds" (Silberstein et al., Yahoo Research, SIGMOD 2010). It showed that the best choice is made per producer–consumer pair, based on how often the producer posts and how often the consumer reads, and that a hybrid beats both pure strategies.
Other companies reached similar designs for different reasons. Facebook has described a feed built mostly at read time, with servers gathering candidate stories from friends and then ranking them. As feeds became ranked by machine learning models, the question changed: ranking needs a pool of candidates, so push or pull now decides how that candidate pool is assembled, and a ranking stage (multi-stage retrieval, L2) sits on top.
how: Step 1: count the cost of each strategy. Let a producer post at rate \( w \) and have \( F \) followers; let a consumer read at rate \( r \) and follow \( G \) accounts. Push costs \( w \times F \) inbox writes per unit time for the producer, and each read costs one inbox fetch. Pull costs nothing at write time, and each read costs \( G \) lookups of followees' recent posts. For one producer–consumer edge, push costs \( w \) writes and pull costs \( r \) lookups, so push is cheaper when posts are rarer than reads. That is the Feeding Frenzy intuition, and it also explains why inactive followers are a waste to push to: a follower who never reads has \( r = 0 \).
Step 2: the push path. A post is stored once (post ID and content in a post store). A fan-out service reads the author's follower list in pages, and for each follower appends the post ID to that follower's inbox, usually a capped sorted list in an in-memory store (Redis lists or sorted sets, about 800 entries). Writes are grouped by the inbox's shard (batching, L2), so one network call writes many inboxes. Only IDs are stored, not post content. At read time the service fetches the inbox (one call), hydrates the top 20 or 50 IDs from a post cache, removes deleted posts and posts from blocked accounts, and returns. Storing IDs means an edit or a deletion changes one record, not millions of copies.
Step 3: the pull path. At read time, the service gets the reader's followee list (G accounts). For each, it fetches that author's recent posts from a per-author timeline store, usually cached. The results from G sorted lists are merged by time with a heap (trees and heaps, L3) in \( O(k \log G) \) to take the top \( k \). The G lookups run in parallel across shards, so the read latency is the slowest of G calls, which is the tail-latency problem: with 300 followees spread over 30 shards, a rare slow shard slows many reads. Caching author timelines and limiting G keep the cost acceptable.
Step 4: the follower graph decides the split. In social graphs the number of followers is heavy-tailed (power laws, L4): most accounts have hundreds, a few have millions. The average hides this. The total push work is the sum over posts of the author's follower count, and a tiny share of accounts, those with the biggest F, can produce most of the deliveries. Graph theory (L4) gives the vocabulary (in-degree of the follow graph); the power law says the heavy tail is the rule, so the system must treat the tail separately.
Step 5: build the hybrid. Set a threshold on follower count (for example tens of thousands). Authors below it are pushed. Authors above it are not fanned out; their posts are written only to their own timeline. At read time the service fetches the reader's inbox (pushed posts) and, for each high-fan-out account that the reader follows (usually a few to a few dozen), fetches that account's latest posts, then merges the two sets by time. The read stays cheap because the pull part is small. Also push only to active followers, such as those who opened the app in the last 30 days, and rebuild a returning user's inbox on demand by pulling.
Step 6: manage the queue. Fan-out is background work, so it runs through a queue with workers. Queueing theory (L4) says that backlog grows when arrivals exceed service capacity, and Little's law gives the delay: with a backlog of \( L \) deliveries and a service rate \( \mu \), a new item waits \( L / \mu \). A post by an account with 10 million followers puts 10 million deliveries into the queue. At 1 million deliveries per second that is 10 seconds of capacity, during which other users' posts wait behind it unless you use separate queues, priorities or a threshold that stops the push. Partition the fan-out by follower shard so that workers write to their own shard, and rate-limit the large jobs.
Step 7: handle graph changes. When a user follows an account, backfill the most recent few posts of that account into the user's inbox (or mark the inbox for pull). When a user unfollows, remove their posts from the inbox lazily by filtering at read time. When an account becomes large, switch it from push to pull; the old pushed entries age out. Always merge the user's own recent posts at read time, so they see what they just posted without waiting for fan-out.

example: A social app has 200 million daily readers who open the feed 10 times a day, and about 95 million posts per day from ordinary authors who have 150 followers on average. In addition, 50,000 large accounts post 5 times a day and have 200,000 followers each.
Push everything. Ordinary authors produce 95 million × 150 = 14.25 billion deliveries per day. The large accounts produce 50,000 × 5 × 200,000 = 50 billion. The total is 64 billion deliveries per day, about 740,000 per second on average, and several times that at peak. The 250,000 posts from large accounts (0.26% of all posts) account for 78% of the work. A single post from a 200,000-follower account arrives as 200,000 inbox writes, and at 1 million writes per second that is 0.2 seconds, but five such accounts posting together take a full second, and every other post waits behind them.
Pull everything. Reads are 200 million × 10 = 2 billion per day, about 23,000 per second. If the average reader follows 300 accounts, each read does 300 author lookups, so the system does 23,000 × 300 ≈ 6.9 million lookups per second. That is 9 times the push write rate for this workload, and every read has to wait for 300 lookups across many shards.
Hybrid with a threshold of 10,000 followers. Only the ordinary authors are pushed: 14.25 billion deliveries per day, or 165,000 per second, which is 78% less than before. Each read fetches one inbox plus the posts of the large accounts that reader follows. If a typical reader follows 10 of them, a read costs 1 + 10 = 11 lookups, so reads cost 23,000 × 11 ≈ 250,000 lookups per second. The total of about 415,000 operations per second is far below either pure strategy (740,000 or 6.9 million). Pushing only to followers active in the last 30 days (say 50% of them) cuts the push part again.
Check the tail behaviour. A pull of 10 cached author timelines in parallel waits for the slowest of 10 calls. If each is slow 1% of the time, 1 − 0.99¹⁰ ≈ 9.6% of reads see a slow call, which is why hybrid designs cache big-account timelines in memory and hedge or time out those lookups. If the pull were 300 followees, the same figure would be 95%.
intuition: Fan-out on write is like a newspaper delivered to each subscriber's door before breakfast, and fan-out on read is like every reader going to the newsstand and collecting from each stack, and the hybrid is delivery for most papers plus a stop at the newsstand for the one with a million readers.
The rule is a balance of frequencies. Work done at write time is paid once per post but repeated for every follower. Work done at read time is paid once per read but repeated for every followee. Whichever side has the smaller product of rate and fan-out should do the work. Because follower counts range from tens to tens of millions while the number of followees per user stays in the hundreds, the write side is the one that explodes, and that is where the exception is needed.
mistakes: People often think fan-out on write is always better because reads are more frequent. In fact reads often outnumber writes, but the write cost is multiplied by followers. An account with 50 million followers makes one post cost 50 million writes, and most of those followers may never open the app.
People often think the hybrid is a special case for celebrities only. In fact the threshold is a cost decision on any producer: a rarely read, high-follower account and an inactive follower both make push wasteful. The right test is the ratio of posts to reads, not the follower count alone.
People often think the inbox stores copies of posts. In fact it stores IDs, and the content is fetched from a post cache at read time. Copying content into millions of inboxes would waste memory and make edits and deletions require millions of updates.
People often think a feed is a sorted list by time. In fact modern feeds are ranked, so the inbox and pulled lists form the candidate pool, and a ranking model orders it. Fan-out choice determines the candidate set and its freshness.
People often think fan-out lag is only a delay. In fact it can break ordering and conversation: a reply may show up in a follower's feed before the post it replies to if the two are fanned out at different speeds.
friction: Push has a cost that scales with the largest accounts and is paid before you know whether anyone will read. It creates huge bursts, and a backlog from one large account delays everyone behind it, so you need separate queues, rate limits and a threshold. It also wastes storage and bandwidth on inactive users, and keeping inboxes for hundreds of millions of users in memory is expensive (800 IDs of 8 bytes is about 6.4 KB per user before overhead, or about 1.3 TB for 200 million users, and several times that with replication and structure).
Pull has the opposite weakness: read latency grows with the number of followees and the tail latency of many parallel lookups, so heavy followers see slower feeds. Hybrid is the most complex: two code paths, a threshold that must be tuned and may flip as an account grows or shrinks, and tricky consistency between a pushed inbox and a pulled timeline (duplicates, ordering, a post appearing late). Moderation adds another problem: deleting, hiding or blocking must apply to every copy, so IDs plus read-time filtering are used, which adds work on the read path.
Finally, ranking and personalisation change the economics. A ranked feed re-scores candidates at read time anyway, so precomputed order is less valuable and the precomputed list becomes a candidate source. Many systems now keep a modest inbox and spend their read budget on ranking. There is no settled rule for the threshold; teams find it by measuring cost and latency on their real follower distribution.
tradeoffs: Choose fan-out on write when reads far exceed writes, follower counts are modest, and read latency matters most (a few milliseconds from an inbox). Choose fan-out on read when authors have huge or highly uneven audiences, most followers are inactive, content is personalised at read time, or storage for inboxes is too expensive. You give up write cost, storage and burst control with push, and read latency and read-time compute with pull.
Choose a hybrid when the follower distribution is heavy-tailed, which is nearly always true for public social networks. Set the threshold where the push cost of an author per day (posts × followers) exceeds the pull cost of the same author's readers (reads of followers × lookup); start with a follower count in the tens of thousands and tune from measurements of fan-out lag and read latency. You give up simplicity.
Choose to push only to active users when a large share of accounts are dormant; choose to push to all followers when the audience is small, notifications must be reliable, or reactivation latency matters. Active-only pushing saves 50% or more of writes and costs a rebuild when a dormant user returns.
Choose exactly-once, ordered delivery per inbox only when the feed is a strict log (a chat conversation). For social timelines choose at-least-once with idempotent appends keyed by post ID, since duplicates are removed by ID and order is by time.

@@ authz
value: Authorization decides whether an identity that has already been authenticated may perform an action on a resource: may Alice read this file, may this service delete that bucket, may this analyst see the column with email addresses. Authentication answers "who are you?"; authorization answers "what may you do?". Getting it wrong is costly and common: broken object-level authorization, where a user changes an ID in a request and sees someone else's record, has been the first item on the OWASP API Security Top 10 list (2023 edition).
Three models dominate. Role-based access control (RBAC) gives users roles and roles sets of permissions; it is simple to audit and fits stable job functions. Attribute-based access control (ABAC) evaluates rules over attributes of the user, the resource and the context (department, classification, time, network); it is flexible and scales without role explosion but is harder to reason about. Relationship-based access control (ReBAC), made well known by Google's Zanzibar paper (2019), stores relationships such as "Alice is an editor of this folder" and "this document is inside that folder" and answers "can user U do action A on object O?" by searching the graph of relationships. It fits sharing models like Docs, Drive and GitHub, where permissions follow containment and group membership.
The layers below give the mechanisms. Sets and relations (L4) are the formal view of roles and permissions: a role is a set of users, a permission is a pair of action and resource. Logic (L4) is how rules such as "allow if department matches and clearance is high enough" are written and combined. Graph algorithms (L3) answer Zanzibar-style checks by reachability search. Digital signatures and PKI (L3) make it possible to carry claims in a token that a service can trust without calling home. The layers above use this at every boundary: cloud IAM, Kubernetes RBAC, Unity Catalog grants, API gateways, OAuth scopes and secret stores.
terms: Principal (subject) — the identity making a request: a user, group, service account or workload.
Permission — the right to perform an action (read, write, delete) on a kind of resource.
Role — a named set of permissions, assigned to principals.
Attribute — a property of the user, resource, action or environment that a policy can test (department, sensitivity, IP range).
Relation tuple — a stored fact in the form `object#relation@user`, such as `doc:q4#editor@bob`.
Userset — a set of users named by a relation on an object, such as `group:eng#member`; it lets one tuple grant access to many users.
Policy decision point (PDP) — the component that evaluates policy and returns allow or deny; the policy enforcement point (PEP) is the code that asks it and enforces the answer.
Default deny — the rule that access is refused unless some policy explicitly allows it.
history: Access control began with access matrices in the early 1970s (Lampson's 1971 "Protection"): a table with subjects as rows, objects as columns, and rights in the cells. Real systems store it by column (an access control list on each object, as in Unix file permissions) or by row (capabilities held by the subject). Government models such as Bell–LaPadula (1973) added mandatory labels. RBAC was developed through the 1990s: Ferraiolo and Kuhn proposed it in 1992, Sandhu and colleagues unified the models in 1996, and the NIST/ANSI standard followed in 2004. It matched how organisations assign work, by job function.
ABAC grew from the limits of roles. The OASIS XACML standard (2003) defined a policy language and the PDP/PEP architecture, and NIST Special Publication 800-162 (2014) defined ABAC for practitioners. Cloud providers adopted attribute rules: AWS IAM policies with conditions and tag-based access control, Azure and Google Cloud conditions. Later policy languages include Open Policy Agent's Rego (a CNCF graduated project) and Cedar, which AWS open-sourced in 2023 with formal analysis in mind.
Relationship-based access control was made practical by Google's Zanzibar, described in a USENIX ATC 2019 paper by Pang and colleagues. It served Calendar, Cloud, Drive, Maps, Photos and YouTube, stored more than two trillion relation tuples (about 100 TB), handled over 10 million client queries per second and answered most checks in under 10 ms with more than 99.999% availability. Open-source systems modelled on it followed: SpiceDB, OpenFGA, Ory Keto and others.
how: Step 1: RBAC with sets (sets and relations, L4). Let U be users, R roles and P permissions. Two relations define the system: user assignment \( UA \subseteq U \times R \) and permission assignment \( PA \subseteq R \times P \). A request by user u for permission p is allowed when
\[ \exists\, r \in R : (u, r) \in UA \ \wedge\ (r, p) \in PA \]
In words, some role of the user carries the permission. Role hierarchies let a senior role inherit a junior role's permissions. Constraints such as separation of duties forbid one person holding two conflicting roles (approver and requester). The check is fast, a lookup in two small tables, and the audit question "who can do X?" is easy: take the roles containing X and list their members. Kubernetes RBAC works this way: a `Role` lists rules (API groups, resources, verbs) and a `RoleBinding` attaches it to users or groups; rules only allow, there is no deny.
Step 2: ABAC with logic (logic, L4). A policy is a rule over attributes: allow if `user.department == resource.department` and `resource.sensitivity <= user.clearance` and `request.time` is within business hours. The PEP sends the request context to a PDP, which collects attributes (from tokens, a directory, resource tags), evaluates all matching policies, and combines results with a rule such as "any explicit deny wins, otherwise any allow wins, otherwise deny". AWS IAM works like this: default deny, explicit deny overrides allow, and conditions can compare principal tags with resource tags. The strength is that one policy covers thousands of resources, and new resources with the right tags are covered automatically. The weakness is the reverse question: "which resources can Alice reach?" requires evaluating the policy against every resource.
Step 3: ReBAC with relation tuples and graph search (graph algorithms, L3). Zanzibar stores facts as tuples `object#relation@user`, where the user can be a userset such as `group:eng#member`. A namespace configuration defines rewrite rules that derive one relation from others. For a document, `viewer` can be defined as: directly granted viewers, plus all editors (computed userset), plus viewers of the parent folder (tuple-to-userset, "follow the parent link and ask for viewer there"). A check "does Alice have `viewer` on `doc:q4`?" becomes a search: expand the rewrite rule into sub-questions, look up tuples for each, follow usersets into groups (member of a group inside a group), and stop on the first path that reaches Alice. This is a reachability search over a graph of tuples, run in parallel across the branches, with cycle protection and caching of sub-results. Containment and group membership are expressed once, and permissions follow them: share a folder and everything inside is shared.
Step 4: make it fast at scale. A deep chain of nested groups makes each check touch many tuples. Zanzibar uses a specialised index, Leopard, that flattens group membership into precomputed sets, so a membership test is a set lookup: it served a median of 1.56 million queries per second with a median response under 150 microseconds. Checks fan out to many shards and replicas, so Zanzibar hedges slow requests to another replica. Results are cached with request deduplication so that a popular document's check is computed once.
Step 5: keep checks consistent with content. If Alice removes Bob from a document and then adds a sensitive paragraph, a stale replica of the access list could still show Bob the new paragraph. The paper calls this the "new enemy problem". The fix is a consistency token (a "zookie"): when content changes, the system records a token that encodes a timestamp of the access list at that time; later checks for that content must read access data at least that fresh. Most checks use an older snapshot (older than about 10 seconds in the paper), which can be answered locally, while checks tied to recent content pay a cross-region read.
Step 6: carry claims safely with tokens (digital signatures, L3). A service often cannot call a central authorizer for every request. A signed token (such as a JWT) carries claims (user ID, roles, scopes) and a signature the service verifies with the issuer's public key. The service then makes coarse decisions locally. The price is staleness: a token stays valid until it expires, so revoking a role takes effect only after the token's lifetime (minutes to an hour) unless the service also checks a revocation list or uses short tokens. Fine-grained, per-object decisions still need a lookup, because the token cannot hold millions of object permissions.
Step 7: place the checks where bypass is hardest. Coarse checks (is this scope allowed on this route?) can run in an API gateway. Business checks (does this customer own this order?) belong in the service on every object access. Data checks (row filters and column masks) belong in the data layer, such as PostgreSQL row-level security or Unity Catalog, where a grant such as `GRANT SELECT ON TABLE sales.orders TO analysts` is inherited down the catalog, schema and table hierarchy and combined with filters.

example: A company stores documents in folders. Alice is in the infra team, which is part of engineering. The folder `plans` is viewable by engineering. The document `q4` is in `plans`, and Bob edits it.
Tuples in the store:
```text
group:infra#member@alice
group:eng#member@group:infra#member
folder:plans#viewer@group:eng#member
doc:q4#parent@folder:plans
doc:q4#editor@bob
```
The namespace configuration, in SpiceDB-style syntax, says a document can be viewed by its viewers, its editors and anyone who can view the parent folder:
```text
definition document {
  relation parent: folder
  relation editor: user
  relation viewer: user | group#member
  permission view = viewer + editor + parent->view
}
```
Check whether Alice can view `doc:q4`. The engine expands `view` into three branches. First, direct `viewer` tuples: none. Second, `editor`: only Bob, so no. Third, `parent->view`: find the parent of `doc:q4`, which is `folder:plans`, and ask whether Alice can view it. The folder's viewers include the userset `group:eng#member`. Is Alice in `group:eng#member`? The tuple `group:eng#member@group:infra#member` says members of infra are members of eng, and `group:infra#member@alice` says Alice is in infra. A path exists, so the answer is allow. Bob's check also succeeds, through the `editor` branch. If Alice leaves infra, only one tuple is deleted, and her access to every document in every folder that reached her through that group ends at once.
Compare with RBAC. To express the same rules with roles you create one role per folder and level, such as `plans-viewer`, and add groups to each. With 50 teams, 10 environments and 4 access levels you would hold 50 × 10 × 4 = 2,000 roles to administer, and containment (the doc is in the folder) is not modelled, so every document needs its own assignment.
Now scale. Zanzibar reports about 2 trillion tuples in about 100 TB, or about 50 bytes per tuple. If one check needs on average 5 tuple lookups, 1 million checks per second needs 5 million lookups per second, which is why caching, Leopard-style indexes and hedging are needed. In a per-request ABAC design, the same 1 million checks per second at 50 microseconds of policy evaluation cost 50 CPU seconds per second, about 50 cores, which is cheap, but the "which documents can Alice open?" listing question has to evaluate all documents.
intuition: Authorization is a bouncer with a list: roles are the colour of your wristband, attributes are the rules written on the door, and relationships are the statement "you are the friend of the host's friend".
The three models are three ways to compress the same large table of who may do what. A table of users by resources is far too large to store and to edit. Roles compress it by grouping users and permissions (good when jobs are stable). Attributes compress it by writing a rule instead of listing cases (good when many resources share a pattern). Relationships compress it by recording a few facts and deriving the rest by following links (good when access follows structure such as folders, teams and ownership). Which compression fits depends on how permissions change in your product.
mistakes: People often think authentication and authorization are the same thing. In fact logging in proves identity only. Many serious breaches were logged-in users reading other users' data because the service never checked ownership of the object they asked for.
People often think a role in a token is enough to protect every object. In fact the role says what kind of thing the user may do, not which instances. "Customer can read orders" needs the extra check that this order belongs to this customer, which is an object-level check.
People often think ABAC is just a more flexible RBAC with no costs. In fact it makes "who can access this?" hard to answer, policies are harder to test and audit, and attributes can be wrong or stale. It also needs a trusted source for every attribute.
People often think ReBAC is only for social products. In fact any resource tree (folders, projects, organisations, cloud accounts) with inheritance and groups fits relationships well, and the cloud IAM hierarchy of organisation, project and resource is the same idea.
People often think revoking a user in the identity provider cuts access at once. In fact signed tokens and caches keep working until they expire, so you need short lifetimes, revocation lists or introspection for sensitive actions.
friction: Authorization is on the critical path of almost every request, so it needs very low latency and very high availability. If the authorizer is down, services must choose between failing closed (safe but an outage) and failing open (dangerous); most choose closed with local caching for the common case. Centralising checks creates a dependency for every team; spreading them across services causes drift and holes.
Each model has its own problems. RBAC suffers role explosion and privilege creep: people collect roles as they change jobs and nobody removes them, so periodic access reviews are needed. ABAC policies interact in ways that are hard to predict, and testing requires a corpus of cases. ReBAC needs a graph store with careful consistency and indexing, and the reverse questions, "list everything Alice can see" (reverse lookup), are far harder than a point check. Answering them over millions of objects requires precomputed indexes or filtering by candidate lists from the application.
Data migrations are painful: moving existing permission data (spreadsheets, ACLs on files, hard-coded checks) into one model is slow, and during the transition two systems disagree. Finally, correctness is difficult to prove. A single missing check on one endpoint is a vulnerability, so automatic enforcement points (middleware, query filters, row-level security) and tests that try to access other tenants' data are worth more than careful code review alone.
tradeoffs: Choose RBAC when permissions follow stable job functions, there are tens of roles, and auditors want a simple answer to "who can do what" (Kubernetes clusters, internal admin tools, data platform groups). Choose ABAC when access depends on properties that change independently of the user list (data classification, region, device state, time) or you have many resources sharing a pattern; tag-based AWS policies and row filters are examples. Choose ReBAC when access follows ownership, sharing and containment (documents, projects, organisations), and you need fine-grained per-object control at scale. You give up simplicity as you move from RBAC to ReBAC.
Choose the combination, which most real systems use: roles for coarse access, relationships for objects, attributes for constraints such as region or time. For example, Unity Catalog grants privileges on hierarchical securables (catalog, schema, table) to groups, and adds row filters and column masks as attribute-like rules.
Choose an embedded policy library (OPA, Cedar, a code-level check) when you have one or a few services and want microsecond decisions without a network call. Choose a central authorization service (SpiceDB, OpenFGA, a Zanzibar-like system) when many services share the same objects and you need one source of truth, consistent revocation and audit. You trade a network hop and an extra dependency for consistency.
Choose short-lived tokens with embedded scopes for coarse decisions at gateways, and online checks for sensitive operations (delete, export, payment) where revocation must be immediate. Choose strong consistency for checks tied to content you just changed (the new enemy case), and bounded staleness (a few seconds) for reads that dominate traffic.

@@ funnel
value: Choosing what to show from a very large set is a search problem with a hard budget. A video app may have 100 million items, an ad exchange has hundreds of thousands of eligible campaigns, a web index holds billions of pages. The model that ranks best is usually expensive: it reads many features per item and runs a neural network. Applying it to every item for every request would take seconds of computation for one answer that must arrive in 100 milliseconds. The standard solution is a funnel: several stages, each using a more expensive model on fewer items. Cheap candidate generators cut millions to a few hundred or thousand, a heavier ranker scores those, and a final re-ranking step applies diversity, freshness, frequency caps and business rules.
This is the structure of nearly every large recommender, search engine and ad server. YouTube's 2016 recommender paper (Covington, Adams and Sargin) describes two neural networks, one for candidate generation that picks a few hundred videos from millions and one for ranking. Web search retrieves from an inverted index, ranks with a learned model and re-ranks the top few with a neural model. Ad selection filters by targeting and budget, then ranks by expected revenue, the predicted click-through rate times the bid.
The layers below make each stage work. Embeddings (L2) and matrix factorization (L2) turn users and items into vectors so that "likely to be liked" becomes "close in space". Approximate nearest-neighbour search (L2) finds the closest vectors among millions in a few milliseconds without checking them all. Sorting and heaps (L3, trees) select the top k at every stage in one pass. What the funnel adds is the discipline of splitting a budget across stages and accepting that each stage can lose good items that later stages never see.
terms: Candidate generation (retrieval) — the first stage, which selects hundreds or thousands of plausible items from the full catalog using cheap methods.
Ranking — the second stage, a heavier model that scores each candidate, for example by predicted click or watch probability.
Re-ranking — a final step that adjusts the order for diversity, freshness, fairness, frequency caps and business rules.
Two-tower model — a model with one network for the user or query and another for the item; their output vectors are compared by a dot product, so item vectors can be precomputed and indexed.
Recall@k — the share of the truly best items that appear among the k items a stage returns.
Pre-ranking — an optional middle stage with a light model that trims a large candidate set before the heavy ranker.
eCPM — expected revenue per thousand impressions; for a click-based bid it is predicted click-through rate × bid × 1000.
Top-k selection — finding the k highest-scored items without sorting all of them, typically with a heap of size k.
history: Search engines were multi-stage early. A query first used an inverted index to find documents containing the terms, and then ranked those documents with a scoring function such as BM25. Learning to rank added a model trained on judged examples, which is too slow to run on every document, so it ran only on the top results. Wang, Lin and Metzler formalised this as a "cascade ranking model" in 2011, where each stage prunes the list for the next, trading cost against quality.
For recommendations, the Netflix Prize (2006–2009) made matrix factorization famous, and companies then faced catalogs too big to score per user. Huang et al. (2013) introduced the two-tower structure for search (DSSM), and Covington, Adams and Sargin (2016) described YouTube's two-stage system with candidate generation and ranking, which became the template. Better vector search followed: product quantization (Jégou et al., 2011), HNSW graphs (Malkov and Yashunin, 2016), the Faiss library (Johnson, Douze and Jégou, 2017) and ScaNN (Google, 2020), which made retrieval over hundreds of millions of vectors practical.
Later work addressed the seams between stages. Yi et al. (Google, RecSys 2019) corrected the sampling bias in training two-tower retrieval models. Alibaba added a pre-ranking stage with the COLD system (2020) that chooses features and model size under a compute budget. Today the funnel is the default in recommendation, search, advertising and the retrieval step of retrieval-augmented generation.
how: Step 1: start from the budget. Suppose a heavy ranking model costs 5 million floating-point operations per item, and the catalog has 100 million items. Scoring everything costs \( 5 \times 10^6 \times 10^8 = 5 \times 10^{14} \) operations, which is about 16 seconds on an accelerator that sustains 30 trillion operations per second, for one request. The latency budget is around 100 ms for the whole request, and each server handles thousands of requests per second. So the heavy model can score only a few thousand items. The funnel splits the budget: a very cheap method must reduce 100 million items to about a thousand, and only then does the expensive model run. Each stage's cost per item is higher, and each stage sees fewer items; the total is roughly the sum of (items × cost) over the stages.
Step 2: candidate generation with embeddings (embeddings, L2; matrix factorization, L2). Train a two-tower model: the user tower maps user features and history to a vector \( u \), the item tower maps item features to a vector \( v \), and the score is the dot product \( u \cdot v \). Matrix factorization is the simplest version, learning a vector per user and per item from the interaction matrix. Item vectors are computed offline and stored in an index. At request time only the user tower runs, producing \( u \), and the system asks the index for the items with the largest \( u \cdot v \). This is why the structure uses two separate towers: it makes "score all items" a nearest-neighbour search.
Step 3: approximate nearest neighbours (ANN, L2). Exact search compares \( u \) with all 100 million vectors. An HNSW index is a layered graph where each vector links to near vectors: a search starts at a coarse layer and walks greedily toward the query, visiting a few thousand vectors, and returns the top 1,000 in a few milliseconds. IVF-PQ clusters the vectors into lists and stores compressed codes: the query scans only the nearest few lists. Both lose a little recall: they may miss some of the true nearest neighbours, and the system tunes this against latency. Production systems also add other generators in parallel: co-visitation ("people who watched X also watched Y"), recent popularity, items from followed channels, and rule-based sources. The union of all sources, deduplicated, forms the candidate set of about 1,000 to 3,000 items.
Step 4: top-k selection (sorting, L3; trees and heaps, L3). Every stage ends with "keep the best k". A min-heap of size k does this in one pass: for each new score, compare it with the heap's smallest; only if it is larger, replace it. For random order the expected number of replacements is about \( k(1 + \ln(n/k)) \), so for k = 1,000 and n = 100 million it is about 12,500 heap operations, while the other 100 million items cost one comparison each. This is much cheaper than a full sort at \( n \log n \). Merging the top lists from several generators is a k-way merge on the same principle.
Step 5: ranking with a heavy model. For each candidate, fetch features (item features, user features, user–item cross features, context), run the model, and output probabilities such as click, watch more than 30 seconds, like, purchase. A combination, often a weighted sum or an expected-value formula, gives the final score. The ranker may use features that the retrieval tower could not (cross features need the pair), which is why it is more accurate on the 1,500 items it sees. Most of its cost in practice is fetching features and moving data, not the arithmetic of the network. A pre-ranking stage with a small model can trim 1,500 to 200 before the heavy model when the budget requires.
Step 6: re-ranking for goals the score does not capture. The ranked list is adjusted: remove items already seen or blocked, enforce frequency caps, apply diversity so that the top 10 are not ten versions of the same thing (for example maximal marginal relevance, which picks each next item by relevance minus similarity to those already chosen), mix in fresh items and explore uncertain ones, and apply policy and fairness rules. This stage works on dozens of items, so it can afford quadratic algorithms.
Step 7: remember the funnel's central property: each stage can only choose among what the previous stage sent. If the best item for the user is not among the 1,000 retrieved candidates, no ranker can find it. Overall quality is capped by retrieval recall, so retrieval is measured with recall@k (does the set contain what the ranker or users would pick?), and the ranker with ranking metrics. Training must also reflect serving: the ranker should be trained on data like what retrieval will actually send it, including items that were never shown, otherwise it is biased by the old system's choices.

example: An ad server must answer a request for a connected-TV impression within 100 ms (the exchange's `tmax`). The system holds 200,000 active line items (campaign + creative + targeting).
Stage 1: retrieval by rules. An inverted index maps targeting attributes (country, device type, daypart, audience segment, content genre) to the line items that accept them. The server intersects the posting lists for this request's attributes: 200,000 items fall to about 3,000 eligible ones in under 2 ms. Budget and pacing filters remove those that have used their daily spend or are throttled, leaving about 1,200.
Stage 2: ranking. The prediction model estimates the click probability (pCTR) and, for performance campaigns, the conversion probability for each of the 1,200. Suppose a pre-ranking model of about 1 microsecond per item scores all 1,200 in 1.2 ms and keeps the top 200 by a rough eCPM. The heavy model then takes about 50 microseconds per item, so scoring 200 items costs 10 ms, plus about 5 ms for feature lookups. If it had scored all 1,200, the model would take 60 ms, which is too much for a 100 ms budget that also has to cover network, auction logic and logging.
Stage 3: auction value. The score is eCPM, which for a cost-per-click bid is pCTR × bid × 1000. Three candidates remain: Ad A has pCTR 0.8% and a $0.50 click bid, so eCPM = 0.008 × 0.50 × 1000 = $4.00. Ad B has pCTR 0.3% and a $2.00 click bid, so eCPM = $6.00. Ad C has a $5.00 CPM bid, so eCPM = $5.00. B is first, C second, A third.
Stage 4: re-ranking and rules. The frequency-cap service reports that this device has already seen B three times today, so B is removed. C wins at $5.00. In a second-price auction the winner pays just above the next-highest eCPM ($4.00 from A); in terms of price per impression C pays about $4.00 per thousand, or $0.004 per impression. A competitive-separation rule would also forbid showing two ads from the same advertiser category in one pod.
The end-to-end cost: about 2 ms retrieval + 1.2 ms pre-ranking + 15 ms ranking + 1 ms re-ranking, about 20 ms of server time, leaving the budget for network and the exchange. The funnel turned a 200,000-item problem into one where the expensive model touches 200 items, a thousand times fewer.
Where it can fail: if the pre-ranker mis-scores an ad that the heavy model would have ranked first (say its pCTR depends on a feature the small model lacks), that ad is lost at stage 2. Measuring "of the ads the heavy model would rank in the top 5 if applied to all 1,200, how many does pre-ranking keep in its 200?" gives the recall of that stage, and it should be above 95%.
intuition: A funnel is like hiring: a quick résumé screen cuts a thousand applicants to fifty, phone calls cut fifty to ten, and only the ten get a full day of interviews.
The design is natural because cost per item and accuracy per item rise together, but the number of items that matter falls fast. Spending a lot on the 99.99% of items that clearly do not fit is waste. The cheap early stages are not trying to find the best item; they only need to make sure it is in the shortlist, which is a much easier task (high recall, low precision). The expensive late stage then needs to pick the best of a few hundred, which is where its accuracy pays.
mistakes: People often think the ranker is the part that determines quality. In fact quality is capped by retrieval: an item that retrieval does not return cannot be shown. Teams often spend months tuning rankers while retrieval recall is the real limit.
People often think retrieval and ranking should use the same model. In fact retrieval must be factorised so item scores can be indexed (two towers, dot product), which restricts the features it can use. The ranker can use cross features between user and item because it only sees a few hundred items. Using one model for both forces either an unaffordable retrieval or a weaker ranker.
People often think evaluating the ranker on logged impressions measures the whole system. In fact logged data only contains items the old system showed, so it is biased. Evaluate retrieval with recall against a broad set of held-out positives, and test the whole funnel with online experiments.
People often think ANN search returns the exact top k. In fact it is approximate by design, and the recall is a parameter (for example the search effort ef in HNSW or the number of lists probed in IVF). Measure it against exact search on a sample.
People often think re-ranking rules are a minor detail. In fact frequency caps, diversity, eligibility and policy rules decide a large share of what the user sees and what revenue the system earns, and bugs there are visible immediately.
friction: Each boundary loses information. Stage models are trained with different objectives and features, so their scores are not comparable, and a good item for the ranker may look weak to the retrieval model. Tuning cut-offs (how many candidates each stage passes) is a cost-versus-recall decision with no fixed answer, and it changes as models change. Feedback loops form: the system learns only from what it showed, so it favours what it already shows, and new items or niche tastes get little exposure unless exploration is added deliberately.
Training and serving can differ. If the ranker is trained on impressions but at serving time sees a different candidate mix, its accuracy drops (training–serving skew). Features must be the same in both places, and feature lookups for hundreds of items per request dominate latency and cost. Freshness is another difficulty: item vectors in an ANN index are rebuilt periodically, so a new item may not be retrievable for minutes or hours unless there is a fast path for fresh items.
Latency has a tail. The stages run in sequence, and each calls many services (index, feature store, models), so the request is as slow as its slowest dependency, which calls for timeouts, fallbacks (return the cheap ranking if the heavy model is late) and hedging. Finally, the many knobs make the system hard to debug: when a good item does not appear, you must find the stage that dropped it, so logging which stage removed which item is part of the design.
tradeoffs: Choose a funnel when the catalog is far larger than the compute budget allows you to score (more than about 10,000 items per request), which is nearly always true for recommendation, search and ads. Choose a single-stage ranker when the candidate set is already small (a few hundred) after hard filters, because extra stages only add loss and complexity.
Choose embedding-based retrieval (two-tower with ANN) when you need personalised recall over a large catalog and have interaction data. Choose rule-based or inverted-index retrieval when constraints are hard (targeting, eligibility, budget, language) or data is sparse (new users, new items). Use both in parallel and merge; embedding retrieval finds what the user may like, and rules guarantee eligibility. The cost is more code paths and deduplication.

value: A classifier produces a score, for example "this impression has a 3% chance of being clicked", and you must say how good it is. A single number such as accuracy is often misleading. If 0.5% of impressions convert, a model that always predicts "no conversion" is 99.5% accurate and useless. Classification metrics are the set of measurements that fix this: they count the kinds of right and wrong answers separately, they judge the ranking of scores independently of any cut-off, and they check whether the probabilities themselves can be trusted.
The core quantities come from four counts at a chosen threshold: true positives, false positives, false negatives and true negatives. Precision is the share of predicted positives that are real, and recall is the share of real positives that are found. Sweeping the threshold gives curves: the ROC curve (true-positive rate against false-positive rate) and the precision–recall curve, each summarised by an area (ROC-AUC, PR-AUC). For ranked lists, recall@k and NDCG judge the top of the list. Calibration asks a different question, whether a predicted 10% really happens 10% of the time, which matters whenever the number is used, as in an ad auction where a click probability is multiplied by a bid.
The layers below make the numbers meaningful. Probability (L4) defines these quantities as rates and conditional probabilities and gives Bayes' rule, which explains why precision falls when positives are rare. Statistics (L4) treats every metric as an estimate from a sample, with a standard error and a confidence interval. Hypothesis testing (L4) says whether the difference between two models is real or noise. The layers above use these metrics to judge models, to choose thresholds, and to decide which model ships.
terms: Confusion matrix — the four counts (TP, FP, FN, TN) of predictions against true labels at a given threshold.
Precision — TP / (TP + FP): of the items predicted positive, the share that are truly positive.
Recall (true-positive rate) — TP / (TP + FN): of the truly positive items, the share found.
False-positive rate — FP / (FP + TN): of the truly negative items, the share wrongly flagged.
ROC-AUC — the area under the ROC curve; equal to the probability that a random positive gets a higher score than a random negative.
PR-AUC (average precision) — the area under the precision–recall curve; its baseline is the positive rate, not 0.5.
Calibration — agreement between predicted probabilities and observed frequencies; a calibrated model's 10% predictions come true about 10% of the time.
Log loss — the average of \( -\log p \) assigned to the true label; it punishes confident wrong predictions heavily.
history: Signal detection theory produced the ROC curve in the 1940s, when radar engineers had to trade missed aircraft against false alarms; psychologists and later medicine used it to compare diagnostic tests. Machine learning adopted it in the 1990s. Spackman (1989) and then Provost and Fawcett (1997–98) argued that accuracy hides the trade-off, and Tom Fawcett's "An introduction to ROC analysis" (2006) became the standard reference. Hanley and McNeil (1982) and DeLong et al. (1988) gave ways to compute confidence intervals for AUC and to compare two AUCs on the same data.
Information retrieval had its own tradition, with precision and recall from the Cranfield experiments in the 1960s and later rank-aware measures such as average precision and NDCG (Järvelin and Kekäläinen, 2002). Davis and Goadrich (2006) showed how ROC and precision–recall curves relate, and Saito and Rehmsmeier (2015) showed why PR curves are more informative when positives are rare.
Calibration came from weather forecasting: Glenn Brier proposed the Brier score for probability forecasts in 1950. Platt (1999) and Zadrozny and Elkan (2002) brought calibration methods to classifiers, and Guo et al. (2017) showed that modern deep networks tend to be over-confident. Online advertising made calibration commercial: He et al. ("Practical Lessons from Predicting Clicks on Ads at Facebook", 2014) tracked normalized entropy and calibration because the auction uses the predicted probability as a price.
how: Step 1: build the confusion matrix. Choose a threshold t. Every example with score at or above t is predicted positive. Compare with the true labels to get TP, FP, FN, TN. Everything else is a ratio of these four. Precision answers "when the model says yes, how often is it right?" Recall answers "of the real yeses, how many did it find?" The two pull in opposite directions: a high threshold gives few predictions that are mostly right (high precision, low recall), and a low threshold finds more but admits more errors. The F1 score, the harmonic mean \( 2PR/(P+R) \), is one way to combine them; \( F_\beta \) weights recall \( \beta \) times as much as precision.
Step 2: understand why rare positives hurt precision (probability, L4). Bayes' rule says precision depends on the positive rate. With recall (TPR) and false-positive rate (FPR) fixed, and prevalence \( \pi \):
\[ \text{precision} = \frac{\text{TPR} \cdot \pi}{\text{TPR} \cdot \pi + \text{FPR} \cdot (1 - \pi)} \]
At \( \pi = 0.5\% \), TPR = 80% and FPR = 5%, precision is \( 0.004 / (0.004 + 0.04975) \approx 7.4\% \). A 5% false-positive rate sounds small, but the negatives outnumber the positives 199 to 1, so most flagged items are negatives. ROC measures are blind to this because TPR and FPR do not depend on prevalence; precision does.
Step 3: sweep the threshold. The ROC curve plots TPR against FPR as t moves from high to low, starting at (0,0) and ending at (1,1). A random scorer follows the diagonal, a perfect one goes through (0,1). The area under it, the AUC, has a direct meaning: it is the probability that a randomly chosen positive has a higher score than a randomly chosen negative, so it measures only the ordering of scores (ties count half). It is therefore independent of any threshold and of the scale of the scores. The precision–recall curve plots precision against recall for the same sweep; its area, average precision, is the mean of the precision at each recall step. Its baseline is the positive rate, so a PR-AUC of 0.10 is excellent when the base rate is 0.5% and poor when it is 30%.
Step 4: judge rankings. Many systems show a list, so only the top matters. Precision@k and recall@k count hits in the first k results. Mean reciprocal rank uses \( 1/\text{rank} \) of the first hit. NDCG gives graded relevance more credit near the top by dividing each gain by \( \log_2(\text{rank}+1) \) and normalising by the ideal ordering (the search evaluation concept covers these in detail). In advertising, group AUC computes AUC within each user and averages, because a model that merely separates heavy users from light users can have a high global AUC without ranking ads well for any one user.
Step 5: check calibration. AUC says nothing about whether a score of 0.03 means 3%. Group predictions into buckets (for example by deciles), and compare the mean predicted probability with the observed rate in each bucket; plotted, this is a reliability diagram. Summaries are the Brier score (the mean of \( (p - y)^2 \)), log loss, and the expected calibration error (the weighted average gap between bucket prediction and observed rate). Normalized entropy, used by Facebook, divides the model's log loss by the log loss of always predicting the average rate, so values below 1 beat the baseline. When a model is mis-calibrated, a monotone mapping fits it afterwards: Platt scaling (a logistic curve), isotonic regression (a step function), or temperature scaling for neural networks. Calibration is often broken by training choices. If negatives are sampled at rate \( w \) to speed up training, the model's output \( q \) is too high and must be corrected:
\[ p = \frac{q}{q + (1 - q)/w} \]
With \( w = 0.1 \) and \( q = 0.04 \), the true probability is \( 0.04 / (0.04 + 9.6) \approx 0.4\% \).
Step 6: choose a threshold from costs. If the model is calibrated, the best decision for a cost \( c_{FP} \) for a false alarm and \( c_{FN} \) for a miss is to predict positive when \( p > c_{FP} / (c_{FP} + c_{FN}) \). If a missed fraud costs 100 times a false alarm, flag everything with probability above about 1%. Without calibration this rule does not work, which is why calibration matters more than AUC for decisions.
Step 7: quantify uncertainty (statistics and hypothesis testing, L4). Every metric is estimated from a finite sample. A proportion such as precision has standard error \( \sqrt{p(1-p)/n} \): 7.4% on 53,750 predictions has a 95% interval of about ±0.2 percentage points. For AUC use the DeLong method or a bootstrap, and compare two models on the same data with a paired test (DeLong's test, or a bootstrap of the difference), not by comparing two separate intervals. Evaluate with a time-based split (train on the past, test on the future) so that leakage does not inflate the numbers.

For AUC count the pairs where the positive scores above the negative. There are 5 × 5 = 25 pairs. The positive 0.95 beats all five negatives, 0.90 beats all five, 0.70 beats four (0.60, 0.40, 0.30, 0.10), 0.55 beats three (0.40, 0.30, 0.10), and 0.20 beats one (0.10). That is 5 + 5 + 4 + 3 + 1 = 18 of 25 pairs, so AUC = 0.72. For average precision, take the precision at each positive in rank order: ranks 1, 2, 4, 6 and 9 give 1/1, 2/2, 3/4, 4/6 and 5/9, which average to (1 + 1 + 0.75 + 0.667 + 0.556) / 5 = 0.79.
Part 2: rare positives. A conversion model scores 1,000,000 impressions, of which 0.5% (5,000) convert. At the chosen threshold it finds 4,000 of them (recall 80%), and flags 5% of the negatives: 0.05 × 995,000 = 49,750 false positives. Precision is 4,000 / 53,750 = 7.4%, F1 is 0.136, and accuracy is 94.9%, which is lower than the 99.5% of the model that predicts "no" every time, yet the model is the useful one. The ROC point (FPR 0.05, TPR 0.80) looks excellent. The PR curve shows the real picture: 92.6% of the flagged impressions are wasted. The 95% interval for that precision is about ±0.2 percentage points.
Part 3: calibration in an auction. The model predicts an average pCTR of 3.0% for a segment but the observed click rate is 4.4%. The ratio 1.47 means every bid in this segment is under-valued by a third. The advertiser with the best true value loses auctions to others, and revenue drops, though AUC is unchanged. A monotone recalibration on a holdout set (isotonic regression) maps 3.0% to 4.4% and fixes this without altering the ranking.
intuition: Accuracy is a single grade on a report card, while the full metric set is the doctor's chart: it shows which kind of mistake you make, how rare the disease is, and whether the doctor's "30% risk" can be trusted.
Each metric answers a different decision. Precision and recall describe the cost of acting on the model's yeses and the cost of missing real cases. ROC and PR areas describe the quality of the ordering independent of any chosen cut-off. Calibration describes whether the numbers can be used as probabilities in further calculations such as expected revenue. They are needed together because a model can be good at ordering and bad at probabilities, or the reverse.
mistakes: People often think accuracy is a good default. In fact with imbalanced classes it rewards predicting the majority class. Use precision, recall, PR-AUC and calibration, and always compare with the baseline of "predict the base rate".
People often think a high ROC-AUC means the model is good in practice. In fact ROC-AUC is insensitive to class imbalance, so a model with AUC 0.95 can still have precision under 10% when positives are rare. Look at the PR curve and at the precision at the operating point.
People often think AUC measures probability quality. In fact AUC uses only the order of scores. Multiply every score by 0.1 and AUC does not change, but the probabilities become wrong. Check calibration separately.
People often think PR-AUC and ROC-AUC are comparable numbers. In fact a PR-AUC of 0.2 can be strong when the base rate is 0.5%, while a random scorer has ROC-AUC 0.5 at any base rate. Report the base rate next to PR-AUC.
People often think a gain of 0.01 in a metric on one test set is a real improvement. In fact with a small or rare-positive sample, the noise can be larger. Compute a confidence interval or run a paired test, and check that the result holds across time periods and segments.
friction: Metrics depend on labels, and labels are imperfect. Conversions arrive days after the click, so recent data has missing positives and makes models look worse (delayed feedback). Labels can be selected by the old system (you only see outcomes for ads that were shown), so offline metrics measure agreement with past behaviour, not the effect of a change. Offline gains often fail to appear online, because user behaviour changes and the metric is not the business goal.
Choosing the threshold, the slice and the time window changes the numbers. Aggregate metrics hide poor performance on small segments (a new country, a new device type), so slice-level metrics and calibration by segment are needed. Calibration drifts when traffic mix, seasonality or the data pipeline changes, and it must be monitored in production, not only at training time. Metrics are also gamed: optimising AUC on a fixed test set overfits it.
Some quantities are hard to estimate. Calibration at very small probabilities (0.01%) needs very large samples: to see 100 positives at that rate you need a million examples per bucket. Ranking metrics depend on how the lists are constructed, and several of them (NDCG, MAP) require relevance judgements that are costly and biased. Finally, no single metric captures fairness, latency or cost, so a model that wins on AUC may still lose once those are included.
tradeoffs: Choose precision when a false positive is expensive (a wrongful fraud block, an alert that wakes an engineer), and recall when a miss is expensive (disease screening, security). Choose F1 or F-beta only when you can state the relative importance; choose the cost-based threshold \( c_{FP}/(c_{FP}+c_{FN}) \) when the costs are known and probabilities are calibrated, which is better than any fixed number.
Choose ROC-AUC to compare models' ranking ability when classes are balanced or when you want a measure that does not depend on prevalence. Choose PR-AUC (average precision) when positives are rare and you care about the positive class, as in conversion, fraud or retrieval. Report both when unsure, with the base rate.
Choose log loss, Brier score and calibration plots when the output is used as a probability (bids, expected value, risk). Choose a ranking metric (NDCG, recall@k) when only the top of a list is shown. In ad ranking, choose group AUC or per-user metrics when user activity differs a lot, to avoid rewarding user-level separation.
Choose offline metrics for fast iteration and screening, and online experiments (A/B tests) to decide shipping, because offline metrics correlate with, but do not equal, business impact. Choose to fix calibration after training (isotonic or Platt) when ranking is already good and only the scale is off; choose to fix the training data or loss when the bias comes from sampling or label delay.

value: A search engine returns a ranked list, and "is it good?" has no answer until you define it. Search evaluation does this with a test collection: a fixed set of queries, a fixed set of documents, and human judgements saying which documents are relevant to each query. Given these, any ranking can be scored with a number, so two versions of a ranker can be compared without asking users every time. The same method evaluates product search, recommenders and the retrieval step of retrieval-augmented generation.
The metrics answer different questions. Precision@k asks how many of the top k results are relevant. Recall@k asks how many of all relevant documents appear in the top k. Mean average precision (MAP) and reciprocal rank reward putting relevant results early. NDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain) also handles graded relevance (perfect, good, marginal) and discounts lower positions, so a perfect result at rank 1 is worth more than at rank 8. Which metric you choose is a statement about the user: whether they read the whole list or only the top, and whether there is one right answer or many.
The layers below matter for how much to trust the numbers. A metric computed over 50 queries is an estimate with sampling error (statistics, L4), and comparing two systems needs a significance test on paired differences (hypothesis testing, L4). A test collection is a benchmark, with the benefits and traps of benchmark evaluation (evals, L2): held-out data, reuse, leaderboards that get overfitted. The layers above use these numbers everywhere: search quality dashboards, learning-to-rank losses designed to raise NDCG, recommender evaluation, and recall@k for the retriever in a RAG system.
terms: Relevance judgement (qrel) — a human label saying how relevant a document is to a query, either binary or graded (for example 0 to 3).
Test collection — documents, queries (topics) and relevance judgements; the Cranfield paradigm.
Precision@k — the share of the top k results that are relevant.
Recall@k — the share of all relevant documents that appear in the top k.
Average precision (AP) — for one query, the mean of the precision values at the ranks of the relevant documents; MAP is its mean over queries.
Reciprocal rank (RR) — 1 divided by the rank of the first relevant result; MRR is its mean over queries.
DCG and NDCG — discounted cumulative gain adds each result's gain divided by \( \log_2(\text{rank} + 1) \); NDCG divides it by the best possible DCG so the score lies between 0 and 1.
Pooling — building judgement sets by judging only the union of the top results from several systems, because judging every document is impossible.
history: The method began with the Cranfield experiments led by Cyril Cleverdon in the late 1950s and 1960s, at the Cranfield college in England. Cleverdon built a collection of documents, queries and exhaustive relevance judgements, and compared indexing languages by measuring precision and recall. The final report of 1966 set the pattern: evaluate the system on a fixed collection, not by asking users each time. Gerard Salton's SMART project used the same approach.
TREC (the Text REtrieval Conference), started in 1992 by NIST with Donna Harman, made it large scale. Participants ran their systems on common collections, and judged documents came from pooling the top results of all systems. Ellen Voorhees showed in 2000 that although assessors disagree on individual documents, the ranking of systems is stable across different assessors, which justified the approach. TREC defined standard measures such as MAP. Järvelin and Kekäläinen introduced cumulative gain measures in 2002 for graded relevance, and Burges et al. (2005) used the form with gain \( 2^{rel}-1 \), now the common NDCG.
Web search needed more. Chapelle et al. proposed Expected Reciprocal Rank (2009), and Moffat and Zobel proposed rank-biased precision (2008), both modelling how users scan results. MS MARCO (2016) provided hundreds of thousands of real Bing queries for neural ranking, scored by MRR@10, and BEIR (Thakur et al., 2021) collected 18 datasets to test retrievers zero-shot with nDCG@10. Online methods such as interleaving (Joachims, 2003; Radlinski et al., 2008) compare rankers on live traffic. In the 2020s large language models also began to produce relevance labels, which makes the question of agreement with human judges central again.
how: Step 1: build the collection. Choose a representative sample of queries (often 50 to several thousand), fix the document set, and have assessors judge documents on a scale, for example 0 not relevant, 1 marginal, 2 relevant, 3 perfect. You cannot judge a whole corpus, so use pooling: take the top 20 to 100 results from several different systems and judge that pool. Documents outside the pool are assumed not relevant. This is a bias to remember: a new system that finds relevant documents no old system found is penalised, because those documents were never judged.
Step 2: count hits with precision and recall. For a ranked list and a query with R relevant documents in total, precision@k is (relevant in top k) / k and recall@k is (relevant in top k) / R. Both ignore the order within the top k, so swapping positions 1 and 10 changes nothing. Precision@k suits tasks where the user scans a fixed page, and recall@k suits cases where missing a relevant document is costly, such as legal discovery, or where a later stage needs the right document in the candidate set, such as the retriever of a RAG system. R is unknown on the open web, so recall is measured against the pooled judgements, which gives a lower bound.
Step 3: reward early hits with average precision and reciprocal rank. Average precision walks down the list and, at each relevant document, records the precision at that rank, then divides the sum by R:
\[ \text{AP} = \frac{1}{R} \sum_{k=1}^{n} P@k \cdot \text{rel}_k \]
A relevant document at rank 1 contributes a precision of 1, at rank 10 at most 0.1. Relevant documents never retrieved contribute zero but still count in R, so AP also punishes low recall. MAP is the mean of AP over queries. Reciprocal rank uses only the first relevant result and is the right measure when one answer is enough (question answering, navigational search): MRR@10 is the MS MARCO measure.
Step 4: handle graded relevance with NDCG. Each result has a gain based on its grade, commonly \( 2^{rel} - 1 \), so a grade-3 document is worth 7, grade-2 is 3, grade-1 is 1. Gains are discounted by position:
\[ \text{DCG@k} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{2^{rel_i} - 1}{\log_2(i + 1)}, \qquad \text{NDCG@k} = \frac{\text{DCG@k}}{\text{IDCG@k}} \]
The discount (1 at rank 1, 0.63 at rank 2, 0.5 at rank 3, 0.39 at rank 5) is a model of user attention that falls as you go down. IDCG is the DCG of the ideal ordering of all known relevant documents for the query, which puts the best first; dividing by it makes scores comparable across queries with different amounts of relevant material. A frequent mistake is to compute the ideal from only the documents the system returned, which inflates the score; use all judged documents.
Step 5: average over queries and measure uncertainty (statistics, L4). The reported score is the mean of per-query scores. Per-query scores vary a lot, so the mean has a standard error \( s/\sqrt{n} \) that may be large for 50 queries. To compare system A and B, compute the per-query difference on the same queries and test whether the mean difference is different from zero: a paired t-test, a randomisation (permutation) test or a bootstrap. Smucker, Allan and Carterette (2007) found that t-test, randomisation and bootstrap agree well, while the sign and Wilcoxon tests are less reliable for this. The paired design removes query difficulty from the comparison and is much more sensitive than comparing two separate averages.
Step 6: watch for multiple comparisons and benchmark reuse (hypothesis testing, L4; evals, L2). If you test 20 variants against a baseline at the 5% level, one will look significant by chance, so use a correction (Bonferroni or Holm) or a fresh holdout of queries. If a team tunes against the same queries for a year, scores rise but generalisation does not, which is the leaderboard problem of every benchmark. Keep a hidden set, refresh queries, and track several collections.
Step 7: connect offline to online. Offline metrics are proxies. Online, clicks are biased by position (higher results get more clicks regardless of quality), so click-through rate cannot be read directly as relevance. Interleaving mixes results of two rankers in one list and counts which gets the clicks, needing far fewer sessions than a split A/B test to detect a preference. Learning to rank trains on judgements or clicks with losses tied to NDCG: LambdaRank weights the gradient of each pair by the NDCG change that swapping them would cause, because NDCG itself cannot be differentiated.

example: Part 1: one query, binary labels. A system returns six results with relevance 1, 0, 1, 0, 0, 1. The judged pool contains four relevant documents in total (R = 4), and the fourth was not retrieved. Precision@3 = 2/3. Precision@6 = 3/6 = 0.5. Recall@6 = 3/4 = 0.75. Average precision takes the precision at each relevant document: 1/1 at rank 1, 2/3 at rank 3, 3/6 at rank 6, sums them (1 + 0.667 + 0.5 = 2.167) and divides by R = 4, giving 0.54. Reciprocal rank is 1 because the first result is relevant. If the same three relevant documents had appeared at ranks 4, 5 and 6 the AP would be (1/4 + 2/5 + 3/6)/4 = 0.29, although precision@6 would be the same 0.5, which shows that AP sees order and precision@k does not.
Part 2: graded labels and the ideal list. The top five results have grades 3, 2, 0, 1, 2. Gains are 7, 3, 0, 1, 3. With discounts 1, 0.631, 0.5, 0.431, 0.387, DCG@5 = 7 + 1.893 + 0 + 0.431 + 1.161 = 10.48. If only these five documents were known, the ideal order is 3, 2, 2, 1, 0 with DCG = 10.82, and NDCG = 0.97. But suppose the assessors also found a second grade-3 document and another grade-2 document that the system did not return. The ideal list is then 3, 3, 2, 2, 1, with gains 7, 7, 3, 3, 1 and IDCG = 14.60, so NDCG@5 = 10.48 / 14.60 = 0.72. Same ranking, very different score, because the ideal is computed from all known relevant documents.
Part 3: is a 0.019 gain real? A new ranker raises mean NDCG@10 from 0.412 to 0.431 over 50 queries, a gain of 0.019. The per-query differences have standard deviation 0.09, so the standard error of the mean difference is 0.09 / √50 = 0.0127. The paired t statistic is 0.019 / 0.0127 = 1.49 with 49 degrees of freedom, a two-sided p-value of about 0.14. The gain is not statistically significant: with this much noise, a difference of 0.019 appears in about one test in seven even if the two rankers are equal. To detect a true 0.019 improvement at the 5% level you would need about (1.96 × 0.09 / 0.019)² ≈ 86 queries, and about 175 for 80% power. More queries are cheaper than more engineering on an effect that cannot be seen.
Part 4: judging cost. Pooling depth 50 over 10 systems gives up to 500 documents per query, fewer after overlaps (say 250). Judging 250 documents at 30 seconds each is about 2 hours per query, or 100 hours for 50 queries, which is why collections are built once and reused.
intuition: Evaluating search is like grading an exam: the questions (queries) are fixed, the answer key (judgements) is written by experts, and every student (ranker) is scored by the same key.
The design is natural because it separates two jobs that are expensive to combine. Humans are slow and costly, but only need to judge once. After that, any number of ranking changes can be scored in seconds, by a program. The discount in NDCG is the same idea in miniature: it writes down a simple model of how a user reads a list (attention falls with rank) so that the program can score a whole ranking without a user. What you get is a repeatable measurement, with known bias, instead of an expensive survey for each change.
mistakes: People often think a higher MAP or NDCG on the test set means users will be happier. In fact it means the ranker agrees better with the assessors on those queries. The judgements may not reflect your users, and the queries may not reflect your traffic, so confirm offline gains online.
People often think precision and recall are fixed properties of a search system. In fact they depend on the cutoff k, the judged pool and the queries. Recall in particular needs the total number of relevant documents, which is unknown on large corpora and only estimated from the pool.
People often think unjudged documents are known to be irrelevant. In fact they are simply unjudged, and most metrics treat them as non-relevant, which penalises systems that find new relevant documents. Report the share of unjudged results at the top ranks, and use measures such as bpref or condensed lists when it is high.
People often think the average over queries is enough. In fact the spread between queries is large, so use paired tests and look at the worst queries, because a small average gain can hide big losses on some query types.
People often think NDCG@10 is a probability or a percentage of satisfied users. In fact it is a normalised weighted gain; a value of 0.72 does not mean 72% of users are satisfied, and comparing NDCG across collections with different judging scales is meaningless.
friction: Judgements are expensive and imperfect. Assessors disagree on borderline documents, relevance is subjective and depends on the user's intent, and queries can be ambiguous. Judgements go stale as documents, products and language change. Pooled collections are biased towards the systems that contributed, and a collection built in one decade can under-rate neural rankers whose results differ from the older systems that formed the pool.
Reuse causes overfitting: after many experiments on the same queries, improvements tell you less and less. Public benchmarks such as MS MARCO leaderboards and BEIR can be saturated or contaminated, particularly when language models trained on web text may have seen the data. Metrics also encode assumptions: NDCG's log discount and RBP's persistence parameter are models of users that may not fit mobile screens, voice results or generated answers where there is no list.
Cheaper labels bring their own risks. Click data is plentiful but biased by position and presentation, and LLM-generated relevance labels are fast but can be wrong in systematic ways or favour the answers of similar models; they need calibration against a human-labelled sample. For RAG, the retrieval metric (does the passage with the answer appear in the top k?) does not measure whether the final generated answer is correct and faithful, so end-to-end checks are also needed. Finally, offline metrics ignore latency, freshness, diversity and fairness, which users notice.
tradeoffs: Choose precision@k when the user sees a fixed page and every wrong item costs attention; choose recall@k when the next stage needs the right item in the candidate set (retrieval for a re-ranker or RAG) or when misses are costly. Choose MRR when there is a single right answer; choose MAP when many relevant documents matter and judgements are binary; choose NDCG when you have graded judgements and care about top positions. You give up simplicity as you go towards NDCG, but gain sensitivity to order and grades.
Choose a shallow cutoff (NDCG@10, MRR@10) when users read only the first page; choose deeper cutoffs (@100) for the retrieval stage that feeds a re-ranker. A shallow cutoff is cheaper to judge but hides problems deeper in the list.
Choose human judgements for the core test set and for any decision with money or risk attached; choose LLM or click-derived labels to scale to many queries, after checking agreement with humans on a sample (for example Cohen's kappa or correlation of system rankings). Mix them: a small gold set for validation and a large noisy set for development.
Choose offline evaluation for fast screening of many ideas, and interleaving for fast online comparison of two rankers with limited traffic; choose a full A/B test when the change affects business metrics, user behaviour beyond rank order, or latency. Offline is cheap and repeatable; online is the final authority.
interview: How would you build an evaluation set for a new product search engine? || Sample queries from logs stratified by frequency (head, torso, tail), including hard and zero-result queries. Pool results from several rankers (the current one, a lexical baseline, an embedding retriever) and have trained assessors judge them on a 0 to 3 scale with guidelines, with overlap to measure agreement. Compute NDCG@10 and recall@100, keep a hidden test set, refresh it periodically and validate against online metrics.
Why does NDCG divide by the ideal DCG, and what goes wrong if the ideal is computed from the returned list only? || Normalising makes queries with many relevant documents and few relevant documents comparable on a 0 to 1 scale. If the ideal uses only returned documents, a system that returns few relevant documents can still reach NDCG near 1 after reordering them, because the missing relevant documents are not in the ideal. Use all judged relevant documents.
Your new ranker has NDCG@10 +0.01 over 40 queries. Do you ship? || Check significance with a paired test or bootstrap on per-query differences; with a standard deviation around 0.08 the standard error is about 0.013, so the gain is within noise. Look at per-query wins and losses, run on more queries, and confirm online with interleaving or an A/B test before deciding.
Compare MAP, MRR and NDCG: when do you use each? || MRR when one answer suffices, as in factoid questions and navigational queries. MAP when relevance is binary and you care about finding all relevant items, with order. NDCG when relevance is graded and the top positions matter most, as in web and product search. Each makes a different assumption about the user.
How do you evaluate the retriever in a RAG system? || Measure recall@k of passages that contain the answer (or are labelled relevant), plus NDCG or MRR for order if the generator is sensitive to position. Then evaluate end-to-end answer correctness and faithfulness, because good retrieval does not guarantee a good answer, and context length and distractors affect generation.
practice: Define the user model before choosing a metric: how many results are seen, whether there is one right answer, whether grades matter. Build a small, high-quality judged set first (even 100 to 200 queries) and use it for every change. Report the number of queries, the metric at two cutoffs and a confidence interval or a paired p-value for each comparison.
Make evaluation a pipeline: a script that takes a ranker, runs the queries, computes the metrics with a trusted library (for example `trec_eval`, `pytrec_eval`, `ir_measures` or `ranx`) and a significance test, and stores per-query results. Look at per-query differences and the biggest losses, not only the mean. Track the share of unjudged documents in the top 10 and add judgements for them when it is high.
Keep offline and online linked: check regularly that offline gains predict online gains, and retire metrics that do not. For recommendation and RAG, evaluate retrieval stages by recall@k against labelled positives, and the final output by task-level metrics. Refresh queries and judgements regularly to follow changes in content and user behaviour, and keep a hidden set that nobody tunes on.
quiz: What is the difference between precision@10 and average precision? || Precision@10 counts relevant results in the top 10 and ignores their order. Average precision averages the precision at each relevant result's rank (divided by the total number of relevant documents), so earlier hits and more complete retrieval both raise it.
Compute reciprocal rank for a list in which the first relevant result is at rank 4, and say when MRR is the right metric. || RR = 1/4 = 0.25. MRR suits tasks with one right answer, such as question answering or navigational search.
Why does NDCG discount gains by \( \log_2(\text{rank}+1) \)? || It models user attention falling with rank: rank 1 has weight 1, rank 3 has 0.5, rank 7 has 0.33. A good result lower in the list is worth less than one at the top.
A ranker scores DCG@5 = 8.0 and the ideal DCG@5 over all judged documents is 12.5. What is NDCG@5, and what if the ideal came only from the returned results with DCG 9.0? || 8.0 / 12.5 = 0.64. Using only returned results would give 8.0 / 9.0 = 0.89, which inflates the score because relevant documents that were missed are left out of the ideal.
Why do you compare two rankers with a paired test instead of comparing their mean scores? || The same queries are used for both, so per-query difficulty cancels in the differences. The paired test has a smaller standard error and tests whether the average difference is different from zero.
