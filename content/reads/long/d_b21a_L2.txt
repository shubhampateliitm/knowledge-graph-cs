@@ auction
value: Every programmatic ad impression is sold in an auction that usually takes less than a tenth of a second. A viewer opens an app or a CTV channel reaches an ad break, the publisher's ad server or SSP describes the opportunity, an exchange asks many bidders (DSPs) for prices, and one rule decides who wins and what they pay. That rule matters far more than it looks. It decides whether bidders should bid their true value or something lower, how much revenue publishers get, how much data-science effort buyers must spend, and how stable prices are from one impression to the next. Trillions of these auctions run every year across display, video, CTV, search and retail media.
For the layer above (programmatic advertising and exchanges built on OpenRTB), auctions turn a fragmented market of millions of publishers and thousands of advertisers into prices that update for every single impression, using each bidder's private knowledge of what that viewer is worth to them. For buyers, the auction format decides the bidding algorithm. For sellers, reserve prices and floors are the main revenue lever they control.
The layer below is game theory (L4). An auction is a game: each bidder chooses a bid knowing others are also choosing. Game theory predicts how rational bidders behave under each rule, shows that second-price auctions make truthful bidding the best strategy, and explains, through the revenue equivalence theorem and Myerson's optimal auction, when different formats earn the same revenue and how reserve prices raise it.
terms: Bid — the price a bidder offers, in ad tech usually expressed as CPM (cost per thousand impressions).
Clearing price — what the winner actually pays.
First-price auction — the winner pays its own bid.
Second-price (Vickrey) auction — the winner pays the second-highest bid (plus a small increment in practice).
Reserve price (floor) — the minimum price the seller accepts; bids below it lose.
Bid shading — bidding below your value in a first-price auction to keep some surplus.
Win-rate curve — the probability of winning as a function of your bid, estimated from past auctions.
Dominant strategy — a strategy that is best no matter what the others do.
Header bidding — collecting bids from several exchanges in parallel before the publisher's ad server decides.
history: William Vickrey analysed sealed-bid auctions in 1961 and showed that charging the winner the second-highest bid makes bidding your true value a dominant strategy. He received the Nobel Prize in economics in 1996, partly for this work. Roger Myerson (1981) found the revenue-maximizing auction for independent private values, which uses a reserve price, and with others proved revenue equivalence: under standard assumptions, first-price, second-price and many other formats give the seller the same expected revenue.
Online advertising tested these ideas at scale. GoTo.com (later Overture) sold search ads in 1998 with first-price bids, and bidders constantly revised bids in saw-tooth cycles, because in a first-price auction your best bid depends on others' bids. Google's AdWords (2002) used a generalized second-price (GSP) auction weighted by ad quality, which was more stable; Edelman, Ostrovsky and Schwarz (2007) and Hal Varian (2007) analysed it and showed it is not truthful in general but has stable outcomes.
Real-time bidding for display ads (from about 2009) adopted second-price auctions with floors. Then the market changed. Header bidding (from about 2014–2015, with Prebid.js as the open-source standard) let publishers run several exchanges in parallel and pass the highest result to the ad server. When the same impression is auctioned in several places and the "second price" becomes a mix of floors, fees and hidden rules, truthful bidding no longer makes sense. Exchanges moved to first-price auctions in 2017–2018, and Google Ad Manager completed its move to a unified first-price auction in 2019. Since then, buyers bid with bid shading, and auction transparency (who ran which auction, with which floor and fee) became a major industry topic.
how: Start with the single-item sealed-bid auction. Each bidder i has a private value vᵢ for the impression, for example the expected revenue from showing an ad to this household, computed by the DSP's models from predicted conversion probability times the value of a conversion. Each bidder submits a bid bᵢ. The highest bid wins. The rule decides the payment.
In a second-price auction the winner pays the highest of the other bids. Why does this make truth-telling dominant? Your bid only decides whether you win; it never changes what you pay when you win. If you bid above your value, the only new auctions you win are ones where the second price is above your value, so you lose money. If you bid below your value, the only auctions you give up are ones where the second price is below your value, so you lose profit. Bidding exactly vᵢ is best whatever the others do. This is the game-theory result that made second-price auctions attractive: buyers can bid simply, and the seller gets prices driven by real values.
In a first-price auction the winner pays its own bid, so bidding your value gives zero profit. The best bid trades off two forces: a lower bid keeps more surplus when you win, but wins less often. With w(b) the probability of winning at bid b, the expected profit is (v − b) × w(b), and the optimal bid maximizes this. DSPs estimate w(b) from their own history: for each kind of impression (by exchange, publisher, format, time), they record which bids won and, when exchanges report it, the minimum bid needed to win. Bid shading models then choose b for each auction, often within a few milliseconds. In game-theoretic equilibrium with symmetric bidders, each bidder bids the expected value of the highest competing value given that its own value is the highest; for two bidders with values uniform on [0, 1], that is exactly v/2.
Revenue equivalence says that, with independent private values, risk-neutral bidders and the same allocation rule, the seller's expected revenue is the same under first-price and second-price auctions: bidders shade in first-price exactly enough to offset the higher payment rule. In practice the assumptions fail (values are correlated, bidders have budgets and imperfect information, exchanges take fees), so formats differ, but the theorem explains why the move to first-price did not simply raise prices: buyers learned to shade.
Reserve prices are the seller's lever. Myerson's analysis shows that a reserve above the seller's own value raises expected revenue, even though it sometimes leaves an impression unsold. For two bidders with values uniform on [0, 1], expected revenue rises from 1/3 without a reserve to 5/12 with the optimal reserve of 0.5. Publishers and SSPs therefore set floors by format, deal and audience, increasingly with learned "dynamic floors". Floors that are too high cause unsold inventory; floors that are too low leave money to bidders.
The real pipeline wraps these rules in systems. An SSP sends an OpenRTB bid request with a timeout (tmax, often 100 to 300 ms) and the auction type (field at: 1 for first price, 2 for second price). Each DSP evaluates targeting, frequency caps and budget pacing, predicts value, applies shading, and responds with a price and creative. The exchange applies floors and fees, ranks bids (sometimes adjusting for quality or deal priority), picks the winner and notifies it. Private marketplace deals and programmatic guaranteed deals sit beside the open auction with fixed or floor prices. In CTV, an ad break with several slots (an ad pod) is filled by running auctions for multiple slots at once, with rules for competitive separation (no two car ads in one break) and duplicate creatives.

example: A DSP has a value of $10 CPM for an impression, so $0.010 per impression. From its history for this kind of inventory, it estimates that the highest competing bid is spread evenly between $0 and $12 CPM. So its win probability at a bid of b (in CPM, for b up to 12) is w(b) = b / 12.
First-price auction. Expected profit per impression (in CPM units) is (10 − b) × b / 12. Try some bids. At b = 10: profit 0, because you pay your full value. At b = 8: (2 × 8) / 12 = 1.33. At b = 5: (5 × 5) / 12 = 2.08. At b = 4: (6 × 4) / 12 = 2.00. The maximum is at b = 5, which is half the value; the derivative of (10b − b²) / 12 is zero at b = 5. The DSP wins 5 / 12 ≈ 42% of these auctions and keeps $5 CPM of surplus each time it wins.
Second-price auction with the same competition. The DSP bids its value, $10. It wins whenever the competing bid is below 10: probability 10 / 12 ≈ 83%. When it wins, it pays the competing bid, which is on average 5 (uniform between 0 and 10). Expected profit = (10 − 5) × 10 / 12 ≈ 4.17 per auction. The buyer wins more often and earns more, because here the competition is fixed; in reality, sellers moved to first price partly because second-price prices had become distorted by floors and fees.
Now add a floor of $6 in the first-price auction. Bids below 6 always lose, so b = 5 is no longer possible. At b = 6: (10 − 6) × 6 / 12 = 2.00, slightly below 2.08 but the best available. The floor forces the DSP to bid $6 instead of $5: the seller gains $1 CPM on every won impression, at the cost of losing impressions to bidders whose values are below $6. This is the reserve-price trade-off from Myerson's theory, seen from the buyer's side.
intuition: The rules decide whether honesty is the best strategy: when your bid only decides whether you win and not what you pay, you should simply say what the item is worth to you.
Seen this way, every auction design is an answer to "how much strategy do we want bidders to need?". Second-price auctions remove strategy from the price and leave bidders to estimate value. First-price auctions are simpler to explain and to audit when several auctions are chained, because the price is just the bid, but they push the strategic work onto every buyer, who must learn the competition. Reserve prices are the seller's natural response to uncertainty: occasionally losing a sale is worth it to stop low bids from setting the price.
mistakes: People often think first-price auctions must give sellers more money, because winners pay their full bid. In fact rational bidders shade their bids, and under standard assumptions expected revenue is the same as in second-price auctions. The 2019 industry switch changed who does the strategic work more than the total amount paid.
People often think a second-price auction is always truthful in ad tech. In fact truthfulness only holds for a single, clean second-price auction. With soft floors, fees taken from the clearing price, chained auctions (header bidding followed by the ad server), or "last look" advantages for one bidder, bidding true value is no longer optimal.
People often think that the bid equals the bidder's value. In first-price markets the bid is value minus shading. Reading winning bids as "what the impression was worth" underestimates value and misleads publisher analytics.
People often think higher floors always mean higher revenue. Above the optimum, floors cut fill rate faster than they raise prices, and buyers adapt by moving spend to other supply paths.
friction: First-price auctions require every buyer to estimate the competition, which favours large buyers with more data and better models. Win-rate curves change with time of day, season, exchange behaviour and other buyers' algorithms, so shading models drift. Exchanges report different feedback (some give the minimum bid to win, others nothing), which makes learning harder.
The supply chain is opaque. The same impression may be offered through several SSPs and resellers, each running its own auction with its own floors and fees, so buyers see duplicate requests and cannot easily tell where an auction really happened. Initiatives such as ads.txt, sellers.json and the SupplyChain object help but do not fully solve this. Regulators have also looked closely at auction practices: the US Department of Justice's case against Google's ad tech business, decided in the government's favour on liability in April 2025, focused largely on exchange and ad server conduct.
In CTV, auctions for ad pods, competitive separation, frequency capping across devices and household-level identity make the problem combinatorial, and many CTV deals still run outside the open auction.
practice: As a buyer, treat bid shading as a measured model, not a fixed discount: estimate win-rate curves per supply path and context, log the minimum bid to win when exchanges send it, and test shading changes with holdouts that measure both cost and outcomes. Keep a clear separation between value prediction (how much is this impression worth) and bidding (how much should I offer).
As a seller, test floors experimentally: compare revenue, fill rate and bid density per segment, and avoid floors that only push demand to other paths. Publish ads.txt and sellers.json accurately, and limit the number of resellers so buyers see clean supply.
For both sides, log the full auction context (auction type, floor, number of bids, clearing price, fees) for each impression you can see. Most disputes and most optimization opportunities in programmatic advertising come down to understanding exactly which auction happened.
quiz: Why is bidding your true value a dominant strategy in a single second-price auction? || Your bid only decides whether you win, not what you pay. Bidding higher only adds wins where you would pay more than your value; bidding lower only removes wins where you would have made a profit.
What does revenue equivalence say about first-price versus second-price auctions? || Under independent private values, risk-neutral bidders and the same winner rule, both give the seller the same expected revenue, because bidders shade their first-price bids exactly enough to offset paying their own bid.
Your value is $8 CPM and the highest competing bid is uniform between $0 and $10. What first-price bid maximizes expected profit, and how often do you win? || Maximize (8 − b) × b/10, which peaks at b = $4. You win 4/10 = 40% of the auctions.
Why did header bidding push the industry toward first-price auctions? || The same impression went through several auctions in sequence or in parallel, and the second price was distorted by floors and fees. A first-price rule makes the price simply the bid, which is easier to compare and audit across chained auctions.
A publisher raises its floor from $3 to $9 CPM and revenue falls. What probably happened? || The floor rose above the revenue-maximizing reserve: many bids fell below it, so fill rate dropped more than prices rose, and some buyers moved spend to other supply paths.

@@ pacing
value: Budget pacing decides how fast a campaign spends its money. An advertiser books $24,000 a day for a CTV campaign. Without pacing, a DSP would win every auction it can afford from midnight onward and could spend the whole budget by mid-morning, missing the evening prime-time audience the campaign was planned for, and often paying high prices in the rush. Pacing spreads spend over the day (or the whole flight) according to a plan, so the campaign keeps reaching people, buys impressions at better prices, and ends close to its budget without overspending.
Every DSP, ad server and retail media platform has a pacing system, and it touches every bid. For the layer above (programmatic advertising), pacing is what turns a budget, a flight and a goal into thousands of per-auction decisions per second: should we take part in this auction, and how much should we bid? It interacts with bid shading, frequency caps and campaign goals such as cost per completed view.
Two lower layers explain it. Control theory (L4) provides the feedback loop: measure the error between planned and actual spend and adjust a control variable, with the classic proportional–integral–derivative (PID) ideas about responsiveness, stability and delay. Linear programming and duality (L4) give the optimal answer for the whole budget problem: the best policy is to bid value scaled by a single "price of budget" λ, the dual variable of the budget constraint, which is exactly the knob a pacing controller tunes.
terms: Budget — the maximum spend for a period (daily or the whole flight).
Flight — the dates during which a campaign runs.
Spend curve (target curve) — how much should have been spent by each point in time, usually shaped by forecast traffic.
Even pacing versus ASAP — spreading spend over the period versus spending as fast as possible.
Throttling — pacing by randomly skipping auctions, with a participation probability p.
Bid modification — pacing by multiplying bids by a factor, so the campaign wins fewer and cheaper impressions.
Pacing multiplier (λ) — the dual price of budget; the optimal bid is value / (1 + λ).
PID controller — a feedback rule using the current error (P), the accumulated error (I) and the error's rate of change (D).
Overdelivery — spending above budget, usually because of reporting delays or many auctions won at once.
history: Early ad servers in the late 1990s and 2000s delivered guaranteed campaigns by spreading impressions evenly over days, using simple rules ("serve at most N per hour"). Search advertising brought budgets to auctions: Google's AdWords let advertisers set daily budgets, and the system had to decide when an advertiser should take part in auctions so the budget lasted. Mehta, Saberi, Vazirani and Vazirani's "AdWords and Generalized Online Matching" (2005, journal version 2007) studied the theoretical version of this problem and gave an algorithm that bids by scaling value with remaining budget.
Real-time bidding made pacing a core DSP function. Industry papers described practical systems: LinkedIn's budget pacing with throttling (Agarwal and others, KDD 2014), Yahoo's "Smart Pacing" (Xu and others, KDD 2015), which combined throttling with performance goals. Research on repeated auctions with budgets showed that the optimal strategy has the form bid = value / (1 + λ) and that λ can be learned online by a simple dual-gradient update with strong guarantees (Balseiro and Gur, 2019). Conitzer and others (from 2018) studied "pacing equilibria", what happens when every advertiser in a market uses multiplicative pacing at the same time, a description of how platforms such as Meta's ad system actually behave. The move to first-price auctions combined pacing with bid shading, and CTV added pod-level decisions and household frequency caps.
how: Pacing has two parts: a plan (where spend should be at each moment) and a controller (how to steer actual spend toward the plan).
The plan starts from a traffic forecast. If 8% of a day's eligible impressions arrive between 20:00 and 21:00, the target for that hour is 8% of the daily budget. The target curve is the running total: by 12:00, perhaps 35% should be spent; by 21:00, 80%. Even pacing follows the traffic shape; front-loaded or back-loaded plans deliberately shift spend. Flight-level pacing redistributes unspent budget from earlier days across the remaining days.
The controller compares actual spend with the target, every few seconds or minutes, and adjusts a control variable. There are two common choices. Throttling keeps bids unchanged and sets a participation probability p: for each eligible auction, the campaign joins with probability p. Bid modification keeps participation and scales bids by a multiplier. Throttling is simple and keeps prices and win rates unchanged, but it skips cheap and valuable impressions as often as expensive ones. Bid modification is more efficient, because lowering bids naturally drops the impressions with the lowest value per cost first, but it changes win rates, prices and shading behaviour.
Linear programming explains why bid modification is the better principle. Write the day as a set of impressions i with value vᵢ and cost cᵢ if won. The campaign wants to maximize total value Σ vᵢxᵢ subject to total cost Σ cᵢxᵢ ≤ B, with xᵢ between 0 and 1. The dual of this linear program has a single variable λ ≥ 0 for the budget constraint. The optimal decision is to buy impression i exactly when vᵢ ≥ (1 + λ) cᵢ, the value per unit of cost is high enough; in an auction this becomes "bid vᵢ / (1 + λ)". λ is the price of a unit of budget: 0 when the budget is plentiful, larger when it is tight. Pacing is then the problem of finding λ online, without knowing the future impressions.
Control theory supplies the update. A simple dual-gradient rule, run every period t, is λ ← max(0, λ + η × (spend_t − target_t)): overspending raises λ and lowers bids; underspending lowers λ toward 0. This is an integral controller: λ accumulates past errors. Practical systems add a proportional term for faster response to large errors, damping so that λ does not oscillate, and limits on how fast λ may move. The usual control-theory lessons apply. Gains that are too high cause oscillation, overspend, then underspend; gains that are too low react slowly to traffic spikes. Delay is the main enemy: if spend is only known after 2 to 5 minutes, because win notifications, impression beacons or, in CTV, ad playback confirmations arrive late, the controller keeps pushing in the old direction and overshoots.
Real systems add many guards. They estimate in-flight spend (bids sent but not yet confirmed) to avoid overdelivery when one campaign wins thousands of auctions at once. They use hard caps near the end of the day. They coordinate many servers bidding for the same campaign, usually by sharing λ and spend through a central budget service with local allowances. They pace several campaigns of the same advertiser that compete for the same users, where pacing one changes the prices the others see. And they combine pacing with goals: a cost-per-view target adds a second constraint and a second dual variable.
The distributed part deserves a concrete picture. A DSP may answer 1 to 5 million bid requests per second across hundreds of bidder servers, and it cannot ask a central database before every bid. A common design gives each server a small local allowance (for example "you may spend up to $5 more for campaign 123 in the next 10 seconds") and the current λ. Servers spend from their allowance locally and report wins asynchronously; a budget service aggregates spend every few seconds, recomputes λ and refills allowances. The allowance size is a trade-off: large allowances reduce coordination traffic but let the fleet overshoot by up to (number of servers × allowance) when traffic spikes; small allowances keep spend tight but make the budget service a hot spot. CTV adds its own twist: an impression is only billable after the ad actually plays, often confirmed seconds or minutes later through server-side ad insertion beacons, so the controller must estimate how many won bids will turn into billed impressions.

example: A CTV campaign has a daily budget of $24,000. The forecast says 30% of the day's eligible traffic arrives by 12:00, and the controller updates every 10 minutes. The current pacing multiplier is λ = 0.25, so a household the model values at $25 CPM gets a bid of 25 / 1.25 = $20 CPM (before bid shading).
At 12:00 the campaign has spent $8,400, which is 35% of the budget, while the target is 30%, $7,200. The error is +$1,200. With an integral gain of 0.0001 per dollar, λ rises by 0.12, to 0.37. The same $25 household now gets a bid of 25 / 1.37 ≈ $18.25 CPM, about 9% lower. Lower bids win fewer auctions and the cheapest wins, so the spend rate drops.
Suppose the delay in spend reporting is 5 minutes and the controller instead updated every minute with a gain five times larger. By the time the reduced spend showed up, it would already have raised λ several more times, cutting bids by 30% or more, then the campaign would underspend in the afternoon and the controller would swing back. This oscillation is the practical reason for small gains, damping and in-flight spend estimates.
Now check the end of the day. If the evening forecast is right and λ settles near 0.35, the campaign spends about $23,700, 98.8% of the budget. If an unexpected sports final doubles evening traffic, λ rises further and the campaign still ends close to $24,000 instead of spending the extra traffic. That is pacing doing its job: the budget is a hard limit, and the controller converts a shortage of budget into a higher bar for which impressions are worth buying.
intuition: Cruise control for spending: measure how far you are from where you should be, and press the accelerator (bids) a little more or a little less, gently enough that the car does not lurch.
The dual view makes it feel natural. A budget is just a price you put on your own money for the day: if money is scarce, every impression must clear a higher bar. Lowering bids by a single factor is the fair way to apply that bar to all impressions at once, keeping the most valuable per dollar and dropping the rest. Feedback is needed because nobody knows tomorrow's traffic, and the standard lessons of feedback control (react proportionally, avoid overreacting to delayed signals) carry over directly.
mistakes: People often think pacing means spending the same amount every hour. Good pacing follows the shape of available traffic and value; a flat hourly spend overpays at night when supply is thin and underspends in prime time.
People often think throttling and bid lowering are equivalent ways to slow spend. Throttling drops random auctions, including the cheapest and most valuable, while lowering bids drops the least valuable per dollar first. For the same spend, bid modification usually buys more value.
People often think overspend comes from bad forecasts. More often it comes from delay: reporting lag and many simultaneous wins across servers mean the controller acts on old information. Estimating in-flight spend and capping local allowances fix more overspend than better forecasts.
People often think pacing is independent of the auction format. In first-price auctions, the bid sent is value / (1 + λ) and then shaded, and both λ and the shading model react to the same win-rate changes. Tuning them separately can make them fight each other.
friction: Traffic is uncertain and changes fast: live sports, news and app outages move supply by large factors within minutes. Spend reporting is delayed and noisy, especially in CTV, where impression confirmation depends on ad playback and server-side ad insertion. Many campaigns from the same advertiser, or from many advertisers using the same DSP, compete for the same users, so one campaign's pacing changes the prices others face; equilibrium can be unstable or unfair.
Goals conflict. Advertisers want even delivery, low cost per outcome, reach across households, frequency caps, and full budget use, and these cannot all be maximized at once. End-of-flight behaviour is a constant source of complaints: catching up underspend late often means overpaying. Distributed implementation is hard as well: hundreds of bidding servers must share budget state in milliseconds without a single slow budget service in the bid path.
practice: Separate the plan from the controller: forecast traffic per campaign segment (hour of day, day of week, special events), build the target curve from it, and let the controller handle the error. Prefer bid modification (a multiplier on value) over pure throttling for efficiency, and keep throttling for situations where bid changes are not allowed (fixed-price deals).
Measure and reduce delay: track in-flight spend, use win notifications rather than billing data for control, and set conservative local allowances on each bidding server. Tune gains with simulation using replayed traffic, and review oscillation as well as final delivery. Add hard caps near the budget limit and decide explicitly what to do with underspend (roll over, give up, or catch up within limits).
Report pacing health to account teams in simple terms: percent of budget spent versus target, current multiplier, and why (traffic, competition, targeting). Most "why is my campaign not delivering?" tickets are answered by those three numbers.
quiz: What is the difference between throttling and bid modification? || Throttling joins a random fraction p of auctions with unchanged bids. Bid modification joins all auctions but scales bids down by 1/(1 + λ), which drops the least valuable impressions per dollar first.
Why is the optimal budget-constrained bid of the form value / (1 + λ)? || In the linear program "maximize value subject to total cost ≤ budget", the budget constraint's dual variable λ prices each unit of spend. Buying is worth it only when value ≥ (1 + λ) × cost, which in an auction means bidding value / (1 + λ).
A campaign's λ is 0.5 and the value is $30 CPM. Spend runs $2,000 ahead of target and the gain is 0.0001 per dollar. What are the new λ and bid? || λ = 0.5 + 0.2 = 0.7. The bid becomes 30 / 1.7 ≈ $17.65 CPM, down from $20.
Why do reporting delays make pacing controllers oscillate? || The controller keeps reacting to an old error that its earlier corrections have already fixed, so it overcorrects. Spend then swings the other way, and the controller overcorrects again.
An advertiser wants perfectly even hourly spend and the lowest cost per completed view. Why can't you fully satisfy both, and what would you suggest? || Cheap, high-completion inventory is not spread evenly over the day, so even spend forces buying worse impressions in some hours. Suggest pacing to forecast traffic, or to a value-weighted curve, with limits on how uneven delivery may be.
