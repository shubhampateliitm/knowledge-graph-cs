@@ wal
value: The write-ahead log is the single most reused idea in storage systems: before changing data in place, append a description of the change to a sequential log and make that log durable. After a crash, replay the log. PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQLite, Oracle, RocksDB, etcd, ZooKeeper, file-system journals, Elasticsearch's translog, Kafka (where the log is the data) and the transaction logs of Delta Lake and Iceberg all rely on it.
It gives two things at once. Durability becomes cheap, because committing means one sequential append and one flush rather than many random writes. And the log becomes a record of history that can be shipped to replicas, read by change data capture, and used for point-in-time recovery.
history: Logging for recovery appeared in the earliest transaction systems (IBM IMS in the late 1960s, System R in the 1970s). Jim Gray's work on transactions (1978 onwards) and Härder and Reuter's 1983 taxonomy (which coined ACID) clarified the rules. C. Mohan and colleagues' ARIES (1992) became the standard recovery design, with log sequence numbers, 'steal' and 'no-force' buffer policies, and three-phase restart (analysis, redo, undo). File systems adopted journaling in the 1990s.
Distributed systems then made the log public. Replicated state machines and consensus protocols are protocols for agreeing on a log. Jay Kreps's 2013 essay argued that the log is the central abstraction of data integration; Kafka exposed it as a product. Lakehouse formats turned the log into a directory of numbered commit files on object storage.
how: Every change gets a log record with a log sequence number (LSN): enough information to redo it (and, in many systems, to undo it). The rule is simple: a data page may not be written to disk before the log records describing its changes are durable, and a transaction may not be reported committed before its commit record is durable. Data pages are written later, lazily, by background writers and checkpoints; a checkpoint records how far back recovery must start.
Making the log durable means calling fsync (or writing with O_DSYNC) on the log file, which costs anything from tens of microseconds on enterprise NVMe drives with power-loss protection to milliseconds on ordinary disks. Group commit amortizes this: many concurrent transactions share one flush. The log is also written sequentially, which is the best possible pattern for both spinning disks and SSDs, and it is easy to replicate: ship the bytes to another machine and replay them.
intuition: Write it in your diary before you rearrange the furniture; if you trip halfway, the diary tells you exactly what you were doing and what is left.
friction: Every write is now written at least twice (log plus data), and fsync latency bounds commit latency, so durability settings trade safety against speed (synchronous_commit in PostgreSQL, acks in Kafka, flush settings in RocksDB). Logs grow and must be truncated after checkpoints, archived or compacted; a stuck replica or replication slot can keep logs forever and fill disks. Recovery time depends on how much log must be replayed. And durability is only as good as the storage stack's honesty about flushes: some devices and virtualized environments acknowledge writes that are not yet safe.
practice: Put logs on fast, low-latency storage separate from bulk data when you can, because commit latency follows log fsync latency. Understand your system's durability knob and set it per use case: losing the last second of telemetry may be fine; losing a payment is not. Monitor log size, checkpoint frequency and replication slots or lagging replicas that hold logs back.
Use the log beyond recovery: logical replication and CDC for integration, archived logs for point-in-time restore, and log positions as consistent markers across systems.

@@ exactlyonce
value: Exactly-once processing means each input event affects the results exactly once, even when machines crash, networks drop messages and work is retried. For counters, billing, ad impressions and financial data, double-counting or losing events is a correctness bug, not a performance problem.
Strictly speaking, distributed systems cannot deliver a message exactly once over an unreliable network; what they can provide is 'effectively once': events may be processed more than once internally, but duplicates never become visible in the results. Kafka transactions, Flink checkpoints with transactional sinks, Spark Structured Streaming's checkpointed sinks and idempotent writes all aim at this.
history: Early messaging systems offered at-most-once (fire and forget) or at-least-once (retry until acknowledged) delivery, leaving deduplication to applications. Database systems had long solved the related problem of atomic commit (two-phase commit, 1970s). Google's MillWheel (2013) showed exactly-once stream processing at scale using per-record deduplication and atomic state-plus-output commits. Flink's barrier snapshots (2015) gave consistent state; Kafka 0.11 (2017) added idempotent producers and transactions, letting Kafka Streams read, process and write atomically. Spark Structured Streaming designed its sinks around idempotent or transactional commits per micro-batch.
how: Three ingredients are needed together. First, a replayable source: input can be re-read from a recorded position (Kafka offsets). Second, consistent state snapshots: the processor's state and its input positions are saved together, atomically (Flink checkpoints, Kafka Streams changelog plus offsets). Third, an output that is either idempotent (writing the same result twice has the same effect, for example an upsert keyed by event ID) or transactional (output is committed atomically with the state and input positions).
In Kafka, an idempotent producer attaches a producer ID and sequence number to each batch so brokers drop duplicate retries. A transactional producer writes to several partitions and also commits the consumer's offsets inside the same transaction; consumers with isolation.level=read_committed see only committed data. In Flink, sinks pre-commit output during a checkpoint and commit it when the checkpoint completes (a two-phase commit coordinated by the checkpoint). After a failure, everything rolls back to the last consistent point and replays, and uncommitted output is discarded.
intuition: You cannot make the postman deliver each letter exactly once, but you can number every letter and keep a record of which numbers you have already acted on.
friction: Guarantees stop at system boundaries: writing to an external database, calling an API or sending an email from inside a job is outside Kafka's or Flink's transactions, so those effects need idempotency keys or the outbox pattern. Transactions add latency (output is visible only after commit, often each checkpoint interval) and overhead. Misconfiguration silently weakens guarantees: consumers reading uncommitted data, sinks without transactions, non-deterministic processing that produces different results on replay. And end-to-end exactly-once is only as strong as the weakest component.
practice: Design for at-least-once plus idempotency first; it is simpler and covers most cases. Give every event a stable unique ID at the source, and make sinks upsert by that ID or by a natural key. Use Kafka transactions or Flink's two-phase-commit sinks where results must be atomic with consumption, and set consumers to read_committed.
Keep processing deterministic (no wall-clock time or random numbers in logic without recording them). Reconcile regularly: compare counts between source and sink for each time window, because the best guarantee is one you can verify.

@@ kvcache
value: The KV cache stores the attention keys and values of every token a language model has already processed, so generating each new token needs only that token's computation plus a read of the cache, instead of reprocessing the whole conversation. Without it, generating a long answer would cost time growing with the square of its length. With it, the KV cache becomes the main consumer of GPU memory in serving and the central object that serving systems manage, share, compress and move.
Many recent advances in LLM efficiency are, at heart, KV-cache engineering: grouped-query and multi-head latent attention (smaller caches), PagedAttention (less waste), prefix caching (sharing), quantized caches, offloading to CPU or SSD, and disaggregated serving (moving caches between GPUs).
history: Caching keys and values during autoregressive decoding was standard in transformer implementations from the start (2017), but became a central engineering topic once models grew and contexts lengthened. Multi-query attention (Shazeer, 2019) and grouped-query attention (2023) shrank the cache by sharing key/value heads. vLLM's PagedAttention (2023) managed it like virtual memory; SGLang's RadixAttention and vLLM's automatic prefix caching (2023–2024) shared it across requests; model providers began offering discounted prompt caching (2024). DeepSeek's multi-head latent attention (2024) compressed keys and values into a small latent vector. By 2025–2026, KV-cache offloading and transfer between machines (for example in NVIDIA Dynamo and LMCache) became standard parts of large serving deployments.
how: In each attention layer, every token produces a key and a value vector per KV head. During prefill, the model computes these for all prompt tokens and stores them. During decode, the new token's query attends over all stored keys and values, and its own key and value are appended. Cache size = 2 (K and V) × layers × KV heads × head dimension × bytes per element × tokens. For example, a model with 80 layers, 8 KV heads of dimension 128 in FP16 needs about 320 KB per token, so a 128K-token context takes about 40 GB for one request, and memory limits how many requests run at once.
Because each decode step reads the entire cache, decoding speed is bounded by memory bandwidth: longer contexts make every token slower. Systems manage the cache in fixed-size blocks (pages) allocated on demand, share identical prefix blocks across requests using hashes of their token content, evict or offload blocks when memory runs low, and transfer blocks between prefill and decode workers.
intuition: Notes on everything said so far in a conversation; you answer by glancing over your notes rather than replaying the whole conversation, but the notes keep growing and must fit on your desk.
friction: Memory grows linearly with context length and number of concurrent users, which caps batch size and makes long contexts expensive. Sharing requires exact prefix matches, so small changes early in a prompt invalidate everything after. Quantizing or evicting cache entries can reduce quality, especially on long-context tasks. Moving caches across machines needs very fast networks, and cache-aware routing complicates load balancing.
practice: Estimate KV-cache memory per token for your model and multiply by expected concurrency and context length before choosing GPUs or settings. Keep prompts' stable parts (system instructions, tools, documents) at the beginning to benefit from prefix caching, and avoid inserting changing content (timestamps, IDs) early in prompts. Consider FP8 KV-cache quantization when accuracy tests allow, and set maximum context lengths to what your application actually needs.
Monitor cache usage, preemptions and prefix-cache hit rates in your serving engine; they explain most throughput and latency changes.

@@ gc
value: Garbage collection automatically frees memory that a program can no longer reach. It removes whole categories of bugs that plague manual memory management (use-after-free, double free, most leaks) and makes languages like Java, Scala, Go, Python, JavaScript and C# productive and safe. Most of the data infrastructure in this map runs on garbage-collected runtimes, so collectors' pauses and overheads shape real-world latency and cost.
history: John McCarthy invented garbage collection for Lisp in 1959 (published 1960), using mark-and-sweep. George Collins described reference counting (1960). Copying collection (Cheney, 1970), generational collection (Lieberman and Hewitt 1983; Ungar 1984) and incremental and concurrent collectors (Dijkstra's tri-colour marking, 1978; Baker 1978) followed. Java (1995) brought garbage collection to mainstream industry, and the JVM became the main testing ground for production collectors: Concurrent Mark Sweep, G1 (2004 paper, default since Java 9), Shenandoah and ZGC (production-ready in Java 15, generational since Java 21). Go's concurrent collector (2015 onwards) prioritized low pauses; Python uses reference counting plus a cycle detector.
how: Tracing collectors find live objects by starting from roots (thread stacks, globals, registers) and following references; everything not reached is garbage. Mark-sweep marks live objects and frees the rest; mark-compact also slides live objects together to remove fragmentation; copying collectors copy live objects to a new space, which is fast when few survive. Generational collectors split the heap by age: new objects go to a small young generation collected frequently (most die young, so this is cheap), and survivors are promoted to an old generation collected rarely. Write barriers track references from old to young objects so young collections need not scan the whole heap.
Concurrent collectors do marking (and sometimes moving) while application threads run, using barriers to stay correct as the program changes references: tri-colour invariants for marking, and load or read barriers (ZGC, Shenandoah) that fix references to moved objects. Reference counting frees objects immediately when their count drops to zero, giving predictable timing but costing work on every pointer update and needing extra handling for cycles.
intuition: A cleaner who periodically checks which things are still connected to something you are using, and throws away the rest; good cleaners work quietly while you are still in the room.
friction: Pauses, CPU overhead and memory headroom are the costs: concurrent collectors need spare CPU and typically 20–50% extra heap to work well. Allocation-heavy code stresses the collector. Large heaps and many threads raise root-scanning and marking costs. Finalizers and native resources (files, sockets, off-heap buffers) are not managed reliably by the collector. Containers add pitfalls when heap and container limits are not aligned.
practice: Measure before tuning: look at pause times (p99, max), GC CPU share and allocation rate from GC logs or JFR. Reduce allocation in hot paths (reuse buffers, avoid boxing, prefer primitive arrays and binary formats). Choose the collector by goal: throughput collectors for batch jobs, G1 as a balanced default, generational ZGC for low-latency services with large heaps. Leave headroom in heap and container memory, and treat long pauses as a capacity problem (too little heap or too much live data), not only a tuning problem.

@@ isolation
value: Isolation levels define which effects of concurrent transactions each transaction may see. They are the contract between a database and the application about concurrency: choose too weak a level and subtle bugs appear (double bookings, negative balances, lost updates); choose too strong a level and throughput falls and transactions abort under contention. Many real production bugs come from teams not knowing which level their database uses by default.
history: The ANSI SQL-92 standard defined four levels (READ UNCOMMITTED, READ COMMITTED, REPEATABLE READ, SERIALIZABLE) by the anomalies each prevents (dirty reads, non-repeatable reads, phantoms). Berenson and colleagues' 1995 'A Critique of ANSI SQL Isolation Levels' showed these definitions were ambiguous and introduced snapshot isolation, noting that it allows a new anomaly, write skew. Adya's thesis (1999) gave precise, implementation-independent definitions using dependency graphs. Cahill, Röhm and Fekete (2008) made snapshot isolation serializable by detecting dangerous dependency patterns, adopted by PostgreSQL in 2011. Kyle Kingsbury's Jepsen analyses (from 2013) repeatedly found databases that did not provide the isolation they advertised.
how: Anomalies are the clearest way to understand the levels. A dirty read sees another transaction's uncommitted change. A non-repeatable read gets different values reading the same row twice. A phantom read sees new rows appear in a repeated range query. A lost update happens when two transactions read, modify and write the same value and one overwrites the other. Write skew happens when two transactions read overlapping data, each makes a decision based on it, and they write different rows, together breaking a rule (two doctors both going off call because each saw the other on call).
READ COMMITTED (the default in PostgreSQL, Oracle and SQL Server) prevents dirty reads only; each statement sees a fresh snapshot. Snapshot isolation (PostgreSQL's REPEATABLE READ; MySQL InnoDB's REPEATABLE READ is similar but differs in details) gives each transaction one consistent snapshot and prevents lost updates on the same row, but allows write skew. SERIALIZABLE guarantees the result equals some serial order; implementations use strict two-phase locking, optimistic validation or serializable snapshot isolation, and they abort transactions that would break serializability, so applications must retry.
intuition: How much of other people's half-finished work you can see, and whether the world can change under you between your first look and your final decision.
friction: Names mean different things in different databases, so 'repeatable read' in MySQL and PostgreSQL are not the same. Stronger levels cause more aborts and lock waits under contention. Distributed databases add further subtleties (stale reads from replicas, clock-dependent guarantees). Many ORMs and frameworks hide transaction boundaries, so developers may not even know what isolation they are getting.
practice: Know your database's default and document it. For read-modify-write on a single row, use atomic updates (UPDATE ... SET x = x + 1) or SELECT ... FOR UPDATE rather than reading and writing separately. For invariants that span rows, use SERIALIZABLE with automatic retries, or enforce the rule with constraints or explicit locks. Test concurrency bugs deliberately by running conflicting transactions in parallel in tests.

@@ lightspeed
value: The speed of light is the one latency no engineering can remove. Light travels about 300,000 km per second in vacuum and about 200,000 km per second in optical fibre: roughly 5 microseconds per kilometre. That fact decides where data centres are built, why content delivery networks and edge caches exist, why globally consistent databases are slow to write, why trading firms pay for straighter cables and microwave towers, and why a chip's clock cannot be arbitrarily fast.
For system designers, it turns distance into a budget. Every synchronous round trip between two places costs at least twice the distance divided by the signal speed, before any queueing, processing or retransmission.
history: Ole Rømer estimated the speed of light from Jupiter's moons in 1676; Fizeau and Foucault measured it on Earth in the 19th century; Maxwell's equations (1865) showed light is an electromagnetic wave and predicted its speed; Einstein's relativity (1905) made it the universal speed limit. Since 1983 the metre has been defined by the speed of light, which is exactly 299,792,458 m/s.
In computing, the limit became visible as systems spread out. Transatlantic cables (from the 1860s) and later fibre networks made global communication fast but not instant. Grace Hopper famously handed out 30-centimetre lengths of wire to show what a nanosecond means. Distributed databases in the 2010s (Spanner, CockroachDB) designed their protocols around cross-region round trips, and high-frequency trading built microwave links between Chicago and New Jersey because signals in air travel about 50% faster than in glass.
how: Signal speed depends on the medium: about 0.67c in fibre (refractive index around 1.47), about 0.5–0.9c in copper cables and circuit-board traces depending on materials, and nearly c in air for radio and microwave. Real network paths are longer than straight lines (cables follow coasts, terrain and routing), so practical round-trip times are often 1.5–2 times the theoretical minimum. Rough examples: within a data centre, under 0.5 ms round trip; Mumbai to Bengaluru about 15–30 ms; India to Singapore about 30–60 ms; India to the US east coast about 200–250 ms; New York to London about 70 ms.
Consequences for design follow directly. A consensus protocol that needs a majority across three continents waits for the second-closest region on every write. A web page that needs ten sequential round trips to a distant server cannot load quickly, no matter how fast the server is. Inside chips, at 4 GHz a clock cycle lasts 0.25 ns, during which a signal moves only a few centimetres in wires, so large chips use multiple clock domains and keep critical paths short.
intuition: Distance is time: every kilometre between two computers is a fixed, unavoidable delay, like a toll paid on every message.
friction: Engineers routinely underestimate it: designs that work on a laptop or within one region become unusably slow when stretched across continents. Strong consistency across regions always costs latency (the PACELC trade-off). New technologies (hollow-core fibre with signal speeds near 0.99c, low-earth-orbit satellite constellations, microwave links) reduce the penalty but cannot remove it, and they bring their own costs and capacity limits.
practice: Count round trips, not just bytes: reduce sequential requests (batch, pipeline, prefetch), and put data and computation close to users with caches, CDNs and regional deployments. For multi-region systems, decide which operations truly need global coordination and keep everything else local (local writes with asynchronous replication, region-pinned data). Use real measured RTTs between your regions when estimating latency budgets, and remember that each extra synchronous hop multiplies them.
