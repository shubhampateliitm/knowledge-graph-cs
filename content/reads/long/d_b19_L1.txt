@@ mediastream
value: Raw video is enormous. One second of 1080p video at 30 frames per second is about 93 MB, roughly 750 Mbit/s, while a home broadband line gives 20 to 100 Mbit/s. With a modern codec, the same 1080p picture fits in 3 to 6 Mbit/s, a reduction of more than 100 times, and a two-hour film shrinks from about 670 GB to a few gigabytes.
Compression alone is not enough, because networks change from second to second. Video streaming therefore has two halves. The codec (coder–decoder) removes everything that is redundant or invisible. The delivery system cuts the compressed video into short segments, encodes each segment at several quality levels, serves them as ordinary files over HTTP and CDNs, and lets the player choose the right quality for each segment. Video is now most of the bytes that flow downstream on the internet, and on connected TV (CTV) the stream is the product: ads, measurement and recommendations all ride on it.
This concept stands on four lower ones. Transform coding with the DCT (L2) turns blocks of pixels into a few significant numbers, which removes redundancy inside one picture. Motion compensation (L2) predicts each frame from frames already sent, which removes redundancy between pictures and gives most of the saving. Adaptive bitrate (L2) lets the player switch quality per segment to match the network. Human vision (L5) decides what can be thrown away without anyone noticing. The products built on top, such as FFmpeg, the H.264 / HEVC / AV1 codecs and the HLS / DASH protocols, are implementations of these ideas.
terms: Chroma subsampling (4:2:0) — storing colour at half the width and half the height of brightness, which halves the raw data.
Macroblock or coding block — a square block of pixels (16×16 in H.264, up to 64×64 in HEVC, up to 128×128 in AV1) that the encoder predicts and codes as one unit.
I, P and B frames — an I-frame is coded alone; a P-frame is predicted from earlier frames; a B-frame is predicted from frames before and after it.
GOP (group of pictures) — the run of frames from one I-frame to the next; a player can only start decoding at an I-frame.
Quantization parameter (QP) — the knob that sets how coarsely coefficients are rounded; higher QP means fewer bits and lower quality.
Bitrate ladder — the list of resolution and bitrate pairs (renditions) that one title is encoded into.
Segment — a short piece of the stream (typically 2 to 6 seconds) stored as its own file, starting with an I-frame.
Manifest — a small text file (an .m3u8 playlist in HLS, an MPD in DASH) that lists the renditions and their segments.
history: The core mathematics came first. Ahmed, Natarajan and Rao published the discrete cosine transform in 1974. H.261 (1988), designed for video calls over ISDN lines, set the pattern that almost every codec since has followed: split the frame into blocks, predict each block from the previous frame using motion vectors, transform the prediction error with a DCT, quantize it and entropy-code it. JPEG (1992) applied the same transform to still images. MPEG-1 (1993) brought video to CDs, and MPEG-2 (1995) carried digital broadcast television and DVDs.
H.264/AVC (2003) was the big step for the internet: roughly half the bitrate of MPEG-2 at the same quality. It is still the codec that every device can decode. HEVC/H.265 (2013) halved the bitrate again for high resolutions, but its licensing was split between several patent pools, which slowed adoption on the web. Google's VP9 (2013) and AV1 (2018, from the Alliance for Open Media, an industry group) offered royalty-free alternatives. VVC/H.266 followed in 2020, and AOMedia released AV2 in June 2026.
Delivery changed in parallel. RealNetworks (1995) and later Flash with RTMP used special streaming servers and protocols such as RTSP, which kept a session open per viewer and passed badly through firewalls. Adaptive streaming over plain HTTP, tried by Move Networks and Microsoft Smooth Streaming in the late 2000s, solved this: video became a series of small files that any web server and CDN can cache. Apple's HTTP Live Streaming (2009, later RFC 8216 in 2017) and MPEG-DASH (2012) standardized the idea, and CMAF (2018) let both use the same fragmented MP4 segments. Netflix's per-title encoding (2015) and its VMAF quality metric (2016) moved the industry from fixed ladders to ladders tuned to each piece of content. Low-latency HLS (announced in 2019) and chunked CMAF cut live delay from 20–30 seconds toward 2–5 seconds for sports.
how: An encoder first changes colour space. Cameras give RGB, but human vision (L5) is much more sensitive to fine detail in brightness than in colour, because the eye has far more brightness-sensitive resolution than colour-sensitive resolution. So the encoder converts RGB to one luma plane (Y) and two chroma planes (Cb, Cr), and stores the chroma planes at half width and half height (4:2:0). This one step removes half the raw data, and viewers almost never notice. A 1920×1080 frame becomes 2,073,600 luma samples plus 2 × 518,400 chroma samples, about 3.1 million bytes at 8 bits.
Next the encoder splits each frame into blocks and chooses a prediction for every block. In intra prediction, a block is predicted from already-coded pixels at its edges in the same frame (for example "continue the pixels above downward"). In inter prediction, which is where motion compensation (L2) contributes, the encoder searches one or more reference frames for the area that best matches the block and records a motion vector, such as "take the 16×16 area that was 3 pixels to the left and 1 pixel up in the previous frame". Modern codecs allow quarter-pixel or eighth-pixel vectors, several reference frames and block sizes from 4×4 up to 64×64 or 128×128. The decoder can repeat the prediction exactly, so only the vector and the prediction error (the residual) need to be sent. For a static background or a smooth camera pan, the residual is almost zero.
The residual goes through transform coding, which is what the DCT (L2) contributes. A block of residual values is transformed into frequency coefficients: one number for the average (the DC coefficient) and others for patterns of increasing detail. Natural images put most of their energy into the few low-frequency coefficients, so most high-frequency coefficients are tiny. H.264 uses small integer approximations of the DCT (4×4 and 8×8) so that encoder and decoder get bit-identical results; HEVC and AV1 add larger sizes and other transform types.
Quantization is the step where information is actually lost. Each coefficient is divided by a step size and rounded. The quantization parameter sets the step: in H.264 and HEVC, every increase of 6 in QP doubles the step size, which roughly halves the bits for detailed content. Perception enters again here: the eye is less sensitive to high spatial frequencies and to errors in busy textures, so encoders quantize those more coarsely. After rounding, a typical block has only a handful of non-zero coefficients. They are scanned in order from low to high frequency and compressed with an entropy coder, today usually arithmetic coding (CABAC in H.264 and HEVC), which approaches the information-theoretic minimum for the predicted symbol probabilities.
Frames come in types. An I-frame uses only intra prediction, so it can be decoded alone, and it is large. P-frames predict from earlier frames, and B-frames from earlier and later frames (the encoder sends frames out of display order so references arrive first). P- and B-frames are often 3 to 10 times smaller than I-frames. The run from one I-frame to the next is a GOP. Errors and decoding always restart at an I-frame, so GOP length is a trade-off between compression (long GOPs) and the ability to seek, join a live stream or switch quality (short GOPs).
The encoder makes millions of choices per second (block sizes, modes, vectors) by rate–distortion optimization: for each choice it minimizes \( D + \lambda \times R \), where D is distortion, R is bits and λ is set from the target quality. Searching more options compresses better but costs CPU, so encoder presets from "ultrafast" to "veryslow" differ 10× or more in speed. Each codec generation adds tools (bigger blocks, more prediction modes, better in-loop filters that remove blocking artefacts) and gains roughly 30 to 50% bitrate at equal quality, for several times the encoding compute.
Delivery begins with the ladder. The same title is encoded into perhaps 6 to 10 renditions, from about 0.3 Mbit/s at low resolution up to 8 to 16 Mbit/s for 4K. Every rendition uses aligned segment boundaries, and every segment starts with an I-frame, so a player can switch rendition at any boundary. The packager writes segments as fragmented MP4 (CMAF) or MPEG-TS files and writes manifests that list them. Because these are ordinary HTTP objects, CDNs cache them like any other file, and one origin can serve millions of viewers.
The player then runs adaptive bitrate (L2) logic. It downloads segments into a buffer of perhaps 10 to 60 seconds. Throughput-based rules pick the highest rendition below the measured download rate times a safety factor. Buffer-based rules, such as Netflix's BBA (2014) and BOLA (2016), pick quality from buffer level: a full buffer allows high quality, a draining buffer forces lower quality. Hybrid rules such as model predictive control combine both. The goal is to maximize quality while avoiding rebuffering, which viewers hate far more than a lower resolution. Around this core sit encryption (Common Encryption with Widevine, PlayReady or FairPlay licences), ad insertion and separate audio and subtitle tracks.

example: Work through the bitrate of one 1080p stream. A 1920×1080 frame in 4:2:0 at 8 bits holds 2,073,600 luma bytes plus 1,036,800 chroma bytes, about 3.11 MB. At 30 frames per second that is 93.3 MB/s, or 746 Mbit/s. Without chroma subsampling (4:4:4) it would be 1.49 Gbit/s, so the perceptual step alone halves the data.
Now compress with H.264 at 6 Mbit/s. The ratio is 746 / 6 ≈ 124. A two-hour film is 7,200 seconds: raw, it is 746 × 7,200 / 8 ≈ 671,000 MB, about 671 GB; compressed, it is 6 × 7,200 / 8 = 5,400 MB, about 5.4 GB. HEVC or AV1 at similar quality might need about 3.5 Mbit/s, which gives 3.2 GB, a ratio of about 210.
Look inside one 2-second segment at 6 Mbit/s. It holds 12 Mbit, which is 1.5 MB, for 60 frames. Suppose the I-frame takes 200 KB. The other 59 frames share 1.3 MB, about 22 KB each, which is under 1% of the raw 3.11 MB frame. That shows where the saving comes from: most frames are mostly motion vectors and small residuals.
Now the transform step on one 8×8 block of a blue sky. The 64 pixel values all lie between 118 and 124. After the DCT, the DC coefficient is about 8 × 121 = 968 (the average times 8, for an orthonormal 8×8 DCT), and one or two low-frequency coefficients capture the slight gradient. With a quantization step of 16, almost all other coefficients round to 0. The entropy coder then sends roughly 3 non-zero values and an "end of block" symbol, a few bytes instead of 64.
Finally the network. The ladder is 0.4 Mbit/s (360p), 1.2 (540p), 2.5 (720p), 4.5 (1080p) and 8 Mbit/s (1080p high). Segments last 4 seconds and the player holds 12 seconds of buffer. The measured throughput is 6 Mbit/s; with a safety factor of 0.8 the budget is 4.8 Mbit/s, so the player picks 4.5. One 4.5 Mbit/s segment is 18 Mbit and downloads in 3 seconds, so the buffer grows by 1 second per segment. Then the Wi-Fi drops to 3 Mbit/s. The same segment now takes 6 seconds, so the buffer shrinks by 2 seconds per segment. From 12 seconds, the player would stall after 6 more segments if it did nothing. A buffer-based rule notices the buffer falling below, say, 8 seconds and steps down to 2.5 Mbit/s, whose 10 Mbit segment downloads in 3.3 seconds, so the buffer grows again. The viewer sees a slightly softer picture instead of a spinning wheel.
intuition: Send only what changed, only as precisely as the eye can tell, in pieces the network can carry right now.
The design follows from three facts. Neighbouring pixels are similar, so a transform can describe a block with a few numbers. Neighbouring frames are similar, so most of a frame can be described as "the last frame, moved a little". People do not see everything a camera records, so some precision can be dropped with no visible harm. Each fact removes a large factor, and the factors multiply: about 2× from chroma, 10× or more from prediction between frames, and another large factor from quantizing transform coefficients.
Delivery follows the logic of the web: video becomes many small, cacheable files, and the decisions move to the player, the only component that knows how fast its own network is right now.
mistakes: People often think resolution sets quality. In fact bitrate and encoder settings matter as much: a 1080p stream at 2 Mbit/s with heavy motion can look worse than a 720p stream at the same bitrate, because the encoder has too few bits per pixel and must quantize harshly. This is why ladders are tuned per title or per scene.
People often think a newer codec is always the right choice. In fact the player must be able to decode it, ideally in hardware. Many older smart TVs and streaming sticks decode only H.264 and HEVC, and software decoding of AV1 on a weak TV processor can drop frames. Services encode several codecs and let each device pick.
People often think lossy compression loses information at every step. In fact prediction, transforms and entropy coding are all reversible. Only quantization (and chroma subsampling) loses information, which is why QP is the main quality knob.
People often think adaptive bitrate can hide any network problem. In fact it can only choose among the renditions that exist and react within one segment. A sudden drop to below the lowest rung, or a very short buffer on live streams, still causes rebuffering.
friction: Encoding is expensive. Each new codec generation saves bitrate but needs several times more encoder compute, and a full ladder multiplies that by the number of renditions and codecs. Services must decide which titles are popular enough to justify slower, better encodes.
Device fragmentation is a daily problem, especially on CTV. Smart TVs from different years and brands support different codecs, profiles, HDR formats, DRM systems and player bugs, and they are updated rarely. A stream that works on one model may stutter on another, so testing matrices are large.
Live streaming has its own tension. Short and partial segments reduce delay, but they raise request rates, shrink the player buffer and make rebuffering more likely. For a large sports event, millions of players request the same new segment within a second, which stresses CDNs and origins.
Ads add complexity. Server-side ad insertion avoids ad blockers and gives a smooth, TV-like experience, but the ad creative must be transcoded to match the content ladder and codec, and measurement must be done on the server or through beacons, which makes impression counting and fraud detection harder. Client-side insertion measures better but often causes visible pauses at ad breaks.
Quality is hard to measure. PSNR (a simple signal-error measure) matches what people see poorly; VMAF is better but needs a reference copy and can be fooled by sharpening.
practice: Measure quality of experience directly: start-up time, rebuffering ratio (time stalled divided by time watched), average delivered bitrate, rendition switches and perceptual quality such as VMAF. Report these by device model, network type and CDN, because averages hide the bad cases.
Build ladders per title, or per scene or shot, instead of one fixed ladder. Netflix's 2015 analysis found, for example, that one drama needed 4.64 Mbit/s instead of the fixed 5.8 Mbit/s for its top 1080p rung at the same quality, a 20% saving. Cartoons and static talk shows need far fewer bits than action films with grain.
Pick codecs by device support and traffic. H.264 is the universal fallback. HEVC is widely supported on TVs and Apple devices. AV1 now has hardware decoding on many recent TVs, phones and browsers and gives the best savings for popular titles, where the extra encoding cost pays back across millions of views. Watch AV2 hardware support before planning around it.
Keep segment boundaries and I-frames aligned across all renditions, and use 2- to 6-second segments for video on demand. For live events, use LL-HLS or chunked CMAF only where low delay matters, and test player buffer behaviour under bad networks.
Use a CDN with origin shielding so a popular new segment is fetched from the origin once, not once per edge. For server-side ads, pre-transcode creatives into the content ladder, and test ad transitions on the slowest devices you support.
quiz: Which step in a video codec actually throws information away? || Quantization of the transform coefficients, plus chroma subsampling. Prediction, the DCT and entropy coding are reversible; QP controls how much quantization loses.
Why are P- and B-frames much smaller than I-frames? || They are predicted from other frames with motion vectors, so only the vectors and a small residual are coded. Most of the picture is "the reference frame, moved", which costs very few bits.
Why must every segment in an adaptive stream start with an I-frame and be aligned across renditions? || The player may switch rendition at any segment boundary, and a decoder can only start from an I-frame. Aligned boundaries mean the new rendition's segment continues the same timeline exactly.
A player has 12 s of buffer and downloads 4-second segments of an 8 Mbit/s rendition over a 4 Mbit/s link. How long until it stalls if it does not switch? || Each 32 Mbit segment takes 8 s to download but adds only 4 s of video, so the buffer falls by 4 s per segment. It empties after about 3 segments, roughly 24 seconds.
When is it worth encoding a title in AV1 in addition to H.264? || When the title is watched enough that bandwidth savings (often 30% or more) outweigh the extra encoding compute and storage, and a large share of its viewers have devices that decode AV1 well, ideally in hardware.
