@@ hpc
value: High-performance computing (HPC) is the craft of making thousands of processors work on one problem as if they were one machine. The hard part is not the arithmetic. It is the talking: after every step, the processors must exchange partial results, and the time spent exchanging can easily be larger than the time spent computing. Collective communication is the name for the group operations that do this exchange: broadcast (one sends to all), reduce (all send, one receives the sum), all-gather (each sends its piece, everyone gets all pieces) and all-reduce (everyone gets the sum of everyone's data).
For modern AI, one collective dominates. In data-parallel training, every GPU computes gradients on its own slice of the batch, and then all GPUs must agree on the average gradient before they update the weights. That is an all-reduce over the whole model. For a 7-billion-parameter model with 16-bit gradients, each GPU holds 14 GB of gradients and must end with the sum of all 64 or 1,024 copies, several times per minute. A naive design (send everything to one machine, add, send back) makes that machine's network link the bottleneck. A good collective algorithm makes every link busy at the same time, so the cost per GPU stays almost constant as you add GPUs.
This concept sits between hardware and training frameworks. Below it, the all-reduce algorithm (L2) gives the pattern of messages, parallelism strategies (L2) decide which tensors must be exchanged and how often, and Amdahl's and Gustafson's laws (L4) explain why communication becomes the ceiling on speed-up. Above it, GPUs and accelerators (NVIDIA H100, Blackwell, Google TPU, AMD Instinct) are designed around the collective: their links, switches and network cards exist to move this traffic fast. A cluster of tens of thousands of GPUs is, in practice, a machine for running collectives with matrix multiplications in between.
terms: Collective operation — a communication step in which a whole group of processes takes part together, such as broadcast, reduce, all-reduce, all-gather or reduce-scatter.
Rank — the number (0 to N−1) that identifies one process, usually one per GPU, inside a communicator.
Communicator — the group of ranks that take part in a collective, together with the topology information used to choose an algorithm.
All-reduce — every rank contributes a vector and every rank receives the element-wise sum (or max, or average) of all vectors.
Reduce-scatter — like all-reduce, but each rank ends with only its own 1/N slice of the summed vector; all-reduce equals reduce-scatter followed by all-gather.
Latency–bandwidth model — a simple cost model: sending n bytes takes α + n/β, where α is the fixed start-up delay and β is the link bandwidth.
NVLink domain — the group of GPUs joined by NVLink and NVSwitch inside a server (8 GPUs on H100 systems) or a rack (72 GPUs in GB200 NVL72), with much more bandwidth than the network between servers.
Bisection bandwidth — the total bandwidth between two halves of the cluster; it limits collectives that must cross the middle of the network.
history: Supercomputing started with vector machines such as the Cray-1 (1976), but by the late 1980s the fastest machines were many ordinary processors connected by a network, and every vendor had its own message-passing library. The Message Passing Interface (MPI), first standardized in 1994 by a committee of vendors and universities, fixed that. Its designers put collective operations (MPI_Bcast, MPI_Reduce, MPI_Allreduce) in the standard on purpose: they knew that a library could implement them far better than an application programmer writing loops of point-to-point sends.
In the 1990s and 2000s, researchers worked out which algorithm is best for which message size. Short messages are limited by latency, so tree algorithms with few steps win. Long messages are limited by bandwidth, so ring and recursive-halving algorithms win. Thakur, Rabenseifner and Gropp (2005) described how MPICH switches between them, and Patarasuk and Yuan (2009) proved that the ring all-reduce uses each link's bandwidth optimally on a simple network.
Deep learning rediscovered all this. Early large-scale training used parameter servers (Google's DistBelief, 2012), where workers push gradients to a few central machines. In 2017, Baidu showed that ring all-reduce from HPC scaled much better on GPUs, and Uber's Horovod (2017, paper by Sergeev and Del Balso in 2018) put it behind a simple API. NVIDIA's NCCL library (first released 2016) became the standard for GPUs and added tree algorithms in version 2.4 (2019), then support for NVSwitch and in-network reduction. AMD's RCCL follows the same interface. Since 2020, models too large for one GPU (Megatron-LM, ZeRO) have made collectives even more central: tensor parallelism calls an all-reduce inside every transformer layer. Clusters of 16,000 to 100,000+ GPUs now exist, and the Ultra Ethernet Consortium (2023) was formed to improve Ethernet for exactly this traffic.
how: Every collective algorithm is a schedule: who sends which bytes to whom in each step. The cost model is simple. If a message of n bytes takes α + n/β, then an algorithm's time is the number of steps times α, plus the bytes each GPU must send divided by β. Good algorithms minimise both terms for the network at hand.
The ring all-reduce is the classic bandwidth-optimal schedule. Arrange N ranks in a ring and cut each rank's vector of size S into N chunks. In the first phase, reduce-scatter, each rank sends one chunk to its right neighbour, receives one from its left neighbour and adds it to its own copy of that chunk. After N−1 steps, each rank holds the complete sum of one chunk. In the second phase, all-gather, each rank passes its finished chunk around the ring, so after N−1 more steps everyone has every chunk. Each rank sends 2(N−1) messages of S/N bytes, so it sends 2S(N−1)/N bytes in total. That is almost exactly 2S, no matter whether N is 8 or 8,000. This is the key result: per-GPU traffic does not grow with the number of GPUs, because all links work in parallel. The price is latency: 2(N−1) steps, each paying α. With N = 1,024 and α of a few microseconds that is several milliseconds of pure start-up cost, which hurts for small messages.
Tree algorithms trade bandwidth for latency. A binary tree reduces up to the root in log₂N steps and broadcasts back in log₂N steps, so the latency term is only about 2 log₂N × α. A single tree wastes bandwidth, because leaves send once but inner nodes receive twice. NCCL's double binary tree (since 2.4) builds two trees in which the inner nodes of one are the leaves of the other and splits the data between them, so all links are used almost fully while the step count stays logarithmic. NCCL measures the cluster at start-up and picks ring or tree per message size.
Real clusters are not flat. Inside a server, 8 H100 GPUs talk through NVLink and NVSwitch at about 900 GB/s per GPU (counting both directions). Between servers, each GPU usually has its own network card at 400 Gb/s (50 GB/s) over InfiniBand or RoCE Ethernet. That is a factor of about 18 between the two levels. So collectives are hierarchical: first reduce-scatter inside the server over NVLink, then all-reduce across servers on one eighth of the data per GPU, then all-gather inside the server. The slow network now carries one eighth of the traffic per link. Switch fabrics are arranged (fat trees, rail-optimised layouts where GPU number i of every server connects to the same leaf switch) so that these cross-server collectives avoid congested links. Some switches can even add the numbers in the network itself (NVIDIA SHARP), so data crosses each link once instead of twice.
The parallelism strategies decide which collectives happen. Data parallelism needs one all-reduce of the gradients per step (or reduce-scatter plus all-gather in ZeRO and FSDP, which also shard the optimizer state). Tensor parallelism splits each matrix multiplication across GPUs and needs an all-reduce, or reduce-scatter and all-gather, in every layer, on the critical path, so it must stay inside an NVLink domain. Pipeline parallelism sends activations between consecutive stages, point-to-point and small. Expert parallelism in mixture-of-experts models uses all-to-all, where every GPU sends different data to every other GPU, which stresses bisection bandwidth most. Frameworks map these onto the cluster topology: tensor parallel within a server, pipeline across servers, data parallel across the rest.
The last idea is overlap. The backward pass produces gradients layer by layer, from the last layer to the first. Frameworks group gradients into buckets (often tens of megabytes) and start the all-reduce of a bucket as soon as it is full, while the GPU keeps computing the earlier layers. When the all-reduce of a layer finishes before the backward pass reaches the end, communication is hidden. Amdahl's law says what happens otherwise: the non-overlapped communication is the serial fraction. If 20% of each step is exposed communication, the speed-up can never exceed 5× no matter how many GPUs you add. Gustafson's view gives the way out: grow the batch or the model with the cluster, so computation per GPU stays large relative to communication.

example: Take a 7-billion-parameter model with 16-bit (2-byte) gradients: S = 14 GB. Train on 64 GPUs (8 servers of 8), each GPU with a 400 Gb/s network card, which is 50 GB/s. We want the time for one gradient all-reduce, ignoring latency.
Naive design, a central parameter server. All 64 GPUs send 14 GB to one machine. Its single 50 GB/s link must receive 64 × 14 = 896 GB, which takes about 17.9 seconds, and it must send the same amount back. The server's link is the bottleneck, and adding GPUs makes it worse in direct proportion.
Flat ring over all 64 GPUs. Each GPU sends 2 × (63/64) × 14 GB = 27.6 GB. At 50 GB/s that is 0.55 seconds, and it would still be about 0.55 seconds with 1,024 GPUs (the factor 1023/1024 is almost the same). Per-GPU cost has become independent of cluster size. This already beats the parameter server by 32×.
Hierarchical version. Step 1: inside each server, reduce-scatter over NVLink. Each GPU now owns an eighth of the summed gradient: 1.75 GB. NVLink at hundreds of GB/s makes this take only a few tens of milliseconds. Step 2: the 8 GPUs that own the same eighth, one in each of the 8 servers, run a ring all-reduce over the network on 1.75 GB. Each sends 2 × (7/8) × 1.75 = 3.06 GB, which takes 0.061 seconds. Step 3: inside each server, all-gather over NVLink. The total is roughly 0.1 seconds, about 5× faster than the flat ring, because the slow network carries only an eighth of the data per link and all 64 network cards are busy.
Now check the effect on training speed. Suppose the forward and backward pass take 1.2 seconds on each GPU. If the 0.1 second all-reduce is fully overlapped with the backward pass, step time is 1.2 s and scaling efficiency is close to 100%. If it is not overlapped at all, step time is 1.3 s and efficiency is 1.2/1.3 = 92%. With the flat ring and no overlap, it would be 1.2/1.75 = 69%. This is why teams care about both the algorithm and the overlap.
intuition: A relay team that passes partial sums around a ring, so that every runner carries a small piece and everyone ends with the total at the same time.
The idea is natural once you see where the time goes. A network link is like a pipe: it is wasteful to leave any pipe idle. In a central design, only the pipes near the centre are busy. The ring keeps every pipe busy with a small chunk, so the whole cluster's bandwidth is used. Cutting the data into N chunks is the trick that converts one big sequential transfer into N small parallel ones, and the sum is computed on the way instead of at the end. Hierarchy follows the same logic: use the fast links for the heavy part of the work and the slow links only for what is left.
mistakes: People often think adding GPUs always shortens training time. In fact every added GPU adds communication, and the exposed part of it is a serial fraction in Amdahl's sense. Real jobs scale well only when computation per GPU is large (large batches, large layers) and communication is overlapped. Reported model-FLOPs utilisation for very large training runs is typically around 40%, not 100%, for this reason.
People often think all-reduce time grows with the number of GPUs. In fact, for large messages, the bandwidth term of a ring all-reduce is almost constant (about 2S/β). What grows is the latency term (2(N−1) steps) and the chance that something breaks. That is why trees and hierarchies are used for small messages and very large clusters.
People often think a faster network card fixes a slow job. In fact a collective runs at the speed of its slowest link, and the weak link is often inside the server (PCIe or CPU path), a mis-configured switch route, or one degraded cable. One GPU that is slightly slow delays every other GPU at every step, because all must wait at the collective.
People often think collectives are only for deep learning. In fact they come from scientific computing (weather models, molecular dynamics, finite elements), where MPI_Allreduce is used for dot products and convergence checks. Deep learning borrowed the machinery.
friction: Failure is the first limit. If one GPU, cable or switch fails, the collective hangs and the whole job stops, because every rank must take part. Meta reported that its 16,384-GPU Llama 3 405B run had roughly 400 unexpected interruptions in 54 days, about one every three hours, and about half were caused by GPU and GPU-memory faults. At that scale, a job survives only because of frequent checkpoints, fast detection of bad nodes, automatic restart and spare capacity. The time lost per failure (detection, restart, lost work since the last checkpoint) is a direct cost of scale.
Second, the tail. Collectives are synchronised, so the step time is set by the slowest participant. Stragglers come from thermal throttling, OS noise, a congested route, or a flaky link running at reduced speed. Finding one slow link among tens of thousands is a monitoring problem that usually needs per-link benchmarks, not just job-level metrics.
Third, network design is expensive and fixed. A fully provisioned fat tree for 100,000 GPUs needs a huge number of switches and optical transceivers, and a cheaper oversubscribed network slows all-to-all traffic. Tensor parallelism cannot cross slow links, so model size and layout are limited by the size of the NVLink domain; this is a main reason rack-scale domains such as NVL72 were built. Finally, the choices interact: batch size cannot grow forever because large batches hurt convergence, so Gustafson's escape has a limit, and numerical details matter (summation order changes the floating-point result, so runs may not be bitwise reproducible).
practice: Match the parallelism to the topology. Keep tensor parallelism (the most communication-heavy and latency-sensitive) inside one NVLink domain, pipeline parallelism across servers, and data parallelism across the rest. Overlap communication with computation: use bucketed gradients (DDP) or prefetching of sharded weights (FSDP), and check in a profiler that the all-reduce kernels run while compute kernels are busy.
Measure before tuning. Run the NCCL tests (nccl-tests: all_reduce_perf) on your cluster at several message sizes and compare the "bus bandwidth" with the hardware limit; a result at 60% of expected means a configuration or cable problem. Do this on every new node, and again regularly, because one degraded link slows the whole job. Set NCCL environment variables (such as the network interface and GPU-to-NIC mapping) only after checking the topology that NCCL reports with NCCL_DEBUG=INFO.
Design for failure: checkpoint often enough that a failure costs minutes, not hours (asynchronous, sharded checkpoints to fast storage), set watchdog timeouts so a hung collective kills the job quickly, keep hot spare nodes and automate replacement. Track metrics that show the cost of communication: exposed communication time per step, model-FLOPs utilisation, and per-link error counters. When a job scales badly, first compute what fraction of the step is non-overlapped communication; Amdahl's law then tells you the best possible gain from fixing it.
tradeoffs: Choose ring all-reduce when messages are large (tens of megabytes and more) and bandwidth is the limit; choose tree or double-binary-tree when messages are small or the cluster is very large, because the ring's 2(N−1) steps of start-up delay dominate. NCCL picks between them automatically by default; override it only when your own benchmark shows a gain. What you give up with the ring is latency, and with the tree a small part of the peak bandwidth.
Choose data parallelism with a full model copy per GPU when the model plus optimizer state fits in memory (a model of a few billion parameters on 80 GB GPUs, with careful use of mixed precision); choose sharded data parallelism (ZeRO, FSDP) when it does not, accepting about 1.5× the communication volume (extra all-gathers of weights) in exchange for memory divided by N. Choose tensor parallelism only inside an NVLink domain and only when a single layer is too large or too slow for one GPU, because it puts an all-reduce on the critical path of every layer.
Choose InfiniBand when you want the most mature, lowest-latency, lossless fabric with in-network reduction and can pay for it; choose RoCE or Ethernet-based fabrics when you want a multi-vendor supply chain, existing operations skills and lower cost per port, and accept that you must tune congestion control and flow control carefully to avoid stalls. Choose a bigger, tightly connected domain (rack-scale NVLink) when your models use heavy tensor or expert parallelism; choose more, cheaper servers when your workload is mostly data parallel and communication is small compared with compute.
Choose frequent checkpoints when the failure rate is high (large clusters); choose less frequent ones when storage bandwidth is the bottleneck. A rough rule: the best checkpoint interval is about √(2 × checkpoint cost × mean time between failures), the Young–Daly formula. If the cost is 2 minutes and the mean time between failures is 3 hours, that is about 27 minutes.
interview: Explain ring all-reduce and why its per-GPU traffic is almost independent of the number of GPUs. || Split each vector into N chunks. Reduce-scatter takes N−1 steps in which each rank sends one chunk to its neighbour and adds the one it receives; all-gather takes N−1 more steps to share finished chunks. Each rank sends 2S(N−1)/N bytes, about 2S, and all links are busy in every step. The cost is 2(N−1) latency terms.
When would you not use a ring, and what would you use? || For small messages or thousands of ranks, where 2(N−1) start-up delays dominate. Use a tree (NCCL double binary tree: two complementary trees, each carrying half of the data), which needs about 2 log₂N steps while keeping most of the bandwidth. A hierarchical scheme also helps: NVLink inside the server, network across servers.
Your 512-GPU job reaches 45% of the throughput predicted from single-node tests. How do you investigate? || First measure exposed (non-overlapped) communication per step with a profiler, then run nccl-tests at the job's message sizes to compare with hardware limits. Check topology detection, GPU-to-NIC mapping, a degraded link or a straggler node (per-node timing), and whether bucket sizes allow overlap. Use Amdahl's law with the measured exposed fraction to see what each fix is worth.
Why does tensor parallelism need an NVLink domain but data parallelism can cross the network? || Tensor parallelism puts an all-reduce in every layer on the critical path, with no work to hide it, so it needs very high bandwidth and low latency. Data parallelism has one all-reduce per step that can overlap with the backward pass, so network bandwidth is enough.
How does a 16,000-GPU training job survive hardware that fails every few hours? || Frequent asynchronous checkpoints to fast storage, health checks that find and cordon bad nodes, watchdog timeouts so a hung collective fails fast, automatic restart from the last checkpoint with spare nodes ready, and a checkpoint interval chosen by the Young–Daly formula. Goal: lose minutes, not hours, per failure.
Why is all-to-all harder than all-reduce on the network? || All-reduce can be arranged as neighbour-to-neighbour transfers with compute on the way. All-to-all sends different data from every rank to every other rank, so total traffic crosses the network bisection and depends on topology and congestion control; the cost cannot be reduced by clever scheduling alone, so bisection bandwidth sets the limit.

@@ cpucomp
value: A general-purpose CPU is built to run any program well: a database, a compiler, a web server, an operating system kernel, a game. It does not know in advance what the program does, so it spends most of its transistors on guessing and hiding delays. It predicts which way a branch will go, executes later instructions while earlier ones wait for memory, and keeps recently used data in several levels of cache. The goal is the shortest possible time for one thread of ordinary, branchy, pointer-heavy code, and then a moderate number of such threads side by side.
That goal is different from a GPU, which gives up single-thread speed for thousands of simple parallel lanes, and from an accelerator built for one kind of arithmetic. A modern server CPU has 64 to 192 cores, each of which can start four to eight instructions per cycle at 3 to 5 GHz. Most of the software in the world (operating systems, databases, application servers, the control code of ML training jobs) runs here, and even GPU programs are launched and fed by a CPU.
Everything above rests on the layers below. The instruction set (L3) is the contract between software and chip, so programs survive new hardware. Pipelining (L3) overlaps the stages of instructions to approach one instruction per cycle per pipeline; out-of-order and superscalar execution (L3) go further and run several ready instructions at once; branch prediction (L3) keeps the pipeline full across if-statements; the memory hierarchy (L3) hides slow DRAM; SIMD (L3) does 8 to 16 operations in one instruction; cache coherence (L3) and NUMA (L3) make many cores share memory correctly, if not equally fast. Moore's law (L5) paid for decades of these tricks. The layers above are the x86, ARM and RISC-V families and products such as Apple M-series, AWS Graviton and AMD EPYC.
terms: Latency — the time one operation takes from start to finish, for example about 80 ns for a DRAM access; the CPU tries to hide it.
Throughput — how many operations finish per unit time; a core can have high throughput even when each operation has high latency.
IPC (instructions per cycle) — the average number of instructions a core completes per clock cycle; modern cores peak at 4 to 8 and typically reach 1 to 3 on real code.
Reorder buffer (ROB) — the hardware window of in-flight instructions (hundreds of entries) that lets the core run later instructions early but retire results in program order.
Speculative execution — running instructions before the CPU knows whether they are needed (after a predicted branch), and discarding the results if the guess was wrong.
Cache line — the unit of data moved between memory levels, 64 bytes on x86 and most ARM server chips.
Memory-level parallelism (MLP) — the number of cache misses the core keeps in flight at once; it divides the effective cost of misses.
Heterogeneous cores — a chip with big performance cores and small efficiency cores sharing one instruction set, as in Apple M-series and Intel hybrid designs.
history: The Intel 4004 (1971) was a 4-bit chip with a few thousand transistors. For the next three decades, designers used each new generation of transistors to make one thread faster. Pipelining arrived in mainstream chips in the 1980s (RISC designs at Berkeley and Stanford, and the Intel 486 in 1989). Tomasulo's algorithm (IBM, 1967) had already shown how to execute instructions out of order, and the Intel Pentium Pro (1995) brought out-of-order execution and speculation to x86 by translating x86 instructions into simpler internal micro-operations. Caches grew from none to three levels.
Two scaling laws drove this. Moore's law (1965) said transistor counts double roughly every two years. Dennard scaling (1974) said that as transistors shrink, their power density stays constant, so clock speed can rise at no extra heat. Around 2005 to 2006, Dennard scaling stopped: leakage current made smaller transistors no longer cooler. Intel cancelled its planned 4 GHz Pentium 4 successors, and clock speeds stayed near 3 to 5 GHz since. Industry turned to multicore (the first dual-core x86 chips arrived in 2005), wider SIMD (MMX 1997, SSE, AVX 2011, AVX-512 2016, ARM NEON and SVE) and better caches.
The instruction-set fight also changed. The x86 family dominated desktops and servers because of software compatibility. ARM, designed for low power, entered the data centre with AWS Graviton (2018), and Apple's M1 (2020) showed that an ARM core with a very wide out-of-order engine and a large cache could beat x86 laptops on performance per watt. RISC-V (2010 at Berkeley, now run by RISC-V International) offers an open instruction set. In January 2018, Spectre and Meltdown showed that speculative execution leaves traces in caches that attackers can read, forcing changes in both hardware and operating systems. Today's design target is performance per watt and per dollar rather than only peak speed.
how: A modern core is a pipeline of stages with a lot of machinery around it. The front end fetches instructions and decodes them: on x86, into micro-operations. Wide cores decode and issue four to eight per cycle; Apple's recent cores are reported to decode eight. The back end holds the micro-operations in a reorder buffer (about 500 entries in recent Intel and AMD cores, and larger in Apple's), sends each one to an execution unit as soon as its inputs are ready, and retires them in program order. This out-of-order machinery is the main way the core hides latency: while one instruction waits for a cache miss, hundreds of later, independent instructions can proceed.
Branch prediction keeps the front end busy. About one instruction in five is a branch. If the core had to wait to learn the outcome, each branch would stall the pipeline for the 15 to 20 cycles it takes to resolve it. Instead, predictors using the history of recent branches guess the direction and target with over 95% accuracy on typical code, and the core fetches along the guessed path. A wrong guess flushes the pipeline and costs those 15 to 20 cycles of wasted work. Because of this, data-dependent branches that are unpredictable (random 50/50 conditions) can dominate run time, and the same loop can be several times faster on sorted data.
The memory hierarchy is what feeds the core. A load that hits the L1 data cache (32 to 64 KB per core) costs about 4 to 5 cycles, roughly 1 ns. L2 (0.5 to 2 MB per core) costs about 14 cycles, L3 (shared across cores, tens to hundreds of MB) costs about 40 to 70 cycles, and DRAM costs 70 to 100 ns, or roughly 250 to 400 cycles. Each level is bigger and slower, and works because programs reuse recent data (temporal locality) and touch nearby data (spatial locality: a whole 64-byte line comes with each access). Hardware prefetchers detect sequential and strided patterns and load lines before they are asked for. Each core can track about 10 to 16 outstanding misses, which gives memory-level parallelism: ten independent misses overlap and cost roughly the time of one.
SIMD adds data parallelism inside one core. An AVX2 instruction operates on 256 bits (8 single-precision floats) and an AVX-512 instruction on 512 bits (16 floats), so a core with two such units can do 16 to 32 floating-point operations per cycle. Compilers vectorize simple loops on contiguous arrays; irregular code gains nothing.
Two more pieces connect the core to the software above it. First, register renaming: programs name only 16 or 32 architectural registers, so many instructions appear to depend on each other only because they reuse the same name. The core maps each write to one of hundreds of hidden physical registers, which removes these false dependencies and exposes the real ones. Second, the instruction set. The ISA (x86-64, ARMv8/ARMv9, RISC-V) fixes what a program may ask for: registers, instructions, memory ordering rules. It does not say how fast or in what order the hardware does it. That freedom is why the same binary runs on a 2015 core and a 2025 core, and why every trick above can change without breaking software. The memory ordering rule is a good example: x86 promises strong ordering of stores, while ARM allows more reordering, which makes cores simpler but exposes concurrency bugs in code that was only tested on x86.
Multicore adds thread parallelism, and with it the problems of sharing. Each core has private caches, so the same line may exist in several of them. A coherence protocol such as MESI tracks each line as Modified, Exclusive, Shared or Invalid and sends invalidation messages so that one core's write becomes visible to others. This costs: when two cores write to different variables on the same cache line (false sharing), the line bounces between them, and a loop that should scale across 16 cores can run slower than on one. In servers with more than one socket (or chiplets with separate memory controllers), memory is attached to specific sockets, so accessing memory on the other socket is slower, typically 1.5 to 2 times the latency. This is NUMA, and it makes thread and memory placement a performance decision. Finally, the chip is limited by power: cores slow down when many are busy (the all-core clock is lower than the single-core turbo), and hybrid designs put the work on efficiency cores when speed is not needed.

example: Start with a core running at 3 GHz that can issue 4 instructions per cycle. Its peak is 12 instructions per nanosecond. A DRAM access takes about 80 ns, which is 240 cycles, or about 960 instruction slots. So one cache miss that the core cannot hide costs as much as nearly a thousand instructions.
Now compare two ways to add up 100 million 4-byte integers (400 MB). Case one: the integers are in an array. The loop reads addresses in order. The hardware prefetcher sees the stream and fetches ahead, so the core is limited by memory bandwidth, not latency. One core can typically stream roughly 10 to 15 GB/s, so 400 MB takes about 30 to 40 ms. With AVX2 the arithmetic is no problem at all.
Case two: the integers are in a linked list whose nodes were allocated at random places in memory. To read node i+1, the core must first finish loading node i to learn its address. The loads form a dependency chain, so out-of-order execution and prefetching cannot help, and memory-level parallelism is 1. Each step costs a full DRAM miss of about 80 ns, so 100 million nodes take 100,000,000 × 80 ns = 8 seconds. That is more than 200 times slower for the same amount of useful data. (If the list nodes were allocated in order, the prefetcher would help, and the gap would be much smaller. Layout, not the algorithm's big-O, is what changed.)
A third case: sum only the values above a threshold in a random array, using an if inside the loop. Half of the branches are mispredicted. Each costs about 15 cycles, so on average 7.5 cycles per element are wasted, against about 0.3 cycles of useful work. The loop runs roughly 10 to 20 times slower than a branch-free version (compilers often turn the if into a conditional move, or you can write the sum as value × (value > threshold)). The same code on sorted data is fast, because the predictor learns the pattern. Both effects come from the core's guessing machinery.
Last, false sharing. Two threads each increment their own 8-byte counter, and the counters sit next to each other in one array. They share one 64-byte line. Every write by one core invalidates the line in the other core's cache, so the line moves back and forth, each transfer costing tens of nanoseconds. Padding each counter to its own 64-byte line removes this, and the loop often gets several times faster.
intuition: A few very skilled generalists, each with a big desk, a personal assistant who fetches files before they are asked for, and a habit of starting the next job while waiting for the first.
The analogy fits the numbers: the desk is the cache, the assistant is the prefetcher, and starting the next job early is out-of-order execution.
The design makes sense once you notice that a general program is mostly waiting: for memory, for a branch to resolve, for a result from the previous instruction. Skilled hardware can fill that waiting time with useful guesses and with independent work. This costs transistors and power, so it only pays off when the workload is irregular and each thread matters. When the work is regular and enormous (matrix multiplication), the same transistors are better spent on thousands of simple lanes, which is the idea of a GPU. A CPU is the best answer when you do not know what the program will do.
mistakes: People often think a faster clock or more cores makes any program faster. In fact many programs are limited by memory latency, locks or one serial dependency chain. A core that waits 240 cycles for DRAM does not run faster at 5 GHz than at 3 GHz by much, and more cores help only if the work splits into independent parts (Amdahl's law).
People often think CISC versus RISC is a big performance difference today. In fact x86 chips translate instructions into RISC-like micro-operations, and the instruction set matters far less than the width of the core, the cache size and the process technology. The ARM versus x86 gap in performance per watt comes mostly from design choices (wide cores, large caches, low clock) and from the process node, not from the ISA alone. The ISA matters mainly for software ecosystem and licensing.
People often think more threads than cores always helps. In fact simultaneous multithreading (hyper-threading) lets two threads share one core's units, and it helps when each thread stalls often, but it gives only about 10 to 30% more throughput on typical code and can hurt latency-sensitive services. ARM server chips such as AWS Graviton do not use it at all: one vCPU is one full core.
People often think speculative execution is invisible to programs. In fact wrong-path instructions are discarded, but their effect on the cache remains, and that is how Spectre reads data across security boundaries. Mitigations (fences, flushing predictors, separate address spaces) cost a few percent to tens of percent on some workloads.
friction: Single-thread performance now improves by roughly 10 to 20% per generation rather than 40 to 50% as in the 1990s. Dennard scaling ended, and designers pay for each extra percent with more area and more power: a wider reorder buffer, a bigger predictor, more cache. The "memory wall" is the second limit: DRAM latency has barely improved in 20 years (about 80 ns), while core speeds rose a hundred-fold, so a growing share of time is spent waiting. Bandwidth per core is shrinking as core counts rise, because the memory channels are shared.
Complexity is the third limit. A modern core has thousands of hidden state machines (predictors, prefetchers, replay queues), and bugs and attacks come from them: Spectre, Meltdown, and later variants showed that performance features can break isolation. Cloud tenants who share a chip can also disturb each other through the shared L3 and memory bandwidth (the noisy neighbour problem). Power is the fourth: a chip can only run part of its cores at full speed within its thermal limit, so peak numbers are rarely reached. Finally, the programming model is demanding: to use the hardware well, code must have good locality, predictable branches and independent work, and none of this is visible in the source language.
practice: Profile before you buy hardware. Use a sampling profiler (perf, async-profiler, VTune) and hardware counters to see IPC, cache-miss rate and branch-miss rate. An IPC below 1 with a high miss rate means the program is waiting for memory, and a faster CPU will not help; changing the data layout will.
Write cache-friendly code: prefer contiguous arrays and structures of arrays over pointer-linked structures, keep hot fields together, process data in blocks that fit in L2, and avoid random access over large tables when sorting or batching the accesses is possible. Keep unpredictable branches out of the innermost loops, and let the compiler vectorize loops over contiguous data (check the compiler's vectorization report). For parallel code, avoid false sharing by padding per-thread data to 64 bytes, and use work-stealing runtimes rather than one thread per task.
In the cloud, benchmark your own workload across instance families. ARM instances (Graviton) often give better price-performance (AWS cites up to 40%) for services written in Java, Go or Rust, but check native libraries and SIMD-specific code. Compare vCPU with care: a vCPU is usually a hardware thread, not a core. On multi-socket machines, pin latency-sensitive processes and their memory to one NUMA node (numactl), and watch for cross-socket traffic. For tail latency, disable features that add noise (frequency scaling surprises, noisy neighbours) only after measuring that they matter.
tradeoffs: Choose a CPU when the work is irregular, branchy, latency-sensitive or needs large memory (databases, web services, compilers, coordination logic, small models); choose a GPU or other accelerator when the work is regular, data-parallel and arithmetic-heavy (matrix multiplication, large model training and inference at scale). Rule of thumb: if more than about 90% of the work is dense, parallel arithmetic, the accelerator wins by an order of magnitude; if the work is full of branches and pointer chasing, it loses, and the data transfer to the device adds cost.
Choose fewer, faster cores (high clock, large cache per core) for latency-bound or license-per-core software such as some databases; choose many slower cores (up to 192 per socket) for throughput services where many requests run in parallel and per-core software cost is zero. You give up single-thread speed in the second case and memory bandwidth per core in both.
Choose x86 when you depend on binary-only software, AVX-512 libraries, or a mature ecosystem; choose ARM (Graviton, Ampere, Apple silicon in development) when you control the software stack and want better performance per watt and per dollar; consider RISC-V for embedded, custom or open-hardware designs where licence cost or customisation matters more than the software ecosystem today.
Choose hyper-threading on when throughput matters and tasks stall often; choose it off (or schedule one thread per core) for tail-latency-sensitive services, security-sensitive multi-tenant hosts or HPC codes that already saturate the core. Choose a single-socket server when you can: it avoids NUMA penalties and often costs less per unit of work than two sockets.
interview: A loop over a 1 GB array is slower than expected on a 3 GHz CPU. How do you find the cause? || Measure with perf: IPC, cache-miss and branch-miss counts. If IPC is low and misses are high, the loop is memory-bound; check access pattern (stride, random access, pointer chasing) and bandwidth. If branch misses are high, look for unpredictable conditions. Then fix the layout, add prefetching or blocking, or use SIMD.
Why can out-of-order execution not hide a linked-list traversal? || Each load needs the address from the previous load, so the loads form a dependency chain with memory-level parallelism of 1. The reorder buffer can only run independent instructions, and there are none to overlap. Each node costs a full miss (about 80 ns for DRAM), so the traversal runs at memory latency rather than bandwidth.
What is false sharing and how do you detect and fix it? || Independent variables on the same 64-byte cache line are written by different cores, so coherence traffic moves the line back and forth. Detect by scaling failure plus high cache-to-cache transfer counters (perf c2c). Fix by padding or aligning each hot variable to its own cache line, or by using per-thread data merged at the end.
Why did single-thread speed stop growing around 2005, and what did the industry do? || Dennard scaling ended: smaller transistors no longer kept power density constant because of leakage, so higher clocks meant too much heat. Designers moved to multicore, wider SIMD, bigger caches and specialised accelerators, so performance now needs parallel software.
What does Spectre exploit, and why can it not be removed completely? || The CPU speculatively runs instructions after a predicted branch and the cache changes stay after the results are discarded. An attacker times cache access to read secrets. Speculation is the core of high IPC, so removal would cost a large part of performance; mitigations limit what speculation can reach.
Would you move a latency-critical service from x86 to ARM? How do you decide? || Benchmark the real service at production load, with tail latency (p99) and cost per request, not only throughput. Check native dependencies and SIMD code for ARM builds, and the effect of vCPU definitions (thread versus core). Roll out with a canary. Move if p99 stays within the SLO and cost per request drops.
quiz: What are the three main ways a general-purpose core hides delay? || It predicts branches so the pipeline stays full, it executes independent instructions out of order while others wait, and it keeps data in a hierarchy of caches (with prefetching) so most loads avoid DRAM.
Why does the same loop run faster on sorted data when it has an if-statement that depends on the values? || Sorted data makes the branch outcome predictable, so the predictor is right almost always. On random data about half the guesses fail, and each failure flushes the pipeline for roughly 15 to 20 cycles.
Why does a linked list traversal run at memory latency rather than memory bandwidth? || Each node's address is stored in the previous node, so loads are serial and the core cannot overlap misses or let the prefetcher run ahead. Memory-level parallelism is 1.
A core runs at 3 GHz with a DRAM latency of 80 ns. How many cycles is one miss? || 80 ns × 3 cycles per ns = 240 cycles. With 4 instructions per cycle that is up to about 960 instruction slots.
When would you prefer many slow cores over few fast cores? || For throughput workloads with many independent requests and little shared state, such as web serving. You get more total work per watt and dollar but slower single-thread latency and less memory bandwidth per core.

@@ searchengine
value: A search engine answers the question "which documents are most relevant to these words?" over a collection that may hold billions of documents, and it answers in tens of milliseconds. A scan of every document would take hours. The engine is fast because it does the work in advance: at indexing time it turns the text into an inverted index (a map from each word to the list of documents that contain it), and at query time it only reads the lists for the query's words, combines them, and scores the few candidates. It is used for web search, product search, log and observability search, enterprise document search and, more and more, as the retrieval step in RAG systems for LLMs.
A search engine is not a database with a LIKE operator. It has to solve three problems at once. First, matching: find documents containing the words, even if the user typed a different form of the word (running, ran) or made a typo. Second, ranking: among thousands of matches, put the ten best on top, using statistics such as how often the word appears and how rare it is. Third, scale: when the index no longer fits on one machine, split it into shards, copy shards for availability, and still return a merged, globally ranked answer quickly.
The concepts below it each solve one part. The inverted index (L2) is the core data structure. Posting-list compression (L2) makes the lists small enough to keep mostly in memory or in the operating system's page cache and fast to scan. BM25 (L2) turns term statistics into a relevance score. The finite-state transducer (L2) stores the dictionary of terms compactly and supports prefix and fuzzy lookups. Doc values (L2) are a column-oriented copy of fields for sorting and aggregations. Partitioning (L2), replication (L2) and scatter–gather (L2) spread the index and the query over a cluster, and PageRank (L2) shows how link structure can be added as a ranking signal for web search. Above this concept sit Lucene, Elasticsearch and OpenSearch.
terms: Inverted index — a map from each term to the sorted list of IDs of the documents that contain it (a posting list), with optional positions and frequencies.
Analyzer — the pipeline that turns text into terms: split into tokens, lowercase, remove stop words, reduce words to stems or lemmas, and add synonyms.
Segment — an immutable mini-index written to disk by Lucene; a shard is a set of segments, and background merges combine small segments into bigger ones.
Term frequency (TF) and inverse document frequency (IDF) — TF is how often a term appears in a document; IDF measures how rare the term is across all documents, so rare terms count for more.
BM25 — the standard probabilistic ranking function that combines TF with saturation, IDF and document-length normalisation.
Shard — one partition of an index, a complete Lucene index on its own; a replica is a copy of a shard on another node.
Query-then-fetch — the two-phase distributed search: first collect the top document IDs and scores from every shard, then fetch the full documents only for the final winners.
Recall and precision — recall is the share of relevant documents that were returned; precision is the share of returned documents that are relevant; ranking quality is also measured with NDCG.
history: Searching text by machine started with Hans Peter Luhn at IBM in the 1950s, who built automatic indexing from word frequencies. Gerard Salton's SMART system (Cornell, from the 1960s) introduced the vector space model, in which queries and documents are weighted vectors of terms. Karen Spärck Jones proposed inverse document frequency in 1972 after noticing that rare words carry more meaning. In the 1990s Stephen Robertson and colleagues developed the probabilistic model and the BM25 formula, tested at the TREC evaluation campaigns that Donna Harman started in 1992.
The web changed the scale. AltaVista (1995) showed that full-text search over tens of millions of pages was possible. Google (1998) added PageRank, a score based on the link graph, and a carefully engineered distributed index; Brin and Page's paper describes sharded indexes and crawling at a scale that was new. Over the next decade Google, Yahoo and Microsoft built huge custom systems with the index split by document across thousands of machines, and Dean and Barroso's "The Tail at Scale" (2013) later described why the slowest shard sets the response time.
For everyone else, Doug Cutting wrote Lucene (1999), which joined the Apache Software Foundation in 2001. It became the standard engine library. Solr (2004, from CNET) put a server and an HTTP API around it, and Elasticsearch (Shay Banon, 2010) added easy sharding, replication and a JSON API, which made distributed search simple for ordinary teams; it became the base of the ELK log stack. In 2021, after Elastic changed its licence, Amazon forked Elasticsearch 7.10 as OpenSearch. Lucene 4 (2012) introduced finite-state transducers for the term dictionary, Lucene 6 (2016) made BM25 the default, and Lucene 8 (2019) added Block-Max WAND for faster top-k queries.
Since 2019 neural methods have been added. BERT-based re-rankers (Nogueira and Cho, 2019) re-order the top few hundred keyword results, and dense retrieval (for example DPR, 2020) maps queries and documents to vectors and finds nearest neighbours. Lucene added HNSW vector search in version 9 (2021). Production systems now commonly run hybrid search: keyword and vector retrieval together, merged by a rank-fusion method.
how: Indexing begins with analysis. A document's text fields are passed through an analyzer: tokenise, lowercase, drop punctuation, optionally stem ("running" to "run") and remove stop words. The result is a list of terms with positions. The engine adds each (term, document ID, frequency, positions) entry to an in-memory buffer. When the buffer is flushed (Elasticsearch does this at a refresh, by default every second), Lucene writes a new immutable segment to disk: a term dictionary, posting lists, stored fields, doc values and, if configured, vector indexes. Immutability is a deliberate choice: segments can be cached freely, read without locks and compressed hard. Updates and deletes are handled by marking the old document as deleted in a bitmap and writing a new version into a new segment. A background merge policy combines small segments into larger ones (and drops deleted documents), so that the number of segments stays low. This is the same idea as the LSM tree, applied to search.
The term dictionary maps each term to the location of its posting list. Lucene stores it as a finite-state transducer, a compact automaton that shares prefixes and suffixes of the terms. It fits in memory even for millions of terms, supports lookup in time proportional to the term length, and allows prefix queries ("run*") and fuzzy matching (all terms within edit distance 2 of a typo) by walking the automaton. The posting lists are sorted by document ID, which has a useful consequence: the IDs can be stored as gaps (differences) between neighbours, which are small numbers, and packed in blocks of 128 with frame-of-reference or PForDelta coding, using a few bits per document. A skip structure lets the engine jump over blocks when it intersects lists. Compression is what makes the index fit in the page cache, and decompression is fast enough that reading compressed lists is quicker than reading raw ones.
Querying works on these lists. A query "red running shoes" is analysed the same way as the documents (this is essential: query and index must use compatible analyzers). For each term, the engine opens the posting list. For an AND query it intersects the lists by walking them in step and skipping ahead; for an OR query it merges them. Every candidate gets a BM25 score for each matching term: the term's IDF, multiplied by a saturating function of term frequency, and normalised by document length. Saturation means that the tenth occurrence of a word adds much less than the second. Length normalisation stops long documents from winning just because they contain more words. The scores of the query terms add up. Then a priority queue of size k keeps the best k results. Block-Max WAND speeds this up: each block of postings stores its maximum possible score, so the engine can skip entire blocks that cannot enter the current top k.
At cluster scale, an index is split into shards by hashing a routing key (by default the document ID) modulo the number of primary shards. Each shard has replicas on other nodes. A search request goes to any node, which becomes the coordinator. It sends the query to one copy (primary or replica) of every shard (scatter), each shard returns its local top (from + size) document IDs with scores, and the coordinator merges these into the global top list (gather). In a second round it fetches the stored fields only for the winning documents. This is query-then-fetch. Because every shard must answer, the response time is the time of the slowest shard, so replicas also help to cut latency: the coordinator can pick the faster copy, and a request can be sent to two copies. Because scores use per-shard statistics by default, IDF can differ slightly between shards; with large shards the difference is small.
Ranking beyond BM25 uses more signals. Web engines add link analysis (PageRank), freshness, click behaviour and many learned features, combined by learning-to-rank models. A common architecture is a cascade: cheap retrieval returns thousands of candidates (BM25 and vector search), a light model re-scores hundreds, and an expensive neural re-ranker scores the last few dozen. Hybrid search merges the keyword and vector lists, for example with reciprocal rank fusion, where each document's score is the sum of 1/(60 + rank) over the lists in which it appears. Doc values, the column-oriented data stored next to the index, are used for sorting, filtering and aggregations (facets such as "count by brand"), because looking up a field value for a document ID in the inverted index would be far too slow.

example: Take three tiny product titles and the query "running shoes". Doc 1: "red running shoes for trail running" (6 terms). Doc 2: "blue running shorts" (3 terms). Doc 3: "trail map and red shoes guide shoes shoes" (8 terms). The average length is (6 + 3 + 8) / 3 = 5.67 terms.
The inverted index has, among others: running → [doc 1 (tf 2), doc 2 (tf 1)] and shoes → [doc 1 (tf 1), doc 3 (tf 3)]. Each term appears in 2 of the 3 documents, so using Lucene's IDF formula ln(1 + (N − n + 0.5)/(n + 0.5)) = ln(1 + 1.5/2.5) = ln(1.6) = 0.47. Use k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75. BM25 for one term is IDF × tf × (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1 × (1 − b + b × length/average length)).
Doc 1, "running": length factor 1 − 0.75 + 0.75 × 6/5.67 = 1.044; denominator 2 + 1.2 × 1.044 = 3.25; numerator 2 × 2.2 = 4.4; part = 0.47 × 4.4/3.25 = 0.64. Doc 1, "shoes": denominator 1 + 1.253 = 2.25, numerator 2.2, part = 0.47 × 0.98 = 0.46. Total 1.10. Doc 3, "shoes" with tf 3: length factor 1 − 0.75 + 0.75 × 8/5.67 = 1.309, denominator 3 + 1.571 = 4.57, numerator 6.6, score 0.47 × 1.444 = 0.68. Doc 2, "running" with tf 1 in a short document: length factor 0.647, denominator 1.78, numerator 2.2, score 0.58.
The ranking is Doc 1 (1.10), Doc 3 (0.68), Doc 2 (0.58). Doc 1 wins because it matches both terms. Doc 3 mentions "shoes" three times, but its score for that term is only 0.68, about 1.5 times the 0.46 that Doc 1 gets for one mention, not 3 times: saturation limits the gain, and the longer length lowers it a little more. Doc 2 matches only one term but is short, so it is close to Doc 3.
Now scale. You have 1 billion product documents of 1 KB each: 1 TB of source, and perhaps 1.2 to 1.5 TB on disk with the index and doc values. With shards of about 30 GB (within the common 10 to 50 GB guidance), you need about 45 primary shards; with one replica, about 90 shards and 3 TB of disk. A query goes to 45 shards in parallel. Each shard scans compressed posting lists for a few hundred thousand matches and returns its top 10, and the coordinator merges 450 entries. Now ask for page 1,000 (results 10,000 to 10,010): each shard must return its top 10,010, the coordinator merges 450,000 entries, and memory and latency explode. That is why engines cap from + size at 10,000 by default and offer search_after (a cursor based on the last sort values) for deep paging.
intuition: A library catalogue prepared in advance: instead of reading every book to find your topic, you look up the word in the index at the back and go straight to the pages.
The idea is natural because the question is asymmetric. Documents are written once and searched many times, and a query has only two or three words, while a collection has billions. So spend effort at write time to flip the data from "document → words" into "word → documents", and the query becomes a few list lookups. Ranking is natural too: a word that appears in every document tells you nothing, a rare word tells you a lot, and repeating a word helps only up to a point. BM25 is just these three observations written as a formula. The distributed design repeats the same logic one level up: split by document so each shard answers independently, and merge only small top lists.
mistakes: People often think a search engine is a database with text columns. In fact it is built differently: immutable segments, per-field analysers, scores instead of boolean matches, and eventual visibility of writes (a new document appears only after the next refresh, about one second later by default). It is a poor system of record, because it does not give transactions and a bad mapping change can force a full reindex.
People often think more shards means faster search. In fact each shard has fixed overhead (memory for term dictionaries, file handles, a thread per search) and every query touches all of them, so thousands of tiny shards make queries slower and clusters unstable. Choose shard count from data size (tens of GB per shard) and keep it small.
People often think relevance is solved by the engine's default scoring. In fact BM25 only knows words and counts. It does not know that "apple" means a company in one query and a fruit in another, or that users prefer newer or cheaper items. Good relevance comes from analyzers, synonyms, field boosts, business signals and judged test queries, and it needs continuous measurement.
People often think vector search replaces keyword search. In fact dense vectors miss exact matches such as product codes, error messages and rare names, while keyword search misses paraphrases. Benchmarks and production systems both show that hybrid retrieval is usually better than either alone.
friction: Relevance work never ends. Language is hard: stemming errors, synonyms that help one query and hurt another, compound words in German, no spaces in Chinese or Japanese, misspellings. Each change to an analyzer needs a reindex, because the index stores analysed terms rather than the original text, and a reindex of billions of documents takes hours or days. Evaluation needs judged queries (human labels or click data), and both are noisy and biased.
Freshness and cost pull against each other. Making new documents visible quickly means creating many small segments, which makes queries slower and merges heavier; large bulk loads need the refresh interval turned off. Updating one field rewrites the whole document, so heavy partial updates are expensive. Merges use a lot of disk I/O and can briefly need extra disk space equal to the merged segments.
At cluster level, the shard count is fixed when the index is created (changing it means splitting or reindexing), and hot shards or uneven shard sizes cause imbalance. Aggregations on high-cardinality fields use a lot of memory. Deep paging and wildcard queries starting with a wildcard can bring a cluster down. Tail latency is the final problem: with 100 shards, even a 1% chance that a single shard is slow gives about a 63% chance that a query is delayed by some shard (1 − 0.99^100), so replicas, timeouts and partial results are needed. Vector indexes add memory cost, since an HNSW graph needs the vectors in RAM for good speed, and filtering combined with vector search remains tricky.
practice: Start from the queries, not the data. Collect a set of real queries with judged results and compute NDCG or recall before and after each change, whether it is a new analyzer, synonym list, field boost or embedding model. Log queries that return zero results and queries with low click-through; they show missing synonyms, content gaps and bad analysis.
Design the mapping carefully. Use text fields (analysed) for full-text search and keyword fields (exact values) for filtering, sorting and aggregations, and turn off indexing for fields that you never search. Keep shards between about 10 and 50 GB, avoid many small indexes, and use time-based indexes or data streams with rollover for logs. Put an alias in front of each index so that you can build a new index, reindex into it and switch the alias without downtime. For bulk loading, raise the refresh interval (or disable it), use bulk requests of a few megabytes, and restore the settings afterwards.
Retrieve broadly, then re-rank. For user-facing search, combine BM25 and vector retrieval (rank fusion), then re-rank the top 50 to 200 with a cross-encoder or learning-to-rank model, and watch the latency budget of each stage. Use filters, which are cached and cheap, in place of scored clauses where scoring is not needed. Avoid leading wildcards and deep from + size pagination. Monitor query latency percentiles (p95, p99), shard sizes, merge and indexing rates, and heap use. If search is not the system of record, keep the source data elsewhere so that you can always rebuild the index.
tradeoffs: Choose a search engine (Lucene-based) when you need relevance-ranked text matching, typo tolerance, facets and filters over up to billions of documents with latency under 100 ms; choose a relational or columnar database when you need transactions, joins and exact analytics, and accept slower text search; choose both when the database is the system of record and the search index is a derived, rebuildable view. You give up consistency: the index lags the source by seconds.
Choose Elasticsearch or OpenSearch when you want a managed distributed system with a rich ecosystem and need log analytics or enterprise search; choose embedded Lucene or a library-style engine when you need tight control over one application's index; choose a columnar real-time OLAP store (Pinot, Druid, ClickHouse) when most queries are aggregations over structured events rather than text relevance. Elasticsearch aggregations are good but memory-hungry at high cardinality.
Choose BM25 only when your users search by words that appear in the documents and you need predictable, explainable results; choose dense vectors when queries are natural language and vocabulary varies; choose hybrid when you cannot afford to miss either exact matches or paraphrases. Dense retrieval adds embedding cost at index time (a model call per document), memory for vectors (4 bytes per dimension per float; 1 million 768-dimension vectors need about 3 GB before graph overhead), and a model you must version.
Choose fewer, larger shards when queries are complex and you want to reduce coordination overhead; choose more, smaller shards when you need to spread write load or recover fast after a node loss. Choose more replicas for read throughput and availability; each replica doubles the disk and indexing cost for its shard. Choose a longer refresh interval (30 s or more) for write-heavy log workloads; choose 1 s only when users need to find a document right after writing it.
interview: How does an inverted index make a multi-word query fast, and how does it stay small? || It maps each term to a sorted list of document IDs, so a query reads only the lists for its terms and intersects or merges them with skipping. Lists are stored as gaps between IDs in blocks with bit-packing (frame-of-reference or PForDelta), and the term dictionary is an FST, so the whole index is a fraction of the text size and mostly cached in memory.
Walk through what happens when a search request reaches an Elasticsearch cluster with 40 shards. || Any node becomes the coordinator, sends the query to one copy of each shard, each shard scores its local documents with BM25 and returns its top (from + size) IDs and scores, the coordinator merges them with a priority queue, then fetches the stored fields for the top results from the shards that own them. Latency is that of the slowest shard.
Why is deep pagination a problem, and what do you do instead? || Each shard has to return from + size results, so the coordinator merges shards × (from + size) entries and holds them in memory; cost grows with the page number. Use search_after with a stable sort and a point-in-time view, or scroll for exports, or limit the depth the UI allows.
Your new document does not appear in search for a second. Is this a bug? || No. Writes go to an in-memory buffer and the translog, and become searchable only when a refresh creates a new segment, by default every second. This near-real-time behaviour is the trade for fast indexing; you can force a refresh in tests, or lengthen the interval for throughput.
How would you improve a search whose top results look wrong? || Build a judged query set and measure NDCG first. Then check analysis (stemming, synonyms, tokenisation), field boosts, and which fields match; look at explain output; add business signals; add a re-ranker or hybrid retrieval. Make one change at a time and measure on the test set, not on one favourite query.
How do you change the analyzer on a field in an index with 2 billion documents? || The index stores analysed terms, so existing segments cannot be reinterpreted. Create a new index with the new mapping, reindex from the source of truth (in parallel slices), keep the old index serving through an alias, validate results, then switch the alias. This is why the source data must live elsewhere.
quiz: What is stored in an inverted index? || For each term, a sorted list of the IDs of the documents that contain it, usually with term frequencies and optionally positions.
Why does BM25 saturate the term frequency instead of using the raw count? || A word repeated many times does not make a document proportionally more relevant, and raw counts would let keyword-stuffed or very long documents win. Saturation (controlled by k1) makes extra occurrences count less.
Why does a search request need a second "fetch" phase after the shards return their top results? || The first phase only needs IDs and scores, which are small, so shards can return their top lists cheaply. Only the final winners' stored fields are needed, so fetching them in a second step avoids moving large documents that are discarded in the merge.
An index of 600 GB is held in 12 primary shards with 1 replica each. What is the shard size and how many shard copies exist? || 600 / 12 = 50 GB per shard, at the top of the usual 10 to 50 GB guidance. With one replica there are 24 shard copies and about 1.2 TB of disk.
When would you choose hybrid search over pure vector search? || When users also search for exact tokens such as product codes, names or error messages that embeddings handle poorly, or when you need explainable matches. Hybrid keeps keyword precision and adds semantic recall.

@@ cloudcomp
value: Cloud computing means renting computing resources (servers, storage, networks, databases, queues, machine learning services) through an API, paying for what you use, and releasing them when you are done. Before the cloud, a team that needed 200 servers filed a purchase order, waited weeks or months for delivery, installed the machines in a data centre and paid for them for four or five years, whether busy or idle. With the cloud, the same team calls an API and has 200 virtual machines in minutes, and deletes them the next day. The change is not only price. It turns infrastructure into software: it can be created, versioned, tested and deleted by code.
The National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) defined the idea in 2011 by five properties: on-demand self-service, broad network access, resource pooling across many customers, rapid elasticity (capacity grows and shrinks quickly) and measured service (usage is metered and billed). It also named three service models: infrastructure as a service (virtual machines, disks, networks), platform as a service (a managed runtime or database) and software as a service (a finished application). Most real systems mix all three.
A cloud is built from a small number of ideas from the layers below. Separating storage from compute (L2) lets durable storage services (object stores such as S3) live apart from short-lived compute. Partitioning (L2) and replication (L2) let each service spread data and load over many machines and survive failures: S3 stores each object redundantly across at least three availability zones and is designed for 99.999999999% (eleven nines) durability. Autoscaling (L2) lets capacity follow load automatically. NAT and firewalls (L3) give private subnets controlled access to the internet. On top of this foundation sit the products people use: AWS, Google Cloud, Azure, Databricks, Snowflake, BigQuery, Redshift and S3.
terms: Region — a geographic area (for example us-east-1 in Virginia) that contains several data centre groups; services and prices differ by region.
Availability zone (AZ) — one or more data centres in a region with independent power, cooling and networking, close enough for low latency (about 1 ms between zones) but far enough that one failure rarely hits two.
IaaS, PaaS, SaaS — infrastructure, platform and software as a service: you manage the operating system and up (IaaS), only the application and data (PaaS), or nothing but your configuration (SaaS).
Elasticity — the ability to add or remove capacity quickly in response to load, usually within minutes, and pay only for what exists.
Shared responsibility model — the split of duties: the provider secures the facilities, hardware and the service itself; the customer secures configuration, identities, data and applications.
Instance — a virtual machine of a given type (vCPUs, memory, network, optional GPU), billed per second or per hour.
Spot instance — spare capacity sold at a large discount (up to about 90%) that the provider can take back with a short warning (two minutes on AWS).
FinOps — the practice of measuring, allocating and optimising cloud cost together with engineering, finance and product teams.
history: The idea of computing as a public utility goes back to John McCarthy in 1961, but it needed three technologies to become real: cheap commodity servers, fast networks and virtualization. Amazon was the first to turn it into a business. Its retail systems had to be rebuilt as services with clean APIs, and the company saw that the infrastructure it had built for itself could be offered to others. Amazon SQS (2004) was the first public service, and in 2006 S3 (storage, March) and EC2 (virtual machines, August) launched, priced by use: a few cents per GB-month and per instance-hour. The first customers were start-ups that could not afford their own data centres.
Competitors followed with different models. Google App Engine (2008) offered a managed platform, and Microsoft Azure (2010) first began as a platform and added virtual machines later. Berkeley's "Above the Clouds" report (Armbrust et al., 2009) explained why this would last: the illusion of infinite capacity, no up-front commitment, and pay-as-you-go use. It also named the obstacles that still exist: availability, data lock-in, data transfer cost and security. Netflix's move to AWS (completed 2016) showed that a large business could run on it.
Over the next decade the offer moved up the stack: managed databases (RDS 2009, DynamoDB 2012), data warehouses (Redshift 2012, BigQuery 2011), queues and streams, containers and Kubernetes (2014 and later), serverless functions (Lambda 2014) and machine learning services. Under the surface, providers moved from standard virtualization to custom hardware: AWS Nitro (2017) moved networking, storage and security to dedicated cards, and AWS, Google and Microsoft now design their own CPUs (Graviton, Axion, Cobalt) and AI chips (Trainium, TPU, Maia). Since 2023 the scarce resource is GPUs for AI, so capacity, power supply and long-term contracts for accelerators dominate cloud strategy, and the leading providers invest tens of billions of dollars a year in new data centres.
how: Underneath the API, a cloud is a very large set of ordinary servers running a control plane that creates and tracks resources. When you ask for a virtual machine, the request is authenticated and authorised (identity and access management), checked against quotas, and passed to a placement service that picks a physical host with enough free capacity. A hypervisor on that host starts the VM from an image, attaches a virtual network interface in your private network and a virtual disk, and starts billing. The key point is multi-tenancy: many customers share the same hardware, and isolation is enforced by the hypervisor, the network virtualization layer and identity policies, so that resource pooling is safe.
Storage is separated from compute. Object storage (S3, GCS, Azure Blob) holds data in many replicas or erasure-coded fragments across zones and is accessed through an HTTP API; block storage (EBS) provides network disks for VMs; file systems and databases are built on the same pieces. Because the data is not on the compute host, a VM can fail or be deleted without data loss, and compute can scale independently of storage. This idea (L2, separating storage and compute) is the foundation of modern data platforms: Databricks, Snowflake and BigQuery all keep data in object storage and start compute clusters on demand. The cost is latency: object storage reads take tens of milliseconds, against fractions of a millisecond for a local SSD.
Reliability comes from replication and failure domains. A region has at least three AZs on the major providers. A well-designed service runs instances in several zones and a load balancer sends traffic to healthy ones. Managed data services replicate data synchronously or asynchronously across zones, so the loss of a data centre does not lose data. The provider carries the burden for the physical layer; the customer still has to build the application so that it tolerates the loss of an instance or a zone.
Elasticity comes from autoscaling and from pooling. Autoscaling groups watch a metric (CPU use, queue length, requests per second) and add or remove instances according to a policy. The provider can offer this only because it serves thousands of customers whose peaks occur at different times; pooling smooths the demand, so the provider keeps much less spare capacity than each customer would on its own. That statistical multiplexing is the economic engine. Prices reflect the commitment: on-demand is the most flexible and most expensive; one- or three-year commitments (reserved instances, savings plans) give large discounts (AWS cites up to about 70%) for steady load; spot capacity gives up to 90% off for interruptible work.
Networking is built as software. A virtual private cloud (VPC) gives you a private IP range, subnets across zones, route tables and security groups (stateful firewalls attached to instances). Machines in private subnets have no public address; outbound traffic to the internet goes through a NAT gateway (L3, NAT and firewalls), which translates private addresses to a shared public one and costs a per-hour fee plus a per-GB processing fee. Traffic inside a zone is usually free, but traffic between zones, between regions and out to the internet is billed per GB. This pricing is part of the design: data has "gravity", so compute is usually placed next to its data.
Providers separate the control plane from the data plane, and this explains much of their reliability. The control plane (create, modify, delete resources) is complex and changes often. The data plane (packets flowing, bytes read from a disk, a running VM) is simple and stable. Good designs make the data plane keep working when the control plane is down: running instances continue to serve traffic even if you cannot launch new ones. Identity is the thread through both: every API call carries a signed identity, and a policy engine decides whether that identity may perform that action on that resource. Metering sits next to it: every call and every byte is counted, and the counts become the bill.
The same API surface makes everything automatable. Infrastructure as code (Terraform, CloudFormation, Pulumi) describes the desired resources, tags carry ownership and cost centre, and the billing system produces line items per resource, so cost can be attributed to teams. Governance (organisations, accounts, service control policies) keeps hundreds of teams from affecting each other.

example: Consider a team that runs a Kafka cluster for ad events. Ingest is 100 MB/s, replication factor 3, with brokers spread over 3 AZs (the standard way to survive a zone loss). Each message is written to the leader and copied to two followers, which sit in the other two zones. So for each 100 MB/s of input, 200 MB/s crosses zone boundaries just for replication. That is 200 MB × 86,400 s = 17.28 TB per day. At a list price of about $0.01 per GB charged in each direction, that is $0.02 per GB, or 17,280 GB × $0.02 ≈ $346 per day, roughly $10,000 per month. This is before producers and consumers, who also cross zones when they talk to a leader in another zone (about two thirds of the time with random placement). The brokers themselves may cost less than the network. A team that knows this reads from the follower in its own zone (Kafka has a feature for this), or places consumers in the same zone, and cuts the bill sharply. Prices change, so check the current rate; the method matters: bytes per second × seconds × price per GB.
Second example: renting versus owning compute. Suppose a 64-vCPU instance costs about $2.70 per hour on demand, which is $23,700 per year if it runs all the time. With a three-year commitment the effective price might be near 40 to 50% of that, about $11,000 per year. A comparable owned server might cost $20,000 to buy, last 4 to 5 years ($4,000 to $5,000 per year), and add power, rack space, network and staff time; a reasonable all-in figure could be $8,000 to $12,000 per year. At 100% steady load, owning and a committed cloud price are close, and owning might win by a small margin if you already run a data centre. At 20% average load, the picture changes: the cloud lets you shut capacity down (pay about $4,700 on demand for 20% of the hours), while the owned server costs the same $8,000 to $12,000 regardless. The decision depends on utilisation, commitment and operating skill, not on a general rule.
Third, elasticity in numbers. A batch job needs 1,000 vCPU-hours of processing every night. On a fixed cluster sized for a 2-hour run you would need 500 vCPUs all day. In the cloud you start 500 spot vCPUs for 2 hours and release them: you pay for 1,000 vCPU-hours at a spot price of perhaps $0.01 to $0.02 per vCPU-hour, about $10 to $20 per night, instead of reserving 500 vCPUs for 24 hours.
intuition: Infrastructure becomes an electricity bill: you plug in, use what you need and pay for it, and someone else runs the power station.
The economics are natural. Hardware is cheaper per unit when bought by the million, run in huge data centres and operated by automation. Demand from one customer is bursty, while demand from a million customers is smooth. So a provider can offer capacity at a price lower than the customer's own cost for the average load, while the customer escapes the cost of the peak. The technology that made this possible is the ability to slice a machine into safe, isolated parts (virtualization) and to put storage behind a network so that compute can come and go. The remaining cost is dependence: you rely on the provider's APIs, prices and availability.
mistakes: People often think the cloud is always cheaper. In fact the cloud is cheaper when load is variable, when you would not run a data centre otherwise, or when speed to market matters. At a high steady load, or with heavy data movement, owned hardware or committed capacity can cost less. Several large companies have moved some workloads out of the cloud for this reason. Treat cost as a design input, measured per team and per workload.
People often think the provider makes the application highly available. In fact the provider makes the building blocks available (zones, replicated storage, managed databases), but a single-instance application in one zone still fails when that zone fails. Reliability is the customer's design, within the shared responsibility model. Several major outages of a single region have taken down customers who had no multi-zone design.
People often think moving to the cloud means moving the servers. In fact a "lift and shift" copy of an on-premises system into virtual machines gives the cost of the cloud with little of its benefit. The gains come from managed services, autoscaling, replacing fixed capacity with on-demand capacity, and automation, which need changes in the application's design.
People often think multi-cloud removes lock-in. In fact portable abstractions (Kubernetes, Terraform, open-source databases) reduce lock-in at the compute level, but the data, identity, networking and the managed services you use remain tied to one provider, and moving data out costs egress fees. Multi-cloud adds complexity and the lowest common denominator of features; it is justified by regulation, acquisition or a specific capability, not by default.
friction: Cost is the first problem. Billing has thousands of line items; waste hides in idle instances, over-sized machines, forgotten snapshots, unattached disks, NAT and cross-zone traffic, and logging. Cloud bills can grow faster than the business, which gave rise to FinOps teams. Data egress is a structural cost: moving data out of a provider is priced per GB while moving it in is free, which creates lock-in and makes multi-region designs expensive.
Second, you do not control the platform. Quotas and capacity limits (especially for GPUs) can block a launch; a region-level incident (several have lasted hours) affects everything in it, including the control plane you would use to recover; services are deprecated on the provider's schedule. Noisy neighbours and variable performance on shared hardware cause tail latency that is hard to diagnose. Cloud services are black boxes: you see metrics, not the machine.
Third, security is shared and easy to misconfigure. A public storage bucket or an over-broad IAM role is a typical cause of data leaks, and the number of settings is enormous. Compliance and data residency rules limit where data may live. Finally, complexity: hundreds of services with overlapping functions, each with its own limits and pricing, and a pace of change that makes skills and designs age quickly.
practice: Design for failure from the start. Spread instances across at least two or three zones behind a load balancer, keep state in managed, replicated services rather than on instance disks, and automate recovery (auto-healing groups, infrastructure as code). Test it: terminate an instance, then a zone, in a pre-production environment and see what happens.
Treat cost as an engineering metric. Tag every resource by team, service and environment from day one, review the bill weekly, and show each team its own number. Right-size instances using measured utilisation. Commit (savings plans, reserved capacity) only for the steady baseline, usually 50 to 70% of the minimum load, and cover the rest with on-demand and spot. Use spot for fault-tolerant work such as batch processing, CI, and stateless services behind a diverse instance pool. Watch the three expensive categories that surprise teams: cross-zone and cross-region data transfer, NAT gateway processing, and logs and metrics volume. Use lifecycle rules to move cold object data to cheaper storage classes.
Keep infrastructure in code, use separate accounts for production and development with least-privilege roles, turn on audit logs, and block public storage by default. Use managed services where they are not your differentiator, because patching and failover are then the provider's job, but write down the exit cost for the services that hold your data. Keep an eye on quotas before big launches, and ask for capacity (especially GPUs) well in advance.
tradeoffs: Choose the public cloud when load is variable or unknown, when you need to start in days, or when you want managed services such as databases, queues and data warehouses; choose your own hardware or colocation when load is large, steady and predictable (for example above 70 to 80% utilisation for years), data movement is heavy, and you have the skills to run it. You give up elasticity and speed in the second case, and cost predictability and control in the first.
Choose on-demand for new, uncertain or short workloads; choose one- to three-year commitments for the steady baseline when you can forecast it, accepting that you pay even if you shrink; choose spot when the work tolerates interruption and can checkpoint. The discount is large (up to about 70% for commitments and 90% for spot at list prices), the price is flexibility or reliability.
Choose managed services (a managed database, Kafka as a service, a managed warehouse) when your team is small or the service is not your core competence; choose self-managed on VMs or Kubernetes when you need features the managed version lacks, need to control cost at large scale, or need portability. Managed services usually cost more per unit of capacity and add lock-in, but save operations effort that, for a small team, is the scarcer resource.
Choose a single region with several zones when your availability target is 99.9 to 99.99% and your users are in one geography; choose multi-region when you need to survive a regional failure, meet data residency rules, or serve global users with low latency. Multi-region multiplies cost and complexity (data replication, consistency choices, failover testing); many teams are better served by good backups and a tested recovery plan in a second region.
interview: What is the difference between an availability zone and a region, and how does that shape the design of a stateful service? || A region is a geographic area; its AZs are separate data centre groups about 1 ms apart with independent power and networking. Spread replicas across AZs for failures of a data centre, using synchronous or quorum replication within the region; use cross-region replication (asynchronous) for regional disasters. Cross-zone traffic costs money and adds latency, so choose replication factors deliberately.
Why can a cloud provider sell capacity cheaper than you can build it? || Scale lowers unit cost (hardware bought in bulk, custom designs, automation), and pooling many customers with different peaks reduces idle capacity (statistical multiplexing). The customer pays for average use instead of provisioning for its own peak.
Your cloud bill doubled in a month with no new features. How do you investigate? || Start from the cost breakdown by service, account and tag. Typical causes: data transfer (cross-zone, NAT, egress), a new logging or metrics stream, instances left running or autoscaling stuck high, snapshots or storage growth, or a changed instance type. Compare with usage metrics, find the owner through tags, fix the cause, and add budget alarms and anomaly detection.
Is multi-cloud a good way to avoid vendor lock-in? || Usually not by default. It adds operational complexity and limits you to common features, while data gravity and egress charges still bind you. It is justified by regulation, customer demand or a specific capability. A better approach is clear boundaries, open formats and an estimated exit cost for key services.
When would you move a workload from the cloud back to owned hardware? || When load is large and steady so that committed cloud prices still exceed the all-in cost of owned hardware, when data egress or storage cost dominates, when latency or sovereignty needs require a specific location, and when the team can run it. Model the full cost including staff, capacity planning and the loss of elasticity.
How do you make an application survive the loss of one availability zone? || Run capacity for at least N+1 zones so the remaining zones can carry the load (for 3 zones, each at no more than 66% utilisation), keep state in replicated stores with quorum across zones, make instances stateless and auto-healing, avoid hard zone affinity, and test with a zone-failure drill. Check that the control plane actions you need for recovery do not depend on the failed zone.
quiz: What are the five essential characteristics of cloud computing in the NIST definition? || On-demand self-service, broad network access, resource pooling, rapid elasticity and measured service.
Why does separating storage from compute help a cloud service scale? || Data lives in a durable, replicated service independent of any machine, so compute nodes can be added, replaced or deleted without moving or losing data, and storage and compute can be sized and billed separately.
Why is a Kafka cluster spread over 3 AZs with replication factor 3 expensive in data transfer? || Each message is copied to two followers in other zones, so ingest of X MB/s causes about 2X MB/s of cross-zone traffic, which is billed per GB in both directions.
A cluster averages 30% utilisation of 100 instances, with peaks to 90%. Roughly how many instances are saved with autoscaling that follows load closely? || Average need is about 30 instances (plus some headroom, say 40 to 45) instead of 100 always on, so 55 to 70 instances (more than half) are saved on average, although the peak still needs capacity.
When would you choose spot instances over on-demand? || For fault-tolerant, interruptible work such as batch processing, CI jobs and stateless workers that can checkpoint, where a 60 to 90% discount outweighs the risk of reclaiming with a two-minute warning.

@@ serverless
value: Serverless is a way of running software in which you do not manage servers, do not choose how many exist, and pay only while your code or request is actually being served. You upload a function (or use a managed service such as a queue, a database or a SQL warehouse), tell the platform when to run it, and the platform finds a machine, starts a sandbox, runs the code, and bills you by the millisecond and the megabyte. When no requests arrive, nothing runs and nothing is billed: the system scales to zero. When a thousand requests arrive at once, it creates a thousand sandboxes.
The problem it solves is the mismatch between how teams provision and how load behaves. A web hook that fires a few times a day, a nightly file-processing job, an image thumbnailer that runs when a photo is uploaded: each would need a server that is idle 99% of the time, plus patching, monitoring and capacity planning. Serverless removes the idle cost and most of the operations work. It also makes the unit of deployment very small: one function, one event, one result.
Serverless is only possible because of the layers below. Separating storage and compute (L2) lets functions stay stateless while data lives in object stores and databases, so any sandbox can be thrown away or created at any time. Namespaces and cgroups (L3), and in stronger form microVMs, isolate thousands of customers' code on shared hardware at low overhead. Autoscaling (L2) adds sandboxes per request instead of per minute. Above it, AWS Lambda made the model popular, and the cloud platforms (L0) offer serverless variants of nearly every service.
terms: Function as a service (FaaS) — a service that runs one function per event in a managed sandbox, billed per invocation and per unit of memory-time; AWS Lambda is the best-known example.
Cold start — the extra delay when a request needs a new sandbox: allocating a host, starting the sandbox, loading the code and starting the language runtime.
Warm start — a request served by an existing, idle sandbox that is reused; it skips the cold start.
Execution environment — the sandbox (a microVM in Lambda) that holds one copy of your function and its runtime; it handles one request at a time.
Scale to zero — the platform keeps no running instances when there is no traffic, so you pay nothing for idle time.
Concurrency — the number of requests being processed at the same moment, which for FaaS equals the number of active execution environments.
Idempotent — safe to run more than once with the same effect; needed because events can be delivered at least once.
Provisioned concurrency — a paid setting that keeps a chosen number of environments initialised in advance to avoid cold starts.
history: The idea has older roots. Platform-as-a-service systems such as Heroku (2007) and Google App Engine (2008) already hid servers, but they scaled in units of whole application instances and could not scale to zero cheaply. The new element in 2014 was the function as the unit: AWS announced Lambda at re:Invent in November 2014 (general availability in April 2015), with billing in 100 ms steps, triggers from events such as S3 uploads, and no server for the customer to name. Billing moved to 1 ms steps in December 2020. Google Cloud Functions, Azure Functions and IBM's OpenWhisk (open source, 2016) followed. Cloudflare Workers (2017) ran code in V8 isolates at the network edge, starting in milliseconds, which avoided the cold-start problem for small scripts at the cost of a restricted runtime.
Early Lambda isolated customers with containers inside VMs, which was either slow to start or wasteful. In 2018 AWS announced Firecracker, a minimal virtual machine monitor written in Rust on top of Linux KVM, open-sourced and described at NSDI 2020. A Firecracker microVM boots to application code in under 125 ms, adds under 5 MB of memory overhead, and one host can create up to 150 of them per second; it gave VM-grade isolation with near-container density. In parallel, the idea spread beyond functions: Fargate (2017) and Cloud Run (2019) run containers without servers; DynamoDB on-demand and Aurora Serverless (2018) scale databases by request; BigQuery, Athena and serverless SQL warehouses bill per query.
The research view arrived in 2019. Berkeley's "Cloud Programming Simplified" predicted serverless would grow because it matches the cloud's promise of paying for use, and Hellerstein et al. ("Serverless Computing: One Step Forward, Two Steps Back") pointed out the costs of stateless functions that have to ship data to the code instead of code to data. Since then, features such as SnapStart (snapshot and restore of an initialised Java runtime, 2022), container image support (up to 10 GB), longer limits and streaming responses have made the model usable for more workloads, and AI inference on demand has added a new case for scale-to-zero GPUs.
how: When a request arrives at a function, the platform runs a short pipeline. A front-end service authenticates the call and looks up the function's configuration. A placement service looks for an idle, already-initialised execution environment for this function. If one exists (a warm start), the request is sent to it directly. If none exists, the platform picks a host with free capacity, starts a new sandbox (a Firecracker microVM in Lambda), fetches the function code or container image, starts the language runtime, runs your initialisation code (imports, connections) and only then calls the handler. That sequence is a cold start. The environment handles exactly one request at a time. When it finishes, the environment stays idle for a while (minutes, but not guaranteed) so that the next request can reuse it, and then it is destroyed.
Scaling is therefore simple: concurrency equals the number of environments, and each new concurrent request that finds none idle creates one. There is no autoscaler waiting for a metric to cross a threshold; the scaling decision is made per request. This is why a function can go from zero to hundreds or thousands of concurrent executions in seconds, limited by account quotas (1,000 concurrent executions per region by default on Lambda, which can be raised) and by a rate at which new environments can be created. Autoscaling (L2) here is reactive at request granularity rather than periodic.
Statelessness is the contract that makes this possible. Between invocations the platform may keep the environment or throw it away, so anything you need later must be in external storage: objects in S3, rows in a database, messages in a queue. Local memory and the /tmp directory (up to 10 GB, but not durable) are only caches. This is separating storage from compute (L2) taken to its limit: compute is disposable and anonymous, and storage is shared and durable. A consequence is that functions do not talk to each other directly; they communicate through storage or queues, and the network between a function and its data adds latency to every step.
Events decide how functions are invoked. In synchronous mode (an API gateway or load balancer calls the function and waits), the caller sees errors directly and handles retries. In asynchronous mode (S3 notifications, SNS), the platform puts the event in an internal queue and retries failures, by default twice, so a handler can run more than once for one event. In polling mode (SQS, Kinesis, Kafka, DynamoDB streams), the platform runs pollers that read batches from the source and call your function with each batch, scaling the pollers according to backlog. In all cases the guarantee is at least once, so handlers must be idempotent (for example by recording an event ID before applying its effect).
Isolation and limits keep the shared hardware safe and fair. Each environment has its own kernel boundary (microVM), a memory size from 128 MB to 10,240 MB, and CPU power proportional to memory (about one vCPU at 1,769 MB). There is a maximum duration (15 minutes on Lambda), payload limits (6 MB for synchronous requests), and a limited writeable disk. The limits shape the design: long jobs are split into steps or moved to containers, and large data goes through object storage instead of the request body.
Platforms work hard to shorten the cold start, and the techniques show where the time goes. Code and container images are not copied whole to every host: Lambda splits an image into chunks, caches the chunks in layers of shared caches, and loads only the chunks that the function actually reads (Brooker et al., 2023), so a large image can still start quickly. SnapStart takes a memory snapshot of an initialised runtime after the init phase and restores it for new environments, which turns seconds of Java class loading into a restore of tens to hundreds of milliseconds. Placement tries to put new environments on hosts that already hold the needed chunks, and keeps spare warm microVMs to be assigned quickly.
Billing is the other half of the model. On Lambda you pay per request (about $0.20 per million) plus compute time as memory in GB times duration in seconds (about $0.0000166667 per GB-second for x86), rounded to the millisecond. Because CPU scales with memory, a bigger setting makes the code run faster, and the total cost can fall: a CPU-bound function that takes 800 ms at 512 MB costs 0.4 GB-s, while the same code at 2 GB, with four times the CPU, might take 150 ms and cost 0.3 GB-s, so the larger setting is both cheaper and faster. Serverless data services bill the same way, per request or per byte scanned.

example: Price a simple API handler on Lambda. It uses 512 MB (0.5 GB) and runs 100 ms. Compute per request: 0.5 GB × 0.1 s = 0.05 GB-s, and at $0.0000166667 per GB-s that is $0.00000083. The request fee is $0.20 per million, or $0.0000002. Total: about $0.000001 per request, or $1.03 per million requests (the free tier and discounts for Arm aside).
Spiky workload: 2 million requests per day, mostly in a few busy hours. Monthly cost: 60 million × $1.03 per million ≈ $62. A single always-on instance that serves this (and a second for availability) costs about 2 × $106 = $212 per month for a 4-vCPU Arm instance at roughly $0.145 per hour, plus patching and monitoring. Serverless is clearly cheaper, and there is nothing to scale.
Steady workload: 100 requests per second all day. That is 100 × 2,592,000 s = 259.2 million requests per month. Cost: 259.2 × $1.03 ≈ $268. If the handler waits mostly on a database (I/O-bound), two instances at about $212 can serve the same load, so costs are comparable and the instances cost operations effort. At 1,000 requests per second, serverless costs about $2,680 per month, while perhaps 10 to 15 instances ($1,000 to $1,600) would serve it. Past a certain constant rate, provisioned capacity wins on price; the break-even is where average utilisation of provisioned capacity stays high.
Now a cold start. A Java function with a large framework takes 3 s to initialise, a Python function 200 ms. A burst arrives: 0 to 200 concurrent requests in one second. The platform must create 200 environments. In the Java case, the first 200 requests each wait about 3 s extra. If bursts like this happen every few minutes, the p99 latency of your API is dominated by cold starts, not by your code. Options: provisioned concurrency of 50 (a fixed cost of 50 × 0.5 GB × 3,600 s × 24 h × 30 ≈ 64.8 million GB-s, at a discounted rate), SnapStart to restore a pre-initialised snapshot, or a lighter runtime. The right choice depends on the latency target.
Finally, retries. An S3 event invokes a function that writes a result row and then calls a payment API. The function times out after the payment, before it can return. The platform retries the event, and the customer is charged twice. The fix is an idempotency key, for example the S3 object version ID, stored in a database with a conditional write before the payment, so the second run sees the key and skips the call.
intuition: Paying for the taxi ride instead of owning the car: you never think about the garage, but if you ride all day, buying a car is cheaper.
The model is natural when you see what the user really wants: to run a piece of logic when something happens. A server is only a means to that end, and it is wasteful whenever it waits. If the provider can start an isolated sandbox fast enough, it can keep no idle machine per customer and instead share one pool of hardware among all of them, using statistical multiplexing: at any moment only a small fraction of all functions are running. The price per second is higher than renting a server by the hour, but you pay for far fewer seconds. The design forces statelessness because the platform is free to destroy your sandbox at any time, and that same freedom is what lets it scale to zero and to thousands.
mistakes: People often think serverless means there are no servers. In fact there are servers, owned and operated by the provider. What disappears is the customer's responsibility to provision, scale and patch them. You still manage code, configuration, permissions, timeouts and concurrency.
People often think serverless is always cheaper. In fact it is cheaper for spiky and low-volume traffic and loses at steady high load, where you pay a premium per unit of compute. Running a function for 15 minutes at high memory is also expensive compared to a container. Costs from other services, such as API gateways, logs and data transfer, can exceed the function cost.
People often think cold starts only matter for user-facing latency. In fact they also affect scale-out speed and cost, and they interact with downstream systems: a burst of 1,000 new environments can each open a database connection and exhaust a database that can handle 200. Functions need connection pooling through a proxy, and reserved concurrency limits to protect dependencies.
People often think scale to infinity is a feature you can rely on. In fact quotas and rate limits bound scale, and the bottleneck is usually downstream: a database, an API with rate limits, or a queue consumer. Serverless moves the scaling problem to the other side of the function.
friction: Cold starts are the best-known cost: typically tens to hundreds of milliseconds for small Node.js or Python functions and one to several seconds for large Java or .NET functions without snapshotting. Mitigation costs money or effort (provisioned concurrency, SnapStart, smaller packages, lazy initialisation). The execution model also has hard limits: maximum duration, memory, payload size and local disk, so some workloads (long jobs, big in-memory models, WebSocket servers with state) do not fit or need other services.
Statelessness has a performance cost. Every access to shared state is a network call, so latency adds up and caching is weak, because each environment has its own memory. Functions cannot share memory or accept direct connections, which makes patterns such as in-memory joins, local caches and long-lived connections hard. Communication through queues and storage brings eventual consistency and many partial-failure cases. The paper "Serverless Computing: One Step Forward, Two Steps Back" argued that this "data shipping" architecture is a step back for data-intensive workloads.
Operational problems follow. Debugging is harder: you cannot attach to a machine, local emulation differs from the real platform, and distributed traces across dozens of functions are needed. Observability costs money because logs are billed per GB. The permission model is detailed, and one over-broad IAM role per function is a common risk. There is vendor lock-in: the function code may be portable, but the triggers, the permission model, the event formats and the surrounding managed services are not. Finally, the cost is hard to predict when load is unbounded: a bug that loops events, or a spike from a scraper, can produce a large bill, so budgets and concurrency limits are needed.
practice: Use serverless where its strengths apply: event-driven glue (an S3 upload triggers processing), spiky or unpredictable web and API traffic, scheduled jobs, webhooks, and workloads that can be split into short, independent tasks. Prefer containers or instances for steady high load, long-running jobs, large in-memory state or special hardware, and compare the monthly cost at your real request rate before choosing.
Write functions that are small, stateless and idempotent. Initialise heavy objects (SDK clients, connections) outside the handler so that warm invocations reuse them. Keep deployment packages small and use only the libraries you need, which is the biggest lever on cold starts. Choose memory by measurement (tools such as Lambda Power Tuning): more memory means more CPU and often lower cost. Set a timeout shorter than the caller's timeout, set reserved concurrency to protect downstream databases, and put an RDS Proxy or a similar pool between functions and relational databases.
Design for at-least-once delivery. Attach a dead-letter queue or failure destination to asynchronous functions, and set a sensible retry count. Use partial batch failure responses for queue-driven functions so that one bad message does not retry the whole batch. Measure cold-start frequency and p99 latency, not only averages. Use infrastructure as code, one function per clear responsibility, and structured logging with correlation IDs so that you can trace a request across functions. Set budget alarms from the first day.
tradeoffs: Choose serverless functions when traffic is spiky or low (the idle fraction is above about 70 to 80%), when the team is small and operations time is scarcer than money, or when you want pay-per-use for experiments; choose provisioned containers or instances when load is steady and high, when you need long-lived connections, special hardware or predictable latency, and accept that you manage capacity. The break-even is often between tens and a few hundred requests per second for short handlers, depending on handler duration and memory.
Choose functions for short event handlers; choose a serverless container platform (Cloud Run, Fargate, Lambda with a container image) when you have an existing containerised service, need more than 15 minutes, or need concurrency within one instance (Cloud Run lets one instance handle many requests at once, which cuts cost for I/O-bound services). You give up the pure function model but keep scale to zero in some of these services.
Choose provisioned concurrency or SnapStart when a latency-sensitive path cannot tolerate cold starts and you accept paying for always-ready capacity; choose to tolerate cold starts for background and asynchronous work, where an extra second does not matter. Choose edge isolates (Cloudflare Workers and similar) when you need single-digit-millisecond starts and global placement for small scripts, and accept a restricted runtime and limits on CPU time and libraries.
Choose serverless databases and warehouses (DynamoDB on-demand, Aurora Serverless, serverless SQL) for unpredictable or intermittent access; choose provisioned capacity when the load is steady and you can predict it, because per-request pricing is usually higher per unit at constant high use. Choose a portable layer (containers, Knative) when avoiding lock-in is worth more than the convenience of the native platform.
interview: What happens, step by step, between an event and your handler running for the first time? || The front end authenticates and finds the function's config. No idle environment exists, so the placement service picks a host, starts a microVM or sandbox, loads the code, starts the runtime, runs your init code, and calls the handler. That is a cold start: tens of ms to seconds. After the call the environment stays idle for reuse and is later destroyed.
How would you reduce the cold-start impact for an API with p99 latency target of 300 ms? || First measure how often cold starts occur and their size. Shrink the package, avoid heavy frameworks, move initialisation off the critical path, pick a faster runtime or use SnapStart. Use provisioned concurrency for the baseline load and let bursts above it use on-demand environments. Or move to a container platform with minimum instances if load is steady.
A Lambda function consuming an SQS queue overloads your PostgreSQL database. Why, and what do you do? || Lambda scales out with queue backlog, and each environment opens its own connections, so connections and query concurrency exceed what the database supports. Limit it with reserved concurrency or the event-source maximum concurrency, add RDS Proxy or another pooler, batch writes, and make processing idempotent.
Why must serverless handlers be idempotent? || Async and queue triggers deliver at least once, and a timeout or crash after a side effect causes a retry. Without idempotency, you duplicate payments or emails. Use an idempotency key stored with a conditional write, or make the operation naturally repeatable (upsert by key).
When is serverless the wrong choice? || Steady high throughput where per-unit price is higher than provisioned capacity; long-running or stateful workloads; large in-memory models or special hardware; latency targets that cold starts would break; and cases with tight vendor-portability requirements. Compare cost per million requests and p99 latency against containers before deciding.
Why is Firecracker important for serverless? || Containers share a kernel, which is a weak boundary for running strangers' code. Full VMs are strong but slow and large. Firecracker is a minimal VMM on KVM with a tiny device model: boot to application code under 125 ms and under 5 MB overhead, so each function can have a hardware-isolated sandbox at high density.
quiz: What is a cold start and what causes it? || It is the extra delay when no idle execution environment exists: the platform must allocate a host, start a sandbox, load code, start the runtime and run initialisation code before the handler runs.
Why do serverless functions have to be stateless? || The platform may destroy an environment at any time and may run different invocations on different environments, so local memory or disk cannot be relied on. Durable state must live in external storage.
Why can a larger memory setting make a Lambda function cheaper? || CPU share is proportional to memory, so more memory shortens the run time. Cost is GB × seconds, so if time falls faster than memory rises (for example 800 ms at 512 MB is 0.4 GB-s, and 150 ms at 2 GB is 0.3 GB-s), cost falls. This works for CPU-bound code, not for code that waits on the network.

value: A container is an ordinary Linux process (or group of processes) that the kernel has been told to isolate: it sees its own file system, its own process list, its own network interfaces and hostname, and it may use only a fixed amount of CPU and memory. There is no second operating system. All containers on a host share one kernel, which is why a container starts in a fraction of a second and costs almost no memory beyond what the program itself uses.
The problem it solves is "it works on my machine". Software depends on a particular operating system version, libraries, language runtime and configuration. Installing these by hand on every server leads to differences between development, test and production, and to conflicts when two applications need different versions of the same library. A container image packs the application and everything it needs, except the kernel, into a single immutable, versioned artifact. The same image runs on a laptop, in CI and in production, and ten different applications can share one server without sharing libraries.
Two kernel features make this possible, and both come from the layers below. Namespaces and cgroups (L3) provide isolation: namespaces control what a process can see, and cgroups control how much it can use. The file system layer (L3) supplies the image: an image is a stack of read-only layers merged with a writable top layer using a union file system (overlayfs), so layers can be shared between images and downloaded once. Above this concept sit Docker (the tools to build and run), Kubernetes (which schedules containers across machines and uses the pod as its unit), the Linux kernel itself, and Cilium (which connects containers' networks).
terms: Namespace — a kernel feature that gives a group of processes their own view of one kind of resource: process IDs, mounts, network, hostname, users, IPC or cgroups.
Cgroup (control group) — a kernel feature that limits, accounts for and prioritises the CPU, memory, I/O and process count of a group of processes.
Image — an immutable package of layers (file system changes as tar archives) plus a configuration file with the default command, environment variables and user.
Layer — one step of the image build; layers are identified by a hash of their content, so identical layers are stored and transferred once.
Overlayfs — the union file system that presents several read-only layers and one writable layer as a single directory tree.
OCI (Open Container Initiative) — the standards body that defines the image format, the runtime specification and the distribution API, so tools from different vendors interoperate.
runc / containerd — runc is the low-level tool that creates the namespaces and cgroups and starts the process; containerd is the daemon that pulls images, manages snapshots and calls runc.
Registry — a server that stores and serves images by name and tag or digest (Docker Hub, ECR, GCR, a private registry).
history: The first step was chroot, added in Version 7 Unix (1979), which changes the root directory of a process. It limited file access but nothing else. FreeBSD jails (2000) added process, user and network separation, and Solaris Zones (2004 to 2005) added resource control. In Linux, Linux-VServer (2001) and OpenVZ (2005) used patched kernels. The mainline kernel got the building blocks one at a time: the mount namespace (2002), further namespaces for UTS, IPC, PID and network (2006 to 2008), cgroups (merged in 2.6.24, released in January 2008, by Paul Menage and Rohit Seth at Google, who needed to share machines between jobs), and finally user namespaces (Linux 3.8, 2013), which let a process be root inside its container without being root on the host.
LXC (2008) joined these pieces into a tool, but it was complex. In March 2013 dotCloud, a platform-as-a-service company, showed Docker at PyCon. Docker added what was missing: a simple build file (Dockerfile), layered images with caching, a one-line command to run anything, and a public registry (Docker Hub). Developers adopted it quickly. Docker first used LXC underneath, then its own libcontainer. In 2015 the Open Container Initiative was formed under the Linux Foundation, and Docker donated its runtime as runc and its image format, which freed the ecosystem from one vendor. containerd became a separate project and later a CNCF graduated project; Kubernetes removed its built-in Docker support (dockershim) in version 1.24 (2022) and talks to containerd or CRI-O directly. Linux cgroup v2, a unified hierarchy, arrived in kernel 4.5 (2016) and is now the default in recent distributions.
Isolation has been strengthened since the shared kernel is a risk. gVisor (Google, 2018) implements a Linux kernel interface in user space, Kata Containers (2017) runs each container in a lightweight VM, and AWS Firecracker (2018) provides microVMs for serverless. These are used for untrusted code, including today's AI-agent sandboxes.
how: When you run a container, a chain of tools does the work. The client asks the container daemon (containerd) to run an image. If the image is not local, containerd pulls it from a registry: first a small manifest that lists the layers by their SHA-256 digests, then only the layers it does not already have. Each layer is unpacked into a directory. The runtime then prepares an overlayfs mount: the image's layers are the read-only lower directories and a fresh empty directory is the writable upper one. When the process writes to a file from a lower layer, the file is first copied up into the writable layer (copy-on-write), and deletions are recorded as marker files. This is the file system contribution (L3): immutable, shareable layers plus a thin writable top.
Next, runc creates the isolation with a small number of system calls. It calls clone (or unshare) with flags such as CLONE_NEWPID, CLONE_NEWNS, CLONE_NEWNET, CLONE_NEWUTS, CLONE_NEWIPC and CLONE_NEWUSER. The PID namespace makes the new process see itself as process 1 and hides all other processes; the mount namespace gives it a private mount table, and pivot_root switches its root directory to the overlayfs mount, so it cannot see the host's files; the network namespace gives it its own interfaces, routing table and ports; the UTS namespace gives it its own hostname; the user namespace can map root inside to an unprivileged user outside. The result is an illusion, not a new machine: the process is a normal process and the host can see it in its own process list.
Resource limits come from cgroups. The runtime creates a cgroup for the container and writes limits into files. In cgroup v2, memory.max sets the hard memory limit: if the container goes above it and the kernel cannot reclaim memory, the kernel's OOM killer ends a process in that group, and the container exits with code 137 (128 plus signal 9). cpu.max holds a quota and period, for example "50000 100000", meaning 50 ms of CPU time in each 100 ms period, which is half of one CPU. cpu.weight gives proportional shares when the CPU is busy, and pids.max limits process count to stop fork bombs; io controllers limit disk bandwidth. Accounting in the same cgroup gives the usage numbers that monitoring tools report.
Security adds more layers. The runtime drops most Linux capabilities (the split-up powers of root, such as loading kernel modules), applies a seccomp profile that blocks dangerous system calls, and may apply AppArmor or SELinux rules. Read-only root file systems, non-root users and user namespaces reduce damage if the application is compromised. None of this changes the fact that all containers call the same kernel, so a kernel bug can break isolation for all of them.
Networking is assembled from standard parts. By default, the runtime creates a virtual Ethernet pair (veth): one end in the container's network namespace, the other attached to a bridge on the host, with iptables or nftables rules for NAT and port publishing. Other plug-ins (CNI in Kubernetes, Cilium) program the kernel with eBPF or routing to give each container an IP address that is reachable across hosts. Storage uses bind mounts and volumes to connect directories outside the container, since the writable layer is temporary and disappears with the container.
The software stack that does all this has several layers, and knowing them helps when something breaks. The Docker command line talks to the Docker daemon, which talks to containerd; containerd starts a small helper process called a shim for each container, and the shim calls runc to create the namespaces and cgroups. Runc then exits, and the shim stays as the parent of the container process. That design is why you can restart containerd or the daemon without killing running containers. Kubernetes skips Docker and talks to containerd (or CRI-O) through the Container Runtime Interface, so the same images and the same runc run underneath.
Build is a separate step but uses the same ideas. A Dockerfile is a list of instructions; each instruction that changes files produces a new layer, which is cached by its content and its parent. If you change one line, only that layer and the ones above it are rebuilt and pushed. So the order of instructions matters: put rarely changing steps (installing dependencies) first and frequently changing ones (copying the source code) last. Multi-stage builds compile in a large builder image and copy only the result into a small runtime image.

example: Look at an image build. A Python service has a base layer of 80 MB (a slim Debian), a layer of installed packages of 200 MB, and a layer with your code of 5 MB. Total: 285 MB. On the first push the registry stores all three layers. You then change one source file and rebuild. Docker sees that the first two layers have the same content and uses the cache; only the 5 MB top layer is rebuilt and pushed. A server that already has the first two layers pulls 5 MB, not 285 MB. If you had put "COPY code" before "install packages" in the Dockerfile, every code change would invalidate the 200 MB package layer too, and each build and pull would move 205 MB. Ten different services built on the same 80 MB base share that one layer on disk.
Now the limits. You run a service with --cpus=0.5, which sets cpu.max to "50000 100000": 50 ms of CPU time in every 100 ms window. A request needs 80 ms of CPU work and the service handles it with 4 threads in parallel. On an unrestricted machine, it would finish in about 80/4 = 20 ms. In the container, the four threads together burn 50 ms of CPU in just 12.5 ms of wall time. The kernel then stops all of them (throttles) until the next 100 ms period begins. After that, the remaining 30 ms of CPU runs in 7.5 ms. The request finishes at about 100 + 7.5 = 107.5 ms. The average CPU use of this container is far below one CPU, and monitoring may show "low CPU", but the latency is more than 5 times higher because of throttling. This is a common cause of mysterious tail latency in Kubernetes.
Memory limits behave differently. Set memory.max to 512 MiB for a Java service with a heap of 400 MiB: the heap, plus metaspace, thread stacks, native buffers and the file page cache that the cgroup charges to the container, may add up to more than 512 MiB. When it does, the kernel kills the process with exit 137. The fix is to size the heap to 60 to 75% of the limit (modern JVMs read the cgroup limit and do this with -XX:MaxRAMPercentage) and to monitor memory working set, not only heap use.
Finally, the cost of starting. A container from a local image starts in about 100 to 500 ms, since it creates namespaces and a cgroup and runs the process. A VM boots a whole kernel and takes seconds to tens of seconds. But a pull of a 1 GB image over a 1 Gbit/s link is about 8 seconds, so on a fresh node, image pull time dominates. Teams keep images small, use caches on nodes, and use lazy-loading formats (such as eStargz or SOCI) that start the container before the whole image arrives.
intuition: A lightweight apartment in a shared building: your own rooms, locks and meter, while the foundation and plumbing (the kernel) are shared with every neighbour.
The comparison with a virtual machine, which is a separate house, shows the trade: the apartment is cheap and quick to get, and a crack in the shared foundation affects every resident.
The design is natural because an operating system already knows how to run many processes. All that was missing was a way to give a group of processes a private view and a budget. Namespaces provide the view and cgroups provide the budget, and the existing process model does the rest. Because there is no second kernel to boot or to keep in memory, starting is as cheap as starting a process. Packing all dependencies into an image gives the other half of the value: the unit of deployment is the same everywhere, so deployment becomes copying an artifact instead of configuring a machine.
mistakes: People often think a container is a lightweight virtual machine. In fact a VM has its own kernel, and a container does not. A container is a process with restrictions. This is why containers start faster and use less memory, and also why their isolation is weaker: a kernel vulnerability or a mis-configured privileged container can give access to the host and to every other container.
People often think containers are secure by default. In fact a container running as root with extra capabilities, a mounted Docker socket or the --privileged flag has almost the power of root on the host. Use a non-root user, drop capabilities, keep the default seccomp profile, mount the root file system read-only, and for untrusted code use a stronger sandbox such as gVisor or a microVM.
People often think data inside a container is persistent. In fact the writable layer is deleted with the container, and writes into it are slower than into a volume because of copy-up. Anything that must survive goes to a volume, a network file system or a database outside the container.
People often think a CPU limit only caps the maximum. In fact it is enforced as a quota per 100 ms period, so a bursty multi-threaded process can be paused even when its average use is low, as the example shows. Many teams set CPU requests (for scheduling) but no CPU limits for latency-sensitive services, and set memory limits equal to memory requests.
friction: Isolation is weaker than for VMs, so multi-tenant platforms that run customers' code need an extra layer (gVisor, Kata, Firecracker) with some performance cost; system-call-heavy workloads can slow down several times under a user-space kernel. Not everything is namespaced: the kernel itself, some /proc and /sys entries, the clock (until the time namespace in Linux 5.6), and kernel modules are shared, and a noisy container can fill the shared page cache or exhaust shared kernel resources such as the number of inotify watches or connection tracking entries.
The supply chain is a real problem. An image contains hundreds of packages, and each carries known vulnerabilities; base images age quickly, and "latest" tags change without notice. Teams need scanning, pinned digests, signed images (Sigstore cosign) and a software bill of materials. Image size and pull time slow down scaling and recovery; a 2 GB image on a fresh node delays every autoscaling event.
Operational friction is also high. Resource limits that are wrong cause OOM kills or throttling, and the symptoms are indirect. Logging and debugging inside a container that has no shell (minimal or distroless images) need ephemeral debug containers. Stateful applications need volumes with correct performance and backup. Networking adds layers (veth, NAT, overlay networks) whose overhead and failure modes surprise people: conntrack tables, MTU mismatches in overlay networks, and DNS timeouts are frequent issues. Finally, containers are Linux-specific at their core: on macOS and Windows, Docker runs a Linux VM, so performance and file sharing differ from production.
practice: Build small, reproducible images. Use a minimal or distroless base, multi-stage builds, a pinned base version or digest, and order the Dockerfile so that stable layers come first. Add a .dockerignore file so the build context does not include large or secret files. Scan images for vulnerabilities in CI, rebuild regularly to get base-image fixes, and sign what you publish.
Run containers with least privilege: a non-root user, a read-only root file system with explicit writable volumes, all capabilities dropped except those needed, no privileged mode and no host path mounts that you do not need. Set resources deliberately. Give each container a memory request equal to its limit so it is neither starved nor killed by surprise; set CPU requests based on measured use; and, for latency-sensitive services, think hard before setting CPU limits, or check the throttling metric (container_cpu_cfs_throttled_periods_total) if you do. Make the runtime aware of the limits: in the JVM use container-aware heap settings, in Go set GOMAXPROCS from the cgroup limit.
Handle signals and lifecycle properly. The main process becomes PID 1 and must forward termination signals (SIGTERM) and reap child processes; use an init process (tini, or docker run --init) if your program does not. Log to standard output and standard error so the platform can collect it. Expose a health endpoint. Keep state out of the container. For untrusted code, such as customer plug-ins or AI-agent tool execution, do not rely on plain containers: use gVisor, Kata or a microVM. For debugging, learn the basics of the underlying features: lsns, nsenter, systemd-cgls, and reading /sys/fs/cgroup files tell you what the container really got.
tradeoffs: Choose containers when you want fast start (sub-second), high density (dozens of services per host), and a standard deployment unit for services you trust; choose virtual machines when you need a different kernel, stronger isolation between tenants, or compatibility with software that needs kernel modules or a full OS. In practice many platforms use both: containers running inside VMs, so the VM is the security boundary and the container is the packaging unit.
Choose a plain runtime (runc) for your own services and a sandboxed runtime (gVisor, Kata, Firecracker) for untrusted code. You give up some performance, typically a few percent for CPU-bound work and more for system-call or I/O heavy work, for a much smaller attack surface.
Choose large general-purpose base images (Debian, Ubuntu) when you need a shell, package manager and easy debugging, and your team values convenience; choose minimal or distroless images when security and size matter more. Minimal images cut size by 10 times or more and remove most vulnerabilities, but make debugging harder. Choose one process per container when you can, so that the platform can restart and scale each part; use a small supervisor only when processes are tightly coupled.
Choose CPU limits when you need to cap noisy neighbours, enforce fair billing or use a multi-tenant cluster; choose requests without limits when tail latency matters and the node is not oversubscribed. For memory, always set limits, because memory cannot be taken back without killing something. Choose volumes or external storage for state; choose the writable layer only for scratch files.

@@ virtualization
value: Virtualization lets one physical computer behave like many separate computers. A piece of software called a hypervisor (or virtual machine monitor) gives each virtual machine (VM) a virtual CPU, virtual memory, virtual disks and virtual network cards, and each VM runs its own complete operating system, called the guest, without knowing that the hardware is shared. The guest believes it controls the machine. In fact, the hypervisor does, and it makes sure that no VM can read another's memory or take more than its share.
The problem it solves is waste and rigidity. In the late 1990s, a typical company server ran at 5 to 15 percent CPU use because each application needed its own machine for isolation, and buying a machine took weeks. With virtualization, ten applications share one host with strong isolation, a VM can be created in seconds, copied, snapshotted, and even moved between hosts while running. This is also what made cloud computing possible: when you rent an EC2 instance, you rent a VM, and the cloud provider can place thousands of customers safely on the same hardware.
Several lower-level ideas make a VM both safe and fast. Hardware virtualization extensions in the instruction set (L3: Intel VT-x, AMD-V, ARM EL2) let guest code run directly on the processor at full speed, trapping to the hypervisor only for sensitive operations. Virtual memory and paging (L3) are extended by nested page tables, so each guest manages its own page tables while the hardware adds a second translation from guest-physical to host-physical memory. Interrupts (L3) are virtualized so each guest gets its own timer and device interrupts. Above this concept, the Linux kernel includes KVM, which turns Linux into a hypervisor, and the public clouds (AWS, GCP, Azure) rent compute as VMs.
terms: Hypervisor (VMM) — the software, or firmware, that creates VMs, schedules their virtual CPUs and mediates access to hardware; type 1 runs on the bare machine (Xen, ESXi), type 2 runs as an application on a host OS (VirtualBox).
Guest and host — the guest is the operating system inside the VM; the host is the machine, and its software, that runs the VMs.
Sensitive instruction — an instruction whose effect depends on or changes the privileged state of the machine, such as changing page tables or disabling interrupts; a safe hypervisor must intercept these.
Trap-and-emulate — the classic technique: run the guest in a de-privileged mode, let the hardware trap when it executes a sensitive instruction, and have the hypervisor emulate the effect on virtual state.
VM exit / VM entry — in hardware-assisted virtualization, the switch from guest to hypervisor (exit) and back (entry); each costs roughly a thousand cycles or more, so good designs avoid them.
Nested (extended) page tables — hardware support (Intel EPT, AMD NPT) that translates guest-virtual to guest-physical to host-physical addresses without hypervisor intervention on each page-table change.
Paravirtualization (virtio) — the guest knows it is virtual and uses a simple, efficient interface to the hypervisor instead of emulating a real device.
Live migration — moving a running VM from one host to another with only a very short pause, by copying its memory while it keeps running.
history: The idea is old. IBM's CP-40 (1964 to 1967) and CP-67 at the Cambridge Scientific Center let many users each have a private virtual copy of a mainframe, and VM/370 (1972) made this a commercial product. The reason was practical: mainframes were expensive, and operating systems needed testing without stopping production. In 1974, Gerald Popek and Robert Goldberg published the formal requirements for a virtualizable architecture. A VMM must provide equivalence (programs behave the same as on real hardware, apart from timing), resource control (the VMM is in charge of resources) and efficiency (most instructions run directly on hardware). Their theorem says an architecture can be virtualized by trap-and-emulate if all sensitive instructions are also privileged, so they trap when run in user mode.
The x86 architecture violated this. Robin and Irvine (2000) counted 17 sensitive instructions that do not trap in user mode (for example POPF, which silently ignores an attempt to change the interrupt flag). VMware (founded 1998; Workstation 1999) solved it in software with binary translation: it rewrote the guest's privileged code on the fly into safe sequences. Xen (Cambridge, SOSP 2003) took another path, paravirtualization: the guest kernel was modified to call the hypervisor directly (hypercalls), which was faster, and Xen powered the early AWS EC2 (2006).
Hardware then removed the need for these tricks. Intel VT-x (2005) and AMD-V (2006) added a guest mode with traps for sensitive operations, and nested page tables (AMD in 2007, Intel EPT in 2008) removed the cost of shadow page tables. Linux KVM, written by Avi Kivity at Qumranet, was merged in Linux 2.6.20 (2007) and turned the kernel itself into a hypervisor, with QEMU emulating devices; the virtio standard (2008) gave guests an efficient paravirtual device interface. Later, hardware offload took over device work: AWS Nitro (2017) moved virtual networking, storage and security into dedicated cards, leaving a very thin KVM-based hypervisor. Confidential computing (AMD SEV, Intel TDX) now encrypts guest memory even from the hypervisor.
how: Hardware-assisted virtualization works with two CPU modes. The hypervisor runs in "root mode" (VMX root on Intel). To run a guest, it loads the guest's register state from a control structure (the VMCS on Intel, the VMCB on AMD) and executes a VM-entry instruction. The CPU switches to "non-root mode" and the guest runs directly on the processor, including its own kernel in ring 0, at full speed. Most instructions (arithmetic, loads, stores, even system calls) never leave non-root mode. The CPU forces a VM exit back to the hypervisor only for conditions the hypervisor chose to intercept: access to certain control registers, I/O port access, certain privileged instructions, external interrupts, and page-table faults at the second level. The hypervisor handles the event (it emulates the instruction, delivers an interrupt, or loads the missing page), then resumes the guest. The instruction set (L3) contribution is exactly this contract: it defines which operations are sensitive, and gives hardware that traps on them selectively.
Memory is the second pillar. A guest kernel manages its own page tables from guest-virtual to what it believes is physical memory (guest-physical). The hypervisor must map guest-physical memory to real host-physical memory. Early systems used shadow page tables, where the hypervisor kept a merged copy and trapped every guest page-table update, which was slow. Nested paging (EPT, NPT) lets the hardware walk both tables: a TLB miss triggers a two-dimensional walk. With four-level tables in both guest and host, this can need up to 24 memory references, compared with 4 natively, but the TLB caches the final translation, and 2 MB or 1 GB huge pages cut the depth. The virtual memory (L3) mechanism is thus applied twice, and the hypervisor can overcommit memory: give VMs more total memory than exists, reclaiming through ballooning (a driver in the guest that takes pages back), page sharing or swapping.
In KVM the work is split in two. The kernel module handles the fast path: VM entry and exit, the second-level page tables, and the interrupt controller. A user-space program, usually QEMU, owns each VM as an ordinary process: it allocates the guest's memory with mmap, creates vCPU threads that call the KVM ioctl to run guest code, and emulates the slower devices. This is why a VM shows up in the host's process list, can be limited with cgroups, and can be debugged with normal tools. Xen and ESXi use a smaller dedicated hypervisor instead, with a privileged helper VM or kernel handling devices.
CPU scheduling treats each virtual CPU (vCPU) as a thread of the host. KVM uses the Linux scheduler; a VM with 4 vCPUs is 4 threads that run on any available cores. Pinning vCPUs to cores, using huge pages and respecting NUMA locality improves performance. A vCPU on a cloud instance is typically a hardware thread (hyper-thread), which is important for sizing.
Devices can be handled in three ways. Full emulation (QEMU presenting a fake network card) works with any unmodified guest, but each register access causes an exit and is slow. Paravirtual devices (virtio) use shared-memory ring buffers: the guest posts many requests in a ring and notifies the host with one "kick", and the host completes them and injects one interrupt, so exits are amortised across a batch. Direct assignment (PCI passthrough, SR-IOV) gives a VM direct access to a physical device or a virtual function of it; an IOMMU (Intel VT-d) translates and restricts the device's DMA so it can only reach that VM's memory. Nitro takes this further: the virtual network, EBS storage and local NVMe are presented to the guest by dedicated cards, so almost no hypervisor code runs in the data path.
Interrupts (L3) and time need virtualization as well. External interrupts arrive in the hypervisor, which injects virtual interrupts into the guest; modern hardware (posted interrupts, APIC virtualization) can deliver some without an exit. Each guest has a virtual timer. When many vCPUs share cores, a vCPU may wait to be scheduled, and the guest sees this as "steal time", which monitoring tools show.
Live migration uses these tools. The hypervisor copies all memory pages of the running VM to the destination host while the VM continues to run; since the VM keeps changing pages, the hypervisor tracks dirty pages (by write-protecting them or using hardware dirty bits) and copies them again in repeated rounds, each round smaller than the last. When the remainder is small, it pauses the VM briefly, copies the last pages and the CPU and device state, and resumes it on the destination. The pause is typically tens to hundreds of milliseconds. The network identity moves with the VM, and storage is shared or copied.

example: Live-migrate a VM with 16 GB of memory over a 10 Gbit/s link, which carries about 1.25 GB/s. The guest dirties memory at 200 MB/s (0.2 GB/s). Round 0 copies all 16 GB and takes 16 / 1.25 = 12.8 s. During those 12.8 s the guest dirtied 0.2 × 12.8 = 2.56 GB, so round 1 copies 2.56 GB in 2.05 s. During that time 0.2 × 2.05 = 0.41 GB were dirtied; round 2 copies 0.41 GB in 0.33 s; round 3 copies 0.066 GB (66 MB) in 0.05 s. Each round is 0.16 times the size of the previous one (the ratio of dirty rate to bandwidth, 0.2 / 1.25). After about 4 rounds and 15 s, the remaining dirty set is only a few MB. The hypervisor pauses the VM, sends the last pages and the device state, and resumes on the target. Downtime is the time of that last copy plus the switch-over, tens of milliseconds.
Now change the numbers. If the guest dirties 1.25 GB/s, equal to the link, the ratio is 1, and the rounds never shrink: the migration does not converge. Hypervisors then throttle the guest's CPU (auto-converge), compress pages, or switch to post-copy (resume the VM on the target immediately and fetch missing pages on demand). The rule is: pre-copy converges only when the dirty rate is clearly below the network bandwidth.
Next, a counting example for address translation. A native four-level page walk needs 4 memory references. With nested paging, each of the 4 guest levels needs its guest-physical address translated by a 4-level host walk, plus the final data address: (4 + 1) × (4 + 1) − 1 = 24 references. If 1% of memory accesses miss the TLB and the page-table entries are mostly in cache (say 4 ns each on average), the extra cost per access is about 0.01 × 20 × 4 ns = 0.8 ns, a modest overhead. For a workload with a large random working set and 20% TLB misses, this cost becomes 16 ns per access, and the workload runs noticeably slower than on bare metal. Huge pages (2 MB) reduce the walk to (3 + 1) × (3 + 1) − 1 = 15 references and cut the number of TLB misses by a factor of hundreds, which is why databases on VMs use them.
Last, the value of batching. A guest sends 1,000 network packets. With a fully emulated card, each packet needs several register writes, each causing a VM exit of about 1 to 2 microseconds: perhaps 3,000 exits, 3 to 6 ms of CPU time. With virtio, the guest places 32 packets in the ring and kicks once, so 1,000 packets need about 32 exits, around 50 microseconds. With SR-IOV or a Nitro-style offload card, the guest writes directly to the device with no exit at all.
intuition: One physical house divided into fully separate apartments, each with its own front door and utility meters, managed by a building superintendent who steps in only when a tenant tries something that affects the others.
The design is natural because an operating system already assumes it owns the machine. Instead of changing the operating system, you give it a machine that is not real but looks real. The hardware helps with the hard part: it runs the guest at full speed and calls the superintendent only for the few things that matter. Almost all instructions run directly, so the cost is small. Isolation is strong because the interface between guest and hypervisor is narrow and well-defined (the instruction set and a few devices), much narrower than the thousands of system calls of a full operating system.
mistakes: People often think a VM is much slower than bare metal. In fact, with hardware support, CPU-bound code typically runs within a few percent of native speed. The cost shows up in specific places: I/O with emulated devices, workloads with many TLB misses, and anything causing frequent VM exits. Modern cloud instances with offload cards approach bare-metal performance for I/O too.
People often think containers have made VMs obsolete. In fact they solve different problems. Containers give light packaging on a shared kernel; VMs give a separate kernel and a hardware-enforced security boundary. Public clouds run containers inside VMs, and sandboxes for untrusted code (Firecracker, Kata) use VMs to give container-like startup with VM-like isolation.
People often think a vCPU is a full core. In fact on most clouds a vCPU is one hyper-thread, so two vCPUs may share one physical core and perform like about 1.2 to 1.3 cores. On ARM instances such as Graviton, one vCPU is one core. Compare instance types by measuring, not by counting vCPUs.
People often think VM isolation is perfect. In fact the hypervisor and hardware have bugs; side channels (cache timing, Spectre-type attacks) cross VM boundaries on shared cores, and hypervisor escape bugs, though rare, have been found. Cloud providers reduce the risk with minimal hypervisors, dedicated hosts, core scheduling and memory encryption, but the risk is lower than for containers, not zero.
friction: Overheads still exist. Every VM carries a full guest kernel, which costs memory (tens to hundreds of MB), boot time (seconds to tens of seconds, though microVMs like Firecracker start in about 125 ms) and patching work for each guest. Memory overcommit looks attractive but causes sudden slowdowns when the host is short of pages. Nested paging makes TLB misses more costly, I/O virtualization adds latency unless offloaded, and vCPU scheduling can add jitter: a vCPU descheduled while holding a lock in the guest makes other vCPUs spin (the lock-holder preemption problem).
Sharing causes interference. Noisy neighbours can saturate the shared L3 cache, memory bandwidth or disk, and "steal time" shows up as unexplained latency. Burstable instances can run out of CPU credits. Live migration is not free for all workloads: heavy memory writers or VMs with passthrough devices cannot move easily, and the pause, though short, can break very latency-sensitive software or cluster heartbeats.
Security is a permanent concern. The hypervisor is a large trusted code base, and side-channel attacks (Spectre, L1TF, MDS) required costly mitigations such as disabling hyper-threading sharing between tenants. Confidential VMs add protection from the provider but with performance costs and limited debugging. Finally, nesting (running a hypervisor inside a VM) is possible but slow, which complicates running Kubernetes with VM-based sandboxes on cloud instances; this is why providers also offer bare-metal instances.
practice: Pick instance types by measurement. Run your own benchmark on the candidate types and look at throughput, tail latency and price. Know what a vCPU means for your provider; for latency-critical services consider instance families with dedicated cores or no hyper-threading, and for CPU-intensive code consider bare metal.
For I/O-heavy workloads, use instances with enhanced networking and local NVMe offload (Nitro-based on AWS), and virtio drivers on your own KVM hosts. Use huge pages for large-memory databases and JVMs, and align VM sizes to NUMA node boundaries when you run your own hypervisors (do not create a VM larger than one socket unless needed). Watch steal time (the "st" column in top or the node_cpu_seconds_total{mode="steal"} metric): sustained steal above a few percent means you are being squeezed by neighbours or by your own CPU credits.
Use VMs as the isolation boundary for multi-tenant and untrusted workloads, and containers for packaging inside them. Where startup time matters, consider microVMs (Firecracker) rather than full VM images, and bake images with the software preinstalled so that boot does not need configuration. Use snapshots for fast restore but remember they capture memory and secrets. For live migration or maintenance events on cloud VMs, test that your software tolerates a pause of up to several seconds, and use the provider's maintenance notifications. Keep guest kernels patched; in a VM, the kernel is yours to update.
tradeoffs: Choose VMs when you need strong isolation between tenants, a different operating system or kernel version, or live migration and snapshots as operational tools; choose containers when you trust the code, want density and sub-second start, and can share a kernel. Choose both together (containers inside VMs) when you run a multi-tenant platform. You give up density and start time with VMs, and kernel-level isolation with containers.
Choose a microVM (Firecracker, Cloud Hypervisor) when you need VM isolation with startup in the 100 ms range for functions and sandboxes; choose a full-featured VM (QEMU with many devices) when you need device compatibility such as GPUs, special NICs or unusual guests. Choose a bare-metal instance when you need direct hardware access, the lowest jitter, nested virtualization, or licence terms tied to physical cores, and accept less flexibility in snapshots and migration.
Choose paravirtual devices (virtio) as a default for portability and good performance; choose SR-IOV or passthrough when you need near-native network or storage performance or a GPU, accepting that migration becomes difficult and you rely on the device vendor. Choose memory overcommit only for workloads with predictable idle memory (desktops, test fleets); choose strict reservation for databases and anything latency-sensitive.
Choose type 1 or kernel-integrated hypervisors (KVM, ESXi, Hyper-V, Xen) for production servers; choose type 2 (VirtualBox, VMware Workstation) for developer laptops. Choose commercial hypervisors when you need their management ecosystem and support, and KVM when you want open source, cloud-scale automation and the Linux scheduler and tools.
interview: Explain trap-and-emulate and why x86 originally could not use it. || The guest runs de-privileged; when it executes a privileged or sensitive instruction the hardware traps to the VMM, which emulates the effect on virtual state. Popek and Goldberg: this works if every sensitive instruction is privileged. x86 had about 17 sensitive instructions that did not trap in user mode (e.g. POPF), so VMware used binary translation and Xen used paravirtualization until VT-x and AMD-V added a guest mode.
How do nested page tables work and what do they cost? || The hardware walks the guest's page tables and the hypervisor's second-level tables on a TLB miss. This removes the need to trap guest page-table updates, but a worst-case miss needs up to 24 memory references instead of 4. Huge pages and large TLBs reduce the cost.
How does pre-copy live migration work, and when does it fail to converge? || Copy all memory while the VM runs, then repeatedly copy pages dirtied since the last round, until the dirty set is small; pause, copy the last pages and state, resume at the target. It converges only if the dirty rate is well below the network bandwidth. If not, use throttling (auto-converge), compression, or post-copy.
Why is virtio faster than an emulated NIC? || Emulation traps on each register access, causing many VM exits. Virtio uses shared-memory rings and one notification per batch, and the host processes many descriptors at once, so exits are amortised. Vhost moves the backend into the kernel or user space for further savings, and SR-IOV avoids the hypervisor entirely.
Your latency-critical service has random 20 ms stalls on a cloud VM. How do you investigate whether virtualization is the cause? || Check steal time and CPU credits, noisy-neighbour symptoms, VM exit counts if you own the host, maintenance or live-migration events, timer and clock source settings, and NUMA or hyper-thread sharing. Compare with a dedicated-host or bare-metal instance. Use scheduler tracing in the guest to see runnable-but-not-running delays.
When would you choose a VM over a container for an isolation boundary? || For untrusted or multi-tenant code, regulatory separation, running different kernels, or when a kernel exploit must not reach other tenants. Use microVMs if you need fast start and density. Accept the memory and startup overhead.
quiz: What are the three Popek–Goldberg properties of a VMM? || Equivalence (the program behaves as on real hardware), resource control (the VMM controls the virtualized resources) and efficiency (most guest instructions run directly on hardware).
Why do nested page tables need up to 24 memory references on a TLB miss? || Each of the 4 guest page-table levels holds a guest-physical address that needs a 4-level host walk, plus the final address: (4 + 1) × (4 + 1) − 1 = 24 references.
Why does batching in virtio reduce overhead? || Each guest-to-hypervisor notification is a VM exit that costs microseconds. A shared ring lets the guest queue many requests and notify once, so the cost of the exit is shared by the whole batch.
A VM with 8 GB of memory is migrated over a 1 GB/s link while dirtying 100 MB/s. What are the sizes of the first three pre-copy rounds? || Round 0 is 8 GB (8 s). Dirty during it: 0.1 × 8 = 0.8 GB (0.8 s). Round 1 is 0.8 GB; during it 0.08 GB dirty. Round 2 is 0.08 GB. Each round is 0.1 times the previous one.
When would you pick SR-IOV or device passthrough over virtio? || When you need near-native I/O performance or access to a GPU or special NIC, for example high-throughput networking or ML training. The price is difficult live migration and dependence on specific hardware.

@@ orchestration
value: A container runs one program on one machine. A production system needs hundreds of programs on hundreds of machines, with the right number of copies of each, restarted when they crash, moved when a machine dies, updated without downtime, reachable at a stable address, and given only the CPU and memory they asked for. Doing this by hand or with scripts does not work at scale. Container orchestration is the control system that does it automatically. You declare what you want ("run 10 copies of this image, each with 2 CPUs and 4 GB, behind this address"), and the orchestrator continuously works to make reality match.
Kubernetes is the dominant orchestrator today. Earlier cluster managers (Google's Borg, Apache Mesos, Hadoop YARN, HashiCorp Nomad, Docker Swarm) solved parts of the same problem. They share one pattern: a central control plane holds the desired state, agents on every machine report the actual state, and controllers close the gap. The value to the layer above is a uniform platform: teams deploy any service the same way, and a failed machine is an ordinary event rather than an outage. Databases, stream processors, ML training jobs and data platforms such as Spark and Databricks increasingly run as workloads on it.
The concepts below it supply the three hard parts. A reconciliation loop (L2) compares desired and actual state and acts on the difference, again and again, which makes the system self-healing. Raft (L2) keeps the desired state in a replicated store (etcd), so the control plane survives the loss of a machine without losing or forking its data. Leader election and leases (L2) ensure that only one copy of each controller acts at a time, even when several are running for availability. Autoscaling (L2) adds a feedback loop on top, changing the desired number of replicas or nodes as load changes.
terms: Desired state and actual state — what you declared should exist (spec) versus what currently exists (status); the orchestrator's job is to reduce the difference.
Pod — the smallest schedulable unit in Kubernetes: one or more containers that share a network address and volumes and always run on the same node.
Controller — a loop that watches objects through the API and acts to move actual state toward desired state; for example the ReplicaSet controller keeps N pods running.
Scheduler — the component that assigns each new pod to a node by filtering out nodes that cannot run it and scoring the rest.
Kubelet — the agent on each node that starts the pod's containers through the container runtime, runs health probes and reports status.
etcd — the Raft-replicated key-value store that holds all cluster state; only the API server talks to it.
Resource request and limit — the CPU and memory a pod asks the scheduler to reserve (request) and the maximum it may use (limit).
Level-triggered — a design where a controller reacts to the current state difference rather than to individual change events, so a missed event is repaired at the next pass.
history: Large companies faced this problem first. Google started Borg around 2003 to 2004 to replace many separate cluster systems. Borg ran nearly all of Google's production and batch jobs on shared clusters, with a median cell of about 10,000 machines, and its job, task and allocation model, bin-packing scheduler and priority classes are the origin of most later ideas. It was described publicly in 2015. Omega (EuroSys 2013) explored a different design: many schedulers working on a shared copy of cluster state with optimistic concurrency control.
Outside Google, Apache Mesos (Hindman et al., NSDI 2011, from Berkeley) introduced two-level scheduling: Mesos offers resources to frameworks such as Marathon or Spark, which decide what to run. Hadoop YARN (2013) separated resource management from MapReduce for the Hadoop world. Docker (2013) made containers easy, and orchestrating them became the next need. Google released Kubernetes in June 2014, written in Go and built on lessons from Borg and Omega but with a declarative REST API and Docker containers; version 1.0 appeared in July 2015 together with the founding of the Cloud Native Computing Foundation. HashiCorp Nomad (2015) offered a simpler single-binary scheduler, and Docker Swarm a simpler built-in one.
By around 2017, the major clouds had all offered managed Kubernetes (Google GKE 2015, Azure AKS and AWS EKS in 2017 to 2018), and it won, largely because its API was extensible. Custom resources and operators (CoreOS, 2016) let anyone teach Kubernetes about new kinds of objects, such as a database cluster or a Spark application. Spark added native Kubernetes scheduling in version 2.3 (2018), moving data workloads off YARN. Today Kubernetes is the base layer for platforms, for GPU scheduling of AI training and inference, and for internal developer platforms. The documented design limit is about 5,000 nodes per cluster.
how: Kubernetes has a control plane and worker nodes. The control plane has four main parts. The API server is the single front door: every client and every component reads and writes cluster objects (Pods, Deployments, Services, Nodes) through its REST API, and it is the only component that talks to etcd. etcd stores the objects as keys, replicated with Raft (L2) across typically three or five members, so a write is committed only when a majority has stored it, and reads can stay consistent after a leader failure. The scheduler assigns pods to nodes. The controller manager runs the built-in controllers. Every object has a spec (desired state, written by users) and a status (observed state, written by controllers), and a resourceVersion that the API server uses for optimistic concurrency: an update based on a stale version is rejected with a conflict, so two writers cannot silently overwrite each other.
The reconciliation loop (L2) is the central mechanism. Each controller uses an informer: it lists the objects it cares about once, then opens a watch that streams changes, and keeps a local cache. When something changes it puts the object's key on a work queue. A worker takes the key, reads the current spec and status, computes what is missing, and takes the action that moves toward the spec: create pods, delete pods, update status. Then it stops. It does not remember what happened before. If the controller crashes, restarts or misses an event, the next list and the next resync read the whole state again and fix the gap. This is level-triggered behaviour, and it is why Kubernetes is robust: actions must be idempotent, so it does not matter if they run twice.
A Deployment shows how controllers layer. You create a Deployment with 3 replicas of image v1. The Deployment controller creates a ReplicaSet. The ReplicaSet controller sees that it owns 0 pods but wants 3, and creates 3 Pod objects. At this point the pods exist in etcd but have no node. The scheduler watches for pods without a node. For each one it runs a filter phase (remove nodes that lack enough free CPU or memory for the pod's requests, that have taints the pod does not tolerate, or that fail affinity rules) and a score phase (rank the remaining nodes, for example to spread pods across zones or to pack nodes tightly). It then writes the chosen node name into the pod. In large clusters it scores only a fraction of the nodes to save time. Finally, the kubelet on that node, which watches the API for pods bound to it, calls the container runtime to pull images and start the containers, runs liveness and readiness probes, and reports status back. The Service and EndpointSlice machinery then adds the pod's IP to the load-balancing list only when it is ready.
Self-healing follows from the same loop. If a container crashes, the kubelet restarts it with exponential back-off. If a node stops sending heartbeats (kubelets renew a Lease object every 10 seconds), the node controller marks it Unknown after about 40 seconds and adds a taint. Pods that do not tolerate it are evicted after a delay (300 seconds by default). The ReplicaSet controller then sees fewer pods than desired and creates replacements, and the scheduler places them on healthy nodes. Nobody wrote a script for "node failure"; the general rule "make actual match desired" handles it.
Rolling updates are also reconciliation. If you change the image to v2, the Deployment creates a new ReplicaSet and shifts replicas gradually from the old one to the new one, within two limits: maxSurge (how many extra pods may exist) and maxUnavailable (how many may be missing). A pod counts as available only when its readiness probe passes, so a bad version stops the rollout before it replaces everything, and rollback is the same process in reverse.
The control plane itself has to be highly available. Several API servers sit behind a load balancer and are stateless. For components that must have one active instance, such as the controller manager and scheduler, each replica tries to acquire a Lease object (leader election, L2) by writing its name and a renewal time to it. The holder retries the renewal every 2 seconds and gives up leadership if it cannot renew within 10 seconds; the lease itself is valid for 15 seconds. If the leader dies, the lease expires and another replica takes over within about 15 seconds. The compare-and-swap on the lease object, which relies on etcd's consistency, prevents two leaders from acting at once, though a paused old leader may briefly act on stale information; this is why controllers must make safe, idempotent changes.
Autoscaling adds feedback. The Horizontal Pod Autoscaler checks metrics (every 15 seconds by default) and sets the desired replica count to ceil(current replicas × current metric / target metric). Cluster autoscalers (the Cluster Autoscaler, or Karpenter) watch for pods that cannot be scheduled and add nodes, and remove nodes that are underused. Three loops thus cooperate: HPA changes pod counts, the scheduler places them, and the node autoscaler changes machine counts.

example: Walk through a rolling update. A Deployment has 10 replicas of v1 with maxSurge 25% and maxUnavailable 25%. Surge rounds up: 25% of 10 = 2.5, so 3 extra pods are allowed. Unavailable rounds down: 2.5 becomes 2 pods that may be missing. So the cluster may hold at most 13 pods and must keep at least 8 available. You change the image to v2. Step 1: the controller creates the new ReplicaSet and scales it up to 3, while the old one stays at 10 (13 pods in total). The old ReplicaSet can also scale down by 2 right away (to 8), keeping 8 available. Step 2: as each v2 pod passes its readiness probe, the controller removes old pods and starts new ones, always staying between 8 available and 13 total. After about 4 or 5 such waves, there are 10 v2 pods and 0 v1 pods. If v2 never becomes ready (it crashes at start), the new pods stay unready, the rollout stalls with 8 healthy v1 pods still serving, and you roll back with one command. Users see no outage.
Now a node failure with the default settings. At time 0, a node loses power. It runs 6 pods from 3 different Deployments. The kubelet stops renewing its Lease. After about 40 seconds without a heartbeat, the node controller marks the node NotReady/Unknown and puts the taint node.kubernetes.io/unreachable:NoExecute on it. Pods get a default toleration for this taint of 300 seconds, so nothing happens for 5 minutes, and during that time the Service may still send requests to dead pod addresses until their readiness status turns false (the endpoint controller removes unready pods once the node is Unknown). At about 340 seconds, the 6 pods are evicted. Their ReplicaSets each see fewer pods than the desired count and create replacements, the scheduler binds them to healthy nodes in under a second, and the kubelets pull the images (cached or not) and start them. Total time to full capacity: roughly 6 minutes. If this is too slow for your service, set a shorter tolerationSeconds (for example 30 to 60) and run enough replicas across several nodes and zones that losing one node does not hurt.
Finally, the scale of the HPA. A service has 10 replicas, each requesting 500 millicores, and the CPU target is 50% of the request. Current average use is 80% of the request. Desired replicas = ceil(10 × 80 / 50) = 16. The HPA raises the Deployment to 16; if the cluster has no room, 6 pods stay Pending, which the node autoscaler sees and answers by adding a node (taking one to several minutes). Setting a lower target (such as 60% instead of 80%) leaves headroom for this delay.
intuition: A thermostat for software: you set the temperature you want, and a loop keeps measuring the room and turning the heating on or off, without ever being told what went wrong.
A thermostat does not know why the room became cold: a window, a door or a cold night. It only compares the number it reads with the number you set, and that is why it keeps working in situations its designer never imagined.
This works because declaring a goal is much simpler than scripting every path to it. A script must handle every failure case in the right order. A reconciliation loop needs only two things: a way to observe the world and a way to take one step toward the goal. Any failure, whether a crash, a lost message, or a human mistake, simply shows up as a difference to be fixed on the next pass. Storing the goal in a replicated, consistent store means the goal itself survives failures, and electing one active controller prevents contradictory actions.
mistakes: People often think Kubernetes guarantees that your application is highly available. In fact it keeps the declared number of pods running, but a single-replica service is down while its pod restarts, and replicas all placed on one node or zone fail together. You need several replicas, spread constraints, readiness probes, disruption budgets and enough capacity to take over.
People often think a pod's resource limit decides where it is placed. In fact the scheduler only uses requests. A pod that requests 100 MB but uses 4 GB can overload a node, and the kubelet or kernel will then evict or kill pods. Limits are enforced on the node, not at scheduling time. Wrong requests are the most common reason for both wasted capacity and instability.
People often think the control plane is in the data path. In fact running pods and their network traffic keep working if the API server or etcd is down; what stops is change: no new scheduling, no scaling, no new rollouts and no healing. That is why control-plane high availability matters for recovery, not for serving existing traffic.
People often think orchestration means microservices. In fact orchestrators run monoliths, batch jobs, databases and ML training equally well. And a platform with ten services might not need Kubernetes at all: the operating cost of a cluster (upgrades, networking, security, observability) is real.
friction: Complexity is the main cost. A production cluster involves the control plane, a container runtime, a network plugin, DNS, ingress, storage drivers, an autoscaler, monitoring, policy and security tooling, each with its own versions and failure modes. Kubernetes releases come about three times a year and each version is supported for roughly a year, so clusters need regular upgrades, and API deprecations break manifests. Many companies buy managed control planes and still need platform engineers.
Scheduling resource accounting is hard. Requests and limits are guesses; too high wastes money (many clusters use well under half of the capacity they have reserved), too low causes throttling and eviction. Bin-packing is a hard problem, and fragmentation leaves capacity unused. Scale limits appear: about 5,000 nodes per cluster, etcd holding large amounts of data (its default storage quota is 2 GB, with 8 GB as the practical advice), watch fan-out from thousands of kubelets, and heavy use of custom resources can slow the API server. Large clusters are therefore split into many clusters, which brings multi-cluster problems.
Stateful workloads are harder than stateless ones. Databases need stable identities, persistent volumes tied to zones, ordered rollout, backups and careful handling of failover; operators help, but each is only as good as its authors. Networking is another source of mysteries (DNS latency, conntrack limits, service mesh overhead). Security needs work: default pods can talk to every other pod, and a compromised pod with a powerful service account can take over the cluster. Finally, the reconciliation model gives eventual convergence, not instant action: failure detection takes tens of seconds to minutes unless tuned.
practice: Treat the cluster as a product for your developers. Offer templates (Helm charts, Kustomize bases or an internal platform) with sensible defaults: resource requests, readiness and liveness probes, a PodDisruptionBudget, topology spread across zones, a non-root security context, and network policies. Make the right way the easy way.
Set requests from measured use (for example the p90 of observed CPU and memory plus headroom); use the Vertical Pod Autoscaler in recommendation mode to learn them. Set memory limits equal to requests, and be careful with CPU limits for latency-sensitive services. Use at least 3 replicas for critical services, spread them across nodes and zones, and add a PodDisruptionBudget so node drains and upgrades never take them all down. Shorten failure detection when needed (tolerationSeconds, probe periods) but not so far that you cause restart storms. Use graceful shutdown: handle SIGTERM, use a preStop hook, and set terminationGracePeriodSeconds to cover in-flight requests.
Keep the platform small and current: few cluster add-ons, upgraded on a schedule, using a managed control plane when you can. Run stateful systems with mature operators only, and test backup and restore. Put everything in Git (GitOps with Argo CD or Flux) so that the cluster state can be reviewed and rebuilt. Monitor what reveals trouble early: pods Pending and the reason, node pressure, scheduler latency, API server request latency and errors, etcd disk latency, and the number of pods restarting. Run game days: drain a node, kill a zone, and see if the service holds.
tradeoffs: Choose Kubernetes when you run many services or jobs, need a common deployment model across teams, want portability across clouds and on-premises, or need an ecosystem of operators; choose a simpler runtime (a managed container service such as ECS or Cloud Run, or a few VMs with systemd) when you have a handful of services and no platform team. The break-even is often around 10 to 20 services or when you employ at least a few engineers to run the platform; below that, the platform cost exceeds the benefit.
Choose a managed Kubernetes service (EKS, GKE, AKS) when you do not want to run etcd and the control plane yourself, and accept the provider's version and feature pace and the fee per cluster; choose self-managed clusters when you need custom control-plane settings, air-gapped operation or very large scale, and accept the operations burden.
Choose one large shared cluster when you want high bin-packing efficiency and simple operations, and use namespaces, quotas and network policy for isolation; choose several clusters (per environment, region or team) when you need blast-radius limits, hard tenant isolation, regional placement or to stay below scale limits. The price of many clusters is duplicated tooling and the need for multi-cluster deployment and networking.
Choose Kubernetes for data and ML workloads when you want one platform for services and jobs and GPU sharing, and your engines (Spark, Ray, Flink) have good operators; choose the engine's own scheduler or the cloud's managed service (YARN, Databricks, EMR) when it is better integrated, or when shuffle-heavy performance and tooling matter more than platform uniformity. Choose cluster-level autoscaling with a fast provisioner (Karpenter-style) for varied workloads, and fixed node groups for steady, predictable ones.
interview: Explain the reconciliation loop and why Kubernetes controllers are level-triggered rather than edge-triggered. || A controller observes desired state (spec) and actual state (status/world), computes the difference, and takes an idempotent step, repeatedly. Level-triggered means it acts on the current difference, not on a stream of events, so missed or duplicated events and controller restarts are harmless; the next pass repairs the state. Edge-triggered designs lose correctness when an event is lost.
Walk through what happens from kubectl apply of a Deployment to a running container. || The API server validates and stores the Deployment in etcd. The Deployment controller creates a ReplicaSet; its controller creates Pod objects; the scheduler filters and scores nodes and binds each pod by setting nodeName; the kubelet on the node sees the pod, pulls the image, starts containers via the CRI, runs probes and updates status; the endpoint controller adds ready pods to the Service.
How does Kubernetes avoid two schedulers or controller managers acting at once? || Replicas run leader election using a Lease object in the API (backed by etcd and Raft). One replica holds the lease and renews it (default lease 15 s, renew 10 s, retry 2 s). If it stops, the lease expires and another acquires it. Writes use resourceVersion for optimistic concurrency, and actions are idempotent, so a briefly stale leader does limited harm.
A node dies at 12:00. When do users see the effect and when is capacity restored with default settings? || Requests to its pods fail until the endpoints are removed (readiness fails or the node is marked Unknown at about 40 s). Pod eviction starts only after the 300 s toleration, then replacements are scheduled, so full capacity returns after about 5 to 6 minutes. Reduce with tolerationSeconds, more replicas, spread across nodes, and faster probes.
Why does the scheduler use requests and not actual usage, and what problems does it cause? || Requests are a declared, stable reservation, which makes placement deterministic and fast and gives a guarantee. Actual usage varies and is unknown for new pods. The problems: over-requesting wastes capacity, under-requesting causes overcommit, throttling and evictions. VPA, measurement and right-sizing reduce the gap.
When would you split one large cluster into several? || When you approach scale limits (nodes, objects, etcd size, API latency), need hard isolation or compliance boundaries between tenants, need regional placement, or want a smaller blast radius for upgrades and failures. You accept duplicated tooling and cross-cluster complexity.
quiz: What does the scheduler use to decide whether a node has room for a pod? || The pod's resource requests compared with the node's allocatable resources minus the requests of pods already there (plus constraints such as taints and affinity), not the actual usage.
Why can Kubernetes recover from a controller crash without special code? || Controllers are level-triggered and idempotent: on restart they list the current state, compare with the spec and take the missing steps, so no event history is needed.
Why is etcd run with an odd number of members, such as 3 or 5? || Raft needs a majority to commit. Three members tolerate one failure and five tolerate two; a fourth member adds cost without tolerating more failures than three.
A Deployment has 12 replicas with maxSurge 25% and maxUnavailable 25%. What are the maximum number of pods and the minimum available during a rollout? || Surge rounds up: 25% of 12 = 3, so at most 15 pods. Unavailable rounds down: 3, so at least 9 must be available.
When would you choose a managed container service instead of Kubernetes? || When you have a few services, no platform team, and want less operational work. You give up portability, the operator ecosystem and fine control.

@@ mlops
value: MLOps is the set of practices and tools that turn a machine-learning model from a notebook result into a service that is trained repeatedly, deployed safely, watched in production and replaced when it gets worse. It applies ideas from DevOps (version control, automated tests, continuous delivery, monitoring) to a system that has an extra, unstable ingredient: data. In ordinary software, behaviour changes only when the code changes. In an ML system, behaviour also changes when the data changes, and the data in the world changes all the time.
The problem is well documented. In 2015 Google researchers (Sculley et al.) showed that in a real ML system the model code is a small box surrounded by much larger boxes: data collection, feature extraction, configuration, serving, monitoring. These bring "hidden technical debt": models depend on the exact data and code that made their features, small input changes silently change outputs, and one model's output often becomes another's input. Most ML projects do not fail because the model is bad. They fail because nobody can reproduce last month's model, retrain it when the data shifts, or tell that it has stopped working.
MLOps stands on two ideas from the layers below. DAG scheduling (L2) lets data preparation, feature computation, training, evaluation and deployment run as a pipeline of steps with explicit dependencies, retries and schedules, so the whole path is code, not a series of manual steps. Idempotence (L2) makes re-running a step safe and its result repeatable: given the same code, data snapshot and parameters, a step produces the same output, and a retry never leaves half-written results. Above this concept, platforms such as MLflow, Databricks, Ray and Kubeflow provide tracking, registry, serving and orchestration.
terms: Training pipeline — an automated sequence (extract data, build features, train, evaluate, register) that produces a versioned model from versioned inputs.
Experiment tracking — recording the parameters, code version, data version, metrics and artifacts of every training run so that runs can be compared and reproduced.
Model registry — a catalogue of model versions with metadata, lineage (which run, which data) and lifecycle labels such as candidate and production.
Training–serving skew — a difference between how features are computed in training and in production, which makes a model perform worse live than in testing.
Data drift and concept drift — drift in the input distribution (data drift) or in the relationship between inputs and the target (concept drift); both erode accuracy over time.
Shadow deployment and canary — shadow runs a new model on live traffic without using its answers; a canary sends it a small share of real traffic and compares outcomes.
Continuous training (CT) — automatically retraining and re-evaluating a model on a schedule or when monitoring shows drift.
Evaluation gate — an automatic check that a new model must pass (metrics on held-out data, slice checks, latency, fairness) before it can be promoted.
history: Software engineering found its way to release discipline in the 2000s with continuous integration and then DevOps (about 2009). Machine learning stayed a research craft for longer. Companies that ran ML at scale met the problems first. Google published "Machine Learning: The High-Interest Credit Card of Technical Debt" (2014) and "Hidden Technical Debt in Machine Learning Systems" (NeurIPS 2015), and described TFX, its production pipeline platform, in 2017. Martin Zinkevich's "Rules of Machine Learning" collected practical advice, and Breck et al. proposed the "ML Test Score" rubric of 28 production-readiness tests (2017).
Other large companies built internal platforms: Uber's Michelangelo (2017), Facebook's FBLearner Flow, Airbnb's Bighead, and LinkedIn's Pro-ML. Their common parts were a feature store, pipelines, a model registry, one-click deployment and monitoring. Open source followed: Kubeflow (announced late 2017, Kubernetes-based pipelines), MLflow (Databricks, June 2018: tracking, projects, models, registry), DVC for data versioning (2017), and later tools such as Metaflow (Netflix), Airflow-based and Dagster-based pipelines, Seldon and KServe for serving. Google's 2020 guidance on "MLOps: continuous delivery and automation pipelines" popularised maturity levels: level 0 is manual, level 1 automates training pipelines (continuous training), and level 2 automates the building and testing of the pipelines themselves.
Since 2022, large language models changed the shape of the work. Many teams no longer train a model; they select one, write prompts, retrieve documents (RAG), and fine-tune occasionally. The same disciplines return in new forms, often called LLMOps: versioned prompts and chains, evaluation sets and automatic or model-based graders in place of a single accuracy number, tracing of every step of an agent, guardrails, and cost and latency monitoring per request.
how: Think of MLOps as a loop with five stages, each automated. First, data and features. Raw data is read from a table or stream at a pinned snapshot (for example a Delta table version or a timestamp), validated (schema, null rates, ranges, distribution checks), and turned into features. Pinning the snapshot is what makes the later steps reproducible. Second, training. A training step reads the features, hyperparameters and code version, trains the model and logs everything to the tracking system: parameters, metrics per epoch, the model file, the environment (library versions, container image digest), the data version and the random seed. Third, evaluation. The candidate is scored on held-out data and compared with the current production model on the same data. Checks include overall metrics, metrics on important slices (new users, regions, device types), calibration, latency and size, and sometimes fairness constraints. Only a model that passes all gates is registered as a candidate.
The pipeline is a DAG (L2). Nodes are steps such as validate_data, build_features, train, evaluate, register, and deploy_canary; edges are data dependencies. An orchestrator (Airflow, Dagster, Databricks Workflows, Kubeflow Pipelines) runs a node when its inputs are ready, retries failures, runs independent branches in parallel (for example training several candidates), caches results of unchanged steps and keeps a history. Idempotence (L2) is what makes this safe. A step writes its output to a path derived from its inputs and parameters (or writes atomically with overwrite semantics), so a retry after a crash replaces rather than duplicates, and re-running last week's pipeline reproduces last week's model. Without it, retries produce duplicate rows, partial files and models nobody can trace.
Fourth, release. The registry stores each version with its lineage: which run, which code commit, which data snapshot. Promotion moves a version through labels or aliases (in MLflow, stages were replaced by aliases such as "champion"). A deployment step packages the model with its dependencies into a container or a serving endpoint. Deployment style depends on the use: batch scoring writes predictions to a table on a schedule; online serving exposes a low-latency endpoint; streaming scoring runs inside a stream processor; edge deployment ships a small model to devices. Releases are staged: shadow mode first (the new model sees live requests, but only logs its predictions), then a canary (for example 5% of traffic), then a gradual rollout, with automatic rollback when a guard metric degrades. The old model stays deployed and ready, so rollback takes seconds.
Fifth, monitoring, which closes the loop. Operational metrics (latency, errors, throughput, cost) are as in any service. ML-specific monitoring watches the inputs and outputs. Input drift is measured by comparing the distribution of each feature in a recent window with the training distribution, using statistics such as the population stability index (PSI), the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test or the Jensen–Shannon divergence. Prediction drift watches the distribution of scores. Data quality checks watch missing values and out-of-range values, which usually signal a broken upstream pipeline rather than a changed world. Where ground truth arrives later (a fraud label after 30 days, a click after seconds), accuracy metrics are computed when labels arrive and compared with the expected level. The monitoring results trigger alerts or start the pipeline again: continuous training.
Serving deserves a closer look because it is where ML meets ordinary operations. A model server loads the registered artifact, exposes it through an API, batches concurrent requests so the hardware is used well (dynamic batching), and reports latency and error metrics. For online use, the features must reach the server within the latency budget: precomputed values come from a low-latency store, and values that depend on the request are computed on the fly with the same code or definition as in training. The server logs each request's features and prediction (with the model version) to a table. This log is the raw material for monitoring, for debugging skew, and for the next training set, so it is part of the pipeline, not an afterthought.
Reproducibility ties these parts together. To rebuild a model one needs the code (Git commit), the data (snapshot or version), the configuration, the environment (container image) and the randomness (seeds). Lakehouse tables with time travel, containers with pinned digests and a tracking server that links them all give this. Governance builds on the same records: who approved a model, what data it used, whether that data was allowed for the purpose. For LLM applications, the registered artifact is often a prompt plus configuration plus a model reference, and the evaluation set is the test suite.

example: A fraud-scoring model is retrained every week at a payment company. Step 1: the pipeline reads transactions up to Sunday 00:00 from a table at a pinned version (say version 4812). A validation step checks that the null rate of "device_id" is under 2% and that the daily volume is within 30% of the 4-week average; if not, the run stops and alerts the data team, so a broken upstream job cannot produce a bad model. Step 2: features are built, and the training step logs to the tracker: gradient-boosted trees, 400 trees, learning rate 0.05, code commit a1b2c3, image digest, data version 4812, seed 42.
Step 3: evaluation. The candidate gets AUC 0.912 on the last two weeks of held-out data (kept out of training and later in time than the training data, to avoid leakage), against 0.905 for the production model on the same data. A gate requires an improvement of at least 0.002 and no drop larger than 0.01 on any of the top 5 slices (for example "new customers" or "mobile app"), and p99 latency under 50 ms in a load test. The candidate passes and is registered as version 37 with the alias "challenger".
Step 4: release. Shadow mode runs version 37 for 24 hours alongside production and logs its scores; the team checks that the score distribution looks similar. Then a canary sends 5% of traffic to it. How long must the canary run? Take a business metric such as a 2% rate of confirmed fraud among flagged transactions, and suppose you want to detect an absolute change of 0.1 percentage points (δ = 0.001). A common rule of thumb for 80% power at 5% significance is n ≈ 16 × p(1 − p) / δ² per group, which gives 16 × 0.0196 / 0.000001 ≈ 314,000 events per group. If the canary gets 5,000 flagged events a day, that is more than two months, so the team uses a guard on a faster proxy metric (the review queue size, decline rate and score distribution) and treats the final judgement as offline.
Step 5: monitoring. The feature "transaction_amount" is binned into four bins that had 25% of the training data each. A month later the live shares are 15%, 20%, 30% and 35%. PSI = sum of (live − train) × ln(live / train) over bins = (−0.10 × ln 0.6) + (−0.05 × ln 0.8) + (0.05 × ln 1.2) + (0.10 × ln 1.4) = 0.0511 + 0.0112 + 0.0091 + 0.0336 ≈ 0.105. A common rule of thumb reads PSI below 0.1 as stable, 0.1 to 0.25 as moderate shift, above 0.25 as major shift. This feature is just in the moderate range: the team looks at the cause (a seasonal sale) before retraining, and chargeback labels, which arrive 30 to 90 days later, will show whether accuracy actually fell.
intuition: Treat a model like a product release, not a notebook result: it needs a build, a version number, tests, a rollout plan and an on-call rota.
A bakery that bakes from a living, changing sourdough starter needs a recipe, a log and a taste test for every batch, because the starter, not the recipe, changes the bread.
The idea is natural because a trained model is a compiled artifact made from code and data, and the world it was compiled from keeps moving. In normal software the source is stable, so a build lasts. In ML, the source includes data, so the same code gives a different program next month. The only way to stay in control is to automate the build and to measure the running product against reality. The pipeline makes retraining cheap, the registry makes it traceable, the gates make it safe and monitoring tells you when to do it again.
mistakes: People often think MLOps is just deploying a model behind an API. In fact deployment is one stage. Most of the effort is in data validation, reproducible pipelines, evaluation, monitoring and retraining, which together decide whether the model stays useful after the first week.
People often think a model that scores well offline will do well in production. In fact offline metrics use historical data and a fixed split; production sees new data, feedback loops (the model's decisions change the data it later learns from), different feature values (training–serving skew) and delayed labels. Offline gains of one percent often vanish or reverse in online tests.
People often think you retrain as often as possible. In fact each retraining adds risk (a bad data day becomes a bad model), costs compute, and can make results harder to compare. Retrain on a schedule matched to how fast the data changes, or on drift triggers, and always with gates and rollback.
People often think drift detection tells you the model is wrong. In fact drift means the inputs changed, which may or may not hurt accuracy; and a model can degrade without any input drift when the relationship between inputs and target changes. Drift metrics are early warnings, and accuracy measured on labels is the real test.
friction: Technical debt is the central problem. ML systems have data dependencies that are hard to see (an upstream team changes a column's meaning), entangled models (changing one input shifts everything, "changing anything changes everything"), feedback loops, and glue code around libraries. Pipelines grow into many scripts and configurations with no clear owner.
Labels are slow. Many labels arrive days or months late (fraud, churn, loan default), so quality monitoring lags behind reality and must rely on proxy metrics and drift signals. Evaluation is also hard: a single metric hides slices, offline data is biased by past decisions of the system, and for generative models there is often no ground truth, so teams use human review or model-based judges that have their own biases.
Organisational friction is large. Data scientists, data engineers, software engineers and platform teams use different tools and incentives, and ownership of a model in production is often unclear. Tool sprawl is real: experiment trackers, feature stores, orchestrators, serving frameworks and monitoring tools overlap. Cost grows with retraining, GPU use and storage of every artifact. Reproducibility is never perfect: GPU non-determinism, library updates and external data sources make bitwise repeatability hard, so teams aim for statistically equivalent results. Governance (privacy law, audit, right to explanation) adds requirements on lineage and retention.
practice: Version everything that determines a model: code in Git, data as an immutable snapshot or a table version, features and their definitions, hyperparameters, and the runtime environment as a container digest. Log all of it to a tracker (MLflow, Weights & Biases) and register models with lineage. If you can not rebuild last month's model from its record, you do not control the system.
Automate the path from data to deployment with a pipeline and make each step idempotent: write outputs to deterministic locations, use overwrite or merge semantics, and avoid side effects that retries repeat. Put gates in the pipeline: data validation before training; evaluation against the current production model on the same recent data and on key slices; latency, memory and cost checks; and a human approval step where the risk justifies it. Start simple. A scheduled job, a tracker and a registry give most of the value before you need a heavy platform.
Release carefully: shadow first, then canary with guard metrics, and keep a one-step rollback to the previous model. Monitor the service (latency, errors), the data (missing values, ranges, drift per feature, prediction distribution) and the outcome (accuracy when labels arrive, business metrics), and give each alert an owner and a runbook. Compare the features that serving computes with those that training computes, ideally from one shared definition (see the feature store). For LLM applications, build an evaluation set from real traffic early, run it on every change to prompt, model or retrieval, trace every request and review failures regularly.
tradeoffs: Choose a simple scheduled retraining job with a registry when your model changes slowly and the team is small; choose a full continuous-training platform with feature store, automated gates and monitoring when you ship many models, retrain often, or the cost of a bad model is high. The platform cost is real; many teams over-build early. A rough test: if you have fewer than 3 to 5 models in production, buy or assemble a minimal stack.
Choose batch scoring when predictions can be computed ahead of time (daily recommendations, churn scores): it is cheaper, simpler and easier to test. Choose online serving when the input is only known at request time (fraud checks, search ranking) and latency matters (tens of milliseconds); you pay for always-on capacity, a feature store for online features and stricter monitoring. Choose streaming scoring when you need seconds-level freshness on event streams.
Choose retraining on a fixed schedule when the data changes steadily and you want predictability; choose drift- or performance-triggered retraining when changes are bursty and labels or proxies are timely; accept more complexity and the risk of triggering on noise. Choose shadow deployment when the model's outputs can not safely affect users and you need real-traffic comparison; choose canary when you can limit the damage and need outcome metrics; choose offline-only for low-risk internal tools.
Choose a managed platform (Databricks, SageMaker, Vertex AI) when you want integrated tracking, registry, serving and governance and accept lock-in; choose open-source components (MLflow, Kubeflow, Airflow, KServe) when portability and control matter more and you have platform engineers to run them. For LLM applications, choose a versioned prompt plus an evaluation suite before investing in fine-tuning infrastructure, because most quality gains and risk reduction come first from evaluation.
interview: How would you make an ML training pipeline reproducible and safe to re-run? || Pin the data (snapshot or table version), code (commit), config and environment (image digest), set seeds, and log them to a tracker. Make each DAG step idempotent: deterministic output paths or atomic overwrite, no duplicate side effects, so retries and re-runs give the same result. Register the model with lineage. Expect statistical, not bitwise, equivalence on GPUs.
Your model's online metrics dropped 3% but offline evaluation is fine. How do you investigate? || Check training–serving skew (compare feature values served and logged against recomputed offline features), input and prediction drift, data-quality problems upstream, label delay or definition changes, a deployment mismatch (wrong model version, preprocessing), and feedback loops. Slice by segment and time to find where the drop is. Roll back if the cause is not found quickly.
What gates would you put before promoting a new model automatically? || Data validation passed; metric on a recent held-out set beats production by a margin and does not regress on key slices; calibration and latency, memory and cost budgets met; no fairness or policy violations; explainability or schema checks; then shadow and canary with guard metrics. High-risk models also need human sign-off.
How do you monitor a model whose labels arrive 60 days late? || Monitor inputs and outputs for drift (PSI, KS), data quality, and score distributions; track proxy metrics available early (rates of flags, reviews, user actions); compute true accuracy on cohorts when labels mature and compare with expectations; use periodic backtests and holdout groups; set conservative retraining and rollback processes.
What changes when the "model" is an LLM application? || Artifacts are prompts, retrieval config and model versions; evaluation is a curated test set with graders (human or model-based) and regression tracking; monitoring covers traces, cost, latency, refusal and hallucination rates, user feedback; deployment is often a config change, so it needs the same gates and rollback as code.
Why not retrain automatically every night? || Cost, the risk of learning from a bad data day, hard-to-compare models, and feedback loops. Retrain at the pace of data change or on triggers, gate each candidate against production, canary it, and keep rollback. Nightly may be right for fast-changing domains such as ads, but only with strong validation.
quiz: What is training–serving skew and how do you avoid it? || A mismatch between how features are computed for training and in production, so the live model sees different inputs than it learned from. Avoid it by using one feature definition for both paths (a feature store or shared code) and by comparing logged serving features with offline ones.
Why does idempotence matter in an ML pipeline? || Pipelines retry failed steps and are re-run to reproduce results. If a step is idempotent, a retry replaces its output rather than duplicating it, and the same inputs and parameters give the same output.
Why use a shadow deployment before a canary? || Shadow mode checks the new model on real traffic without any effect on users, which reveals errors, latency problems and distribution differences before any user is exposed.
A feature has 4 equal bins in training. Live shares are 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%. value: A feature is a number or category that a model uses as input, such as "number of ad impressions this device saw in the last 24 hours" or "average order value of this customer in 90 days". A feature store is a system that computes, stores, serves and documents features, so that the same feature has the same definition and the same value whether it is used to train a model or to answer a live request. It has two faces: an offline side for training (large history, point-in-time correct, queried in batch) and an online side for serving (only the latest value, looked up by key in a few milliseconds).
It exists to solve two costly problems. The first is training–serving skew. Data scientists write feature code in SQL or Spark over history to train a model; engineers re-implement the same logic in a service for live traffic. The two versions drift apart (a different time window, a different null rule, a different timezone), and a model that scored well in testing performs worse in production, with no error message to explain it. The second is duplication: ten teams compute "user purchases in 30 days" ten different ways, and each pays for its own pipeline.
A feature store answers both by defining each feature once and generating both offline and online values from that one definition, and by providing a registry so teams can find and reuse features. The correctness of the offline side depends on a idea from databases: multi-version concurrency control (L2, MVCC). Keeping timestamped versions of every value lets you ask "what was the value as of time t?", which is what point-in-time correct training data needs. Above this concept sit products such as Databricks Feature Engineering with Unity Catalog and the open-source Feast.
terms: Feature — a measurable input to a model, computed from raw data about an entity (a user, device, item, merchant).
Entity and entity key — the thing a feature describes and the ID used to look it up, such as device_id or customer_id.
Offline store — the large historical store (a lakehouse table, warehouse or files) used to build training sets and to run batch scoring.
Online store — a low-latency key-value store (Redis, DynamoDB, Cassandra and similar) that holds the latest feature values for each entity key.
Materialization — the job that computes feature values and writes them to the online store (and keeps the offline store up to date).
Point-in-time join (as-of join) — a join that attaches to each training example the feature values that were known at the example's timestamp, and no later ones.
Data leakage — using information in training that would not have been available at prediction time; a leaked feature makes offline metrics look better than reality.
Feature view / feature group — a named, versioned definition of a set of features with their source, entity, timestamp column, owner and freshness expectations.
history: The problem appeared wherever companies ran many models. Uber described Michelangelo, its internal ML platform, in a 2017 post ("Meet Michelangelo"), with a shared feature store that let teams register features once and use them for both training and serving. Airbnb built Zipline for feature definitions with backfill, and others did similar work at Twitter, Facebook, LinkedIn and Spotify. These systems had common elements: a registry, batch and streaming computation, an offline store and an online store.
In 2018 and 2019, the idea went open and commercial. Hopsworks (Logical Clocks) presented a feature store in 2018. Gojek and Google released Feast in 2019; its design started as a way to share features across Gojek's models and became a widely used open-source project. Tecton, founded in 2019 by members of the Michelangelo team, built a managed feature platform with streaming support. Cloud and data platforms followed: Amazon SageMaker Feature Store (2020), Vertex AI Feature Store (2021), and the Databricks Feature Store (announced in 2021), which stores features as Delta tables in a governed catalogue. In 2025 Databricks agreed to acquire Tecton to strengthen real-time features for AI applications.
The idea has also been questioned and reshaped. Some teams found that a feature store was more than they needed and used well-managed tables and a shared transformation library instead. Others found they needed more: features computed at request time ("on-demand features"), features over streams with second-level freshness, and now vector embeddings and retrieved context for LLM applications, which are served in much the same way as classic features.
how: The core design has four parts. First, a registry holds feature definitions: name, entity, data type, source tables or streams, transformation logic, timestamp column, owner, freshness target, and a version. This metadata is what makes features discoverable and reviewable, and it also records lineage, which models consume which features. Second, computation pipelines turn raw data into feature values. Batch pipelines (Spark or SQL jobs on a schedule) compute slow-changing features such as 90-day averages. Streaming pipelines (Flink or Spark Structured Streaming) compute fast-changing windowed aggregates such as clicks in the last 10 minutes. On-demand features are computed at request time from the request itself, such as the time since the last event. The aim is that one definition drives all three.
Third, the offline store keeps the full history of feature values with timestamps, in a lakehouse table. Every row says: for entity E, feature F had value V from time T. This is the multi-version idea from MVCC (L2): old versions are not overwritten; each value is valid for an interval, so the table can answer "as of" queries. To build a training set, the store takes a list of labelled examples, each with an entity key and an event time (the moment of the prediction), and performs a point-in-time join: for each example and each feature view, find the latest feature row for that entity whose feature timestamp is not later than the event time (and, optionally, not older than a time-to-live). Rows from the future never match. Implemented in Spark, this is an as-of join, usually done by sorting both sides on (key, time) and merging, or with a window function that ranks candidate rows by timestamp, partitioned by entity key.
Fourth, the online store serves live predictions. A materialization job writes the latest value of each feature for each entity into a key-value store: key is the entity ID (and feature view), value is the feature vector. At request time the model service looks up the key for all needed entities and gets the whole vector in one round trip, typically in 1 to 10 milliseconds for Redis or DynamoDB at p99. Streaming pipelines update it within seconds. A freshness metric tracks how old each value is. The online store holds only the current value (and maybe a short TTL), so it is small compared with the offline store.
Consistency between the two sides is the main engineering goal. Strong designs use one transformation definition for both paths. For batch features, the same SQL or Spark code computes the offline history and the values written online. For streaming features, the same stream job writes both stores, or the online store is the source and a log of its values is appended to the offline store, so that the training data contains exactly the values that serving saw. This "log and wait" approach (log the features used at prediction time, then train on the log) removes skew by construction, at the cost of waiting for data to accumulate. Backfills use the batch path to compute history for a new feature, so that it can be used in training immediately without waiting weeks.
A concrete picture of the serving path helps. A request for an ad auction arrives with a device ID, an app ID and a placement. The service needs features from three feature views: device_stats keyed by device ID, app_stats keyed by app ID, and a request-time feature, the hour of day, computed from the request. It sends one batched read to the online store for the two keyed views, receives two vectors, appends the on-demand value, and passes the combined vector to the model. If the device key is missing (a new device), the store returns nothing and the service substitutes the default recorded in the registry, and a counter records the miss. Everything that touches the vector, including the order of the features and their encoding, comes from the registry so that the model and the data always agree on the schema.
Operational details complete the design. Late-arriving data must be handled: if an event arrives two hours late, the offline store may be corrected, while the online value was already served; monitoring should measure how often this changes values. Features are monitored for freshness, null rate and distribution drift. Access control and governance (which teams may use which features, particularly those with personal data) sit in the registry. In Databricks, feature tables are Delta tables registered in Unity Catalog with primary keys and an optional timestamp key, so lineage and permissions come from the catalogue, time travel gives point-in-time lookups, and publishing to an online store serves the same features at low latency.

example: Take an ad click-through model. The entity is a device, and the feature is impressions_7d, the number of ads the device saw in the previous 7 days, recomputed daily at midnight. For device D1 the offline store has rows: Jan 1, value 20; Jan 8, value 35; Jan 15, value 80 (the device became very active on Jan 11). Each row is valid from its timestamp until the next one.
Training examples for D1: example A happened on Jan 10 at 14:00 with label click = 0; example B on Jan 16 at 09:00 with click = 1. A point-in-time join gives A the Jan 8 value (35), the latest row at or before Jan 10, and gives B the Jan 15 value (80). Now see the mistake. If we join on the latest value without a time condition (which is easy to do by accident with a plain join to a "current" table), then both A and B get 80. For A, 80 includes impressions from Jan 11 to 15, which had not happened yet when the ad was shown: the model learns that "high impressions" goes with the label in a way it can never use in production. Offline AUC looks better; online AUC is worse. This is leakage, and it is the reason for the point-in-time join.
Now the online side, and a second trap about time. Suppose the daily job runs after midnight and finishes at 00:30. The value for Jan 16 is computed from data up to the end of Jan 15, but it only exists in the online store from 00:30 on Jan 16. If the offline table stamps this row with the logical date Jan 16 00:00, then a training example at 00:10 on Jan 16 is joined with a value that serving could not have had at that moment: the job had not finished. The offline and online worlds now disagree for 30 minutes every day. The fix is to stamp each feature row with the time it became available (Jan 16 00:30), so that the point-in-time join and the online store behave the same. The lesson: timestamp features by when they become available, not by the period they describe.
Sizing the online store: 50 million active devices, 200 features of 8 bytes each is 50,000,000 × 200 × 8 = 80 GB of raw values. Redis adds overhead (keys, pointers, hash structures), so plan for 2 to 3 times that, 160 to 240 GB, or store the features of a view as one packed binary blob per device to cut the overhead. Serving 100,000 requests per second with 5 feature views each means 500,000 key lookups per second, which needs a sharded cluster, not a single node. A cheaper design keeps only active devices (say 10 million, 16 GB raw) in the online store and falls back to defaults for others.
Last, a freshness calculation. A stream job computes clicks_10min with a 10-second micro-batch and writes to Redis. The end-to-end delay is the event time to the Kafka arrival (about 1 s), plus the batch wait (up to 10 s), plus processing and write (about 1 s), so values are 2 to 12 seconds old. For an ad model where behaviour changes over minutes, this is fine. For a fraud rule on card testing, where an attacker tries 20 cards in 5 seconds, it is too slow and you compute such a feature at request time from the live event.
intuition: A shared pantry with labelled ingredients, stocked from one recipe, so the test kitchen and the restaurant cook with exactly the same things.
A pantry also has dates on its labels: the test kitchen must only use ingredients that were on the shelf on the day of the dish it is reproducing.
The idea is natural because the two worlds want opposite things from the same data. Training wants years of history in bulk and the exact state of the world at each past moment. Serving wants only the newest values and an answer in milliseconds. A single store cannot be good at both, so you keep two, but you derive both from one definition, so they can not disagree about what a feature means. The time dimension is the subtle part. A feature is a value that is true for an interval, so asking for it "as of" a past moment is a question about versions, which is exactly what multi-version storage answers.
mistakes: People often think a feature store is a database for features. In fact the database part is the smallest piece. The value is in the definitions, the shared computation, the point-in-time join, the consistency between training and serving, and the metadata that lets teams find and reuse features.
People often think a normal join of labels to a feature table is good enough. In fact a plain join to the latest value leaks future information into training. The error is silent: nothing fails, and offline metrics only look better. Always join as of the example time, and test by checking that no feature timestamp is later than its example's timestamp.
People often think one feature store removes training–serving skew by itself. In fact it removes the skew that comes from two code paths, not skew caused by data issues: late events, different filtering of bots, a missing upstream feed, or feature values that are fresher online than offline. You still need monitoring that compares served values with recomputed offline values.
People often think every ML team needs a feature store. In fact if you have one model, batch scoring only, and a few well-tested tables, a feature store adds machinery you do not need. It pays off with many models sharing features, real-time serving, streaming aggregates, or strict governance.
friction: The hardest problem is keeping offline and online consistent. Streaming windows are defined on event time, processing time or ingestion time, and these give different numbers. Late and out-of-order events change history after the fact. Backfills may use a different engine from live computation, so results differ at the edges. Teams spend a large part of their effort on tests that compare the two paths.
Cost and complexity are real. Streaming feature pipelines need always-on infrastructure, state stores and careful operations; the online store must be sized for peak read throughput and kept highly available, since a failed feature lookup either breaks the prediction or forces a default value that degrades quality. Wide feature sets with many entities multiply storage. Point-in-time joins over billions of examples are expensive: they shuffle and sort large tables, and they need good partitioning by entity key and time.
There are also design limits. Features that depend on the request (a user's current query) can not be precomputed, so on-demand computation is needed, which brings back two code paths unless the same function serves both. Entity-level features may not fit models that use sequences or embeddings, which are served by other systems. Governance is hard: a shared feature can break many models when its definition changes, so versioning and deprecation policies are needed. And a central feature platform can become a bottleneck for teams that want to move fast.
practice: Define each feature once, with owner, entity, source, timestamp semantics, freshness target, and null handling, and generate both offline and online values from that definition. Put the definition in code review. Version features; when logic changes, create a new version and migrate models rather than editing in place.
Build training sets only with point-in-time joins, and add an automatic test that the feature timestamp never exceeds the event time. Stamp each value with the time it became available, not the period it describes. Limit how far back a value may be used (a TTL) so that a stale value does not appear to be fresh. Where possible, log the exact features used at prediction time and train on those logs, at least for the most important models, since this removes skew from the design.
Keep the online store small and fast: store only the entities and features that are needed for serving, use compact encodings, set TTLs, and monitor lookup latency percentiles, hit rate and freshness. Provide safe defaults for missing keys and monitor how often they are used. Monitor distributions of served values against training, and alert on features with high null rates or sudden shifts. Start with batch features and one online store, add streaming only for features whose value really depends on the last minutes, and consider whether managed tables with strict conventions meet your needs before adopting a large platform.
tradeoffs: Choose a feature store when many models share features, when you serve in real time, or when skew has already hurt you; choose plain, well-managed tables and shared transformation code when you have a few models and batch scoring. A feature store adds a platform to run; the saving is reuse and fewer silent skew bugs. A rough threshold: more than about 5 models, or more than 2 teams reusing features, or any online model that uses aggregated history.
Choose batch-computed features (hourly or daily) when the signal changes slowly and a delay of hours is acceptable; choose streaming features when value depends on the last seconds or minutes (fraud, bidding, trending content) and you can pay for stateful streaming; choose on-demand computation when the feature depends on the request itself. Each step towards freshness raises cost and the risk of skew.
Choose a managed or integrated store (Databricks, SageMaker, Vertex) when you want governance, lineage and low operational effort and your data already lives there; choose open source (Feast with your own offline and online stores) when you need portability, or a custom stack. Integrated tools reduce glue code but tie you to one platform.
Choose Redis when you need sub-millisecond latency, accept memory cost and have a small active key set; choose DynamoDB or Cassandra-style stores for very large key sets, predictable single-digit-millisecond latency and operational simplicity at lower cost per GB; choose a feature store with a "log and train" approach when exact skew-free training matters more than being able to create training data for new features immediately from history. You can combine: backfill for exploration, logged features for final training.

value: Publish–subscribe (pub/sub) is a way for programs to talk without knowing each other. A producer publishes a message to a named topic ("order-placed", "ad-impression"). Any number of subscribers have told the system they are interested in that topic, and each one receives its own copy. The producer does not know who the subscribers are, how many there are, or whether they are running right now. Subscribers do not know who produced the message.
This decoupling is the reason for the pattern. Without it, the order service must call billing, then analytics, then search indexing, then notifications, and every new consumer means a change and a redeploy of the producer, and one slow consumer slows the order service. With pub/sub, the order service publishes one event, and each consumer team adds a subscription on its own schedule. The producer's cost does not grow with the number of consumers, and a consumer failure does not hurt the producer. Eugster and colleagues describe three kinds of decoupling: in space (no references to each other), in time (they need not be online together) and in synchronisation (the producer does not wait for consumers).
Pub/sub differs from a message queue in one way that matters. In a queue, each message is taken by exactly one of the competing workers, to share the work. In pub/sub, each message is delivered to every subscription, to share the information. Real systems combine them: each subscription is a queue shared by the workers of that subscriber. The layers below make this work at scale. Partitioning (L2) splits a topic into independent ordered streams so that many machines can share the load while order is kept per key. Consumer groups (L2) divide the partitions of a topic among the members of one subscriber, and rebalance when members join or leave. Delivery guarantees (L2) define what happens on failure: when a message is acknowledged or its position committed decides whether it can be lost or repeated. Above pub/sub sit Apache Kafka and Apache Pulsar.
terms: Topic — a named stream of messages to which producers publish and from which subscribers read.
Subscription — one subscriber's registered interest in a topic, with its own position or backlog; in Kafka, a consumer group plays this role.
Broker — a server that receives, stores and delivers messages.
Partition — an ordered, append-only slice of a topic; a topic with N partitions can be written and read by N machines in parallel.
Offset — the position of a message in a partition; a consumer group tracks the offset it has processed up to.
Acknowledgement (ack) — a signal from a subscriber that it has processed a message, after which the system need not redeliver it.
Fan-out — delivering one published message to many subscriptions.
Retention — how long the system keeps messages (a time or size limit), which decides whether new or slow subscribers can read the past.
history: The idea comes from research on distributed event notification in the 1980s. The Information Bus at Teknekron (Oki, Pfaff, Siegel and Skeen, SOSP 1993), later TIBCO, let trading applications publish quotes by subject and subscribe by subject pattern, without knowing each other. It spread through message-oriented middleware: TIBCO Rendezvous, IBM MQ with its topics, and the Java Message Service (JMS, 1998), which defined both queue and topic models. Hohpe and Woolf's "Enterprise Integration Patterns" (2003) gave the vocabulary that engineers still use, and Eugster et al. (2003) surveyed the field and separated topic-based, content-based and type-based subscription.
Two developments pushed pub/sub to internet scale. The first was cloud services: Amazon SNS (2010) for fan-out to queues, email and HTTP endpoints; Google Cloud Pub/Sub (beta 2015, general availability 2016), a global, managed service where each subscription has its own backlog; and Azure Event Grid and Service Bus. The second was the log. LinkedIn built Kafka (2010, open-sourced 2011, described by Kreps, Narkhede and Rao at NetDB 2011) on a simple idea: a topic is a partitioned, replicated, append-only log kept on disk, and each subscriber is only a position in that log. That removes per-subscriber state from the broker, makes replay possible and allows very high throughput. Pulsar (Yahoo, 2016, Apache 2018) separated storage from serving with BookKeeper, and Redpanda and others reimplemented the Kafka protocol. At the other end, MQTT (Andy Stanford-Clark and Arlen Nipper, 1999; OASIS standard 2014) became the standard for small devices and the Internet of Things, with topic wildcards and three delivery levels.
how: A pub/sub system has producers, a broker layer and subscriptions. The first design choice is how the broker keeps messages. In a log-based system (Kafka, Pulsar, Redpanda), each topic is divided into partitions, and each partition is an ordered log on disk, replicated to several brokers. The producer chooses a partition for each message: if the message has a key (for example the order ID), the partition is a hash of the key modulo the number of partitions, so all messages with the same key land in the same partition and keep their order; without a key, messages are spread across partitions. The broker appends the message and returns an offset. Order is guaranteed only inside a partition, never across partitions; this is the price of parallelism (L2, partitioning).
Subscribers read by position. A consumer group in Kafka is a named set of consumers that together read the topic. The group coordinator assigns each partition to exactly one consumer in the group; a consumer may own several partitions, but a partition has one owner at a time, so within the group order per partition is kept and no message is handled by two members. A consumer pulls batches from its partitions, processes them, and commits the offset of the last processed message, stored by the broker in an internal topic. A second group, say analytics, has its own assignments and its own committed offsets and reads the same log independently, at its own speed. This is fan-out in a log: adding a subscriber adds read traffic but no extra copies of the data on disk. The maximum parallelism of a group is the number of partitions, because extra consumers would have no partition to read. When a member joins, leaves or dies (its heartbeats stop), the group rebalances: partitions are reassigned, ideally moving as few as possible.
Delivery guarantees (L2) depend on when the offset is committed relative to processing. If the consumer commits first and then processes, a crash between the two loses the message for that group: at-most-once. If it processes first and commits after, a crash between the two leads to reprocessing after restart: at-least-once, the usual default. Exactly-once processing is possible only inside a closed system. Kafka offers idempotent producers (the broker discards duplicates using producer ID and sequence numbers) and transactions that write output records and commit the input offset atomically, which gives exactly-once for read-process-write flows between Kafka topics (since 2017). For side effects outside Kafka, such as a database write or an email, consumers must be idempotent, for example by recording the message ID or using an upsert by key.
Broker-tracked systems work differently. In Google Cloud Pub/Sub, Amazon SNS with SQS and most classic brokers, each subscription has its own queue of messages on the broker. When a message is published to the topic, the service copies a reference into each subscription's backlog. A subscriber receives a message with an acknowledgement deadline (for Google Pub/Sub, 10 seconds by default, extendable); if it does not ack in time, the broker redelivers it. Because the broker tracks every message per subscription, it can retry individual failed messages, route to a dead-letter topic after N attempts, and scale out workers without partitions. The cost is that ordering is weak unless you ask for it (ordering keys), replay is limited to the retention window, and per-message state limits throughput compared with a log. Lightweight systems such as Redis pub/sub and MQTT brokers keep no history: if a subscriber is offline, it misses the message, unless a persistent session or QoS level is used.
Routing models vary. Topic-based pub/sub, the common model, uses names (MQTT adds wildcards, so "sensors/+/temperature" matches every device). Content-based pub/sub lets subscribers filter on message attributes (Pub/Sub filters, SNS filter policies), so a subscriber sees only "order-placed where country = DE". Filtering at the broker saves network and consumer work but costs broker CPU. Schemas matter because producers and many consumers must agree on message formats: a schema registry with Avro, Protobuf or JSON Schema enforces compatibility rules (for example, only add optional fields) so that a producer change can not break subscribers.
Backpressure and retention close the design. A slow subscriber does not slow the producer: in a log, the slow group simply falls behind (consumer lag grows) while data stays on disk until retention (7 days by default in Kafka) deletes it; if lag exceeds retention, the group loses data. In broker-queue systems, the backlog of the slow subscription grows until its retention limit. Lag, the difference between the latest offset and the group's committed offset, is the main health metric of a pub/sub system.

example: Design pub/sub for an online store. The "orders" topic has 6 partitions, keyed by order ID, so all events of one order (placed, paid, shipped) stay in order. Throughput is 12,000 messages per second at 1 KB each, which is 12 MB/s of input. Three groups read it: billing (3 consumers), analytics (6 consumers) and search indexing (2 consumers).
Storage: 12 MB/s × 86,400 s = about 1.04 TB per day. With 7 days of retention and replication factor 3, the cluster stores 1.04 × 7 × 3 = about 21.8 TB. The log is written once (and replicated), and read three times by three groups, so read traffic is about 36 MB/s, three times the input. Adding a fourth group adds 12 MB/s of reads and no extra storage.
Capacity of a group: suppose billing processes 1,500 messages per second per consumer. With 3 consumers it handles 4,500 per second, but 12,000 arrive. The deficit is 7,500 messages per second, so lag grows by 7,500 × 60 = 450,000 messages every minute, 4.5 million after 10 minutes. To keep up it needs at least 12,000 / 1,500 = 8 consumers. But the topic has only 6 partitions, so the group can not use more than 6 consumers: 6 × 1,500 = 9,000 per second, still short. The fix is to increase the number of partitions to at least 8 (say 12, which leaves headroom for peaks) and then run 8 or more consumers. Note the catch: adding partitions changes hash(key) % N, so the same key may map to a new partition and the order across the change is not preserved; for strict per-key order, plan the partition count up front.
Failure and delivery: a billing consumer reads a batch of 100 messages, charges 100 customers, and crashes before committing. After the rebalance, another consumer reads from the last committed offset and sees the same 100 messages. With at-least-once delivery, some customers would be charged twice unless the charge is idempotent. The consumer therefore sends each charge with the order ID as an idempotency key, or records processed order IDs in the same database transaction as the charge, so the duplicate is detected and skipped. If instead the consumer committed before charging and crashed, those customers would never be charged: at-most-once. Billing almost always chooses at-least-once with idempotent processing.
Finally, a new subscriber. The fraud team wants to read the last 3 days of orders to train a model, then follow live. In a log-based system they create a new group, set its starting offset to a timestamp three days ago, and read. In a classic broker without history, they could only receive messages published after they subscribed, and would have to ask another system for the past.
intuition: A radio station: the broadcaster does not know who is listening, new listeners can tune in at any time, and with a recording (the log) they can also rewind and hear what they missed.
The recording also changes who is responsible for remembering: the station keeps one copy of the tape, and each listener keeps only a bookmark.
The design is natural because information has many readers and few writers, and readers differ in speed and interest. If the writer had to know its readers, every new reader would change the writer. If instead the writer puts facts into a shared, ordered record and each reader remembers its own place, the writer's cost is constant and readers are free to be fast, slow, late or repeated. Splitting the record into partitions gives parallelism; keeping order only within a partition is the compromise that makes this scale.
mistakes: People often think pub/sub and message queues are the same. In fact a queue gives each message to one worker, to divide the work, while pub/sub gives each message to every subscription, to share the information. The two combine: in Kafka, the consumers inside one group divide work like a queue, and different groups each get all messages.
People often think a message is delivered exactly once. In fact most systems offer at-least-once delivery, because after a failure the only safe choice is to send again. Exactly-once is achievable inside one system with transactions and idempotent writes, but side effects outside it need idempotent consumers.
People often think ordering is guaranteed on a topic. In fact order is kept only within a partition (or an ordering key). Messages for different keys can arrive in any relative order, and a consumer that handles partitions in parallel sees an interleaving. If you need order for an entity, use that entity's ID as the key; if you need a global order, you accept a single partition and its throughput limit.
People often think more consumers always means more throughput. In fact within a group the useful number of consumers is at most the number of partitions. Beyond that, extra consumers sit idle. Also, a slow consumer does not slow others, but one stuck message ("poison message") can block a partition, so you need a dead-letter path.
friction: Fan-out multiplies load and cost: every extra subscriber adds read traffic, network and, in broker-queue systems, storage. Slow subscribers fall behind and must be watched through lag; if lag grows past retention, data is lost to them. Retention itself is a trade between cost and the ability to replay and recover.
Ordering and parallelism pull against each other. Per-key order requires a stable key-to-partition mapping, but hot keys (one very large customer) overload one partition, and changing the partition count breaks the mapping. Rebalances pause consumption: in a large group, a rebalance can stop reading for seconds to minutes, and frequent rebalances (caused by slow processing exceeding the poll timeout or by rolling deployments) create lag spikes. Cooperative rebalancing and static membership reduce this.
Data contracts are hard. With many unknown subscribers, a producer can not know what a field change breaks, so schema compatibility rules, versioned topics and a catalogue of who consumes what are needed. Debugging is difficult because the flow is asynchronous: you must trace a message across producers, brokers and consumers with correlation IDs. Security and privacy are harder, too: access control per topic, encryption, and the right to delete personal data in an append-only log that is replicated and copied by many subscribers (compaction and crypto-shredding are partial answers). Finally, duplicates, retries and out-of-order events mean every consumer needs idempotent, order-tolerant logic.
practice: Decide early whether subscribers need history. If new subscribers must read the past, or you want replay for recovery and backfills, use a log-based system (Kafka, Pulsar, Redpanda) with retention set to match the longest recovery or backfill you need. If only live delivery matters, or you want simple per-message handling with retries and dead-letter queues, a broker-based service (SNS with SQS, Google Pub/Sub, RabbitMQ) is easier to operate. For devices and mobile clients, MQTT.
Name topics after business events ("order-placed"), not after consumers, so a new subscriber does not require any change in the producer. Choose message keys deliberately (the entity whose order matters), and choose the partition count with growth in mind: it should exceed the parallelism you will need in a year, because it is hard to change. Define schemas in a registry from the start and enforce compatibility. Put a small, stable envelope on every message: event ID, event time, type, schema version and a trace ID.
Make consumers idempotent and tolerant of reordering across keys: use the event ID or an upsert by key; commit offsets only after the work is done and durable; set the poll timeout above the worst-case processing time; send poison messages to a dead-letter topic after a few attempts, and alert on it. Monitor consumer lag per group and partition (and time lag, not only message count), broker disk and network, under-replicated partitions, and rebalance frequency. Keep a catalogue of topics with owner, schema, retention and subscribers, and review it, since unknown consumers are the main risk when you change a topic.
tradeoffs: Choose log-based pub/sub (Kafka, Pulsar) when you need high throughput (hundreds of MB/s), replay, many independent consumers and per-key ordering; choose a broker-queue service (SNS plus SQS, Google Pub/Sub, RabbitMQ) when you need per-message acknowledgement, retries, delays, dead-letter handling and elastic workers without thinking about partitions, at moderate volume. You give up replay and strong ordering with the second; you give up per-message flexibility with the first.
Choose many partitions when you need high parallelism and expect growth (partitions are the unit of scaling, commonly 3 to 10 times the current consumer count); choose few partitions when you need strict global ordering, small topics or low broker overhead. Every partition costs file handles, replication traffic and recovery time (clusters with hundreds of thousands of partitions are hard to run), and the count is hard to reduce later.
Choose at-least-once with idempotent consumers as the default, since it is simple and robust; choose exactly-once transactions only for read-process-write pipelines inside Kafka where the cost (extra latency, complexity) is worth the guarantee; choose at-most-once only for data where loss is cheap, such as metrics and telemetry.
Choose a long retention (days or weeks) when replay, late subscribers and incident recovery matter and storage is cheap, often with tiered storage to object storage; choose short retention for sensitive or high-volume data where cost or privacy dominates. Choose a managed service when you do not want to operate brokers, and accept cost per GB and limits; choose self-managed when you need control over performance, cost at large scale, or data location.
interview: How is pub/sub different from a message queue, and how does Kafka offer both? || A queue delivers each message to one of several competing workers; pub/sub delivers each message to every subscription. In Kafka a topic is a partitioned log and each consumer group has its own offsets, so groups are independent subscriptions (pub/sub), while members of one group split the partitions (queue-like work sharing).
Walk through what happens when a consumer in a group crashes. || Its heartbeats stop; after the session timeout the coordinator removes it and triggers a rebalance; its partitions are assigned to other members; they resume from the last committed offsets of the group, so messages processed but not committed are processed again (at-least-once). Consumption pauses during the rebalance unless cooperative rebalancing is used.
How do you get ordering in a pub/sub system, and what does it cost? || Use the entity ID as the message key so that all its events go to one partition (or ordering key); order is then kept per key. The cost: parallelism is bounded by partition count, hot keys overload a partition, and changing partitions breaks the key mapping. Global order requires one partition and its throughput limit.
Your consumer group's lag is growing. How do you diagnose and fix it? || Compare producer rate with group throughput; check per-partition lag for hot partitions or a stuck poison message, slow downstream calls, GC pauses or frequent rebalances. Fix by scaling consumers up to the partition count, adding partitions, batching and parallelising processing inside a consumer, removing the slow dependency or sending bad messages to a dead-letter topic. Check lag against retention.
How would you achieve exactly-once effects from a topic to a database? || Treat delivery as at-least-once and make the write idempotent: store the message ID or offset with the data in the same transaction, or upsert by a natural key. With Kafka to Kafka, use idempotent producers and transactions that commit output and input offsets atomically. A pure exactly-once delivery across systems is not possible.
How do you evolve a message schema when you do not know all the consumers? || Use a schema registry with compatibility rules (backward and forward compatible changes: add optional fields, never reuse or retype fields), version topics for breaking changes and run both during migration, keep a catalogue of consumers, and use consumer-driven contract tests. Announce deprecation dates.
quiz: What decides whether a pub/sub consumer gets at-most-once or at-least-once delivery? || The order of processing and commit. Committing the offset (or acking) before processing risks loss on a crash (at-most-once); processing first and committing after risks reprocessing (at-least-once).
Why does Kafka keep order only within a partition? || Each partition is a single ordered log appended by one leader; different partitions are written and read in parallel with no coordination between them, so no global order exists across partitions. This is what allows throughput to scale.
Why can a consumer group not use more consumers than partitions? || Each partition is assigned to exactly one consumer in the group at a time to keep order and avoid duplicates, so extra consumers get no partition and stay idle.
A topic gets 5,000 messages/s of 2 KB; three groups read it; retention is 3 days, replication factor 3. How much disk is needed? || Input is 10 MB/s, which is 864 GB per day, so 3 days is about 2.6 TB, and with replication factor 3 about 7.8 TB. The three groups add read traffic (30 MB/s) but no extra storage.
When would you choose Google Pub/Sub or SNS plus SQS instead of Kafka? || When you want fully managed, per-message acknowledgement, retries and dead-letter queues, elastic workers without partition planning, and do not need replay or very high throughput or strict per-key ordering.
