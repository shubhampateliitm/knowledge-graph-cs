@@ privacytech
value: Privacy technologies let an organization learn useful facts from data about people, or share such facts with a partner, without exposing any one person. The two most important families are differential privacy, a mathematical guarantee that a published result barely depends on any individual, and data clean rooms, controlled environments where two parties can join their data but only take out approved aggregate results. Around them sit k-anonymity and aggregation thresholds, secure multi-party computation (for example private set intersection), and trusted execution environments that keep data encrypted even from the machine's operator.
In advertising and media measurement this is now core infrastructure, not an extra. Third-party cookies and mobile advertising IDs became unreliable, privacy laws tightened (GDPR in Europe from 2018, CCPA/CPRA in California, India's DPDP Act of 2023 with its rules notified in November 2025 and phased in over 12 to 18 months), and advertisers still want to know "did people who saw my CTV ad buy my product?". Clean rooms answer that by joining the advertiser's purchase data with the publisher's exposure data under rules both sides accept. Differential privacy answers a different need: publishing statistics (the US 2020 Census, Apple and Google telemetry, LinkedIn and Microsoft analytics) with a provable bound on what anyone can learn about a single person.
The layers below supply the guarantees. Probability (L4) gives the noise distributions and the exact definition of privacy loss. Statistics (L4) explains why noise that hides one person costs almost nothing when you count thousands of people. Cryptography, including zero-knowledge proofs and secure computation (L3), lets parties compute on data they never see in plain form.
terms: Differential privacy (DP) — a property of an algorithm: its output distribution changes by at most a factor e^ε when any one person's data is added or removed.
ε (epsilon) — the privacy-loss parameter; smaller ε means stronger privacy and more noise. Values from about 0.1 to 10 are used in practice.
Sensitivity — the most one person can change a query's true answer; a count has sensitivity 1.
Laplace mechanism — adds noise from a Laplace distribution with scale sensitivity/ε to a numeric answer.
Privacy budget — the total ε a dataset may spend; every released answer uses part of it, and costs add up.
Data clean room — a controlled environment where parties combine data and can only export queries and results that pass agreed rules.
Aggregation threshold — a rule that a result row must cover at least k people (for example 50) before it may leave the clean room.
Private set intersection (PSI) — a cryptographic protocol that tells two parties which identifiers they share without revealing the others.
history: Statistical agencies worried about disclosure for decades and used ad hoc methods: suppressing small cells, swapping records, rounding. Researchers kept showing that these were weak. Latanya Sweeney found in 2000 that ZIP code, gender and date of birth alone uniquely identify roughly 87% of Americans, and she re-identified the medical record of the Governor of Massachusetts from "anonymized" hospital data. Her k-anonymity proposal (2002) required every record to look like at least k − 1 others. In 2008 Narayanan and Shmatikov re-identified Netflix Prize users by matching their movie ratings with public IMDb reviews. The lesson: removing names is not anonymization, because the remaining attributes are a fingerprint.
Cynthia Dwork, Frank McSherry, Kobbi Nissim and Adam Smith introduced differential privacy in 2006 ("Calibrating Noise to Sensitivity in Private Data Analysis"). Its key move was to define privacy as a property of the algorithm, not of the data, so it holds against any attacker with any side information. Deployment followed slowly: Google's RAPPOR for Chrome telemetry (2014), Apple's local differential privacy in iOS 10 (2016), and the US Census Bureau's 2020 Census, which used a differentially private algorithm (TopDown) with a total privacy-loss budget of ε = 17.14 for person data and 2.47 for housing units, set in June 2021.
Clean rooms grew from the advertising side. Google's Ads Data Hub (2017) let advertisers join their data with Google's event data under a 50-user minimum per result row. Amazon Marketing Cloud, Disney's clean room, InfoSum, Habu (bought by LiveRamp in 2024), AWS Clean Rooms (generally available in 2023, with a differential privacy option added later) and Snowflake's clean rooms (after it bought Samooha in late 2023) made the pattern common across retail media and CTV. Google's Privacy Sandbox tried to put similar ideas (noisy aggregate attribution reports) inside the browser, but in October 2025 Google announced it would retire most of those APIs.
how: Differential privacy starts from a definition. Take any two datasets D and D′ that differ in one person's records. A randomized algorithm M is ε-differentially private if, for every possible set of outputs S, Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε × Pr[M(D′) ∈ S]. In words: whatever the algorithm outputs, that output was almost as likely without you. With ε = 1, e^ε ≈ 2.72, so no output can become more than 2.72 times more likely because of your data. A common relaxation, (ε, δ)-DP, also allows a tiny probability δ (for example 10⁻⁹) of exceeding that bound.
The simplest way to achieve it is the Laplace mechanism. Compute the true answer f(D), then add noise drawn from a Laplace distribution centred at 0 with scale b = Δf / ε, where Δf is the sensitivity. For a count, one person changes the answer by at most 1, so Δf = 1 and with ε = 1 the noise has scale 1 and a standard deviation of √2 × b ≈ 1.4. For a sum, you must first bound each person's contribution (clip spend per person at, say, 500 rupees), because otherwise one very large value could reveal itself; the sensitivity is then that cap. The Gaussian mechanism, used for (ε, δ)-DP, adds normal noise instead and composes more gracefully over many queries.
Privacy loss adds up. Under basic composition, answering k queries with budgets ε₁ … ε_k gives total privacy loss ε₁ + … + ε_k, which is why systems keep a privacy budget per dataset and refuse queries when it runs out. Tighter accounting methods (advanced composition, Rényi DP, zero-concentrated DP) show that the real loss grows closer to the square root of the number of queries for Gaussian noise, which matters for machine learning. DP-SGD (Abadi and others, 2016) trains models with differential privacy by clipping each example's gradient and adding Gaussian noise to every batch. Post-processing is free: anything computed from a DP output (rounding, plotting, training on it) keeps the same guarantee, so you add noise once, early, and then use the result freely.
Here statistics does the useful work. The noise has a fixed size, roughly 1/ε, independent of how many people are counted. A count of 50,000 with noise of ±1.4 is accurate to 0.003%; a count of 12 with the same noise is off by about 12%. Differential privacy is therefore cheap for large aggregates and expensive for small groups and rare events, which is exactly the trade you want: populations are visible, individuals are not.
Clean rooms work at a different level: they control what queries may run and what leaves. Each party loads data into an environment run by a neutral provider or one of the parties' cloud accounts. Identifiers are matched, usually after normalizing and hashing emails or phone numbers with SHA-256, or through a shared identity provider's IDs; stronger setups use private set intersection so neither side learns the other's non-matching IDs. Analysts may run only approved query templates or queries that pass analysis rules: no row-level output, aggregation thresholds such as at least 50 or 100 users per row, limits on joins and columns, and sometimes differential privacy noise on outputs with a tracked budget. Results go out as aggregates; raw data never leaves.
The strongest versions add cryptography and hardware. Secure multi-party computation lets parties compute a joint result while each holds secret shares, so no single machine sees the inputs. Trusted execution environments (Intel SGX and TDX, AMD SEV-SNP, NVIDIA confidential computing GPUs) run code in hardware-isolated memory and can prove to a remote party, through attestation, exactly which code is running. Zero-knowledge proofs (L3) let one side prove a statement about its data, such as "this user is over 18", without revealing the data. These tools reduce how much each party must trust the operator, at a cost in speed and complexity.

example: An advertiser wants to know whether households that saw its CTV campaign bought more. The publisher has 48,200 exposed households and 51,800 unexposed households in a holdout. The clean room matches them against the advertiser's purchase records by hashed email.
True results: 1,206 exposed households bought (2.502%) and 1,036 unexposed households bought (2.000%). Lift = 2.502 / 2.000 − 1 ≈ 25%.
Now add differential privacy with ε = 0.5 per count and the Laplace mechanism. Sensitivity is 1, so noise scale b = 1 / 0.5 = 2 and the standard deviation is 2√2 ≈ 2.8. Suppose the released counts are 1,208 and 1,033. The released rates are 2.506% and 1.994%, and the lift is 25.7%. The noise moved the lift by less than one percentage point, far smaller than the sampling uncertainty already in the data (a 95% interval for this lift is roughly ±8 percentage points). Privacy cost: two counts at ε = 0.5 each, so ε = 1.0 spent from the budget.
Now the analyst breaks results down by city and finds a segment, "exposed households in a small town", with a true count of 9 buyers. With the same noise (±2.8), the answer is unreliable, and by itself the number could help someone guess about specific households. This is where the aggregation threshold acts: if the segment contains fewer than 50 households, the row is suppressed whatever the noise says. Finally, budget accounting: if the room allows a total ε of 10 per campaign, the analyst can afford about 20 counts of this kind. Spending it on many tiny segments is the classic mistake; spending it on a few well-designed queries gives clean, trustworthy answers.
intuition: Report what the crowd said with a little deliberate blur, so no one can pick out a single voice, and only let people ask about crowds, never about one seat.
Both halves match how we already think about privacy. A survey that says "about 2.5% of viewers bought" feels harmless; a report that says "the household at this address bought" does not. Differential privacy turns that feeling into a precise rule: your participation must not noticeably change any answer. Because noise of a fixed size disappears inside large numbers and dominates small ones, the method automatically protects exactly the cases where privacy is at risk. Clean rooms apply the same principle to process: if raw rows can never leave and every output must describe a crowd, most of the risk is removed before anyone writes a query.
mistakes: People often think that hashing emails or removing names makes data anonymous. In fact a hashed email is a stable identifier: anyone with the same email can compute the same hash and match it. Laws such as GDPR treat hashed identifiers as personal data (pseudonymous, not anonymous). Linkage attacks on "anonymized" data, like the Netflix Prize case, show that remaining attributes are often enough.
People often think differential privacy means "the data is encrypted" or "the data is safe". In fact it is a property of what is released, not of how the data is stored. The raw data inside the system still needs ordinary security. And a large ε (say 20 or more per query) gives a guarantee too weak to mean much.
People often think a clean room guarantees privacy by itself. In fact guarantees vary widely. A room that allows arbitrary SQL with only a minimum-count rule can be attacked with differencing queries: ask for a count over a group of 100, then over the same group minus one person, and subtract. Real protection needs query restrictions, overlap checks, noise or all three.
People often think that adding noise once is enough forever. In fact privacy loss accumulates with every query on the same data. Without a budget, repeated noisy answers can be averaged until the noise disappears.
friction: Accuracy is the first cost. Small segments, rare events and long-tail breakdowns become noisy or are suppressed, which frustrates analysts used to slicing data freely. Choosing ε is a policy decision with no universal answer, and reported budgets in industry range from below 1 to well above 10, which makes guarantees hard to compare.
Clean rooms are hard to operate across companies. Identity matching rates are often low (30–60% is common when matching by hashed email), every partner uses a different platform, and many rooms require both sides to be customers of the same cloud or vendor. Query restrictions block exploratory analysis, and approved templates must be negotiated. Cryptographic options such as MPC and PSI add latency and cost, and TEEs depend on hardware vendors' security, which has had real vulnerabilities.
Regulation is still moving. India's DPDP rules are being phased in, US state laws differ, and regulators have not agreed on when aggregated or noisy outputs stop being personal data. Finally, privacy technology protects individuals inside a dataset; it does not decide whether a use is fair or expected by the people involved.
practice: Start by deciding what must be protected and from whom: a partner, the public, your own analysts? That decides the tool: aggregation thresholds and query templates for partner sharing, differential privacy for public releases or broad internal dashboards, PSI or TEEs when even the matching step must be hidden.
For clean rooms, define a small set of high-value queries (reach, frequency, conversion lift, overlap) with fixed templates; set minimum group sizes (50 or more is common); block row-level output; and check for differencing by limiting overlapping queries. Measure match rates per partner and per identifier type, because a 35% match rate changes how you read every result.
For differential privacy, use a maintained library rather than writing noise code yourself: OpenDP, Google's differential-privacy libraries, Tumult Analytics, or the DP features built into AWS Clean Rooms. Clip contributions per person before summing, account for the budget per dataset and per time period, and publish fewer, better aggregates. For machine learning on sensitive data, consider DP-SGD (supported by Opacus for PyTorch) and measure the accuracy cost on your own task.
Keep a record of every release and its ε. Treat hashed identifiers as personal data in retention and deletion processes, including requests under deletion laws.

@@ mtls
value: Mutual TLS (mTLS) is TLS in which both sides prove who they are with certificates. In ordinary HTTPS only the server shows a certificate, so the browser knows it reached the real bank, but the bank learns nothing about the client from TLS and relies on a password or token instead. In mTLS the client also presents a certificate and proves it holds the matching private key. The result is a connection where both ends are authenticated and all traffic is encrypted, before any application byte is exchanged.
This is the foundation of zero-trust networking for services. The old model trusted anything inside the company network ("the castle and moat"); one compromised machine could then talk to everything. The zero-trust model, popularized by Google's BeyondCorp papers (2014 onwards) and written down by NIST in SP 800-207 (2020), says that network location grants nothing: every connection must be authenticated and authorized. Service meshes such as Istio and Linkerd, SPIFFE/SPIRE identity systems, and certificate authorities such as Vault give every workload a short-lived certificate so that every service-to-service call is mutually authenticated by default.
It rests on two lower concepts. TLS (L3) provides the handshake, key exchange and encryption; mTLS changes only one step of it. Digital signatures and PKI (L3) provide certificates: a trusted authority signs a statement binding a public key to an identity, and the private key proves possession by signing the handshake.
terms: Certificate (X.509) — a signed document binding a public key to an identity, with a validity period and the issuer's signature.
Certificate authority (CA) — the issuer whose signature makes a certificate trusted; a mesh usually runs its own private CA.
Private key — the secret half of a key pair; whoever holds it can sign as the certificate's identity.
CertificateRequest — the TLS message in which the server asks the client to authenticate with a certificate.
CertificateVerify — the client's signature over the handshake transcript, proving it holds the private key right now.
SPIFFE ID — a standard workload identity URI such as spiffe://prod.example.com/ns/ads/sa/bidder.
SVID — a SPIFFE Verifiable Identity Document, usually an X.509 certificate carrying the SPIFFE ID.
Attestation — checking facts about a workload (its node, Kubernetes service account, image) before giving it an identity.
history: SSL came from Netscape in the mid-1990s and became TLS 1.0 in 1999 (RFC 2246). Client certificates were part of the design from the start, and banks, governments and some enterprises used them for users, often on smart cards. They were painful for people: certificates had to be installed in browsers and renewed by hand, so the web settled on passwords and later tokens for users, with certificates only on servers.
Machines were a better fit. As companies moved to microservices and the cloud, the number of internal connections exploded and network perimeters stopped meaning much. Google described BeyondCorp in 2014, moving trust from the network to device and user identity. Inside Google, ALTS (Application Layer Transport Security) authenticated and encrypted RPCs between services. In the open-source world, Istio (2017) and Linkerd 2 (2018) made automatic mTLS between pods a standard feature of service meshes, and SPIFFE (2017, now a CNCF graduated project with its SPIRE implementation) standardized how a workload gets an identity and a certificate.
TLS 1.3 (RFC 8446, 2018) simplified the handshake to one round trip and encrypted the certificates themselves, so observers can no longer read client identities from the wire. More recently, sidecar-free designs such as Istio's ambient mode (generally available in late 2024) and eBPF-based meshes such as Cilium moved mTLS into node-level proxies, cutting the per-pod overhead.
how: A TLS 1.3 handshake with mutual authentication goes like this. The client sends ClientHello with its key share. The server replies with ServerHello and its key share; both sides now compute the same shared secret with elliptic-curve Diffie-Hellman, and everything after this point is encrypted. The server sends its Certificate, a CertificateVerify (a signature over the whole handshake so far, made with its private key) and, for mTLS, a CertificateRequest listing the CAs it accepts. The client checks the server's certificate chain against its trusted roots, checks the server's signature, then sends its own Certificate and CertificateVerify. The server validates the client chain and signature. Both send Finished messages, and application data flows. All of this costs one network round trip plus a few signature operations.
Validation is where digital signatures and PKI do the work. Each certificate in the chain is signed by the next one up, ending at a root the verifier already trusts. The verifier checks every signature, the validity dates, the allowed uses, and the identity inside the certificate, today usually a URI or DNS name in the Subject Alternative Name field. The CertificateVerify signature matters because a certificate is public: anyone can copy it. Only the holder of the private key can sign this particular handshake transcript, which includes fresh random values, so a recorded handshake cannot be replayed.
In a service mesh, nobody installs certificates by hand. Each workload proves what it is to the identity system, a step called attestation: for example, the node agent checks with the Kubernetes API which service account a pod runs as. The workload then generates a key pair locally and receives a short-lived certificate, valid for hours rather than years. SPIRE's default lifetime for X.509 SVIDs is one hour; Istio's default workload certificates last 24 hours and are rotated well before they expire. Short lifetimes replace revocation: instead of maintaining lists of revoked certificates, which clients often fail to check, a stolen key simply stops working soon.
Authentication then feeds authorization. Once the server knows "the caller is spiffe://prod/ns/ads/sa/bidder", a policy can say that only the bidder may call the budget service, and only on certain paths. In a mesh, the sidecar or node proxy terminates mTLS, checks the policy and forwards the request to the application over localhost, so the application code does not change. In other designs the application's own TLS library does the handshake, which avoids an extra proxy hop but requires every service to load and rotate certificates correctly.
Performance is usually acceptable. The handshake costs one round trip and a few signatures, typically tens of microseconds each for ECDSA P-256 keys, much less than RSA-2048 signatures. Bulk encryption with AES-GCM runs at several gigabytes per second per core on CPUs with AES instructions. Connection reuse and session resumption mean the handshake cost is paid rarely. The real costs are in operations: issuing, rotating and trusting certificates across clusters, and debugging failed handshakes.
Where the handshake happens has changed over time. In the classic sidecar design, every pod runs an Envoy proxy; the application talks plain HTTP to its own sidecar over localhost, the two sidecars do mTLS across the network, and each sidecar holds the pod's certificate. This works without code changes but adds two proxy hops per call and a proxy's memory to every pod. In Istio's ambient mode, a per-node proxy called ztunnel holds the certificates of all pods on that node and carries their traffic inside mTLS tunnels (HTTP CONNECT over mTLS, which Istio calls HBONE); full layer-7 features such as HTTP routing are added only where needed by separate "waypoint" proxies. eBPF-based meshes intercept traffic in the kernel and hand it to a node proxy in a similar way. The trust model is the same in all three: a private CA, short-lived workload certificates, and identity-based policy. What changes is how many proxies sit on the path and who holds the keys.
Trust also has boundaries. A trust domain is the set of workloads whose certificates chain to one root, usually one mesh or one company. To let two trust domains call each other, for example two clusters run by different teams or a partner's platform, each side imports the other's trust bundle (its root certificates) and writes policies that name the foreign identities explicitly. SPIFFE standardizes this federation, so a service in one domain can verify spiffe://partner.example.org/... identities without sharing a CA. Without such explicit federation, the safe default is that a certificate from another domain is rejected, even if it is otherwise valid.

example: Consider an ad platform with two services in Kubernetes: a bidder that must call a budget service before each bid. The mesh's CA root is distributed to every proxy. The bidder pod starts; its node agent attests that it runs as service account "bidder" in namespace "ads", and the pod receives a certificate with SAN spiffe://prod.example.com/ns/ads/sa/bidder, valid for 24 hours.
The bidder opens a connection to the budget service. The handshake adds one round trip: inside one data centre zone, roughly 0.2 to 0.5 ms. Each side performs one ECDSA signature and verifies two (the peer's certificate chain and its CertificateVerify), roughly 0.1 ms of CPU per side in total. The bidder then sends thousands of requests over this one connection, so the handshake cost per request is negligible: at 5,000 requests per second over 50 long-lived connections, handshakes happen only when connections are recycled.
The budget service's policy says: allow spiffe://prod.example.com/ns/ads/sa/bidder to POST /reserve; deny everything else. An attacker who compromises a reporting pod in another namespace tries to call /reserve. The connection completes TLS, because the reporting pod also has a valid mesh certificate, but its identity is spiffe://prod.example.com/ns/reports/sa/etl, so the policy denies the request with a 403. The network path existed, yet trust did not.
Now the attacker steals the bidder's private key from a memory dump. With a 24-hour certificate, the stolen identity works for at most a day, and rotation every 12 hours, plus alerting on certificates used from unexpected nodes, shrinks the window further. With a two-year certificate the same theft would have lasted until someone noticed and revoked it, and many clients never check revocation.
intuition: Everyone in the building shows a badge to everyone they talk to, and badges expire every day, so a lost badge is useless by tomorrow.
The idea is natural once you stop believing in a safe inside. People already verify both sides in important human exchanges: a bank checks your ID and you check that the branch is real. mTLS gives machines the same symmetric check, built into the connection itself, so it cannot be skipped by a forgetful developer. Short-lived certificates match how we treat visitor passes rather than passports: cheap to issue, automatically renewed, and harmless when lost.
mistakes: People often think mTLS means "encrypted traffic". Ordinary TLS already encrypts. The "mutual" part adds client authentication, and its value depends entirely on what the server does with the client's identity. Accepting any certificate from the mesh CA, without authorization policies, encrypts traffic but still lets every service call every other service.
People often think a certificate proves identity on its own. A certificate is public and can be copied freely. Identity is proven by the CertificateVerify signature, made with the private key, so key protection and short lifetimes matter more than the certificate file.
People often think mTLS replaces application authentication with tokens. The two answer different questions. mTLS says which workload is calling; tokens such as OAuth access tokens say which end user or which permission the request carries. Systems handling user data usually need both: mTLS between services and a token for the user on whose behalf the call is made.
People often think the performance cost is the main obstacle. Measured handshake and encryption costs are small with modern keys and connection reuse. The hard parts are certificate distribution, rotation, trust across clusters and clouds, and diagnosing handshake failures.
friction: Operating a private PKI is real work. You must protect the root and intermediate keys, rotate them, distribute trust bundles to every workload, federate trust between clusters or companies, and handle clock skew, because certificate validity depends on correct time. When something breaks, the error is often a terse "handshake failure" with little detail about which check failed.
Identity is only as good as attestation. If any pod that can claim a service account can get its certificate, then a compromised Kubernetes API or a misconfigured namespace leaks identities. Legacy services that cannot do TLS, protocols that do not run over TCP, and connections leaving the mesh (to managed databases or third-party APIs) need special handling. Proxies add latency, memory and another component that can fail; sidecar-free and eBPF-based designs reduce this but are newer. Finally, quantum computers would break today's elliptic-curve key exchange and signatures; hybrid post-quantum key exchange (such as X25519 combined with ML-KEM) is already deployed in browsers and is coming to service meshes, while post-quantum certificates are still being standardized and rolled out.
practice: Turn on mTLS everywhere in a strict mode once services are ready, not in a permanent "permissive" mode that also accepts plaintext. Write authorization policies by identity from the start; default-deny between namespaces is a good baseline. Use short certificate lifetimes (hours to a day) with automatic rotation, and alert on rotation failures, because an expired certificate takes a service down.
Prefer standard identities (SPIFFE IDs) so the same identity works across meshes, clusters and clouds. Keep the root CA offline or in an HSM or managed CA service, and issue from intermediates. Monitor handshake failures and certificate expiry as first-class metrics.
For calls on behalf of users, propagate a user token alongside the mTLS identity and check both. When integrating with partners over the internet, mTLS is a strong option for server-to-server APIs (for example data exchange or reporting endpoints), but agree on certificate formats, rotation schedules and how to report compromises before going live.
quiz: In mTLS, which message proves that the client actually holds the private key for its certificate? || CertificateVerify: a signature over the handshake transcript made with the client's private key. The certificate itself is public and proves nothing alone.
Why do service meshes use certificates valid for hours instead of years? || A short lifetime limits how long a stolen key is useful and replaces unreliable revocation checks. Automatic rotation makes the short lifetime cheap.
A service makes 2,000 requests per second over 20 connections that are recycled every 10 minutes. How many mTLS handshakes per second does it perform? || 20 handshakes every 600 seconds, about 0.03 per second. The handshake cost is negligible compared with the request rate.
Your mesh enables mTLS but has no authorization policies. What does an attacker inside one compromised pod gain or lose? || Traffic is encrypted and the attacker's identity is known, but the attacker can still call every service, because nothing checks whether that identity is allowed. mTLS without policy gives authentication but not authorization.
When would you terminate mTLS in the application instead of in a sidecar or node proxy? || When the extra proxy hop's latency or memory matters, or the service is outside the mesh, and the team can manage certificate loading and rotation in the application's TLS library. Otherwise the proxy keeps application code simple.

@@ oauth
value: OAuth 2.0 and OpenID Connect (OIDC) solve two related problems. OAuth answers "may this application act on my behalf, for this limited purpose?" without giving the application your password. OIDC, built on top of OAuth, answers "who is this user?" so applications can offer "Sign in with Google" or company single sign-on. Both work by issuing tokens: short-lived, signed or opaque strings that a service can check on every request.
Almost every modern login and API uses them. Company SSO (Okta, Microsoft Entra ID, Google Workspace), cloud APIs, mobile apps, partner integrations in advertising (pulling reports from a DSP, pushing audiences to a platform) and now AI agents calling tools through the Model Context Protocol all use OAuth flows and bearer tokens. The design separates three jobs that used to be mixed: proving identity (done once, at the identity provider), granting permission (scopes and consent), and checking each request (verifying a token, cheaply, at every service).
The lower layers make this safe. Digital signatures (L3) let any service verify a JSON Web Token without calling the issuer, using the issuer's public key. Cryptographic hash functions (L3) power PKCE, which proves that the app finishing a login is the same app that started it. Web security (L3) shapes the flows: redirects, origins, CSRF protection and cookie rules decide where tokens may travel.
terms: Resource owner — the user whose data or account is being accessed.
Client — the application asking for access (a web app, mobile app, CLI or agent).
Authorization server — the service that authenticates the user and issues tokens (also called the identity provider in OIDC).
Access token — a short-lived credential sent with API calls, usually as "Authorization: Bearer <token>".
Refresh token — a longer-lived credential the client uses to get new access tokens without asking the user again.
Scope — a named permission requested by the client, such as "reports:read".
ID token — an OIDC token, always a signed JWT, stating who the user is and how and when they logged in.
JWT — JSON Web Token: base64url-encoded header, claims and signature joined by dots.
PKCE — Proof Key for Code Exchange: the client sends a hash of a secret at the start and the secret itself at the end.
history: Before OAuth, applications asked for your password to another site. Early social apps asked for your email password to import contacts, which gave them full, permanent access. In 2006–2007 developers from Twitter, Ma.gnolia and Google designed OAuth 1.0 to replace this "password anti-pattern" with delegated, revocable tokens. OAuth 1.0 required clients to sign every request with a shared secret, which was secure but hard to implement correctly.
OAuth 2.0 (RFC 6749, with bearer tokens in RFC 6750, both in 2012) simplified this by relying on TLS and bearer tokens, and defined several "grant types" for different clients. The simplification made adoption explode, but the flexibility caused many security bugs. OpenID Connect 1.0 (2014) added a standard identity layer with ID tokens, replacing older federated login protocols such as OpenID 2.0, and became the common way to do SSO alongside the older XML-based SAML.
The years since have tightened the rules. PKCE (RFC 7636, 2015) was created for mobile apps and is now recommended for all clients. JWT was standardized as RFC 7519 (2015). The OAuth 2.0 Security Best Current Practice (RFC 9700, January 2025) formally discourages the implicit flow and the password grant, requires exact redirect URI matching, and recommends sender-constrained tokens using mTLS (RFC 8705) or DPoP (RFC 9449, 2023). OAuth 2.1, still an IETF draft, folds these lessons into one simpler specification. The Model Context Protocol's authorization specification builds on this modern profile, so AI agents obtain scoped tokens to call tools.
how: The main flow is the authorization code flow with PKCE. First, the client creates a random secret, the code verifier (43 to 128 characters), and computes code_challenge = BASE64URL(SHA-256(verifier)). It redirects the user's browser to the authorization server with its client_id, the requested scopes, a redirect_uri, a random state value and the code challenge. The user logs in there (password, passkey, multi-factor), sees what is requested and consents. The authorization server redirects the browser back to the redirect_uri with a short-lived authorization code (often valid for about a minute) and the same state.
Second, the client's backend sends the code, the code verifier and its own client credentials (if it has them) directly to the authorization server's token endpoint over TLS. The server hashes the verifier, compares it with the challenge stored at the start and, if they match, returns an access token, often a refresh token, and, for OIDC, an ID token. This is where hash functions add value: someone who steals the code from the browser's redirect cannot redeem it without the verifier, which never left the client. The state value, checked by the client, protects against CSRF: it ties the response to a request this client actually started, which is the web-security concept at work.
Third, the client calls APIs with "Authorization: Bearer <access token>". If the token is a JWT, each API verifies it locally. It decodes the header to find the key ID (kid), fetches the issuer's public keys from its JWKS endpoint (and caches them), and checks the signature. It then checks the claims: iss (the expected issuer), aud (this API), exp (not expired), and the scopes. This is the digital-signature concept at work: verification needs only the public key, so thousands of services can check tokens with no call to the issuer and no shared secret. If the token is opaque, the API asks the authorization server's introspection endpoint (RFC 7662), which gives instant revocation at the cost of a network call.
Tokens are deliberately short-lived. Access tokens commonly last 5 to 60 minutes, and refresh tokens last hours to months, often with rotation: each use returns a new refresh token and invalidates the old one, so a stolen refresh token used twice reveals the theft. Other flows cover other clients: client credentials for service-to-service calls with no user, the device authorization grant (RFC 8628) for TVs and CLIs where the user types a code on another device, and token exchange (RFC 8693) when one service calls another on the user's behalf.
Bearer tokens have a weakness: whoever holds one can use it. Sender-constrained tokens fix this. With mTLS-bound tokens, the authorization server records a hash of the client's certificate in the token, and APIs accept it only over a TLS connection using that certificate. With DPoP, the client signs a small proof for each request with its own key, and the token is bound to that key. A stolen token alone is then useless.
It helps to look inside a JWT access token. It has three parts separated by dots. The header, for example {"alg":"ES256","kid":"2026-10","typ":"at+jwt"}, says how it was signed and with which key. The claims say who issued it (iss), for which API (aud), about which user or client (sub, client_id), when it was issued and when it expires (iat, exp), with which scopes, and often a unique token ID (jti). The signature covers the first two parts. A typical token is 600 to 1,200 bytes, so it travels on every request; putting large permission lists inside tokens makes every call heavier. Keys rotate: the authorization server publishes several public keys in its JWKS, signs new tokens with the newest key, and keeps old keys published until all tokens signed with them have expired. Clients and APIs find all endpoints and keys from a discovery document at /.well-known/openid-configuration (or /.well-known/oauth-authorization-server), so configuration is mostly "trust this issuer URL".
Audience binding matters even more when many independent services accept tokens from the same issuer. The resource indicators extension (RFC 8707) lets the client say exactly which API a token is for, and the issuer puts that API in aud, so a token for one service cannot be replayed at another. Protected resource metadata (RFC 9728) lets an API tell clients which authorization server to use and which scopes it understands. The Model Context Protocol uses both: an MCP server advertises its authorization server, and an agent obtains a token bound to that one MCP server through the code flow with PKCE, so a malicious tool server cannot reuse a token it receives against a different service.

example: A reporting tool wants to pull campaign data from an ad platform's API on behalf of an analyst. The tool registers with redirect_uri https://reports.example.com/callback and asks for scope "campaigns:read".
1. The tool generates the verifier "dBjftJeZ4CVP-mB92K27uhbUJU1p1r_wW1gFWFOEjXk" (the example value from RFC 7636) and computes its SHA-256 hash in base64url, "E9Melhoa2OwvFrEMTJguCHaoeK1t8URWbuGJSstw-cM". It redirects the analyst to the platform's login page with that challenge and a random state value "x7Fq2".
2. The analyst logs in with a passkey and approves "read your campaigns". The platform redirects to https://reports.example.com/callback?code=SplxlOBe&state=x7Fq2. The tool checks that state matches the value it stored.
3. The tool's server posts the code and the verifier to the token endpoint. The platform hashes the verifier, finds it equals the stored challenge, and returns an access token valid for 900 seconds (15 minutes) and a refresh token valid for 30 days with rotation.
4. The tool calls GET /v2/campaigns with the bearer token. The API reads kid "2026-10" from the JWT header, finds that key in its cached JWKS, verifies the ES256 signature in roughly 0.1 ms, checks iss, that aud is "ads-api", that exp is in the future and that the scope includes campaigns:read, and returns data.
5. After 15 minutes the access token expires. The tool uses the refresh token and receives a new access token and a new refresh token; the old refresh token is now invalid. If an attacker had copied the old refresh token and tried to use it, the server would see a reused token and revoke the whole token family.
Count the trust: the reporting tool never saw the analyst's password; it can only read campaigns, not change budgets; its access dies within 15 minutes unless refreshed; and the analyst or an admin can revoke the grant at any time.
intuition: A hotel key card: the front desk checks your identity once, then gives you a card that opens only your room, only until checkout, and the door never needs to know your passport details.
The design follows from separating who you are from what you may do. Checking identity is expensive and sensitive, so it happens rarely and in one trusted place. Checking permission happens on every request, so it must be cheap and local: a signature check on a small token. Short lifetimes keep the damage from any leak small, and refresh tokens keep the user experience smooth. Each mechanism (state, PKCE, audience checks, exact redirect URIs) closes a specific attack that actually happened, which is why the modern flow looks complicated but has a reason for every step.
mistakes: People often think OAuth is an authentication (login) protocol. OAuth by itself only grants access; an access token does not tell a client reliably who the user is or that they just logged in. That is OIDC's job, through the ID token with its audience, nonce and authentication time. Using an access token as proof of login has caused real account-takeover bugs.
People often think a JWT is encrypted. A standard signed JWT is only base64url-encoded: anyone can read its claims. The signature prevents changes, not reading, so never put secrets or sensitive personal data in JWT claims.
People often think verifying the signature is enough. An API must also check the issuer, audience, expiry and scopes, and must pin the allowed algorithms. Libraries that accepted "alg": "none", or confused an RSA public key with an HMAC secret, let attackers forge tokens.
People often think JWTs can be revoked instantly like sessions. A self-contained JWT stays valid until it expires, because APIs verify it without asking anyone. Instant revocation needs short lifetimes, introspection or a deny list, each with a cost.
friction: The specifications are large and flexible, and most real vulnerabilities come from choosing the wrong options: open or pattern-matched redirect URIs, missing state checks, tokens stored in browser localStorage where any cross-site scripting bug can read them, or long-lived bearer tokens leaking into logs and URLs. Every identity provider adds its own variations, so integration with many partners is slow.
Revocation and session management are still awkward. Logging out of one app does not end sessions everywhere; single logout across many apps is unreliable. Machine-to-machine and agent use cases stretch the model: an AI agent acting for a user needs narrowly scoped, short-lived, auditable tokens, and consent screens do not fit automated workflows well. Bearer tokens remain stealable until sender-constrained tokens (DPoP, mTLS) become common, which requires work from clients and APIs. Finally, centralizing identity creates a single point of failure: when an identity provider is down or compromised, every application that trusts it is affected.
practice: Use the authorization code flow with PKCE for every interactive client, including server-side web apps, and the client credentials flow for service-to-service calls. Do not use the implicit or password grants. Register exact redirect URIs and always check state (and nonce for OIDC).
Keep access tokens short (5 to 15 minutes is a good default for sensitive APIs), rotate refresh tokens, and store them server-side or in secure, HttpOnly cookies rather than in JavaScript-readable storage. In APIs, use a well-maintained library, pin allowed algorithms, and check iss, aud, exp and scopes on every request. Cache JWKS keys and support key rotation through kid.
Design scopes around real permissions (read reports versus change budgets), and give partners and agents the narrowest scopes that work. For high-value APIs, add sender-constrained tokens with DPoP or mTLS. Log token issuance and refresh events so you can investigate misuse, and never log the tokens themselves.
quiz: What is the difference between an access token and an ID token? || An access token is for the API: it carries permissions (scopes) for calls. An ID token is for the client: an OIDC JWT that says who the user is and how they logged in.
How does PKCE stop an attacker who intercepts the authorization code? || The token endpoint only accepts the code together with the original code verifier, whose SHA-256 hash was sent at the start. The attacker has the code but not the verifier, which never left the client.
An API verifies JWTs locally, with 15-minute access tokens. An employee is fired at 10:00 and their account is disabled immediately. Until when might their existing token still work, and how could you shorten that? || Until the token's expiry, up to about 10:15. Shorter lifetimes, token introspection or a deny list of revoked token IDs would close the gap, at the cost of more refreshes or lookups.
Why must an API check the "aud" claim and not only the signature? || The same issuer signs tokens for many APIs. Without an audience check, a token issued for a low-risk API could be replayed against a high-risk one, because its signature is valid everywhere.
When would you choose opaque tokens with introspection over self-contained JWTs? || When instant revocation and hiding token contents matter more than avoiding a network call per check, for example for high-risk admin APIs, or when the number of calls is small enough that introspection latency is acceptable.
