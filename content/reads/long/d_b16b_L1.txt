value: Machine learning (ML) builds programs from examples instead of hand-written rules. You collect inputs with known answers (an ad impression and whether it was clicked, an email and whether it is spam), choose a family of functions with adjustable numbers inside, and let an optimizer set those numbers so the function's outputs match the answers. The result is a model: a function you can call on new inputs. The approach wins when the rules are too many, too subtle or change too fast for people to write. Nobody can write rules that score a billion ad requests a day for click probability, but a model trained on last month's logs can, and it can be retrained tomorrow when behaviour changes.
ML runs a large part of modern software: spam and fraud filters, search and feed ranking, ad click and conversion prediction, credit scoring, demand forecasting and anomaly detection. For the layer above (libraries such as scikit-learn and XGBoost, distributed frameworks such as Ray, and every product feature that "predicts"), it supplies one repeatable recipe: define a loss, fit, validate, deploy, monitor.
Each step of that recipe is a lower concept. Loss functions (L2) define what "good" means. Gradient descent (L2) does the fitting for most models. Linear and logistic regression (L2), kernels and support vector machines (L2), and decision trees with boosting (L2) are the main model families beside deep learning; genetic algorithms (L2) search when there is no gradient. Regularization (L2) and train/validation/test splits (L2) stop a model from memorizing. Bayesian inference (L4) gives a principled view of priors and uncertainty. Clustering (L2) and PCA (L2) find structure without labels. Evaluation and benchmarks (L2) and A/B experiments (L1) decide whether a new model is really better.
terms: Parameters — the numbers inside the model that training sets, such as the weights of a linear model or the split thresholds of a tree.
Hyperparameters — settings chosen before training, such as tree depth or learning rate; they are tuned on validation data.
Loss — a number that measures how wrong the model is on the data; training makes it as small as possible.
Generalization — doing well on new data from the same source, not only on the training data.
Overfitting — fitting the noise in the training data, so training error is low but error on new data is high.
Data leakage — information in the training features that will not exist at prediction time; it makes offline results look much better than production.
Calibration — predicted probabilities match observed frequencies: of impressions scored 2%, about 2% are clicked.
Drift — a change in the data after training (new devices, new content, a new season) that makes the model worse.
history: Arthur Samuel of IBM popularized the term in his 1959 paper "Some Studies in Machine Learning Using the Game of Checkers". His program learned a scoring function for board positions, partly by playing against itself, and in the end played better than Samuel did. Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) learned a linear classifier from examples, but Minsky and Papert's book "Perceptrons" (1969) showed the limits of single-layer models, and interest in neural methods fell for years.
The field then built its theory and its classic methods. Vapnik and Chervonenkis (1971) and Leslie Valiant's PAC learning (1984) asked when a model fitted on a finite sample can be trusted on new data. Decision trees arrived with CART (Breiman, Friedman, Olshen and Stone, 1984) and ID3 (Quinlan, 1986). Support vector machines (Cortes and Vapnik, 1995) combined a margin-based loss with kernels. Ensembles followed: AdaBoost (Freund and Schapire, 1995), random forests (Breiman, 2001) and gradient boosting (Friedman, 2001), which fits each new tree to the errors left by the trees before it.
Industry adopted ML as logs grew. Google described logistic regression trained on billions of ad impressions ("Ad Click Prediction: a View from the Trenches", 2013), and Facebook combined boosted trees with logistic regression for ad clicks (2014). Libraries made the methods standard: scikit-learn (started in 2007, first public release in 2010), XGBoost (2014), LightGBM and CatBoost (2017). After 2012, deep learning, the neighbouring concept, took over images, speech and text. On tabular business data, boosted trees stayed the default: a NeurIPS 2022 benchmark (Grinsztajn, Oyallon and Varoquaux) found tree models still beat deep networks on typical medium-sized tables, although tabular foundation models such as TabPFN (Nature, 2025) now compete on small datasets.
how: Every supervised problem has the same shape. The training data is a set of pairs (xᵢ, yᵢ). The model is a function f(x; θ), where θ are the parameters. Training solves one problem: choose θ to minimize (1/n) × ∑ ℓ(f(xᵢ; θ), yᵢ) + λ × R(θ). The loss functions concept supplies ℓ: squared error for numbers, cross-entropy (log loss) for probabilities. Log loss is −[y ln p + (1 − y) ln(1 − p)], and it punishes confident mistakes hard: predicting 0.01 for an impression that was clicked costs 4.6, while predicting 0.5 costs 0.69. The second term, R(θ), comes from regularization. Choosing the loss is choosing what the model cares about.
Next comes the model family, which fixes what kinds of patterns the model can express. Linear and logistic regression compute a weighted sum w·x + b; logistic regression passes it through the sigmoid σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e^(−z)) to get a probability. They are fast, easy to read and well calibrated when trained with log loss, so they are the baseline every other model is compared with. Kernels and SVMs draw non-linear boundaries by comparing examples through a similarity function; they led the field before deep learning but scale badly beyond roughly a hundred thousand examples, because the kernel matrix has n² entries. Decision trees split the data with threshold questions ("hours watched > 3?") into regions with similar labels. Boosting adds hundreds of small trees, each one fitted to the remaining error of the ones before. Boosted trees handle mixed feature types, missing values and feature interactions with little preparation, which is why they win on tables. Deep networks learn features from raw pixels, audio or text.
Then the parameters are fitted. For models with a gradient, gradient descent does the work: compute the gradient of the loss on a mini-batch of examples, step θ ← θ − η∇L, and repeat. For logistic regression the gradient for one example is simply (p − y) × x, so each example pulls the weights in proportion to its error. Trees are fitted greedily, choosing at each node the split that reduces the loss most; gradient boosting is gradient descent in function space, where each step adds a tree instead of changing a weight. When no gradient exists (choosing features, an architecture or hyperparameters), search methods try many configurations and keep the best: grid search, random search, Bayesian optimization, or genetic algorithms, which mutate and recombine good candidates.
The goal is low error on future data, not on training data, and this is where most of the craft lies. Error on new data has three sources: bias (the model family cannot represent the true pattern), variance (the fitted model depends too much on the particular sample) and noise that no model can remove. Flexible models have low bias but high variance. Regularization trades a little bias for much less variance: an L2 penalty λ∑w² shrinks weights, an L1 penalty sets many weights to exactly zero, trees limit depth and leaf size, boosting uses a small learning rate and early stopping, and networks use weight decay and dropout. Train/validation/test splits measure the result: fit on training data, choose hyperparameters on validation data, and report once on test data that was never used for decisions. Cross-validation repeats the split k times when data is scarce. For data that has a time order, which is almost all business data, the split must follow time: train on the past and validate on the future, or the model will have seen the answers.
Bayesian inference explains many of these choices. It treats θ as uncertain and computes a posterior, p(θ | data) ∝ p(data | θ) × p(θ). Minimizing log loss is maximizing the likelihood p(data | θ), and adding an L2 penalty is the same as a Gaussian prior on the weights, so regularization is a prior belief that weights are small. The same view supplies uncertainty estimates, which matter when data is small, as in Thompson sampling for bandits.
Without labels, unsupervised methods find structure. Clustering groups similar examples: k-means alternates between assigning each point to its nearest centre and moving each centre to the mean of its points, and Gaussian mixture models do a soft version with the EM algorithm. PCA finds the directions of largest variance and projects data onto the top few, compressing hundreds of correlated features into a handful for plotting, denoising or speed. These methods build audience segments, flag anomalies and create features for supervised models.
Last comes judgment. Offline metrics on held-out data (log loss, AUC, precision and recall, RMSE) and shared benchmarks compare models on fixed data. But a deployed model changes what users see, and so changes the data, so the final judge is an online A/B experiment on the business metric. A model with better offline AUC can lose online if it is slower, badly calibrated, or relies on a pattern that does not hold in live traffic.

example: A display ad network wants to predict the click probability of each impression, so that it can bid pCTR × value per click. The base rate is 2%. Start with the simplest model: predict 0.02 for everything. Its log loss is −(0.02 × ln 0.02 + 0.98 × ln 0.98) = 0.02 × 3.912 + 0.98 × 0.0202 ≈ 0.0980.
Watch one gradient step. Logistic regression with one feature, is_sports (1 if the ad runs in sports content), starts with weight w = 0 and bias b = ln(0.02 / 0.98) ≈ −3.892, so every prediction is 0.02. A clicked sports impression (y = 1, x = 1) has gradient (p − y) × x = (0.02 − 1) × 1 = −0.98. With learning rate 0.1, w becomes 0 − 0.1 × (−0.98) = 0.098. A non-clicked sports impression has gradient 0.02 and moves w down by only 0.002. After many examples, w settles where the average error on sports impressions is zero, which means the predicted rate equals the observed rate. If sports impressions are clicked 3% of the time, w ends at ln(0.03 / 0.97) + 3.892 ≈ −3.476 + 3.892 = 0.416. Log loss makes the model calibrated as a side effect.
Now a realistic run on 10 million impressions from four weeks, with 60 features. Train on weeks 1 to 3 (7.5 million rows) and validate on week 4 (2.5 million rows). The numbers are illustrative but typical. Logistic regression: training log loss 0.0905, validation 0.0912. Boosted trees with depth 10 and 2,000 trees: training 0.0780, validation 0.0925. The big gap is overfitting, and the model is worse than logistic regression on new data. Boosted trees with depth 6, learning rate 0.05 and early stopping at 400 trees: training 0.0860, validation 0.0884. The ratio to the base-rate loss, called normalized entropy, is 0.0884 / 0.0980 ≈ 0.90, so the model removes about 10% of the uncertainty of the base rate.
Two checks remain. First, leakage: a random split instead of a time split gave validation log loss 0.0850, too good to be true. The feature "clicks by this user today" included the clicks that were being predicted, and a random split hid it. Second, calibration: on week 4 the model's average prediction is 2.4% while the true rate is 2.0%. With $0.50 value per click, the bid becomes 0.024 × 0.50 × 1,000 = $12 per thousand impressions instead of $10, so the network overpays by 20% in every auction it wins. AUC cannot see this error, because multiplying every score by 1.2 does not change the ranking. Recalibrating on recent data (Platt scaling or isotonic regression) fixes it.
intuition: Machine learning is curve fitting with a referee: the optimizer bends the curve to fit the examples, and held-out data checks that the curve also fits examples it has never seen.
Writing rules works when you know the rules. For most prediction problems you do not, but you do have millions of past outcomes. Choosing a loss turns "make good predictions" into a number, and an optimizer can push any number down. The danger is that a flexible function can push the training loss to zero by memorizing, so the second half of the idea is equally important: keep data the optimizer never touches, and believe only what that data says. Everything else in the field, from regularization to time splits to A/B tests, is a more careful version of that referee.
mistakes: People often think a more complex model is always better. In fact extra flexibility raises variance, and on tabular data a tuned boosted-tree model usually beats a deep network. Better data, better labels and better features give larger gains than a fancier model.
People often think high accuracy means a good model. With a 2% click rate, a model that always says "no click" is 98% accurate and useless. Use log loss, AUC or precision and recall, and compare with the base rate.
People often think a random train/test split is safe. For data with time or repeated users, a random split lets the model learn from the future or from the same user's other rows. Split by time or by entity, as production will.
People often think a predictive feature is a cause. A model learns correlations; changing a feature in the real world may not change the outcome. Causal questions need experiments or causal methods.
friction: Data is the main cost. Labels can be expensive (fraud is confirmed weeks later), noisy, or biased toward what the old system chose to show. Leakage is easy to create and hard to see. Drift is constant: new devices, new content, holidays and competitors change the input distribution, so models decay and need monitoring and retraining.
Deployed models change their own training data. A ranker only learns from items it showed, and a fraud model only learns labels for transactions it let through, so errors can reinforce themselves; some random exploration is the usual fix. Offline and online results often disagree. Training–serving skew, where a feature is computed one way in a Spark batch job and another way in the online service, is one of the most common production bugs.
Many models are hard to explain. Tools such as SHAP give approximate per-feature attributions, but they describe the model, not the world. Fairness is hard because a model trained on historical decisions can repeat historical bias even without sensitive features, through correlated ones such as location. Finally, the model code is a small part of a production ML system (Sculley and others, 2015); pipelines, undeclared consumers of predictions and tangled configuration create hidden technical debt.
practice: Start with a baseline and a test that mirrors production. Compute the base-rate metric first, then fit logistic regression, then gradient-boosted trees (XGBoost or LightGBM), and only then try something more complex. Split by time, and by user or household when the same entity appears many times. Pick one primary offline metric that matches the use: log loss when probabilities feed bids or budgets, AUC or NDCG when only the order matters.
Check calibration on every model that feeds a price or a threshold, with a reliability plot by score bucket and the ratio of mean prediction to observed rate. Look for leakage whenever a result is surprisingly good: list each feature and ask whether it is known at prediction time.
Compute features once and serve them from the same code path for training and inference (a feature store, or shared Spark and online code). Track experiments, data versions and model versions (MLflow is common on Databricks). Ship through a shadow deployment and then an A/B test, and monitor input distributions, prediction distributions and calibration after launch, with automatic retraining on a schedule that matches how fast the data drifts.
quiz: What does a regularization term such as λ∑w² do, and how is it chosen? || It penalizes large weights, trading a little bias for lower variance. λ is a hyperparameter chosen by comparing scores on validation data.
Why does training logistic regression with log loss tend to give calibrated probabilities? || The gradient for each example is (p − y) × x. Training stops changing the weights when the average error is zero for every feature, which means predicted rates match observed rates on the training data.
Why must time-ordered data be split by time instead of randomly? || A random split puts later events in the training set, so the model learns from the future or from leaked features. Production always predicts the future from the past, so only a time split measures that.
A model predicts a 2.5% average click rate, but the true rate is 2.0%. With $0.40 value per click, what is the bid per thousand impressions, and how far off is it? || 0.025 × 0.40 × 1,000 = $10 instead of 0.020 × 0.40 × 1,000 = $8, which is 25% too high.
When would you choose gradient-boosted trees over a deep network? || For tabular data with mixed numeric and categorical features, where trees train fast, need little preparation and usually score as well or better. Choose deep networks for raw images, audio, text, or huge interaction logs such as recommendations.

value: Reinforcement learning (RL) learns how to act. An agent observes a situation, takes an action, and receives a reward and a new situation; it repeats this many times and adjusts its behaviour to collect more total reward. No one tells it the right action at each step. It only learns the consequences, often much later: a chess move is good or bad because of the result thirty moves on. This makes RL the method for sequential decisions, where each action changes what happens next.
RL learned to play Atari games from pixels, beat the world's best Go players, and controls robots and industrial systems. Since 2022 its largest use has been post-training language models. The GPT, Claude, Gemini and DeepSeek models in the layer above are first pretrained to predict text, then shaped by RL: rewards from a model of human preferences make them helpful assistants, and rewards from automatic checkers (unit tests, exact answers) train the long step-by-step reasoning of reasoning models.
The lower layers supply each part. Markov decision processes and Bellman equations (L4) give the formal problem and the recursive equation that every method approximates. Q-learning and DQN (L2) learn how good each action is. Policy gradients and PPO (L2) adjust the policy directly and are the workhorse for language models, including the GRPO variant. Monte Carlo tree search (L2) adds planning by look-ahead, as in AlphaGo. Gradient descent (L2) trains the neural networks that represent policies and values. RLHF (L2) is the application of all this to language models. Dopamine and reward prediction (L5) is the biological echo: dopamine neurons signal the same "prediction error" that temporal-difference learning uses.
terms: State — what the agent observes about the situation, such as a board position or the tokens written so far.
Action — a choice the agent makes in a state, such as a move, a motor command or the next token.
Reward — a number the environment returns after an action; the agent wants the total over time to be high.
Return — the sum of future rewards, with each step further away multiplied by a discount γ (for example 0.99).
Policy — the agent's rule for choosing actions, written π(a | s), the probability of action a in state s.
Value function — the expected return from a state, V(s), or from taking action a in state s, Q(s, a).
TD error — the surprise δ = r + γ × (value of next state) − (value of current state); learning moves estimates to reduce it.
Exploration — trying actions that look worse now, to find out whether they are better; exploitation is choosing the best known action.
On-policy and off-policy — learning only from data produced by the current policy, versus learning from any data, such as old logs.
history: The mathematics came first. Richard Bellman's dynamic programming (1950s) showed how to compute optimal decisions by working backwards from the future, and his equation is still the core of RL. Arthur Samuel's checkers program (1959) already adjusted its evaluation of positions toward the evaluation of later positions, an early form of temporal-difference learning.
The modern field grew from work by Andrew Barto and Richard Sutton at the University of Massachusetts Amherst from the late 1970s. Barto, Sutton and Anderson (1983) balanced a pole on a cart with an actor–critic system. Sutton (1988) formalized temporal-difference (TD) learning, Chris Watkins (1989) introduced Q-learning, and Ronald Williams (1992) gave the REINFORCE policy-gradient algorithm. Gerald Tesauro's TD-Gammon (1992) learned backgammon at near world-champion level by playing itself, the first striking success. In 1997 Schultz, Dayan and Montague showed that dopamine neurons in monkeys fire like a TD error. Sutton and Barto's textbook (1998, second edition 2018) defined the field, and they received the 2024 ACM Turing Award for it, announced in March 2025.
Deep networks made RL scale. DeepMind's DQN (2013, Nature 2015) learned dozens of Atari games from raw pixels. AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol 4–1 in March 2016; AlphaGo Zero (2017) learned without human games, and AlphaZero and MuZero generalized the method. TRPO (2015) and PPO (2017) made policy gradients stable enough to become the default. Christiano and others (2017) trained agents from human preference comparisons, which led to InstructGPT (2022) and ChatGPT. From late 2024, RL with verifiable rewards trained reasoning models such as OpenAI's o1 and DeepSeek-R1, the latter with GRPO, a critic-free variant of PPO introduced in DeepSeekMath (2024).
how: The formal problem is a Markov decision process: a set of states, a set of actions, transition probabilities P(s' | s, a), a reward r for each step, and a discount γ between 0 and 1. The return from time t is G = r₁ + γ × r₂ + γ² × r₃ + …, so γ sets the horizon: with γ = 0.99, rewards more than roughly 1 / (1 − γ) = 100 steps away count for little. The goal is a policy π that maximizes the expected return. The Bellman optimality equation describes the best action values: Q*(s, a) = E[r + γ × max over a' of Q*(s', a')]. In words, an action is worth its immediate reward plus the discounted value of acting optimally afterwards. If you know P and r and the states are few, you can solve this exactly by dynamic programming. RL is what you do when the transitions are unknown or the state space is far too large to enumerate.
Value-based methods learn Q from experience. Q-learning takes one observed transition (s, a, r, s'), computes the TD error δ = r + γ × max Q(s', ·) − Q(s, a), and updates Q(s, a) ← Q(s, a) + α × δ, where α is a learning rate. The target uses the agent's own estimate of the next state; this "bootstrapping" lets value flow backwards one step per update without waiting for the end of an episode. Q-learning is off-policy: it learns the values of the best policy while behaving differently, for example acting randomly 10% of the time (ε-greedy). With a table of values, it provably converges if every state–action pair keeps being tried. The dopamine concept connects here: dopamine neurons fire for an unexpected reward, shift their firing to the cue that predicts the reward after learning, and dip when a predicted reward does not arrive, which is the pattern of δ.
DQN replaced the table with a neural network Q(s, a; θ) trained by gradient descent on δ². Naively this diverges, because successive frames are highly correlated and the target moves with every update. DQN added two fixes: an experience replay buffer (about a million past transitions, sampled at random to break correlations) and a target network, a frozen copy of Q used for the target and refreshed every few thousand steps.
Policy-gradient methods skip values and adjust the policy directly. With a policy network π_θ(a | s), the policy gradient theorem gives ∇J = E[∇ log π_θ(a | s) × A(s, a)], where the advantage A says how much better the action was than average in that state. In words: make actions that turned out better than expected more likely, and worse ones less likely. REINFORCE estimates A from the full return minus a baseline, which is unbiased but very noisy. Actor–critic methods train a second network, the critic, to estimate V(s) and use A ≈ r + γ × V(s') − V(s), which lowers the noise. Policy gradients handle continuous actions and huge action spaces, such as a vocabulary of 100,000 tokens, where a max over Q values is impractical.
Big policy steps can destroy a good policy, and then the agent collects bad data and cannot recover. PPO limits each update. It computes the ratio ρ = π_new(a | s) / π_old(a | s) and maximizes min(ρ × A, clip(ρ, 1 − ε, 1 + ε) × A) with ε around 0.2, so moving an action's probability by more than about 20% earns no extra credit. This lets PPO reuse each batch of data for several gradient steps. GRPO, used for reasoning models, removes the critic: it samples a group of answers to the same prompt and uses each answer's reward minus the group mean, divided by the group standard deviation, as the advantage. That saves the memory and compute of a value network as large as the policy.
Planning adds search when a model of the environment exists. AlphaZero's network outputs a policy prior and a value for a position. Monte Carlo tree search runs hundreds of simulated continuations guided by both, and the resulting visit counts form a better policy than the network alone. The network is then trained to imitate the search result and to predict the game outcome, so search and learning improve each other. MuZero learned the model of the environment as well.
For language models, the state is the prompt plus the tokens generated so far, each action is one token, and an episode is one complete answer. The reward usually arrives only at the end: from a reward model trained on human comparisons (RLHF), or from a program that checks the answer (RL with verifiable rewards). A penalty on the KL divergence from the starting model keeps the policy from drifting into strange text that fools the reward model. Most of the cost is generation: each training step must first sample thousands of complete answers from the current policy.

example: Take a corridor with three cells A, B, C and a goal G to the right of C. Moving right from C into G gives reward +10 and ends the episode; every other move gives 0. Use γ = 0.9. The true values of moving right are Q(C) = 10, Q(B) = 0 + 0.9 × 10 = 9, and Q(A) = 0.9 × 9 = 8.1. Now let Q-learning discover this. Start every Q at 0, use α = 0.5, and suppose the agent walks right from A in each episode (in practice exploration would sometimes take it left).
Episode 1. A to B: δ = 0 + 0.9 × 0 − 0 = 0, nothing changes. B to C: also 0. C to G: δ = 10 + 0 − 0 = 10, so Q(C) = 0 + 0.5 × 10 = 5. Only the last step learned anything.
Episode 2. A to B: Q(B) is still 0, so no change. B to C: δ = 0 + 0.9 × 5 − 0 = 4.5, so Q(B) = 2.25. C to G: δ = 10 − 5 = 5, so Q(C) = 7.5.
Episode 3. A to B: δ = 0.9 × 2.25 − 0 = 2.025, so Q(A) ≈ 1.01. B to C: δ = 0.9 × 7.5 − 2.25 = 4.5, so Q(B) = 4.5. C to G: δ = 10 − 7.5 = 2.5, so Q(C) = 8.75.
Two patterns appear. Value flows backwards one cell per episode, which is why long corridors, or long games, need many episodes. And the surprise at the goal shrinks (10, then 5, then 2.5) while surprise appears earlier in the corridor. That is exactly the dopamine pattern: once a reward is predicted, the signal moves to the earliest predictor.
Now a policy-gradient step. A policy chooses between two actions with logits 0 and 0, so each has probability 0.5. It samples "right" and gets reward 1. The average reward so far is 0.5, so the advantage is +0.5. The gradient of log π(right) is +0.5 for the right logit (1 − 0.5) and −0.5 for the left logit. With step size 1, the right logit becomes 1 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 and the left logit −0.25. The new probability of "right" is 1 / (1 + e^(−0.5)) ≈ 0.62.
Scale explains the cost. DQN trained on about 50 million frames per Atari game, roughly 38 days of play, to learn what a human learns in hours. AlphaGo Zero generated about 4.9 million self-play games over three days of training, after which it beat the version that had defeated Lee Sedol by 100 games to 0.
intuition: Learning to ride a bicycle: no one tells you the right move at each moment, only the consequence, staying up or falling.
The natural idea underneath is prediction. If you can predict how good each situation is, you can judge every action immediately by whether it made your prediction rise or fall, instead of waiting for the final result. TD learning does exactly this: each step's surprise trains the predictions, and the predictions then make credit assignment possible over long delays. Animals seem to have arrived at the same design, which is one reason the dopamine finding mattered so much. Policy gradients add a second simple idea: do more of what turned out better than expected.
mistakes: People often think any learning with a reward signal needs RL. If actions do not change future states, the problem is supervised learning or a bandit, which are cheaper and more stable. Choosing an ad creative for one impression is a bandit; pacing a budget across a day is closer to RL.
People often think a rising reward curve means the agent learned the intended behaviour. Agents exploit any gap in the reward. In OpenAI's 2016 boat-racing example, the agent circled forever hitting the same reward targets instead of finishing the race. Language models learn to please reward models with long, confident or flattering answers. Read samples of behaviour, not only the curve.
People often think RL starts from nothing. Most successful systems start from a strong prior: AlphaGo first learned from human expert games, and language-model RL starts from a pretrained, instruction-tuned model. RL refines behaviour; it rarely creates it from scratch at this scale.
People often think value-based and policy-gradient methods are rival schools. Most modern algorithms combine them: PPO uses a critic, and AlphaZero learns both a policy and a value.
friction: RL is sample-hungry. Even simple tasks need millions of interactions, which is acceptable in a simulator and expensive or dangerous in the real world. Policies trained in simulation often fail on real hardware because the simulator is not exact (the "sim-to-real gap"). Learning from logged data without exploration (offline RL) is hard, because the agent overvalues actions the logs rarely tried.
Results are fragile. Henderson and others ("Deep Reinforcement Learning that Matters", 2018) showed that random seeds and implementation details can change results as much as the choice of algorithm. Training can collapse suddenly, and evaluations vary widely between runs.
Reward design is the hardest part. A reward that is easy to compute is rarely exactly what you want, and optimization finds the difference. For language models this appears as reward hacking: tests that pass because the model special-cased them, or answers that a reward model scores highly but people dislike. Verifiable rewards help only where an automatic checker exists. Long-horizon credit assignment remains open: when a 50-step task fails, it is hard to know which step was wrong. Finally, RL for large models is costly, because every update needs fresh samples from the current model.
practice: First decide whether the problem is really RL. If decisions do not affect future states, use supervised learning or a contextual bandit. If they do, check whether classical control or optimization (such as a PID controller or a linear program for budget pacing) already solves it, since these are easier to trust.
If you need RL, invest in the environment and the reward before the algorithm. Build a simulator or replay system, write the reward with guardrail terms (cost, safety, constraint violations), and evaluate with off-policy methods such as importance sampling on logged data before acting live. Start with standard implementations (Stable-Baselines3 or CleanRL for control; TRL, verl or OpenRLHF for language models) and well-known defaults, such as PPO with ε = 0.2, before writing your own.
Run several seeds and report the spread. Log the reward, the KL from the starting policy, entropy and episode length, and regularly read or watch sampled behaviour, because reward can rise while behaviour gets worse. For language-model RL, prefer verifiable rewards where possible, hold out an evaluation set that the reward never touched, and watch for the policy finding shortcuts in the checker.
quiz: What does the discount factor γ control? || It weights rewards further in the future less. With γ = 0.99, rewards beyond roughly 1 / (1 − γ) = 100 steps count for little, so γ sets the effective planning horizon.
Why does Q-learning need many episodes to propagate a reward back through a long chain of states? || Each update uses only the next state's current estimate. Value therefore moves back about one state per visit, so a reward 50 steps away needs many passes before early states learn about it.
Why did DQN need experience replay and a target network? || Consecutive frames are strongly correlated and the target r + γ max Q changes with every update, which makes neural Q-learning unstable. Random replay of old transitions breaks the correlation, and a frozen target network keeps the target steady for thousands of steps.
In GRPO, four answers to one prompt get rewards 1, 0, 0 and 1. What are their advantages? || The mean is 0.5 and the standard deviation is 0.5, so the advantages are (1 − 0.5) / 0.5 = +1 for the two correct answers and −1 for the two wrong ones.
When would you pick PPO with a learned critic over GRPO for language-model training? || When you can afford a value network and want finer per-token credit, or when sampling many answers per prompt is too expensive. GRPO saves the critic but needs a group of samples per prompt and gives every token of an answer the same advantage.

value: A recommender system decides which few items each person sees out of a catalog far too large to browse: videos, products, songs, posts, ads, apps or TV shows on a home screen. A user looks at perhaps 20 items, the catalog holds millions, and the decision must be made in roughly 100 to 200 milliseconds per request, billions of times a day. The quality of that choice drives engagement and revenue. Netflix reported in 2015 that recommendations influenced about 80% of hours streamed and estimated their value at more than $1 billion a year. Ad targeting and audience building are close cousins: they rank users for an ad instead of items for a user.
The core idea is that people's tastes have structure. People who watch the same five cricket highlight videos tend to like a sixth one, and this pattern can be learned from interaction logs without anyone describing the videos. Modern systems combine that collaborative signal with content features and context (device, time, what the user just did) in a pipeline of stages.
The lower concepts carry the design. Matrix factorization (L2) models the user–item matrix as the product of two thin matrices, and linear algebra (L4) explains why a low-rank approximation captures taste. Embeddings (L2) generalize this to learned vectors for users, items and any ID-like feature. Approximate nearest-neighbour search (L2) finds the best few thousand items among millions of vectors in milliseconds. Multi-armed bandits (L2) decide how much traffic to spend on new or uncertain items. In the layer above, PyTorch and TensorFlow train these models, and vector databases such as Milvus and Pinecone serve the retrieval step.
terms: Implicit feedback — signals people give without rating: clicks, watch time, purchases, skips; abundant but noisy.
Candidate generation — the first stage, which cheaply narrows millions of items to a few thousand.
Ranking — the later stage, which scores the candidates with a richer, slower model.
Two-tower model — one network turns a user into a vector, another turns an item into a vector, and their dot product is the score.
Embedding table — a large lookup table holding one learned vector for each ID (user, item, creator, advertiser).
Cold start — the problem of recommending to new users, or recommending new items, before there is interaction data.
Position bias — items in top positions get more clicks regardless of quality, which distorts the training data.
Recall@K and NDCG — offline metrics: the share of truly relevant items in the top K, and a score that rewards putting them near the top.
history: Collaborative filtering was named in Xerox PARC's Tapestry system (1992), where people annotated email and news so that others could filter by them. GroupLens (1994) automated it for Usenet news by predicting a user's rating from the ratings of users with similar histories. Amazon's item-to-item collaborative filtering (Linden, Smith and York, 2003) flipped the computation: precompute, offline, which items are bought by the same customers, so that serving a page only needs a lookup. That scaled to tens of millions of customers.
The Netflix Prize (October 2006 to September 2009) offered $1 million for a 10% improvement over Netflix's Cinematch on 100 million ratings from about 480,000 users and 17,770 films. The winning team, BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos, reached a test RMSE of 0.8567, a 10.06% improvement, with a blend of factorization and neighbourhood models. Koren, Bell and Volinsky's 2009 article made matrix factorization the standard. Netflix later said it adopted some of the prize methods but not the full blend, whose engineering cost was too high for the gain, and the business had moved from DVD ratings to streaming behaviour.
Deep learning came next. YouTube's 2016 paper split recommendation into a candidate-generation network and a ranking network trained to predict watch time. Google's Wide & Deep (2016) and Meta's DLRM (2019) combined huge embedding tables with neural networks. Yi and others at Google (2019) described two-tower retrieval trained with in-batch negatives and a correction for sampling bias. Sequence models followed (SASRec 2018, BERT4Rec 2019), and ByteDance's Monolith (2022) described training updated from live streams within minutes. Recent work treats recommendation like language modelling: Google's TIGER (2023) generates item IDs token by token, and Meta's HSTU-based generative recommender (2024), with 1.5 trillion parameters, improved online metrics by 12.4%.
how: Everything starts from an interaction log: user, item, time, event type (impression, click, 30-second watch, purchase, skip) and context. As a matrix, users are rows and items are columns, and the matrix is extremely sparse. The Netflix Prize data filled only about 1.2% of its 480,000 × 17,770 cells, and production matrices are far sparser. Most signals are implicit, and a missing entry does not mean dislike: usually the user was never shown the item. So the task is framed as predicting the probability or amount of engagement for a (user, item, context) triple, and then ranking by it.
Matrix factorization is the classic model. It approximates the rating matrix R (users × items) as U × Vᵀ, where U has one row of k numbers per user and V one row per item, with k typically between 32 and 256. The predicted affinity is the dot product u · v, often plus a global mean and per-user and per-item biases. Linear algebra explains why this works: if tastes are driven by a few underlying factors (genre, language, mood, budget), R is close to a low-rank matrix, and the truncated SVD gives the best rank-k approximation of a full matrix. Real matrices are mostly missing, so instead of an SVD the model fits only observed entries, minimizing ∑ (r − u · v)² + λ(|u|² + |v|²) with stochastic gradient descent or alternating least squares (ALS). ALS fixes V and solves every user's small least-squares problem exactly, then swaps roles; each half-step is embarrassingly parallel, which is why Spark MLlib ships it. For implicit data, Hu, Koren and Volinsky (2008) weight each cell by a confidence such as 1 + α × (number of views).
The embeddings concept generalizes this. In a two-tower model, the user tower takes the user's recent item IDs, profile and context and outputs a vector u; the item tower takes the item ID, metadata and content features (title text, image or audio embeddings) and outputs v. The score is still u · v. Training uses a sampled softmax: for each positive pair (user, item they engaged with), the other items in the same training batch serve as negatives, and the loss is −log( exp(u · v⁺) / ∑ⱼ exp(u · vⱼ) ). Popular items appear as in-batch negatives far more often than rare ones, which unfairly pushes them down, so systems subtract the log of each item's sampling probability from its score during training (the "logQ correction"). Content features let the item tower embed a brand-new item before anyone has watched it, which eases cold start.
Keeping the towers separate is what makes retrieval fast. Item vectors are computed offline for the whole catalog and loaded into an approximate nearest-neighbour index. At request time the system computes one user vector and asks the index for the items with the highest inner product. An exact scan of 50 million 64-dimensional vectors would cost 3.2 billion multiply-adds and read 12.8 GB per request; an HNSW graph or an IVF index with quantization visits a few thousand vectors and returns the top 1,000 in a few milliseconds, at the cost of missing a few percent of the true top items. Production systems merge several candidate sources: two-tower retrieval, item-to-item co-occurrence ("people who watched this also watched"), the follow graph, trending items and fresh items.
Ranking then scores the few hundred to few thousand survivors with a much richer model. Now features can cross user and item: has this user watched this creator before, how long since they last opened the app, how is this item performing in the last ten minutes. DLRM-style models look up embeddings for sparse IDs, pass dense features through an MLP, and combine them through pairwise dot products and more layers. Rankers are usually multi-task, predicting click, long watch, like, share and hide at once, and a weighted formula turns these into one value score; the weights encode product goals. Embedding tables dominate model size: one billion IDs × 64 dimensions × 4 bytes is 256 GB before optimizer state, so tables are sharded across GPUs or parameter servers, and new IDs must be inserted while training runs.
Re-ranking shapes the final list of perhaps 20 items: limit items from one creator, mix in freshness, apply licensing and policy rules, and reserve slots for exploration. Bandits supply the exploration logic: a new item with uncertain appeal gets a share of impressions proportional to the chance that it is actually good (Thompson sampling) or to an optimistic estimate (UCB). Without this, the system shows only what it already believes in and never collects data on anything else. Position bias needs similar care: training uses the shown position as a feature, or weights examples by the inverse probability of being seen, and serving sets position to a fixed value.
Evaluation happens twice. Offline, the team holds out the most recent interactions and measures recall@K for retrieval and NDCG or log loss for ranking. Online, A/B tests measure watch time, retention and revenue, with guardrails on diversity and creator health. The two often disagree, because offline data was produced by the old system and only contains items it chose to show.

example: Take a factorization with k = 2. Asha's user vector is [1.0, 0.2] and Ben's is [0.1, 1.0]. The items are cricket highlights [0.9, 0.1], a cooking show [0.1, 0.8] and a news show [0.5, 0.5]. Asha's scores are 1.0 × 0.9 + 0.2 × 0.1 = 0.92 for cricket, 0.1 + 0.16 = 0.26 for cooking and 0.5 + 0.1 = 0.60 for news. Ben's are 0.19, 0.81 and 0.55. The first dimension behaves like "sport" and the second like "food", but nobody named them; training found them.
Now Asha watches the cooking show to the end, so the target is 1 and the error is e = 1 − 0.26 = 0.74. With learning rate η = 0.1 and regularization λ = 0.01, SGD updates both vectors using the old values. Asha: u + η(e × v − λ × u) = [1.0 + 0.1 × (0.074 − 0.010), 0.2 + 0.1 × (0.592 − 0.002)] = [1.006, 0.259]. Cooking show: v + η(e × u − λ × v) = [0.1 + 0.1 × (0.740 − 0.001), 0.8 + 0.1 × (0.148 − 0.008)] = [0.174, 0.814]. The new score is 1.006 × 0.174 + 0.259 × 0.814 ≈ 0.386, up from 0.26. One update moved Asha toward "food" and the show toward Asha's sports-heavy taste; millions of such updates shape the space.
Next, the two-tower softmax for one training example. The user's score for the true item is 2.0, and two in-batch negatives score 1.0 and 0.0. The softmax probability of the true item is e² / (e² + e¹ + e⁰) = 7.39 / 11.11 ≈ 0.665, so the loss is −ln 0.665 ≈ 0.41. The gradient pushes the true item's score up and the negatives' scores down in proportion to their probabilities.
Finally, the cost of serving a catalog of 50 million items with 64-dimensional float32 vectors. The vectors take 50 million × 256 bytes = 12.8 GB. An exact scan per request reads all 12.8 GB. Even a whole CPU socket streaming a few hundred GB/s needs about 40 ms per request, so one socket could serve only about 25 requests per second. An HNSW index that visits about 3,000 vectors reads under 1 MB and answers in about a millisecond. The heavy ranker might cost 20 MFLOPs per candidate; 500 candidates × 20 MFLOPs = 10 GFLOPs per request, and at 2,000 requests per second that is 20 TFLOP/s of model compute, a handful of GPUs at realistic utilization. Running the heavy ranker on all 50 million items would cost 100,000 times more, which is why the funnel exists.
intuition: A good shop assistant remembers what people like you bought, notices what you just looked at, and brings out one short shelf instead of walking you through the whole warehouse.
Low-rank structure is natural because tastes are not random. A few hundred hidden factors explain most of why people choose what they choose, so a short vector per person and per item captures most of the signal. The funnel is natural too: spend a little compute on everything, then a lot on the few items that might win, the way a recruiter screens résumés quickly and interviews only a shortlist. Exploration is the price of learning: a system that only shows what it already likes cannot discover that it was wrong.
mistakes: People often think an item the user did not click is an item they dislike. Usually they never saw it, or saw it in a bad position. Treating every non-click as a strong negative teaches the model the old system's exposure choices, not the user's taste.
People often think better offline recall means a better product. Offline logs only contain items the previous system chose to show, so a new model that finds good items the old one ignored can look worse offline. Online A/B tests are the real measure.
People often think one strong model should score the whole catalog. At millions of items and thousands of requests per second this is far too expensive, and the funnel's cheap stages are where most items are removed, so their recall limits the final quality.
People often think embedding vectors from different training runs can be mixed. Each run learns its own coordinate system. If the user tower and the item index come from different model versions, dot products are meaningless, so both must be deployed together.
friction: Feedback loops are the deepest problem. The system learns from what it showed, shows more of what it learned, and can narrow what users see and starve new items and creators. Popularity bias, position bias and the gap between offline and online metrics all come from the same root: the data is not a neutral sample of preferences.
Objectives are hard to choose. Clicks are fast to measure but reward clickbait. Watch time is better but can reward endless low-value viewing. Long-term retention is what matters, but it takes weeks to measure, so teams optimize proxies and check them with long holdouts. Multi-task weights are often tuned by hand.
Systems are heavy. Embedding tables with billions of rows need sharding, careful memory planning (optimizer state can triple their size) and handling of new IDs. Fresh behaviour matters within minutes, so streaming features and online training must stay consistent with batch training. Cold start remains hard for new users with no history. Privacy changes, such as the loss of third-party cookies and mobile tracking restrictions, remove signals that many systems relied on.
practice: Build in steps. Start with popularity and item-to-item co-occurrence baselines; they are hard to beat for new products. Add matrix factorization (Spark ALS works well on Databricks for implicit data), then a two-tower retriever with content features, then a feature-rich ranker. Keep at least two retrieval sources so one bug cannot empty the funnel.
Evaluate with time-based splits (train on the past, test on the next day or week), and report retrieval recall@K separately from ranking metrics. Log the position, the candidate source and the probability with which each item was shown; without propensities you cannot correct for bias or evaluate counterfactually.
Reserve a small exploration share (1 to 5% of slots is common) for new and uncertain items, and measure catalog coverage and diversity as guardrails. Version the user tower and the item index together and rebuild the index on every model release. Run A/B tests long enough to see retention, keep a long-term holdout group, and for TV and household devices remember that several people may share one profile, so recent context often predicts better than long history.
quiz: What do candidate generation and ranking each optimize for? || Candidate generation optimizes recall at low cost: it must keep the good items while cutting millions to thousands. Ranking optimizes the order of those candidates with a slower, richer model.
Why can matrix factorization predict ratings for user–item pairs that never appeared together? || It learns a vector for every user and item from their other interactions. The dot product of any user and item vector gives a score, so taste learned from similar users transfers to unseen pairs.
Why does a two-tower model allow fast retrieval while a ranking model with user–item cross features does not? || In a two-tower model the item vectors do not depend on the user, so they are precomputed and indexed for nearest-neighbour search. Cross features need both user and item at once, so every pair must be scored separately.
A catalog has 20 million items with 128-dimensional float32 vectors. How much memory do the vectors need, and what does an exact scan cost per request? || 20 million × 128 × 4 bytes = 10.24 GB. An exact scan reads all of it and computes about 2.56 billion multiply-adds per request, which is why an ANN index is used.
Your new ranker wins offline on NDCG but loses an online A/B test. What are likely causes and what do you trust? || Offline data reflects the old system's exposure and position bias, and the offline metric may reward short-term clicks rather than the business goal. Trust the online test, check latency and calibration, and improve the offline evaluation (propensity weighting, better labels).
