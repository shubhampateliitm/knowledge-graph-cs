@@+ layernorm
tradeoffs: BatchNorm, LayerNorm or GroupNorm. Choose BatchNorm for convolutional networks trained with at least about 16 to 32 images per GPU, where per-channel statistics are stable and the layer can be folded into the preceding convolution at inference for free. Choose GroupNorm (32 groups is the usual default) when the per-GPU batch is 1 to 8, as in detection and segmentation with large images, because BatchNorm error rises sharply below a batch of about 8. Choose LayerNorm or RMSNorm for any sequence model, where tokens in a batch are not interchangeable and the batch size changes between training and serving. You give up, with BatchNorm, independence between examples (output depends on batch-mates, train and inference behave differently, and synchronising statistics across GPUs adds an all-reduce per layer); with GroupNorm, a few tenths of a point of accuracy against a healthy-batch BatchNorm.
LayerNorm or RMSNorm in a new LLM. Choose RMSNorm: it removes the mean pass and the bias, trains to the same loss in published comparisons, and is what LLaMA, Mistral, Qwen and DeepSeek use. The saving is small in end-to-end time (a few percent of a step) but real, because a norm is memory-bound and every pass over a 4096-wide row costs bandwidth. Choose LayerNorm when you must load or reproduce an existing checkpoint (BERT, GPT-2, ViT) or when your features really carry a useful mean that a shift-invariant layer would erase. You give up shift invariance and the bias term with RMSNorm; nothing measurable at LLM scale.
Pre-norm, post-norm or something in between. Choose pre-norm for anything deeper than about 12 layers or trained with a large learning rate: it trains without warm-up and rarely diverges. Choose post-norm only for shallow, well-tuned recipes (original BERT-base at 12 layers), where it can reach slightly better final quality. Pre-norm's price is that the residual stream grows with depth, so late layers contribute relatively less and the last blocks are partly wasted; fixes that exist are sandwich or peri-norm (an extra norm on the sub-block output), DeepNorm (post-norm with scaled residuals, used to train 1,000-layer models) and QK-norm for attention logits. Pick QK-norm when you see loss spikes at large scale or in low precision.
Fused kernel or plain framework ops. Choose a fused kernel (Triton, Apex, torch.compile) that does residual add, statistics in FP32 and scale in one pass over the row: eager PyTorch launches 5 to 8 small kernels and reads the row several times, so a fused norm is typically 2 to 4 times faster, and at 8 KB per token per norm it matters for decode latency. You give up some debuggability and take on a kernel to maintain. Keep the statistics in FP32 either way; BF16 sums over 4,096 values are visibly wrong.
No normalization at all. Possible in shallow networks (under about 10 layers) with careful initialisation, in ReZero or Fixup style residual scaling, and in normalizer-free ResNets with adaptive gradient clipping. Choose it only when you cannot afford the bandwidth or need exact batch independence and have time to tune; you give up the robustness to learning rate and initialisation that is the reason everyone uses norms.
interview: Why does a transformer use LayerNorm and not BatchNorm? || Batch statistics need a batch of comparable items. Tokens differ in position and sentence, padding distorts the mean, and at inference you may serve one sequence, or a batch that changes every step during generation, so train and test would behave differently. LayerNorm uses only the token's own features, so it is identical at any batch size, needs no running averages and has no cross-GPU synchronisation. The cost is that it cannot exploit cross-example statistics, which does not matter for text.
Where does the norm go in a block and why does it change training stability? || Post-norm puts LN after the residual add, so every gradient path crosses every LN; at initialisation gradients near the output are large and you need warm-up. Pre-norm puts LN at the sub-block input, leaving an un-normalized identity path from output to input, so gradients are well scaled at all depths. The price is that hidden-state norms grow with depth. Almost all current LLMs are pre-norm.
Loss spikes appear after weeks of a large training run. Where do you look in terms of normalization? || Attention logit growth (add QK-norm or a z-loss on the logits), the output-head logits (z-loss), epsilon too small relative to BF16 precision, statistics computed in low precision, and learning-rate or Adam epsilon settings. Check the max pre-softmax logit per layer and the norm of the residual stream per layer; a steady upward drift in either precedes most spikes.
What does the gain gamma do if the layer already outputs unit variance? || It restores representational freedom: without it each feature would be forced to the same scale and mean, which constrains what the next layer can express. With gamma and beta the network can learn any per-feature scale, including undoing the normalization. It also lets the model weight features, and in practice gamma values reveal which dimensions the model treats as large outlier features.
How would you implement and speed up the backward pass? || Save the per-row mean (or just rstd for RMSNorm) from forward. The gradient needs two row-wise reductions, one of dy times gamma and one of dy times x-hat, so a single kernel can compute dx with one read of dy and x per row; dgamma and dbeta need a reduction over all tokens, usually done in two stages to avoid atomics. It is bandwidth-bound, so the target is one read and one write of the activations.
@@+ attention
tradeoffs: Full exact attention or an approximation. Choose exact attention with FlashAttention (2 or 3) up to roughly 32k to 128k tokens: the n^2 term costs about n / (6d) of the rest of a layer, so for d = 4096 it only equals the projections and MLP at about 25k tokens, and the kernel never stores the n x n matrix. Choose a sliding window (4,096 in Mistral 7B), interleaved local and global layers (Gemma, Llama 4 style) or a hybrid with state-space or linear-attention layers (Mamba-style, Jamba) when you need 256k to millions of tokens and cannot pay quadratic compute or a linearly growing cache. You give up exact recall: a window of W with L layers only reaches about W x L tokens through stacking, and a fixed-size recurrent state cannot answer a needle-in-a-haystack lookup as reliably as an explicit cache.
MHA, MQA, GQA or MLA. The KV cache per token is 2 x layers x kv_heads x head_dim x bytes. For an 80-layer model with head_dim 128 in FP16, full multi-head attention with 64 KV heads is 2.6 MB per token; grouped-query attention with 8 KV heads is 320 KB (8x smaller); multi-query attention with one KV head is 40 KB but loses some quality; DeepSeek's latent attention stores about 576 values per layer, around 70 KB per token for a 61-layer model, with quality close to MHA. Choose GQA with 4 to 8 KV groups as the default for a new model, and MLA if you can accept the more complex kernels and up-projection absorption. You cannot change this after training without uptraining, so it is a decision about the serving bill made on day one.
Causal, bidirectional or cross attention. Choose causal masking for generation: it makes the KV cache valid, since earlier tokens never change. Choose bidirectional attention for encoders used in embeddings, classification, reranking and retrieval (BERT-style), where every token should see the whole input, and where there is no generation loop to cache. Choose cross-attention when a second sequence conditions the first (an image or audio encoder feeding a decoder, a text prompt feeding a diffusion model); you pay a second set of projections and a separate cache for the encoder side.
Naive or fused kernel, and how to treat masks. For n up to about 512 and small models, a plain softmax(QK^T)V is fine and easier to debug. At n = 32,768, one head stores 32,768^2 x 2 bytes = 2 GB of scores per layer, which is why FlashAttention is not optional for long contexts: it is exact and uses O(n) memory. You give up flexibility, because custom score modifications (ALiBi, document masks, soft-capping) need support in the kernel; use FlexAttention or a framework-provided mask hook before writing your own CUDA.
When attention is the wrong tool. For a short sequence of 100s of tokens the MLP dominates, so spending effort on attention variants brings little. For very long streams that never need random access to the past (audio, telemetry) a convolution or state-space model has constant memory per step. For tabular or low-dimensional data, a gradient-boosted tree is usually better than a transformer. Self-attention pays off when the relevant pairs are unknown in advance and data is plentiful.
interview: Why divide by the square root of d? What would break if you did not? || With unit-variance q and k components, the dot product has variance d, so scores have standard deviation sqrt(d) (about 11 for d = 128). Softmax over such spread-out numbers is almost one-hot, so gradients to all but the largest score vanish and training stalls or becomes unstable. Dividing by sqrt(d) restores unit variance. The same reasoning motivates QK-norm when logits still grow during training.
Walk through the memory and compute cost of attention for 128k tokens, and how you would serve it. || Compute is 4 n^2 d per layer, so quadratic: going from 8k to 128k multiplies attention FLOPs by 256. A naive n x n matrix per head is 32 GB in FP16, so you need a fused kernel. The KV cache is linear in n: with GQA (320 KB per token for a 70B-class model) that is about 40 GB for one sequence, so concurrency is limited by cache memory, not FLOPs. Options are cache quantization to FP8, sliding-window or hybrid layers, context parallelism (ring attention) and prefix caching.
Explain GQA and what you lose compared with MHA. || Several query heads share one K and one V head, which divides the cache and the K/V projection cost by the group size. In published ablations 8 groups lose very little quality versus MHA, while MQA loses a bit more. What you give up is some capacity for heads to specialise their keys; what you gain is up to 8x less cache read per decoded token, which is the dominant decode cost.
Why does decoding with attention tend to be memory-bound while training is compute-bound? || In training you multiply large Q, K, V blocks for thousands of tokens at once, so each byte loaded supports many FLOPs. In decoding, one new query reads the entire cache for that sequence and does about 2 FLOPs per cached value, an arithmetic intensity of roughly 1, far below the GPU's balance point of 100 to 300 FLOPs per byte. Batching helps weights but not the cache, because each sequence has its own K and V.
How do you extend a model trained on 8k tokens to 64k? || With RoPE, rescale the rotation frequencies (position interpolation, NTK-aware scaling, YaRN) and fine-tune briefly on long documents; models extrapolate poorly without it. Then evaluate with retrieval tasks at several depths, not just perplexity, because perplexity can look fine while recall in the middle of the context fails. Expect serving cost to be dominated by the cache from then on.
@@+ kvcache
tradeoffs: Keep the cache in BF16 or quantize it. Choose FP8 (E4M3) storage when memory limits concurrency: it halves the cache with an evaluation loss typically well under a point, and doubles the number of resident sequences or the context length. Choose INT4 or INT2 schemes (KIVI-style, with keys quantized per channel and values per token because keys have outlier channels) only when you need 4x or more and have checked long-context retrieval, which is where errors show first. Keep BF16 for short contexts where memory is not the limit, and for models that are very sensitive (small models, heavy reasoning). You give up a little accuracy, plus dequantization work in the attention kernel.
Keep everything, drop tokens, or move tokens elsewhere. Keep everything by default: any dropped token is information the model can no longer use. Choose eviction (sliding window, attention sinks with the first 4 tokens plus a recent window as in StreamingLLM, or heavy-hitter selection as in H2O) for unbounded streams where you accept forgetting; it fails when a later question depends on a token that was evicted. Choose offload to CPU or NVMe for conversation history that will be reused: for Llama-3-8B a token is 128 KB, so 8k tokens is 1 GB, which a PCIe 5 link at about 50 GB/s moves in roughly 20 ms, while recomputing the same prefix costs about 0.3 s of GPU time. Loading beats recomputing by around 10x, but loading in every decode step is impossible (HBM is about 50x faster than PCIe).
Cache layout: contiguous or paged. Contiguous preallocation to the maximum length wasted 60 to 80 percent of cache memory in pre-2023 servers, because most requests end far below the limit. Choose paged blocks (16 tokens per block, as in PagedAttention) for any multi-user server: waste falls to a few percent, copy-on-write allows beam search and shared prefixes. You give up a block table lookup in the kernel and a few percent of attention speed, and you must handle fragmentation of block sizes. For a single-user local tool, contiguous is simpler and fine.
Share prefixes across requests or isolate them. Choose prefix caching (hash of the token blocks) when many requests share a long system prompt, tools or document: a hit skips prefill and can cut time to first token by 5 to 10x on 10k-token prompts. The trade-off is memory pinned by cached blocks (managed with LRU eviction) and a timing side channel: a fast response reveals that someone else sent the same prefix. Multi-tenant services should salt the hash per tenant or per key, giving up cross-tenant sharing.
Sizing: batch against context. Take an 80 GB H100 with an 8B model: 16 GB of weights leaves about 60 GB for cache, or about 470,000 tokens at 128 KB each. That is 470 sequences at 1k tokens or only 14 at 32k. Choose FP8 cache, GQA models, more GPUs with tensor parallelism (weights are split, freeing cache) or a disaggregated design when long contexts dominate; choose larger batches when contexts are short. Whatever the choice, decode throughput is set by bytes of cache read per token, not by FLOPs.
interview: Why is a KV cache needed, and what exactly is cached? || Without it every new token recomputes keys and values for the entire prefix, making generation O(n^2) in projections as well as attention. With it, each step computes K and V only for the new token, appends them per layer, and the query attends to the stored ones. Only K and V are cached (not Q or activations) because earlier tokens' K and V never change under causal masking.
Compute the KV cache size for a model with 32 layers, 8 KV heads, head dim 128, FP16, for 4k tokens and 64 concurrent users. || Per token 2 x 32 x 8 x 128 x 2 bytes = 131,072 bytes (128 KB). For 4,096 tokens that is 512 MB per sequence, and 64 users need 32 GB. That exceeds what remains on a 40 GB GPU after weights, so you quantize to FP8 (16 GB), cap concurrency, or add GPUs. It also shows why GQA matters: with 32 KV heads it would be 4x bigger.
Why is decode memory-bound, and what does the cache do to throughput as batch grows? || Each step reads all weights once (shared by the batch) and each sequence's own cache. The weights are amortized over the batch, but cache bytes grow linearly with batch and context, so beyond some batch the step time is dominated by cache reads and throughput flattens. Memory capacity is also exhausted before compute is.
What happens when the cache fills up? || The scheduler must preempt: either swap a sequence's blocks to CPU (cheap if reused soon, uses PCIe) or drop them and recompute the prefill later (no transfer, GPU time). Better is admission control that does not start requests it cannot finish, and a watermark of free blocks. Preemption thrashing is a sign that max concurrency is set too high.
How does speculative decoding interact with the cache? || The draft model has its own cache and the target verifies k tokens in one pass, writing k entries; rejected tokens must be rolled back, which is trivial with paged blocks (just move the length pointer). Extra memory is needed for the draft model's cache, and the benefit shrinks as batch grows and decoding becomes compute-bound.
@@+ contbatch
tradeoffs: Static batching or continuous batching. Choose continuous (iteration-level) batching for any autoregressive generation with varying output lengths, which is nearly all chat and API traffic: in static batching the whole batch waits for the longest sequence, so with output lengths ranging from 20 to 1,000 tokens the GPU is mostly idle on finished slots; published systems (Orca, vLLM) report 2x to over 20x throughput versus naive batching. Choose static batching for single-pass workloads (embeddings, classification, reranking) and offline jobs where you can sort by length, because they have no per-token loop to interleave. You give up simplicity: shapes change every step, CUDA graphs must be captured for bucketed batch sizes, and the scheduler is now part of your latency.
How large a batch. Decode is memory-bound until the batch is large enough to reuse the weights well; on an H100 the crossover for a dense model is on the order of 100 to 300 sequences. Choose max concurrency of 128 to 256 for throughput-oriented serving, with the cache as the real limit; choose 8 to 32 when a tight per-token latency target (say 30 ms) matters more than cost per token. You give up either latency (bigger batch, each step takes longer) or cost (smaller batch, the GPU is underused). Choose speculative decoding for small batches, where spare compute is free, and avoid it at high batch where compute is already busy.
Mixing prefill with decode. A long prompt in the same batch stalls every decode step by the time of its prefill (an 8k prompt on an 8B model is ~0.3 s), which shows up as a latency spike for other users. Choose chunked prefill with a per-step token budget (about 512 to 2,048 tokens) on small and medium deployments: decode tokens ride along with prefill chunks and inter-token latency stays flat. Choose disaggregated prefill and decode pools (DistServe, Splitwise, Mooncake) when you serve at scale with strict time-to-first-token and per-token SLOs and have a fast interconnect for moving the cache; you give up the transfer cost and operational complexity.
Scheduling policy and admission. FCFS is predictable and avoids starvation; choose it by default. Choose priority classes or shortest-predicted-job-first only when you have a trustworthy length predictor and mixed interactive and batch traffic, because it lowers mean latency at the price of starving long requests. Queueing theory sets the limit: as utilization approaches 100 percent, waiting time grows like 1 / (1 - utilization), so keep utilization at 70 to 85 percent or shed load. Choose admission control (reject or queue early) over accepting everything and preempting later, since preemption wastes work (recompute) or bandwidth (swap).
interview: Explain continuous batching and why it improves throughput. || The scheduler runs one forward pass per iteration over all active sequences. When a sequence finishes, its slot and cache blocks are released immediately and a waiting request is admitted at the next iteration, so the batch stays full. In static batching the batch lasts until the slowest sequence completes, wasting slots; with variable output lengths this wastes a large share of GPU cycles.
A user complains that streaming speed is uneven. What do you check? || Prefill chunks from other users interfering with decode steps (check token budget per step), batch size growth raising step time, preemption and recompute due to a full cache, CPU scheduling overhead between steps (should be under 1 ms of a 20 ms step), or the GPU being shared. Look at the distribution of inter-token latency, not the mean, and correlate with scheduler logs.
How do you decide max batch size and max tokens per step? || Measure throughput and p99 inter-token latency at increasing concurrency on real length distributions; pick the knee where throughput gains drop below the latency cost. Cap by cache memory (free blocks) first. Set token budget so a step fits the latency SLO. Revisit when you change model, quantization or context length.
Why is continuous batching harder with tensor parallelism or CUDA graphs? || All ranks must agree on the batch each step, so the scheduler broadcasts it and it must be cheap. CUDA graphs need fixed shapes, so you capture graphs for several batch sizes and pad, wasting some compute. Prefill uses eager mode because token counts vary widely.
How does Little's law help you size a fleet? || Concurrent requests \( L = \text{arrival rate} \times \text{time in system} \). If you receive 50 requests per second and each takes 10 s end to end, you need 500 concurrent slots; divide by per-GPU concurrency (cache-limited, say 100) to get 5 GPUs, plus headroom for 70 to 80 percent target utilization and bursts.
@@+ toolcall
tradeoffs: Native tool calling or prompted JSON. Choose the provider's native tool-calling interface with a model trained for it when you can: the model emits calls in a format it has seen in training, so schema adherence is high (typically 95 percent or better on well-described tools) and parallel calls, call IDs and streaming are supported. Add constrained decoding (grammar or JSON-schema masking) when a malformed call is expensive, since it makes the syntax valid by construction, with a small latency cost and sometimes slightly worse reasoning when the grammar forces early commitment. Choose prompt-based formats (ReAct text, XML tags) for open-weight models without tool training or for portability. You give up robustness and provider features.
Few coarse tools or many fine ones. Every tool definition is paid for in input tokens on every request: 50 tools at 200 tokens each is 10,000 tokens, and selection accuracy drops as the list grows past about 20. Choose a small set of coarse, well-named tools (a run_sql or search tool, a code-execution tool) when tasks are open-ended; choose many narrow tools with strict schemas when each action must be auditable or permissioned separately (payments, infrastructure). Choose tool search or retrieval (load definitions on demand) once you pass about 30 to 50 tools. A general code-execution tool replaces dozens of narrow ones, but widens the security surface.
Parallel or sequential calls. Choose parallel calls when the calls are independent (read three files, query three services): latency becomes the maximum rather than the sum, often a 2 to 3x speed-up for a research step. Choose sequential calls when a later call depends on an earlier result, or when calls mutate shared state and ordering matters. You give up simpler error handling (partial failures need a policy) and you can hit rate limits sooner.
In-process function, local MCP server or remote service. Choose an in-process function for latency-critical, trusted logic: a call takes microseconds, and there is no extra protocol. Choose MCP when tools are reused across several hosts or vendors, or are owned by another team: stdio for local tools (a process start and JSON-RPC over pipes, about milliseconds per call) and streamable HTTP for remote ones, with OAuth. You give up a network or process hop (10 to 200 ms), an authentication surface, and a new route for prompt injection through tool descriptions and results.
Execution policy: auto-run, approval or sandbox. Run read-only, idempotent tools automatically. Require human approval for irreversible or external effects (sending mail, deleting, spending money), but know that approval fatigue sets in after a handful of prompts and users click through. Choose a sandbox (container with namespaces and cgroups, gVisor, Firecracker microVM at 100 to 200 ms start) for model-written code, with no network by default and a time and memory limit. Add idempotency keys when a retry could repeat an effect, and truncate or paginate large results (a 1 MB tool output is 250k tokens that you will pay for again every turn).
interview: Walk through what happens between the model deciding to call a tool and the answer. || The model emits a structured call (name, JSON arguments, ID) and stops. The host parses and validates it against the schema, checks permissions, runs it (possibly in a sandbox with a timeout), and sends the result back as a tool-result message tied to the call ID. The model then continues with the new context and may call again or answer. The model never executes anything itself; all safety lives in the host.
The model calls a tool with a wrong argument. What do you do? || Validate before running and return a precise, machine-readable error (which field, what was expected) as the tool result, not a stack trace; models usually correct themselves in one retry. Cap retries, log the failure for evaluation, and improve the tool description or enum if the same mistake repeats. For critical actions, use constrained decoding or a stricter schema so the invalid call cannot be produced.
How do you defend against prompt injection through tool results? || Treat tool output as untrusted data: it can contain instructions. Limit privilege (least-privilege tokens, read-only by default), separate untrusted content from instructions, require approval for sensitive actions after untrusted content entered the context, scan or sandbox fetched content, and never let a single agent hold both access to private data and an exfiltration channel (the lethal trifecta). No model-side defence is complete, so enforce limits in the host.
How would you evaluate and monitor tool use in production? || Track per-tool call rate, schema-validation failure rate, success rate, latency percentiles, retries and tokens per task; build a test set of tasks with expected tool sequences or end states; replay traces after changing a prompt or tool description. The usual regressions come from description edits and added tools confusing selection.
Why not let the model generate code for everything instead of defining tools? || Code is flexible and composes many steps in one turn (loops, filters, joins), saving round trips and tokens. But it needs a strong sandbox, makes permissions harder to express, and failures are less structured. Tools give typed, auditable, rate-limited actions. A common design is a few tools plus a sandboxed code tool for glue.
@@+ reactloop
tradeoffs: ReAct or plan-then-execute. Choose the interleaved reason, act, observe loop when the environment is unpredictable and each observation changes what you should do next: debugging, web research, operating a codebase. Choose a plan-first design (Plan-and-Solve, ReWOO, a planner with workers) when the steps are known and mostly independent: one planning call replaces N reasoning calls, steps can run in parallel, and cost and latency are lower. You give up adaptivity with a fixed plan, which goes stale after the first surprise; the usual compromise is to plan, execute, and re-plan only when a step fails or returns something unexpected.
One agent or several. A single loop with tools is the default: simplest to debug, one context. Multi-agent systems have shown clear gains on broad, parallelizable research (an orchestrator with sub-agents each searching a branch), but Anthropic reported that agents use about 4x the tokens of a chat and multi-agent systems about 15x, so the task must be worth that. Choose sub-agents to isolate a noisy sub-task and return a short summary (1,000 to 2,000 tokens) to keep the main context clean, or to parallelize; avoid them for tightly coupled work like editing one file, where agents conflict and share too little context.
Step budget, cost and reliability. Errors compound: if each step succeeds 95 percent of the time, a 20-step task succeeds 0.95^20, about 36 percent. So choose short loops with verification after risky steps over long unchecked chains. Cost also compounds: if the whole history is resent every step, total input tokens grow with the square of the number of steps (about N^2 x c / 2), so use prompt caching (cached tokens cost about a tenth), trim stale tool results, and set a hard step limit (10 to 50) plus a loop detector for repeated identical calls. You give up some autonomy for predictability.
Visible thoughts, hidden reasoning or reflection. With reasoning models that think natively (extended thinking, interleaved with tool calls), an explicit Thought text line adds tokens for little benefit; choose native reasoning for hard multi-step tasks and accept more latency and cost. Choose explicit text traces for open-weight models without that training and for audit logs. Reflection (Reflexion-style self-critique) helps mainly when the feedback comes from an external signal: unit tests, compiler errors, a validator. Self-critique without such a signal often does not fix mistakes, so prefer a checkable verifier.
Termination and escalation. Let the model end the loop by answering without a tool call, or by calling an explicit done tool with a structured result (safer for pipelines). Escalate to a human or a stronger model after N failed attempts or when confidence is low; retrying the same action more than 2 or 3 times rarely helps. The feedback-control view warns of two failures: too little gain (the agent ignores errors) and too much (it overreacts and oscillates between two fixes).
interview: Why can an agent loop fail even if each step is mostly right? || Step errors multiply (0.95^20 is about 36 percent), a wrong observation poisons later reasoning, context fills with noise, and the agent may repeat an action in a cycle. Mitigate with verification after key steps, small reversible actions, state checkpoints, loop detection, context compaction and an external success check (tests, assertions) rather than the model's own judgement.
How do you stop an agent from looping forever or running up cost? || Hard limits on steps, tokens, wall time and money per task; detect repeated identical tool calls or no state change; budget remaining shown in the prompt; use caching so cost per step is near linear; and make long tasks resumable from checkpoints so a kill is cheap.
ReAct versus a workflow with fixed steps: when would you choose which? || If you can draw the flowchart in advance, use a workflow: it is cheaper, testable and predictable. Use an agent loop when the path depends on what is found (open-ended debugging, research). Often the right design is a workflow whose individual nodes are small agent loops.
How do you evaluate an agent? || Define end-state checks on realistic tasks (does the test pass, is the record correct), run each task several times because behaviour is stochastic, measure success rate, steps, tokens, time and cost, and inspect failed traces by category (wrong tool, bad args, premature stop, loop). Keep a regression set and rerun after every prompt or tool change.
Where does the control-theory analogy break down? || A thermostat has a known plant and a scalar error. An agent's world is partially observable, the observation is text the model must interpret, feedback can be misleading or adversarial, and actions can be irreversible, so you add safety constraints outside the loop instead of relying on convergence.
@@+ contexteng
tradeoffs: Put information in the window or retrieve it on demand. Choose to place material directly in context when it is small (under roughly 50 to 100k tokens), used repeatedly and stable: with prompt caching, the repeated prefix costs about a tenth of normal input price and is fast. Choose retrieval when the corpus is larger than the window, changes often, or only a tiny fraction is relevant per query. Within retrieval, choose a vector index for fuzzy semantic lookup across millions of documents, and agentic search (grep, file listing, reading on demand) for code and structured corpora: no index to build or keep fresh, but more tool calls and latency. You give up recall with retrieval (a missed chunk is invisible) and tokens with stuffing.
Long context or compaction. Models degrade before the advertised limit (the middle of a long prompt is used worse, and distractors hurt), so a 200k window is not 200k of reliable use. Choose compaction (summarize older turns, keep recent turns and key decisions verbatim) when a session passes about 70 to 80 percent of the window; choose structured notes or memory files outside the context for long tasks that must survive resets; choose sub-agents to explore and return short summaries. You give up exact details, since a summary cannot hold a stack trace or a number you did not know you would need. Keep identifiers, file paths, decisions and open issues, and drop raw tool output.
Ordering for cache hits. Stable content first (system prompt, tool definitions, reference documents), changing content last. A cache read is roughly 10 percent of the input price and a write about 125 percent, and any edit to an earlier token invalidates everything after it. So choose append-only history by default, and rewrite or prune earlier content only when the saving exceeds the lost cache (for example clearing old tool results after the context passes a size threshold). Dynamic few-shot examples chosen per query can improve accuracy a few points but break the cached prefix; put them after the stable part.
Tool results and instructions. Large results (a 20k-token file or query result) swamp the window and are re-billed every turn. Choose paginated or filtered tools, return identifiers and summaries with a way to fetch details, and clear stale results. Keep instructions specific but short: long rule lists are followed less reliably and cost tokens on every call. Separate trusted instructions from untrusted retrieved text with delimiters and a clear statement of the role of each, understanding that this reduces but does not remove prompt-injection risk.
Cost and quality as a budget. Treat tokens like a budget: at 100k input tokens per call and 30 calls, a task reads 3M input tokens; at 2 dollars per million uncached that is 6 dollars, about 0.6 dollars with caching. Measure with an evaluation set before cutting context, because removing tokens saves money but can silently drop accuracy by several points. Choose the smallest context that passes your quality bar, not the largest that fits.
interview: A long-running agent starts forgetting earlier decisions. How do you redesign its context? || Keep a durable, structured state outside the window (task list, decisions, file notes) that is reloaded each turn; compact older turns by summarizing with decisions, constraints and open questions retained verbatim; clear stale tool results; delegate exploratory subtasks to sub-agents that return short summaries; and test with questions that depend on early information to check what survived.
What is lost-in-the-middle and how does it affect prompt layout? || Models tend to use information at the start and end of a long prompt more reliably than in the middle. Put the instructions and the most relevant evidence near the end or beginning, keep irrelevant documents out, rank retrieved chunks by relevance, and ask the model to quote the supporting passage before answering.
RAG or long context for a 5 million token knowledge base? || It does not fit, so retrieve. Use hybrid retrieval (BM25 plus embeddings), rerank to a few thousand tokens, and measure recall at k. For a 50k-token manual that is queried constantly, putting it all in a cached prompt may be simpler and more accurate. The deciding numbers are corpus size versus window, update rate, query volume and cost per call.

Context layout helps but is not a security boundary.
What would you measure to decide how much context to give? || Task success rate and cost as a function of context size on a fixed evaluation set, cache hit rate, latency, and failure categories (missing information versus distraction). @@+ tcp
tradeoffs: TCP or UDP and QUIC. Choose TCP when you need an ordered reliable byte stream that works everywhere, with kernel and NIC offloads (segmentation, checksums) and mature tooling: databases, RPC inside a datacenter, file transfer. Choose QUIC (HTTP/3) for user-facing traffic over lossy or mobile networks: a new connection with TLS 1.3 takes 1 round trip instead of TCP's 2 (0 on resumption), a lost packet blocks only its own stream instead of every request on the connection, and the connection survives a change of IP address. Choose plain UDP for DNS, games and media with their own loss handling. You give up with QUIC higher CPU per byte (user space, often 1.5 to 2x), and that UDP is blocked or throttled on a few percent of networks, so keep a TCP fallback.
Congestion control algorithm. Choose CUBIC (the Linux default) for general traffic: loss-based, widely deployed and fair to other CUBIC flows. Choose BBR on long, high-bandwidth, slightly lossy paths (cross-region replication, video delivery), where loss-based algorithms collapse the window on random loss and BBR has reported multi-fold throughput gains; its early versions were unfair to CUBIC flows and ignored loss, which later versions improved. Choose DCTCP, with ECN marking in the switches, inside a datacenter, which keeps queues to tens of kilobytes and cuts tail latency. You give up uniform behaviour: mixing algorithms on one bottleneck makes shares depend on which one wins.
Latency tuning for small messages. Choose TCP_NODELAY for request and response protocols (RPC, databases): Nagle's algorithm holds a small segment waiting for an ACK, and with delayed ACKs on the other side it produces stalls of up to 40 ms. Write whole messages with one call (or writev) so you do not produce many tiny packets. Choose to leave Nagle on for bulk streams of tiny writes where efficiency matters more than latency. You give up header efficiency: a 1-byte payload in a 54-byte packet is 98 percent overhead.
Buffer and window sizing. The window must cover the bandwidth-delay product: 1 Gbit/s at 100 ms is 12.5 MB in flight, but the Linux receive buffer auto-tunes to a default maximum of about 6 MB, so one stream on a long fat path tops out near 480 Mbit/s until you raise tcp_rmem and tcp_wmem. Choose larger buffers for transcontinental bulk transfers or use parallel streams; choose small buffers and a fair queue discipline to limit bufferbloat for interactive traffic. You give up memory (10,000 connections at 12 MB is 120 GB) and queueing delay. Raising the initial congestion window above the default of 10 segments helps small responses finish in one round trip.
Connection pooling, keepalive and timeouts. Opening a connection costs 1 RTT plus 1 for TLS, and starts in slow start, so choose long-lived pooled connections for calls to the same backend; choose short-lived connections when clients are many and idle (to avoid holding state). You give up freshness: idle connections are silently dropped by load balancers and NATs (often after 60 to 350 s), so set keepalive below that. With no keepalive and unacknowledged data, Linux gives up after about 15 minutes (tcp_retries2), so set TCP_USER_TIMEOUT or an application-level deadline. Short-lived connections leave sockets in TIME_WAIT for 60 s; with about 28,000 ephemeral ports that caps new connections to one destination at roughly 470 per second.
interview: A service sees p99 latency spikes of about 200 ms on RPCs inside a datacenter. What could TCP be doing? || Retransmission timeouts (minimum RTO is 200 ms on Linux) after a tail loss where there are no duplicate ACKs to trigger fast retransmit; incast at a switch (many senders to one receiver overflowing the buffer); a Nagle plus delayed-ACK interaction; or slow start after an idle connection. Check retransmit counters (ss -ti, nstat), enable TCP_NODELAY, consider lower min RTO, ECN with DCTCP, and tail loss probe.
How does TCP know how fast to send? || Two windows: the receiver's advertised window (flow control, protects the receiver's buffer) and the sender's congestion window (protects the network). The sender may have min(cwnd, rwnd) bytes in flight. Cwnd starts at 10 segments, doubles each RTT in slow start, then grows slowly and shrinks on loss or delay signals, depending on the algorithm. Throughput is roughly window / RTT.
Why can a single TCP connection not fill a 10 Gbit/s link between continents? || With 150 ms RTT the bandwidth-delay product is about 190 MB, and default buffer limits and slow start keep the window far smaller. Also any loss cuts a loss-based window sharply, and with 0.01 percent loss the Mathis bound limits a single flow to about 1.22 x MSS / (RTT x sqrt(p)), which is only about 10 Mbit/s at 150 ms and a 1,460-byte MSS. Fixes are bigger buffers, BBR, parallel streams, and clean paths.
What are the problems with HTTP/2 over TCP, and why did HTTP/3 move to QUIC? || HTTP/2 multiplexes many streams over one TCP connection, but TCP delivers bytes in order, so one lost packet stalls every stream (head-of-line blocking), and TCP plus TLS needs several round trips to start. QUIC puts streams and encryption in user space over UDP, so loss affects one stream and handshakes are 1 RTT or 0 with resumption.
What happens when a server crashes and restarts while clients hold connections? || The new kernel has no state for them and answers with RST, so clients see connection reset on their next write; clients that are only reading may wait until a keepalive or timeout detects the failure. Applications need deadlines, reconnection with backoff and idempotent requests.
@@+ vmem
tradeoffs: 4 KB pages or huge pages. A modern core has an L2 TLB of roughly 1,500 entries, which with 4 KB pages covers only about 6 MB; with 2 MB pages the same TLB covers about 3 GB. Choose huge pages (explicit hugetlbfs, or transparent huge pages) for databases with large buffer pools, JVM heaps of tens of GB, in-memory analytics and ML jobs with random access over big arrays: speed-ups of 10 to 30 percent are common, more under virtualization where a TLB miss costs a two-dimensional walk of up to 24 memory references. Stay on 4 KB for small processes, fork-heavy servers and memory-tight containers. You give up memory (internal fragmentation, bloat) and predictability: transparent huge pages can cause compaction stalls, and Redis and several databases advise turning them off because copy-on-write after fork copies 2 MB instead of 4 KB.
Swap, no swap, or compressed memory. Swap to SSD costs about 100 microseconds per fault against 100 nanoseconds for DRAM, a thousandfold. Choose no swap (or very low swappiness) for latency-sensitive servers and databases, where a swapped page means a latency violation and you would rather the process fail or be restarted; choose swap for desktops, batch machines and as a safety margin for rarely touched pages. Choose zram or zswap (compress pages in RAM, usually 2 to 3x) as a middle path costing microseconds per fault. You give up with no swap: the OOM killer is your only response to overcommit, so set container memory limits and monitor, instead of relying on paging.
Memory-mapped files or an explicit buffer pool. mmap is simple and gives zero-copy access through the page cache, which suits read-mostly analytics, search indexes and prototypes. For a serious database, choose its own buffer pool (as in PostgreSQL, MySQL, Oracle): with mmap the OS decides eviction without knowing about transactions, cannot do I/O with error handling at page granularity, stalls threads on page faults, and suffers from TLB shootdown costs when many cores unmap pages (the argument of the 2022 paper "Are you sure you want to use MMAP in your DBMS?"). You give up a lot of code and double-buffering care when you write your own.
Overcommit and fault policy. Linux by default lets processes allocate more virtual memory than exists and backs pages on first touch. Choose the default heuristic or always-overcommit for ordinary servers (fork of a large process would otherwise fail), and strict accounting (vm.overcommit_memory=2) for systems where an OOM kill is unacceptable and the working set is known. For latency-critical code, avoid first-touch faults at request time (a minor fault is about 0.5 to 1 microsecond, a major fault from NVMe about 100 microseconds) by pre-faulting (MAP_POPULATE, touching at startup) and locking with mlock; you give up startup time and resident memory.
When not to use virtual memory. Hard real-time and small embedded systems (no MMU, or physical addressing) need deterministic access times, and a page fault is unbounded. Unikernels and some accelerators use a flat address space for speed. GPU memory is paged in newer systems but the GPU deliberately manages large allocations manually (and uses the same idea as vmem, a block table, in PagedAttention) because a miss costs far more than on a CPU.
interview: Walk through what happens on a memory access that misses the TLB and then page-faults. || The MMU looks up the virtual page number in the TLB, misses, and walks the page table (4 levels on x86-64, 4 memory reads). If the entry is not present, the CPU raises a page fault; the kernel checks that the address is within a valid mapping, allocates a frame (zeroing it, or reading from the file or swap, which is a major fault and may block), updates the page table, and restarts the instruction. The TLB is then filled and later accesses hit.
Why is fork() cheap for a 50 GB process, and what can still go wrong? || fork copies the page tables, not the data, and marks pages read-only (copy-on-write); the child shares frames until a write, then the kernel copies just that page. Problems: copying 50 GB of page tables still takes tens of milliseconds, a write-heavy parent triggers a storm of copy faults (latency spikes in Redis background saves), and with huge pages each fault copies 2 MB. Overcommit settings can make fork fail.
Your service has high CPU in the kernel and many minor faults. How do you investigate? || Use perf and /proc/pid/status or vmstat to see fault rates, check for memory churn (malloc and free returning pages to the OS, then touching again; fix with allocator tuning or arenas), THP compaction (khugepaged, direct compaction), transparent huge page splitting, NUMA balancing faults (numa_balancing scans the address space), and many small mmaps. Pre-fault memory and keep a long-lived allocator pool.
How do virtual machines complicate address translation? || A guest virtual address goes to a guest physical address (guest page table), which goes to a host physical address (nested or extended page tables). A TLB miss then needs up to 24 memory references instead of 4. Hardware caches partial walks, but huge pages in guest and host are the main mitigation, plus keeping the working set smaller.
Why do two processes at the same virtual address not interfere, and how is memory shared between them? || Each process has its own page table root, so identical virtual addresses map to different frames. Sharing is done by mapping the same physical frames into both page tables: shared libraries, shared memory segments, copy-on-write after fork, memory-mapped files. The TLB tags entries with an address-space ID (PCID) so a context switch need not flush it.
@@+ memhier
tradeoffs: Optimize for the cache or for asymptotic complexity. A contiguous array scan streams at 10 to 20 GB/s per core with the hardware prefetcher; a pointer-based structure costs a full memory latency (80 to 100 ns) per node when it misses. So a linear scan of a sorted array beats a binary tree or hash map for up to hundreds or low thousands of elements, and a B-tree with 4 KB or 64 B-aligned nodes beats a binary search tree at any size beyond cache. Choose the asymptotically better structure when n is large and accesses are random (hash index, B-tree); choose a flat, contiguous layout when n is small or the loop is hot. You give up generality and sometimes readability.
Row layout or column layout. Choose structure-of-arrays or columnar when a hot loop reads one or two fields of many records (analytics, simulation, ECS in games): every 64-byte line you load is full of useful values, often a 5 to 10x gain. Choose array-of-structures or row layout when you access most of a record together (OLTP lookups, serialization). Hybrids (PAX pages, small struct-of-arrays blocks of 64 to 1,024 rows) get both. You give up simple code and flexible schema.
Blocking, prefetching or relying on hardware. Choose tiling for dense loops with reuse: matrix multiply with three b x b tiles of doubles fits a 32 KB L1 when b is about 32, and blocked code reaches an order of magnitude over the naive triple loop for large n. Choose software prefetch for pointer chasing where you know the next addresses (hash joins with batched probes, B-tree traversals), usually 1.3 to 2x, and avoid it for sequential or strided access which the hardware prefetcher handles; wrong distances pollute the cache. Choose cache-oblivious recursive layouts when portability matters more than the last 20 percent; choose tuned, cache-aware blocking (BLAS, FFTW with autotuning) when it does not.
Spend on more RAM, faster storage or smarter caching. DRAM is about 100 ns and a few dollars per GB; NVMe about 80 to 100 microseconds and a few cents per GB: a 1,000x latency gap for roughly a 30 to 50x price gap. If a workload has a hot set that fits in memory with a 95 percent hit rate, average access time is 0.95 x 100 ns + 0.05 x 100,000 ns, about 5 microseconds, dominated by the 5 percent misses; raising the hit rate to 99 percent halves that again. Choose RAM for the hot set up to the point where the next gigabyte adds under a percent to the hit rate, and tiered or object storage beyond it. The old five-minute rule (keep data in memory if re-read within minutes) is the same calculation.
Sharing across cores. Cache coherence makes writes to a shared line cost 50 to 100 ns while ownership moves between cores, and false sharing (independent variables on one 64-byte line) can slow a loop 10x or more. Choose per-core or sharded counters and padding to 64 or 128 bytes for write-hot data; choose a shared structure with mostly reads, where lines stay in shared state at no cost. You give up memory (a padded counter per core) and an aggregation step on reads.
interview: How would you find out whether a slow loop is cache-bound? || Measure instead of guessing: perf stat for cache-misses, LLC loads, and cycles per instruction; compare achieved memory bandwidth to the machine peak (STREAM) with a roofline view; vary the problem size and look for a performance cliff when the working set passes L2 or L3 size. Then change the layout or blocking and check that miss counts drop.
Why is traversing a linked list so much slower than an array of the same size? || Array elements are adjacent, so one 64-byte line brings 8 to 16 elements and the prefetcher streams ahead. List nodes are scattered, each load depends on the previous one (no memory-level parallelism), and every hop is a potential DRAM miss of about 100 ns versus about 1 ns per element for the array. The difference is often 10 to 100x despite equal big-O.
What is false sharing and how do you fix it? || Two threads write different variables that happen to share a cache line, so the line ping-pongs between cores even though no data is logically shared. Pad or align hot per-thread variables to line size (64 B, 128 B on some CPUs), use per-thread accumulators merged at the end, and confirm with perf c2c.
How do you choose data structure sizes for the cache hierarchy? || Think in lines and levels: L1 about 32 to 48 KB at 4 cycles, L2 about 1 to 2 MB at 14 cycles, L3 tens of MB at 40 to 70 cycles, DRAM 200+ cycles. Size hot indexes, buffers and tiles to fit in L2 (not L1 only), align nodes to 64 B, pack fields by access pattern, and keep per-request working sets small enough that many concurrent requests do not evict each other.
What changes with multi-socket machines? || NUMA: memory attached to another socket costs about 1.5 to 2x latency and lower bandwidth. Pin threads and allocate memory locally (first-touch policy, numactl), shard data per socket, and avoid sharing write-hot lines across sockets, where coherence traffic crosses the interconnect.
@@+ isolation
tradeoffs: Read committed, snapshot or serializable. Choose read committed (default in PostgreSQL and Oracle) when each statement only needs committed data and your invariants live inside single rows or are protected by constraints: it has the lowest overhead and no read blocking in MVCC systems. Choose snapshot or repeatable read when one transaction needs a consistent view across several queries (reports, multi-step reads) without blocking writers. Choose serializable (PostgreSQL SSI, CockroachDB by default, Spanner) when invariants span rows and are checked in application logic: the on-call doctor example, the minimum-balance check across accounts, the unique-booking check by range. You give up throughput and retries: SSI costs typically single-digit to a few tens of percent in throughput and aborts transactions on conflict, so the application must catch serialization failures (SQLSTATE 40001) and retry; budget 1 to 5 percent retries at typical load.
Locks or optimistic validation. Choose pessimistic locking (SELECT FOR UPDATE, two-phase locking) when contention on the same rows is high and transactions are short: a hot counter under optimistic control produces retry storms where most work is wasted. Choose optimistic concurrency (SSI, version column checks) when conflicts are rare (under a few percent), because readers never block and there are no deadlocks. You give up deadlock handling and lock waits with locks; wasted work and mandatory retry logic with optimism.
Raise the isolation level or fix the one invariant. Often a cheaper local fix is enough: an atomic update (UPDATE ... SET balance = balance - 10 WHERE balance >= 10), a unique constraint or exclusion constraint, a version column with compare-and-swap, an advisory lock around one critical section. Choose these when you know the invariant and it concerns one or a few rows; choose serializable when the set of invariants is large or unknown and correctness matters more than speed (financial ledgers, inventory with many rules). You give up generality with local fixes: each new feature needs someone to reason about interleavings again.
Single node or distributed. Strict serializability across regions (Spanner with commit wait of about 7 ms, plus cross-region round trips of 50 to 150 ms) is the safe choice for money and inventory; read committed or snapshot with per-region reads is right for feeds and counters. Choose bounded-staleness or follower reads for read-heavy workloads that tolerate seconds of lag. Every step toward stronger guarantees across regions costs at least one wide-area round trip per transaction.
Long transactions and snapshots. A snapshot holds old row versions; a transaction open for minutes or hours blocks vacuum or purge, bloating tables and indexes (the classic idle in transaction incident). Choose short transactions (well under a second), batch large updates into chunks, and run long reports on a replica or a snapshot export. You give up an easy single-transaction migration or report.
interview: Give an example of write skew and which isolation levels prevent it. || Two doctors on call; the rule is at least one must remain. Each transaction reads that two are on call, then removes itself; under snapshot isolation both see two, both commit, and zero remain, because they wrote different rows. Read committed and snapshot allow it; serializable (or an explicit lock on the shared fact, or a constraint) prevents it.
What is the difference between repeatable read in MySQL and in PostgreSQL? || PostgreSQL's repeatable read is snapshot isolation (a fixed snapshot for the whole transaction, with first-updater-wins errors on conflicting writes). MySQL InnoDB's repeatable read uses a snapshot for plain reads but locking reads and updates see the latest committed data, and it uses gap locks to prevent phantoms in some cases, so lost updates and other anomalies behave differently. Do not assume a level name means the same thing across databases.
How does a database implement serializable without locking everything? || Serializable snapshot isolation tracks read-write dependencies between concurrent transactions and aborts one when it finds a dangerous structure (two adjacent read-write edges in a cycle). Readers do not block writers, but some transactions abort and must retry. Alternatives are strict two-phase locking with predicate or range locks (blocking, deadlock-prone) and timestamp ordering.
An engineer proposes SERIALIZABLE everywhere. What do you say? || It is the simplest correctness story, and if the workload has low contention, the cost is acceptable. But hot rows will cause aborts, every call site needs a retry loop with idempotent side effects (no emails sent inside the transaction), and long transactions become more likely to fail. I would set it for the business-critical paths, measure abort rate and latency, and use read committed with targeted constraints elsewhere.
How do you debug a deadlock or serialization failure in production? || Read the database's log of the deadlock (which statements and locks), find the inconsistent ordering of row access, and make all code paths take locks in one order or lock coarser. Keep transactions short, add the right indexes (a missing index widens locks), and retry the victim with backoff.
@@+ gc
tradeoffs: Tracing GC, reference counting or ownership. Choose a tracing collector (JVM, Go, .NET, JavaScript) when programs share complex object graphs and developer time matters: allocation is a pointer bump of a few nanoseconds, and cycles and shared structures need no thought. You pay with pauses, CPU used by the collector, and extra memory; the classic Hertz and Berger study found tracing GC needed a heap several times the live data to match explicit freeing. Choose reference counting (Swift, CPython, C++ shared_ptr) when predictable, immediate release matters, at the cost of an atomic increment on each copy and leaks from cycles. Choose ownership and regions (Rust, arenas per request) for the lowest latency and memory overhead, giving up some convenience for graphs and shared mutable structures.
Which collector. On the JVM, choose Parallel GC for batch jobs where only total throughput matters and multi-second pauses on large heaps are tolerable. Choose G1 (the default since Java 9) for general services with heaps from a few GB to tens of GB and a pause target of 100 to 200 ms. Choose ZGC or Shenandoah when p99 latency matters or heaps are large (tens of GB to terabytes): pauses stay under a millisecond or a few milliseconds regardless of heap size, at the cost of roughly 5 to 15 percent lower throughput, extra CPU for concurrent threads and more memory headroom. Go's collector is concurrent and non-generational and trades CPU for pause time too.
Heap size. A larger heap means fewer collections and less CPU on GC; a smaller one means shorter full-collection pauses (for non-concurrent collectors) and less memory cost. A practical rule is to keep live data under 30 to 50 percent of the heap so the collector has room, to set min and max heap equal for servers, and to size young generation to hold the objects of several requests so most die young (the generational hypothesis: typically over 90 percent of objects). Remember that heap is not process memory: metaspace, thread stacks, direct buffers and native libraries add up, so size -Xmx to about 70 to 75 percent of a container limit or you get OOM kills that are not Java OutOfMemoryErrors.
Tune the collector or allocate less. First reduce allocation rate and live size: avoid boxing, use primitive arrays, reuse buffers for large or long-lived objects, stream instead of materializing. Object pools usually hurt with a generational collector for small objects (old objects pointing to young ones cost write-barrier and card-marking work) but help for large buffers. Move huge caches off-heap (direct buffers, Arrow, RocksDB, Chronicle) so the collector does not scan hundreds of GB. You give up manual lifetime management and leak risk off-heap, in exchange for pauses that no longer scale with data size.
Diagnosing. Not every stall is GC: safepoints for other reasons, page faults, CPU throttling in containers, and noisy neighbours look similar. Enable GC logs, watch allocation rate, promotion rate and pause distribution, and correlate p99 latency with them before changing flags; change one flag at a time against a load test, because defaults are good and folklore flags often are not.
interview: Why do generational collectors work, and when do they fail? || Most objects die young, so collecting a small young generation frequently is cheap: the cost is proportional to live objects, not to garbage, and you only copy survivors. Old objects that point to young ones are tracked with a remembered set or card table. They fail when many objects live a medium time (caches, buffers that survive a few collections get promoted and clog the old generation) or when the live set is very large.
Your Java service has 2-second pauses every few minutes. What do you check? || GC logs: is it a full collection or mixed collection failure (to-space exhausted), humongous allocations (G1 big arrays), heap too small or live set too large, promotion failure, or a memory leak raising the old gen. Remedies include more headroom, fixing leaks, avoiding huge arrays, switching to ZGC or Shenandoah, or reducing allocation. Also rule out safepoint stalls and swapping.
How can GC be concurrent without being wrong? || Mutators and collector run together, so the collector must not miss an object a mutator hides: write barriers (G1, Shenandoah) or load barriers with coloured pointers (ZGC) record or fix up references as they change, and the collector keeps the tri-colour invariant (no black object points to a white one). That is what makes sub-millisecond pauses possible, at the price of work on each reference access.
GC versus Rust ownership versus manual malloc: how would you choose for a latency-sensitive service? || If p99 is critical and the team accepts the cost, Rust or C++ with arenas gives the tightest, most predictable latency and lowest memory. If most code is business logic with shared structures, a JVM with ZGC meets a few-millisecond SLO with far more productivity. Decide with a benchmark of the real workload and the actual p99 target, including memory cost.
What are the signs of a memory leak in a GC language and how do you find it? || Live set after each full collection rising steadily. Take heap dumps or use allocation profiling (JFR, async-profiler, pprof), look at the dominant retainer paths: static maps, listeners never unregistered, thread locals, caches without bounds, classloader leaks.
@@+ linalg
tradeoffs: Direct or iterative solvers. To solve \( Ax = b \), choose a direct method (Cholesky for symmetric positive definite, LU otherwise) when the matrix is dense and n is up to about 10,000 to 50,000: cost is \( O(n^3) \) (a 10,000 x 10,000 solve is about 7 x 10^11 FLOPs, a second or so on a GPU) and the answer is accurate to the conditioning. Choose iterative methods (conjugate gradient, GMRES) when the matrix is sparse or too large to factor: each step costs \( O(\text{nnz}) \), and CG needs about \( \sqrt{\text{condition number}} \) iterations, so an ill-conditioned problem needs a preconditioner. You give up exactness and a guaranteed iteration count. Never form the inverse explicitly: it is slower, less accurate and destroys sparsity.
Least squares: normal equations, QR or SVD. The normal equations \( (A^\top A)\, x = A^\top b \) are the fastest, about \( mn^2 \) FLOPs, but they square the condition number: with condition number 10^4 in FP32 (precision 6 x 10^-8) the squared value 10^8 leaves no correct digits. Choose QR (about \( 2mn^2 \)) as the default, SVD (several times more expensive) when the matrix may be rank-deficient or you need the numerical rank, and normal equations with a ridge term for well-conditioned, tall problems solved at scale (distributed regression). You give up speed for accuracy; use FP64 when conditioning is uncertain.
Exact or low-rank approximation. Choose the full SVD or eigendecomposition for matrices up to a few thousand dimensions. Choose truncated or randomized SVD (Halko et al.: multiply by a random matrix, orthogonalize, then do a small SVD; cost about O(mn log k)) for huge matrices when you want the top k components: recommendation factors, PCA on millions of rows, compressing a weight matrix. Low rank is a good model when singular values decay fast: LoRA with rank 8 to 64 works because fine-tuning updates have low intrinsic rank, and a d x d weight (4096^2 = 16.8M) becomes 2 x 4096 x 16 = 131k parameters (0.8 percent). It breaks down when the spectrum is flat (noise-like data, tasks that need large changes), where the top k components capture a small share of the energy and error is large.
Dense, sparse or structured. Sparse formats (CSR, COO) beat dense when density is below roughly 1 to 10 percent, but irregular memory access means they often reach 5 to 10 percent of peak FLOPs, so a matrix 30 percent dense is faster as dense. GPUs reward structure: 2:4 structured sparsity gives up to 2x on tensor cores, block-sparse patterns map to tiles, and Kronecker, circulant (FFT-based) and low-rank forms cut cost to O(n log n) or O(nk). Choose the structure that matches the hardware, not just the sparsity count.
Precision and the limits of the linear model. Floating-point error in a solve scales like the condition number times the machine epsilon: FP64 about 10^-16, FP32 6 x 10^-8, BF16 about 4 x 10^-3. Neural-network matmuls tolerate BF16 or FP8 with FP32 accumulation because training is noisy; ill-conditioned solves, covariance inversion and eigenvalue problems do not. Linear algebra also assumes linearity: a Jacobian is only a local approximation, and a deep network's behaviour far from the point is not captured by it, nor are heavy-tailed, non-Gaussian structures captured by second-moment methods like PCA.
interview: Why are matrix multiplies the core of GPU and accelerator design? || Multiplying two n x n matrices does 2n^3 FLOPs on 3n^2 numbers, so arithmetic intensity grows with n, and with tiling data loaded into fast memory is reused many times. That makes matmul compute-bound rather than memory-bound, so hardware (tensor cores, systolic arrays) can be built to sustain near-peak throughput. Most of a transformer's FLOPs are matmuls for the same reason.
How would you run nearest-neighbour search over 10 million 768-dimensional vectors? || Normalize vectors so cosine similarity is a dot product. Brute force is a matrix-vector product: 10M x 768 x 4 bytes = 31 GB in FP32 and 15 GFLOPs per query; on a GPU, bandwidth-limited at about 10 ms per query, and a batch of queries makes it a matrix-matrix product that amortizes the read. For lower latency or larger sets, use quantization (product quantization, FP16 or INT8) and approximate indexes (HNSW, IVF), trading recall for speed.
When does PCA fail, or the low-rank assumption not hold? || PCA finds linear directions of maximal variance, so it fails for manifolds that are curved, for data whose important signal has low variance, for heavy-tailed data and when features are not scaled. Check the explained-variance curve; a flat curve says low rank is not a good approximation.
Why does the condition number matter in practice, for example in training? || It measures how much errors and small changes are amplified; for the loss Hessian it controls how much gradient descent zig-zags (convergence needs about condition-number iterations); normalization, adaptive optimizers (Adam) and preconditioning exist to reduce it. For numerical solvers it determines how many digits you lose.
How is PageRank or an eigenvector computed at web scale? || Power iteration: repeatedly multiply a vector by the sparse link matrix (with damping), which converges at a rate set by the gap between the first and second eigenvalues (about 0.85 per iteration with damping 0.85, so 50 to 100 iterations). Each iteration is a sparse matrix-vector product, easily distributed (MapReduce or Pregel-style) and cheap in memory.
