@@+ recsys
history: Collaborative filtering began with Xerox PARC's Tapestry (1992) and GroupLens (1994). Amazon's item-to-item collaborative filtering (2003) scaled recommendations to millions of products. The Netflix Prize (2006–2009) made matrix factorization famous. Deep learning brought YouTube's two-tower candidate generation and ranking network (2016), Google's Wide & Deep (2016), and large embedding-based systems at Meta (DLRM, 2019), TikTok and others. Recent work explores generative and transformer-based sequential recommenders.
practice: Structure systems in stages: candidate generation (cheap retrieval of thousands of items, often by approximate nearest-neighbour search over embeddings), ranking (a richer model scoring hundreds) and re-ranking for diversity, freshness and business rules. Evaluate offline with time-based splits, but trust online A/B tests, since offline metrics often disagree with user behaviour. Watch for feedback loops (the system only learns from what it showed) and reserve some exploration traffic.

@@+ cv
history: Computer vision began as a 1966 MIT summer project expected to take a few months. Decades of hand-designed features followed: edge detectors, SIFT (1999), HOG (2005), with classifiers on top. ImageNet (2009, Fei-Fei Li) and AlexNet (2012) launched deep learning; ResNets (2015), object detectors (R-CNN family, YOLO), segmentation (U-Net, Mask R-CNN) and Vision Transformers (2020) followed. CLIP (2021) and Segment Anything (2023) brought foundation models to vision, and multimodal language models now read images directly.
practice: Start from a pre-trained backbone and fine-tune; collect data that covers the real conditions (lighting, devices, angles) and label edge cases deliberately. Evaluate per slice (by camera, location, object size) rather than one average. Test robustness to compression and noise, and monitor for drift when cameras or environments change.

@@+ nlp
history: NLP started with rule-based systems and machine translation experiments in the 1950s (the Georgetown–IBM demonstration, 1954). Statistical NLP (1990s) used n-gram language models, hidden Markov models and statistical machine translation (IBM models). Word embeddings (word2vec 2013), sequence-to-sequence networks with attention (2014–2015), transformers (2017) and pre-trained models (BERT and GPT, 2018) transformed the field; large language models now handle most NLP tasks with prompting or light fine-tuning.
practice: For a new text task, try a strong LLM with good prompts and examples first, then decide whether a smaller fine-tuned model is cheaper at your volume. Build an evaluation set with real examples and edge cases (other languages, slang, long inputs). For classification and extraction, require structured outputs and validate them. Watch tokenization costs for non-English text.

@@+ diffusion
history: Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues introduced diffusion probabilistic models in 2015; Song and Ermon's score-based models (2019) and Ho, Jain and Abbeel's DDPM (2020) made them competitive. Guided diffusion beat GANs on image quality (2021); DALL·E 2, Imagen and Stable Diffusion (2022, latent diffusion) brought text-to-image to the public. Diffusion transformers (DiT, 2023) and flow matching scaled to video (Sora, 2024, and successors), audio and 3D.
practice: Use pre-trained models and adapt them (LoRA, ControlNet-style conditioning) rather than training from scratch. Control cost with fewer sampling steps (distilled or consistency models) and lower resolution previews. Evaluate with human preference and task-specific checks, not only automatic scores. Address content provenance (watermarking, metadata) and licensing of training data and outputs.

@@+ gpucomp
history: GPUs began as fixed-function graphics accelerators; programmable shaders (2001) and CUDA (2007) made them general-purpose parallel computers. Researchers found them ideal for neural networks (2009–2012), and NVIDIA added tensor cores (2017) and low-precision formats specifically for deep learning. AMD's ROCm, Google's TPUs (2016), and other accelerators followed; compilers such as Triton (2019–2021) made custom GPU kernels easier to write.
practice: Think in terms of arithmetic intensity and memory traffic: fuse operations, use mixed precision and batch work to keep tensor cores busy. Profile with vendor tools before optimizing, and check whether you are compute-, memory- or communication-bound. Treat GPU capacity as a scheduled, shared resource, and monitor utilization per job, since idle GPUs are among the most expensive waste in modern infrastructure.

@@+ hpc
history: Supercomputing (Cray-1, 1976) and parallel scientific computing gave rise to the Message Passing Interface (MPI, 1994), whose collective operations (broadcast, reduce, all-reduce) are now the backbone of distributed deep learning. Baidu brought ring all-reduce to deep learning (2017), Horovod popularized it, and NVIDIA's NCCL became the standard collective library for GPUs. AI training clusters with tens of thousands of GPUs and high-speed fabrics now rival or exceed traditional supercomputers.
practice: Match parallelism to the network topology: keep the most communication-heavy splits (tensor parallelism) inside a server's NVLink domain, and data parallelism across servers. Overlap communication with computation and use gradient bucketing. Monitor collective performance per link; one slow or flaky link can stall an entire job, so health checks and fast replacement of bad nodes matter.

@@+ cpucomp
history: Microprocessors went from the Intel 4004 (1971) to pipelined, superscalar and out-of-order designs (Pentium Pro, 1995). Clock speeds rose rapidly until about 2005, when power limits ended frequency scaling and multicore chips took over. SIMD extensions (MMX 1997, SSE, AVX, AVX-512, ARM NEON and SVE) added data parallelism. ARM-based server and laptop chips (AWS Graviton 2018, Apple M1 2020) challenged x86 on performance per watt, and RISC-V offers an open instruction set.
practice: Before buying more CPUs, profile: many programs are limited by memory access or locks rather than core speed. Write cache-friendly code (contiguous data, fewer pointers), let compilers vectorize simple loops, and parallelize with work-stealing runtimes. When comparing instance types, benchmark your own workload, since ARM versus x86 and core counts versus clock speeds change results.

@@+ webarch
history: Tim Berners-Lee created the web at CERN in 1989–1991 (HTTP, HTML, URLs). The LAMP stack (Linux, Apache, MySQL, PHP) powered the early web; AJAX (2005) and single-page applications made pages interactive; Node.js (2009) brought event-loop servers; REST (Fielding, 2000) and later GraphQL (2015) shaped APIs. CDNs, HTTP/2 (2015) and HTTP/3 (2022) improved delivery, and frameworks such as React (2013) and server-side rendering patterns (Next.js) defined modern front ends.
practice: Optimize for the user's critical path: fewer round trips, cached and compressed assets, server-side rendering or streaming for first content, and measured Core Web Vitals. Keep APIs versioned and documented, put stateless application servers behind load balancers, and push static content to a CDN. Secure by default: HTTPS everywhere, secure cookies, and a content security policy.

@@+ vcs
history: Early version control locked files (SCCS 1972, RCS 1982); CVS (1990) and Subversion (2000) used a central server. Distributed systems (BitKeeper, then Git and Mercurial in 2005) gave every developer a full copy of history; Linus Torvalds wrote Git in about two weeks after Linux lost its BitKeeper licence. GitHub (2008) added pull requests and social coding, and Git became nearly universal. Large companies use monorepos with custom systems (Google's Piper, Meta's Sapling).
practice: Keep commits small and focused with clear messages; protect main branches with required reviews and CI. Use trunk-based development with short-lived branches and feature flags for large changes. Never commit secrets (use scanning tools), store large binaries in LFS or artifact stores, and sign commits or tags where supply-chain security matters.

@@+ nosql
history: Google's Bigtable (2006) and Amazon's Dynamo (2007) papers showed alternatives to relational databases for web-scale workloads. Open-source systems followed: Cassandra (Facebook, 2008), HBase, Riak, Voldemort, and document stores such as MongoDB (2009) and CouchDB. The 'NoSQL' label (2009) covered key–value, wide-column, document and graph stores. Many later added SQL-like query languages, secondary indexes and transactions, and DynamoDB (2012) became a widely used managed key–value service.
practice: Model data around access patterns: design keys so each query reads one partition, and denormalize deliberately. Understand the consistency settings (quorum levels, read-your-writes) and pick them per operation. Avoid hot partitions with well-distributed keys, plan for tombstones and compaction in Cassandra-style stores, and keep a relational or analytical store for queries the key–value model cannot serve.

@@+ newsql
history: Michael Stonebraker and colleagues argued that one-size-fits-all databases were obsolete (2005–2007), and H-Store/VoltDB (2008) showed fast in-memory OLTP. Google's Spanner (2012) delivered globally distributed SQL transactions with TrueTime, and F1 ran Google's ad business on it. Open-source and commercial systems followed: CockroachDB (2015), TiDB (2015), YugabyteDB (2016), and cloud services such as Amazon Aurora (2014, with distributed storage) and Spanner as a service.
practice: Choose distributed SQL when you need SQL transactions beyond one machine's capacity or across regions; otherwise a single well-tuned PostgreSQL with replicas is simpler. Design primary keys to avoid hot ranges (no monotonically increasing keys without hashing or bucketing). Place leaders near the users who write most, and measure cross-region latency on every write path.

@@+ graphdb
history: Network databases (CODASYL, 1960s–1970s) stored links between records before the relational model replaced them. Modern graph databases arrived with Neo4j (2007) and the property-graph model, query languages Cypher (2011) and Gremlin, and RDF triple stores with SPARQL for the semantic web. Large-scale graph processing systems (Pregel 2010, GraphX, Giraph) and knowledge graphs (Google's 2012 Knowledge Graph) followed; GQL became an ISO standard query language in 2024.
practice: Use a graph database when queries traverse many relationships of variable depth (fraud rings, recommendations, network dependencies); for simple joins a relational database is fine. Model relationships with properties and direction deliberately, index entry points, and watch for super-nodes (nodes with millions of edges) that slow traversals. For large batch graph analytics, use graph frameworks over a lakehouse instead.

@@+ storageengine
history: Berkeley DB (1991) was an early embedded key–value engine. SQLite (2000) became the most deployed database engine in the world, inside phones, browsers and applications. LevelDB (Google, 2011) and RocksDB (Facebook, 2012) brought LSM trees to embedded use; LMDB (2011) offered a copy-on-write B-tree; WiredTiger (2014) became MongoDB's engine. Distributed databases increasingly embed these engines (TiKV and many others on RocksDB, CockroachDB on its own Pebble).
practice: Choose the engine by workload: B-tree engines for read-heavy and range workloads with predictable latency, LSM engines for write-heavy ingestion. Benchmark with realistic key and value sizes, read–write mix and data size larger than memory. Tune caches and compaction, monitor write and space amplification, and test crash recovery explicitly.

@@+ langruntime
history: FORTRAN's compiler (1957) showed high-level languages could be efficient. Lisp (1958) introduced garbage collection and an interpreter; Smalltalk and Self (1980s) pioneered JIT techniques; the JVM (1995) and .NET CLR (2002) made managed runtimes mainstream; JavaScript engines (V8, 2008) brought fast JIT compilation to browsers. LLVM (2003) provided a reusable compiler infrastructure behind Clang, Rust, Swift and many others; WebAssembly (2017) offered a portable compilation target.
practice: Know your runtime's performance model: allocation and GC behaviour, JIT warm-up, threading and concurrency model, and how native code is called. Measure with the runtime's own profilers. Pick languages by team skills and ecosystem first, and by runtime characteristics (start-up time, memory use, latency predictability) for the specific service.

@@+ dimmodel
history: Ralph Kimball's 'The Data Warehouse Toolkit' (1996) set out dimensional modelling: facts at a declared grain, surrounded by descriptive dimensions in star schemas, with techniques such as slowly changing dimensions. It competed with Bill Inmon's normalized enterprise warehouse but became the dominant style for analytics. Modern tools (dbt, semantic layers, BI tools) still assume star schemas, even on lakehouses, while wide denormalized tables ('one big table') became common for real-time OLAP.
practice: Declare the grain of every fact table first ('one row per ad impression'), and keep measures additive where possible. Use surrogate keys and slowly changing dimension type 2 when history of attributes matters. Conform shared dimensions (date, customer, campaign) across fact tables so metrics agree. Document metric definitions in one semantic layer instead of in each dashboard.

@@+ datamesh
history: Zhamak Dehghani introduced data mesh in 2019 (at Thoughtworks) as a response to central data teams becoming bottlenecks: treat data as a product owned by domain teams, with a self-serve platform and federated governance. Data contracts (popularized from about 2022) formalized the producer–consumer agreement: schemas, semantics, quality and service levels, often enforced in CI and pipelines.
practice: Start with contracts for the few datasets many teams depend on: schema, meaning of each field, freshness and quality guarantees, owner and change process. Enforce them automatically (schema checks in CI, data-quality checks in pipelines). Build the central platform so domain teams can publish data products easily; without a good platform, data mesh turns into duplicated effort and inconsistent data.

@@+ multitenancy
history: Time-sharing systems in the 1960s served many users on one computer. Salesforce (1999) popularized multi-tenant software-as-a-service with a shared database schema and metadata-driven customization. Cloud providers built multi-tenancy into everything, from hypervisors to managed databases, and research on performance isolation (noisy neighbours, fair scheduling) followed.
practice: Choose an isolation model per risk: shared tables with tenant IDs (cheapest), schema or database per tenant, or separate clusters for the largest or most sensitive tenants. Enforce tenant filters in one place (row-level security, query layer) rather than in every query. Apply per-tenant quotas and rate limits, track cost per tenant, and test that one tenant's load cannot degrade others.

@@+ durableexec
history: Workflow engines for business processes (BPM, 1990s–2000s) and AWS Simple Workflow (2012) recorded progress of long-running processes. Uber's Cadence (2017) and its successor Temporal (2019) introduced durable execution for ordinary code: workflow functions whose every step is recorded in an event history and replayed after failures. Azure Durable Functions, AWS Step Functions and newer systems (Restate, DBOS) offer variants of the same idea.
practice: Keep workflow code deterministic (no direct random numbers, clocks or network calls; use the framework's APIs for those) and put side effects in activities with retries and idempotency keys. Version workflows carefully, since running instances replay old code paths. Use durable execution for multi-step business processes, payments and long-running agent tasks, and keep each workflow's history bounded.

@@+ cicd
history: Extreme Programming (late 1990s) popularized continuous integration; CruiseControl (2001) and Hudson/Jenkins (2005/2011) automated builds and tests. Continuous delivery (Humble and Farley's book, 2010) and DevOps practices made deploying many times a day normal, measured by the DORA metrics (2014 onwards). Hosted CI (Travis, CircleCI, GitHub Actions 2019) and progressive delivery (feature flags, canaries) became standard.
practice: Keep the main branch always releasable: fast unit tests on every change, integration tests in CI, and automated deployment with canaries and quick rollback. Track DORA metrics (deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate, time to restore). Add deterministic simulation, property-based and chaos tests for distributed systems, where ordinary tests miss rare interleavings.

@@+ blockchain
history: Haber and Stornetta's timestamping (1991) chained hashes of documents; Bitcoin (2008–2009) combined a hash-linked chain with proof of work to create a decentralized currency. Ethereum (2015) added smart contracts. Permissioned ledgers (Hyperledger Fabric) targeted enterprises; Ethereum moved to proof of stake (2022) and layer-2 rollups using zero-knowledge proofs scaled transaction throughput. Stablecoins and tokenization became the main real-world uses.
practice: Use a blockchain only when parties who do not trust each other need a shared, tamper-evident record without a central operator; otherwise an ordinary database with audit logs is cheaper and faster. Treat smart-contract code as high-risk: audits, formal verification and bug bounties. Manage keys with hardware or multi-party custody, since lost or stolen keys are unrecoverable.

@@+ mediastream
history: Streaming began with RealPlayer (1995) and proprietary protocols (RTSP, Flash's RTMP). Apple's HTTP Live Streaming (2009) and MPEG-DASH (2012) delivered video as small HTTP segments with adaptive bitrate, which scaled through ordinary CDNs. Codecs advanced from H.264 (2003) to HEVC (2013) and the royalty-free AV1 (2018). Netflix, YouTube and connected-TV platforms made streaming the dominant way to watch television; low-latency variants (LL-HLS, CMAF chunks) narrowed the delay for live sports.
practice: Build encoding ladders per title or per scene rather than one fixed ladder, and pick codecs by device support (H.264 everywhere, HEVC and AV1 where supported). Measure quality of experience directly: start-up time, rebuffering ratio, average bitrate and perceptual quality (VMAF). Use a CDN with origin shielding, and design ad insertion (server-side or client-side) so ad breaks do not cause rebuffering.

@@+ identity
history: Third-party cookies (late 1990s) let ad networks recognize browsers across sites. Mobile advertising IDs (Apple IDFA 2012, Google AAID) and cross-device graphs (Tapad, Drawbridge, from 2010) linked phones, computers and TVs. Privacy regulation (GDPR 2018, CCPA 2020, India's DPDP Act 2023) and platform changes (Safari's tracking prevention from 2017, Apple's App Tracking Transparency 2021) reduced available signals, shifting the industry toward first-party data, hashed email identifiers, clean rooms and household-level identity for CTV.
practice: Keep identity data minimal and consented; record the source and consent basis of every identifier. Separate deterministic links (logins, hashed emails) from probabilistic ones and store confidence. Guard against over-merging with limits on component size and regular audits. Implement deletion across identity graphs and all derived audiences, and prefer clean-room matching when sharing with partners.

@@+ experimentation
history: Randomized controlled trials go back to Fisher's agricultural experiments (1920s–1935). Online A/B testing spread with web companies in the 2000s; Google, Microsoft (Ron Kohavi's team), Amazon and later Netflix and LinkedIn built platforms running thousands of experiments a year. Techniques such as CUPED variance reduction (2013), sequential testing and switchback or geo experiments for marketplaces and advertising extended the toolkit.
practice: Decide the primary metric, minimum detectable effect and sample size before starting, and do not stop early when results look good (or use sequential methods designed for peeking). Check sample-ratio mismatch and guardrail metrics on every test. Use CUPED or similar to reduce variance, and use geo or switchback designs when users interact or share budgets, as in advertising and marketplaces.

@@+ reasoningmodels
history: Chain-of-thought prompting (2022) showed that letting models write out reasoning improved accuracy. OpenAI's o1 (September 2024) was trained with reinforcement learning to reason at length before answering, and DeepSeek-R1 (January 2025) showed similar results openly, using reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards and GRPO. Anthropic, Google and others released models with extended or adjustable thinking, and test-time compute became a scaling axis alongside model size and data.
practice: Use reasoning models for multi-step problems (maths, code, planning, complex analysis) and cheaper standard models for simple tasks; route between them. Set thinking budgets to balance quality, latency and cost. Evaluate on your own tasks, since benchmark gains do not always transfer, and remember that visible reasoning text may not faithfully reflect the model's actual process.

@@+ multimodal
history: Early multimodal systems paired separate image and text models (image captioning with CNN plus RNN, 2014–2015). CLIP (2021) aligned images and text in one embedding space; Flamingo (2022) and LLaVA (2023) connected vision encoders to language models; GPT-4V (2023), Gemini (natively multimodal, December 2023) and Claude 3 (2024) brought image understanding to mainstream assistants. Audio and video understanding and generation followed, with models that take and produce several modalities in one network.
practice: Test multimodal models on your actual documents and images (charts, tables, screenshots, scanned forms), since quality varies widely by content type. Provide text alongside images when available (OCR, alt text, metadata) to reduce errors. Resize images to what the model needs to control token costs, and verify extracted numbers, because models can misread small text.

@@+ declpipelines
history: Materialized views in databases (from the 1980s research on view maintenance, in commercial systems from the late 1990s) kept query results up to date. Data-engineering tools brought the idea to pipelines: dbt (2016) made SQL models with dependencies the standard way to build warehouses; Delta Live Tables (2021–2022) added declarative streaming tables and expectations on Databricks; Materialize and other streaming databases maintained views incrementally. Spark Declarative Pipelines (Spark 4.1, December 2025) brought the approach to open-source Spark.
practice: Declare datasets as queries with clear inputs, and let the engine own scheduling, retries and dependencies. Write data-quality expectations next to each definition. Prefer queries that can be maintained incrementally (avoid non-deterministic functions and full-table window functions where possible), and check refresh plans to confirm incremental processing actually happens. Use dry runs in CI to catch broken dependencies before deployment.
