@@ logic
value: Formal logic is the language of exact reasoning: propositions, connectives, quantifiers and rules of inference. It underlies programming conditions, SQL WHERE clauses, type systems, database theory, verification tools and the very idea that a machine can reason by following rules.
how: Propositional logic combines true/false statements with AND, OR, NOT and IMPLIES. First-order logic adds variables, predicates and quantifiers ('for all', 'there exists'). A proof system derives conclusions from axioms by rules such as modus ponens. Soundness means only true things can be proved; completeness (Gödel, 1929, for first-order logic) means every valid statement can be proved. SQL is roughly first-order logic over tables; Datalog adds recursion.
intuition: Rules for moving from things you know to things you can be sure of.
friction: First-order validity is undecidable in general; real-world reasoning is uncertain and needs probability; SQL's NULL adds a third truth value that surprises people.
ref: book | Logic in Computer Science, 2nd ed. | Huth & Ryan, 2004 |
ref: book | forall x: Calgary. An Introduction to Formal Logic | Magnus, Button et al. | https://forallx.openlogicproject.org/

@@ boolalg
value: Boolean algebra (Boole, 1854) is algebra over two values, true and false. Claude Shannon showed in 1937 that it describes switching circuits exactly, which turned circuit design into mathematics and made digital computers possible.
how: Operations AND (·), OR (+) and NOT, with laws such as distributivity and De Morgan's laws (NOT (A AND B) = NOT A OR NOT B). Any Boolean function can be built from NAND gates alone. Truth tables, Karnaugh maps and modern logic synthesis tools minimize circuits. The same laws simplify conditions in code and query predicates.
intuition: Arithmetic where every number is 0 or 1, and 1 + 1 = 1.
friction: Minimizing large circuits is computationally hard; real gates have timing and power behaviour that pure algebra ignores.
ref: book | An Investigation of the Laws of Thought | George Boole, 1854 |
ref: paper | A Symbolic Analysis of Relay and Switching Circuits | Claude Shannon, MIT thesis 1937/1938 |

@@ settheory
value: Set theory is the foundation most of mathematics is built on, and its ideas run through computing: tables are sets of rows, types are sets of values, SQL's UNION, INTERSECT and EXCEPT are set operations, and Cantor's diagonal argument is the template for the halting problem proof.
how: A set is a collection of distinct elements. Operations: union, intersection, difference, complement, Cartesian product, power set. Cardinality compares sizes; Cantor showed the real numbers are 'more infinite' than the integers. Axiomatic set theory (ZFC) avoids paradoxes like Russell's (the set of all sets that don't contain themselves).
intuition: Thinking in groups of things and what they share.
friction: Naive set theory leads to paradoxes; SQL works with bags (duplicates allowed), not pure sets; infinite sets behave unintuitively.
ref: book | Naive Set Theory | Paul Halmos, 1960 |
ref: book | Book of Proof, 3rd ed. | Richard Hammack | https://richardhammack.github.io/BookOfProof/

@@ relations
value: Relations and functions formalize how things connect: a relation is a set of ordered pairs, a function gives exactly one output per input. A database table is literally a relation, foreign keys and joins are relation composition, and pure functions are the core of functional programming and idempotent processing.
how: A binary relation R ⊆ A × B. Properties: reflexive, symmetric, transitive (equivalence relations partition a set; used in deduplication and identity resolution); antisymmetric and transitive (partial orders). Functions can be injective (one-to-one), surjective (onto) or bijective. Composition chains them. Functional dependencies in databases are statements that one attribute is a function of others.
intuition: Who is related to whom, and machines that give one answer per question.
friction: Real data violates intended properties (a 'function' from user to email that isn't one); transitive closure over large relations is expensive.
ref: book | Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications | Kenneth Rosen |
ref: book | Mathematics for Computer Science | Lehman, Leighton & Meyer | https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.042/spring18/mcs.pdf

@@ proofs
value: Proof techniques let you know a design is correct rather than hoping. Invariants and induction are how you prove an algorithm, a consensus protocol or a data structure works, and the same thinking guides writing assertions, tests and TLA+ specifications.
how: Direct proof; proof by contradiction; proof by induction (a base case plus 'if it holds for n it holds for n + 1'), which matches recursion and loops; loop invariants (true before and after every iteration); safety invariants for distributed protocols (Raft's 'at most one leader per term'). Proof assistants (Coq/Rocq, Lean, Isabelle) check proofs mechanically and are used to verify compilers (CompCert) and kernels (seL4).
intuition: A chain of steps where each one is undeniable, so the conclusion is too.
friction: Proofs of real systems are long and expensive; models can differ from the code; most engineering relies on testing instead.
ref: book | How to Prove It, 3rd ed. | Daniel Velleman, 2019 |
ref: paper | How Amazon Web Services Uses Formal Methods | Newcombe et al., CACM 2015 |

@@ combinatorics
value: Combinatorics counts arrangements and selections. It tells you how big a search space is, how many hash collisions to expect, how many ways a schedule or partition can fail, and how many test cases you need.
how: Rule of product and sum; permutations n!; combinations C(n, k); pigeonhole principle (put n + 1 items in n boxes and some box has two, the reason hash collisions are inevitable); inclusion–exclusion; generating functions; recurrences.
intuition: Counting without listing.
friction: Numbers explode quickly (combinatorial explosion), so exhaustive search is rarely possible; exact counts are often intractable and need approximation.
ref: book | Concrete Mathematics, 2nd ed. | Graham, Knuth & Patashnik, 1994 |
ref: book | Mathematics for Computer Science | Lehman, Leighton & Meyer | https://courses.csail.mit.edu/6.042/spring18/mcs.pdf

@@ graphtheory
value: Graph theory studies nodes connected by edges. Networks, dependency DAGs, social graphs, identity graphs, knowledge graphs, state machines and neural network computation graphs are all graphs, so its results apply everywhere.
how: Graphs can be directed or undirected, weighted or not. Key concepts: paths, cycles, connectivity, trees, DAGs (no cycles, so a topological order exists), bipartite graphs (matching), cuts and flows (max-flow min-cut), colouring (register allocation, scheduling), planarity, and spectral properties (eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix, used in PageRank and clustering). Euler founded the field in 1736 with the bridges of Königsberg.
intuition: Dots and lines, and the surprising number of questions they answer.
friction: Many graph problems are NP-hard; real graphs are huge and skewed; representations (adjacency lists versus matrices) matter for performance.
ref: book | Introduction to Graph Theory, 2nd ed. | Douglas West |
ref: book | Networks, Crowds, and Markets | Easley & Kleinberg, 2010 | https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/networks-book/

@@ ordertheory
value: Order theory studies partial orders and lattices. It explains why CRDTs converge, how vector clocks capture causality, why data-flow analysis in compilers terminates, and how version histories relate.
how: A partial order is reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive, but some pairs may be incomparable (two concurrent events). A join-semilattice has a least upper bound (join) for every pair. If replica states form a semilattice and updates only move up, merging by join is commutative, associative and idempotent, so replicas converge regardless of message order or duplication. Happens-before is a partial order on events; vector clocks represent it.
intuition: Not everything can be ranked, but any two things may have a smallest thing above both.
friction: Lattices must be designed carefully (deletes need tombstones or special structures); metadata can grow.
ref: book | Introduction to Lattices and Order, 2nd ed. | Davey & Priestley, 2002 |
ref: paper | Conflict-free Replicated Data Types | Shapiro, Preguiça, Baquero & Zawirski, SSS 2011 |

@@ numbertheory
value: Number theory, the study of integers, once called the 'purest' and most useless mathematics, became the foundation of public-key cryptography, hashing and error detection.
how: Divisibility and primes; greatest common divisor (Euclid's algorithm); modular arithmetic (clock arithmetic); Fermat's little theorem and Euler's theorem (the basis of RSA); the Chinese remainder theorem; discrete logarithms (the basis of Diffie–Hellman); elliptic curves over finite fields. Fast modular exponentiation makes these practical with huge numbers.
intuition: Arithmetic on a clock face, where some operations are easy forward and very hard backward.
friction: Security rests on unproven hardness assumptions; Shor's quantum algorithm would break factoring and discrete logarithms.
ref: book | A Computational Introduction to Number Theory and Algebra | Victor Shoup | https://shoup.net/ntb/
ref: book | An Introduction to Mathematical Cryptography, 2nd ed. | Hoffstein, Pipher & Silverman |

@@ finitefields
value: Finite fields are number systems with a finite number of elements where you can add, subtract, multiply and divide. They are the arithmetic of Reed–Solomon codes (QR codes, storage erasure coding, deep-space links), AES, elliptic-curve cryptography and CRC checksums.
how: GF(p) is integers modulo a prime p. GF(2^8) represents bytes as polynomials over bits, multiplied modulo an irreducible polynomial; addition is XOR. Polynomials over finite fields can be evaluated and interpolated, so k data values define a polynomial and any k of n evaluations recover it, which is exactly how erasure coding works.
intuition: A small, closed world of numbers where division always works and nothing overflows.
friction: Abstract and unfamiliar to most engineers; efficient implementation needs lookup tables or special CPU instructions (carry-less multiply).
ref: book | Error Correction Coding: Mathematical Methods and Algorithms | Todd Moon, 2005 |
ref: paper | A Tutorial on Reed-Solomon Coding for Fault-Tolerance in RAID-like Systems | James Plank, 1997 |

@@ calculus
value: Calculus is the mathematics of change and accumulation. Derivatives tell you which way to adjust parameters to reduce a loss, which is all of neural network training; integrals give probabilities, averages and areas.
how: The derivative is the instantaneous rate of change; the gradient collects partial derivatives of a function of many variables and points in the direction of steepest increase. The chain rule differentiates compositions, which is what backpropagation applies. Taylor series approximate functions locally. Integrals accumulate quantities; the fundamental theorem connects them to derivatives.
intuition: Zoom in far enough and any smooth curve looks like a straight line; the slope of that line is the derivative.
friction: Real loss surfaces are high-dimensional and non-convex; non-differentiable operations need workarounds.
ref: video | Essence of Calculus | 3Blue1Brown (Grant Sanderson) | https://www.3blue1brown.com/topics/calculus
ref: book | Mathematics for Machine Learning, ch. 5 | Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, 2020 | https://mml-book.github.io/

@@ optimization
value: Optimization finds the best choice among many under constraints. Machine learning is optimization of a loss; query planning, scheduling, bidding, resource allocation and compiler register allocation are optimization too.
how: Convex problems (a single valley) can be solved reliably: linear programming (simplex, interior-point), quadratic and convex programming. Non-convex problems (neural networks) use local methods such as gradient descent and its variants (momentum, Adam), which work surprisingly well in high dimensions. Lagrange multipliers handle constraints; duality gives bounds and prices. Discrete problems use integer programming, dynamic programming or heuristics.
intuition: Walk downhill in the fog, feeling the slope under your feet.
friction: Local minima and saddle points; constraints make problems harder; integer problems are NP-hard in general.
ref: book | Convex Optimization | Boyd & Vandenberghe, 2004 | https://web.stanford.edu/~boyd/cvxbook/
ref: paper | Adam: A Method for Stochastic Optimization | Kingma & Ba, ICLR 2015 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.6980

@@ diffeq
value: Differential equations describe systems by how they change from moment to moment. They model circuits, heat flow in chips, control loops, epidemics, physical simulations and, since 2018, some neural networks (neural ODEs) and diffusion models (stochastic differential equations).
how: An ordinary differential equation relates a function to its derivatives, such as dx/dt = −kx (exponential decay, like a capacitor discharging or a cache entry's relevance fading). Partial differential equations involve several variables (heat, waves, Maxwell's equations). Numerical solvers step forward in small time steps (Euler, Runge–Kutta). Diffusion model sampling is solving a differential equation backward from noise.
intuition: Describe the rule for the next tiny step, and the whole path follows.
friction: Most equations have no closed-form solution; numerical methods can be unstable; stiff systems need special solvers.
ref: book | Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos, 2nd ed. | Steven Strogatz, 2015 |
ref: paper | Neural Ordinary Differential Equations | Chen, Rubanova, Bettencourt & Duvenaud, NeurIPS 2018 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1806.07366

@@ fourier
value: Fourier analysis represents any signal as a sum of sine waves of different frequencies. It is the basis of audio and video compression, Wi-Fi and 5G (OFDM), audio fingerprinting, image processing and the sinusoidal position encodings in transformers.
how: The Fourier transform converts a signal from time (or space) into frequencies; the inverse converts back. The fast Fourier transform (Cooley and Tukey, 1965) computes it in O(n log n) instead of O(n²), one of the most important algorithms ever. The convolution theorem says convolution in time equals multiplication in frequency. The discrete cosine transform is a real-valued relative used in JPEG and video codecs. The sampling theorem (Nyquist–Shannon) says a signal must be sampled at more than twice its highest frequency.
intuition: A prism for signals: split a complex wave into pure tones.
friction: Assumes stationary signals; time–frequency trade-offs (you can't know both exactly); aliasing when sampling too slowly.
ref: paper | An Algorithm for the Machine Calculation of Complex Fourier Series | Cooley & Tukey, Mathematics of Computation 1965 |
ref: video | But what is the Fourier Transform? A visual introduction | 3Blue1Brown, 2018 | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=spUNpyF58BY
ref: book | The Scientist and Engineer's Guide to Digital Signal Processing | Steven W. Smith | https://www.dspguide.com/

@@ numerical
value: Numerical analysis is the study of computing with approximate numbers: how errors arise, grow and can be controlled. It decides whether a simulation, a matrix factorization or a mixed-precision training run gives trustworthy results.
how: Key ideas: floating-point rounding error; conditioning (how sensitive a problem is to small input changes); stability (whether an algorithm amplifies errors); iterative methods (Newton's method, conjugate gradient); numerically careful formulas such as the log-sum-exp trick for softmax and Kahan summation for long sums. Libraries such as BLAS and LAPACK encode decades of this knowledge.
intuition: Arithmetic with slightly blurry numbers, and how to keep the blur from spreading.
friction: Low precision (FP8, FP4) makes errors more likely; parallel reductions give different rounding in different orders, hurting reproducibility.
ref: book | Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms, 2nd ed. | Nicholas Higham, 2002 |
ref: book | Numerical Linear Algebra | Trefethen & Bau, 1997 |

@@ tensoralg
value: Tensor algebra extends matrices to arrays with any number of dimensions. Deep learning frameworks manipulate tensors (batch × sequence × heads × features), and understanding shapes, broadcasting and contractions is necessary to read or write modern ML code.
how: A tensor is a multi-dimensional array. Operations include element-wise maths with broadcasting (expanding dimensions of size one), reshaping and transposing, reductions (sum over an axis), and contractions that generalize matrix multiplication by summing over shared indices, written compactly with Einstein notation (einsum). Attention is a pair of tensor contractions. Low-rank tensor decompositions compress models.
intuition: Matrices with more directions; einsum is a precise recipe for which directions to multiply and sum.
friction: Shape bugs are among the most common ML errors; memory layout (contiguity, strides) affects speed; mental models fail beyond three dimensions.
ref: blog | Einsum is All You Need | Tim Rocktäschel, 2018 |
ref: book | Dive into Deep Learning, ch. 2 | Zhang, Lipton, Li & Smola | https://d2l.ai/

@@ metricspaces
value: Metric spaces and geometry formalize distance and nearness. Vector search, clustering, k-nearest neighbours, embeddings, recommendation and anomaly detection all depend on choosing the right distance.
how: A metric d(x, y) is non-negative, zero only for identical points, symmetric, and obeys the triangle inequality. Common choices: Euclidean (L2), Manhattan (L1), cosine distance (angle between vectors, ignoring length), Hamming (differing bits, for perceptual hashes), edit distance (strings), Jaccard (sets, estimated by MinHash). In high dimensions, distances concentrate (most points are about equally far apart), the 'curse of dimensionality', which is why approximate search and learned embeddings matter.
intuition: Define what 'close' means, and many problems become 'find the nearest'.
friction: The right distance depends on the task; cosine and dot product give different rankings for unnormalized vectors; high-dimensional geometry defies intuition.
ref: paper | When Is 'Nearest Neighbor' Meaningful? | Beyer, Goldstein, Ramakrishnan & Shaft, ICDT 1999 |
ref: book | Mining of Massive Datasets, ch. 3 (similarity, MinHash, LSH) | Leskovec, Rajaraman & Ullman | http://www.mmds.org/

@@ statistics
value: Statistics turns data into conclusions with honest uncertainty. It is behind A/B testing, campaign measurement, anomaly detection, capacity planning and model evaluation, and it protects decisions from noise and bias.
how: Descriptive statistics summarize data (mean, median, percentiles, variance). Sampling and estimation infer population properties from samples, with confidence intervals. Regression models relationships between variables. Causal inference (randomized experiments, difference-in-differences, instrumental variables) separates correlation from causation. Robust statistics (medians, trimmed means) resist outliers; for latency, percentiles (p99) matter more than averages.
intuition: Learning about the whole pot from a few spoonfuls, and saying how sure you are.
friction: Multiple comparisons and p-hacking; selection bias; Simpson's paradox; heavy-tailed data breaks normal-based methods; correlation is not causation.
ref: book | All of Statistics | Larry Wasserman, 2004 |
ref: book | Statistical Rethinking, 2nd ed. | Richard McElreath, 2020 |
ref: book | The Book of Why | Judea Pearl & Dana Mackenzie, 2018 |

@@ bayes
value: Bayes' theorem updates beliefs with evidence: posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. It is the basis of spam filters, probabilistic models, Bayesian A/B testing, Thompson sampling for exploration in ads and recommendations, and the reasoning that 'rare events with imperfect tests produce mostly false alarms'.
how: P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E). Start with a prior belief about a hypothesis, observe evidence, and compute the updated belief. Conjugate priors (such as Beta for click-through rates) make updates simple: add clicks and non-clicks to the counts. For complex models, Markov chain Monte Carlo or variational inference approximate the posterior. Naive Bayes assumes features are independent and still often works well.
intuition: Strong claims need strong evidence, and how much you believe afterwards depends on how plausible it was before.
friction: Choosing priors can be subjective; exact computation is often intractable; people systematically neglect base rates.
ref: book | Probability Theory: The Logic of Science | E. T. Jaynes, 2003 |
ref: book | Bayesian Data Analysis, 3rd ed. | Gelman et al., 2013 | http://www.stat.columbia.edu/~gelman/book/
ref: video | Bayes theorem, the geometry of changing beliefs | 3Blue1Brown, 2019 | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=HZGCoVF3YvM

@@ birthday
value: The birthday paradox says collisions happen much sooner than intuition suggests: among 23 people, two share a birthday with over 50% probability. In computing, it sets how long hashes and random IDs must be, and how quickly hash-based structures see collisions.
how: With N possible values, collisions become likely after about √N random draws (more precisely, about 1.18√N for 50%). So 32-bit hashes collide after about 77,000 items; 64-bit after about 5 billion; 128-bit random UUIDs are safe for practical purposes. Cryptographic hash functions need 2× the bit length of the desired security against collision attacks.
intuition: You're not looking for a match with one person, but any pair, and the number of pairs grows quadratically.
friction: Easy to under-size identifiers (32-bit IDs in large systems); non-uniform hashes collide even sooner.
ref: book | Probability and Computing, 2nd ed. | Mitzenmacher & Upfal, 2017 |
ref: blog | Hash Collision Probabilities | Jeff Preshing, 2011 | https://preshing.com/20110504/hash-collision-probabilities/

@@ markov
value: Markov chains model systems where the next state depends only on the current state. They underlie PageRank, language models (an n-gram model is a Markov chain; an LLM is a very rich conditional model), queueing theory, MCMC sampling and reinforcement learning (Markov decision processes).
how: A transition matrix gives the probability of moving from each state to each other state. Under mild conditions, the chain converges to a stationary distribution regardless of the starting state; PageRank is the stationary distribution of a random web surfer. Markov decision processes add actions and rewards; the Bellman equation describes optimal value.
intuition: Where you go next depends only on where you are, not how you got here.
friction: The memoryless assumption often fails, so states must be enlarged to include history; state spaces explode; convergence can be slow.
ref: paper | The PageRank Citation Ranking: Bringing Order to the Web | Page, Brin, Motwani & Winograd, 1998 |
ref: book | Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, 2nd ed., ch. 3 | Sutton & Barto, 2018 | http://incompleteideas.net/book/the-book-2nd.html

@@ stochastic
value: Stochastic processes model quantities that evolve randomly over time: request arrivals, stock prices, network delays, gradient noise and the noise schedule in diffusion models. They give the mathematics for capacity planning, performance modelling and generative models.
how: Poisson processes model independent arrivals at a constant average rate (inter-arrival times are exponential), a standard assumption in queueing. Random walks and Brownian motion model accumulated random steps. Martingales formalize 'fair games' and appear in proofs of algorithms. Stochastic differential equations add noise to dynamics; diffusion models define a forward noising process and learn its reverse.
intuition: The mathematics of things that wander.
friction: Real traffic is bursty and correlated, not Poisson; heavy tails break averages; models must be validated against real data.
ref: book | Introduction to Probability Models, 12th ed. | Sheldon Ross |
ref: paper | Score-Based Generative Modeling through Stochastic Differential Equations | Song et al., ICLR 2021 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.13456

@@ learningtheory
value: Statistical learning theory explains when learning from examples can generalize to new data, and how much data is needed. It gives the bias–variance trade-off, the logic of regularization and validation, and the open puzzle of why huge neural networks generalize.
how: PAC learning (Valiant, 1984) asks for a hypothesis that is probably approximately correct. VC dimension measures a model class's capacity; generalization bounds say the gap between training and test error shrinks with more data relative to capacity. The bias–variance trade-off: simple models underfit, complex ones overfit. Over-parameterized deep networks break classical bounds; 'double descent' shows test error can fall again as capacity grows beyond fitting the data exactly.
intuition: Learning means finding a pattern simple enough that it's probably not a coincidence.
friction: Classical bounds are too loose for deep learning; theory lags practice; generalization under distribution shift is poorly understood.
ref: paper | A Theory of the Learnable | Leslie Valiant, CACM 1984 |
ref: book | Understanding Machine Learning: From Theory to Algorithms | Shalev-Shwartz & Ben-David, 2014 | https://www.cs.huji.ac.il/~shais/UnderstandingMachineLearning/
ref: paper | Reconciling modern machine learning practice and the bias-variance trade-off | Belkin, Hsu, Ma & Mandal, PNAS 2019 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.11118

@@ codingtheory
value: Coding theory adds structured redundancy so data can survive errors and erasures. ECC memory, SSDs, QR codes, Wi-Fi and 5G, satellite links, RAID and cloud erasure coding all depend on it.
how: Shannon (1948) proved reliable communication is possible at any rate below channel capacity using clever codes. Hamming codes (1950) correct single-bit errors (the basis of ECC memory). Reed–Solomon codes (1960) correct bursts of errors using polynomials over finite fields. LDPC codes and turbo codes approach Shannon's limit and are used in Wi-Fi, 5G and SSDs; polar codes are used in 5G control channels. CRCs detect, but don't correct, errors.
intuition: Add carefully chosen extra information so the original can be rebuilt from damaged copies.
friction: Redundancy costs bandwidth and storage; decoding powerful codes takes computation and latency; the right code depends on the error pattern.
ref: paper | Error Detecting and Error Correcting Codes | Richard Hamming, Bell System Technical Journal 1950 |
ref: book | Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms | David MacKay, 2003 | https://www.inference.org.uk/mackay/itila/

@@ control
value: Control theory designs feedback loops that hold a system at a target despite disturbances. Autoscalers, TCP congestion control, Kubernetes reconciliation, ad budget pacing, thermostats and cruise control are all feedback controllers.
how: Measure the output, compare with the setpoint to get an error, and apply a correction. A PID controller combines proportional (react to current error), integral (remove steady offset) and derivative (dampen fast changes) terms. Stability analysis checks the loop won't oscillate or diverge; delay in measurement is the main source of instability. Maxwell analysed governors in 1868, founding the field.
intuition: Steer by watching how far you are from the lane centre, and correct gently but continuously.
friction: Delays and noisy measurements cause oscillation; tuning gains is tricky; non-linear systems (queues near saturation) behave badly with linear controllers.
ref: book | Feedback Systems: An Introduction for Scientists and Engineers, 2nd ed. | Åström & Murray | https://fbswiki.org/
ref: book | Feedback Control for Computer Systems | Philipp Janert, O'Reilly 2013 |

@@ gametheory
value: Game theory studies strategic decisions where each player's best choice depends on others' choices. It explains ad auctions, bidding strategies, incentive design in protocols (blockchains), negotiation and why systems built on self-interest can fail (tragedy of the commons).
how: A game has players, strategies and payoffs. A Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies where no player gains by changing alone. Dominant strategies are best regardless of others (truthful bidding in second-price auctions). Mechanism design reverses the question: design rules so self-interested behavior produces good outcomes. Repeated games enable cooperation through reputation.
intuition: Think about what others will do, given that they're thinking about what you'll do.
friction: Real players are not perfectly rational; equilibria may be many or hard to compute; models simplify information and incentives.
ref: book | Twenty Lectures on Algorithmic Game Theory | Tim Roughgarden, 2016 |
ref: book | Algorithmic Game Theory | Nisan, Roughgarden, Tardos & Vazirani (eds.), 2007 |

@@ randomgraphs
value: Random and small-world graphs show that a few random long-range links create short paths between any two nodes. This explains 'six degrees of separation', why gossip protocols spread information in O(log n) rounds, and why HNSW's graph search finds nearest neighbours quickly.
how: Erdős–Rényi graphs (1959) connect each pair with probability p; a giant connected component suddenly appears when the average degree exceeds 1. Watts–Strogatz (1998) small-world graphs combine local clustering with a few random shortcuts, giving short average paths. Barabási–Albert (1999) preferential attachment produces scale-free graphs with power-law degree distributions, like the web. Kleinberg (2000) showed when greedy routing can find short paths.
intuition: A village where most friends live next door, but a few know someone in another city.
friction: Real networks differ from simple models; hubs make networks robust to random failure but fragile to targeted attacks; high-degree hub nodes create hotspots.
ref: paper | Collective dynamics of 'small-world' networks | Watts & Strogatz, Nature 1998 |
ref: book | Network Science | Albert-László Barabási, 2016 | http://networksciencebook.com/

@@ lambdacalc
value: Lambda calculus (Church, 1936) is a model of computation built from just functions: define a function, apply a function. It is equivalent to Turing machines and is the theoretical core of functional programming, closures, lambdas in Java and Scala, and type theory.
how: Three forms: variables, function abstraction (λx. body) and application (f a). Computation is substitution (beta reduction). Numbers, booleans, pairs and recursion (via fixed-point combinators like Y) can all be encoded as functions. Typed lambda calculi add types, leading to programming language type systems.
intuition: Everything is a function, and running a program is plugging values into functions.
friction: Encodings are impractical for real computation; evaluation order (lazy versus eager) changes behavior and performance.
ref: paper | An Unsolvable Problem of Elementary Number Theory | Alonzo Church, 1936 |
ref: book | Types and Programming Languages, ch. 5 | Benjamin Pierce, 2002 |

@@ typetheory
value: Type theory studies systems of types as logic. The Curry–Howard correspondence shows that types are propositions and programs are proofs, which underlies proof assistants (Lean, Coq/Rocq), dependently typed languages and much of modern type system design.
how: Simply typed lambda calculus adds types that prevent nonsense terms. System F adds generics (polymorphism). Dependent types let types depend on values (a vector of length n). Under Curry–Howard, a function of type A → B is a proof that A implies B; writing a program of a type proves its proposition. Lean's mathlib formalizes large amounts of mathematics this way.
intuition: A type is a promise; a program that type-checks keeps it.
friction: Expressive type systems are hard to use and slow to check; proving real programs correct remains labour-intensive, though AI-assisted proof is advancing quickly.
ref: book | Types and Programming Languages | Benjamin Pierce, 2002 |
ref: paper | Propositions as Types | Philip Wadler, CACM 2015 | https://homepages.inf.ed.ac.uk/wadler/papers/propositions-as-types/propositions-as-types.pdf

@@ category
value: Category theory is the mathematics of composition: objects and arrows between them, and how arrows combine. It gives functional programming its vocabulary (functors, monads) and explains why some abstractions compose cleanly, for example Spark's map and flatMap on datasets.
how: A category has objects and morphisms (arrows) with associative composition and identities. Functors map one category to another, preserving structure (List.map is a functor). Monads describe sequenced computations with context, such as Option, Future or IO (flatMap is monadic bind). Monoids (an associative operation with an identity) are why aggregations like sum, count and HyperLogLog merge can be parallelized and combined in any grouping.
intuition: Focus on how things connect and compose, not what they're made of.
friction: Highly abstract; practical benefit is mostly in a few patterns (functors, monads, monoids); jargon can obscure simple ideas.
ref: book | Category Theory for Programmers | Bartosz Milewski, 2019 | https://github.com/hmemcpy/milewski-ctfp-pdf
ref: paper | Monoidify! Monoids as a Design Principle for Efficient MapReduce Algorithms | Jimmy Lin, 2013 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.7544

@@ formallang
value: Formal language theory classifies languages by the machines needed to recognize them (the Chomsky hierarchy). It tells you when a regex is enough, when you need a parser, and why some questions about programs are undecidable.
how: Regular languages (finite automata, regular expressions) < context-free languages (pushdown automata, grammars for programming languages and JSON) < context-sensitive < recursively enumerable (Turing machines). The pumping lemmas prove a language isn't in a class (balanced parentheses aren't regular). Grammars in BNF define programming languages precisely.
intuition: A ladder of machines, each able to handle more structure than the one below.
friction: Real languages mix levels (C's typedef makes parsing context-sensitive); natural language doesn't fit neatly into any class.
ref: book | Introduction to the Theory of Computation, 3rd ed. | Michael Sipser |
ref: paper | Three Models for the Description of Language | Noam Chomsky, 1956 |

@@ asymptotics
value: Asymptotic analysis studies how functions behave as inputs grow large. It gives Big-O notation, explains why logarithms and exponentials dominate, and lets engineers compare algorithms and predict scaling.
how: Notation: O (upper bound), Ω (lower bound), Θ (tight bound), o (strictly smaller). Growth hierarchy: constants < log n < √n < n < n log n < n² < 2^n < n!. Recurrences like T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n are solved with the master theorem. Stirling's approximation for n! and harmonic numbers (≈ ln n) appear often. Logarithms appear whenever a problem is repeatedly halved.
intuition: Who wins the race when the track gets very long, not who is ahead at the start.
friction: Hides constants and lower-order terms that matter for real input sizes; galactic algorithms are asymptotically better but never practical.
ref: book | Concrete Mathematics, 2nd ed., ch. 9 | Graham, Knuth & Patashnik, 1994 |

@@ amdahl
value: Amdahl's law says the speed-up from parallelism is limited by the part that remains serial: if 5% of a job is serial, no number of machines can make it more than 20× faster. Gustafson's law adds that with more machines we usually solve bigger problems. Together they guide decisions about scaling Spark jobs, GPU clusters and team workflows.
how: Speed-up = 1 / ((1 − p) + p / n), where p is the parallel fraction and n the number of processors. Gustafson (1988) instead holds time fixed and grows the problem, giving scaled speed-up (1 − p) + p·n. The Universal Scalability Law (Gunther) adds a coherence term, showing throughput can fall when adding nodes because of coordination costs.
intuition: Ten people can paint a fence faster, but they can't make the paint dry faster.
friction: Real serial fractions are hidden (coordination, shuffles, locks, stragglers); communication costs often grow with n, making things worse than Amdahl predicts.
ref: paper | Validity of the Single Processor Approach to Achieving Large Scale Computing Capabilities | Gene Amdahl, AFIPS 1967 |
ref: paper | Reevaluating Amdahl's Law | John Gustafson, CACM 1988 |
ref: book | Guerrilla Capacity Planning | Neil Gunther, 2007 |

@@ powerlaws
value: Power laws and heavy tails describe data where a few items are enormous and most are tiny: popular keys, celebrity users, viral content, file sizes, latency outliers, word frequencies (Zipf's law). Designing for averages fails under them; designing for the tail is what makes systems robust.
how: In a power law, P(X > x) ∝ x^(−α): the tail decays slowly, so extreme values are common. Zipf's law: the k-th most frequent item has frequency ∝ 1/k. Consequences: caching a small fraction of keys serves most requests; hot partitions appear in Kafka and Spark; averages are unstable and p99 latency is dominated by rare events; skew-handling (salting keys, AQE skew joins) is necessary.
intuition: A few giants and a long crowd of dwarfs.
friction: Hard to estimate exponents correctly from data; many claimed power laws are actually log-normal; heavy tails make capacity planning and testing harder.
ref: paper | Power-Law Distributions in Empirical Data | Clauset, Shalizi & Newman, SIAM Review 2009 | https://arxiv.org/abs/0706.1062
ref: book | The Black Swan | Nassim Nicholas Taleb, 2007 |

@@ clt
value: The law of large numbers and the central limit theorem explain why averages of many random things are stable and approximately normal. They justify sampling, A/B test statistics, confidence intervals and why aggregated metrics are reliable even when individual events are noisy.
how: Law of large numbers: the sample mean converges to the true mean as samples grow. Central limit theorem: the sum (or mean) of many independent variables with finite variance is approximately normally distributed, with standard error σ/√n, so accuracy improves with the square root of sample size (4× the data halves the error).
intuition: One coin flip is unpredictable; a million flips are very predictable.
friction: Fails for heavy-tailed data with infinite variance (some latency and revenue distributions); correlated samples (same user many times) shrink the effective sample size; convergence can be slow for skewed data.
ref: book | All of Statistics, ch. 5 | Larry Wasserman, 2004 |
ref: video | But what is the Central Limit Theorem? | 3Blue1Brown, 2023 | https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zeJD6dqJ5lo

@@ hypothesis
value: Hypothesis testing decides whether an observed difference (a new ranking model, a new ad creative) is real or just noise. It is the statistical core of A/B testing and experimentation platforms.
how: State a null hypothesis (no difference). Compute a test statistic and the p-value: the probability of seeing a result at least this extreme if the null were true. Reject the null if p < α (often 0.05). Power is the probability of detecting a true effect of a given size; it decides how many users an experiment needs. Corrections handle multiple comparisons; sequential testing allows safe early stopping; variance-reduction methods like CUPED use pre-experiment data to need fewer users.
intuition: Assume nothing changed, and see how surprised you should be by the data.
friction: p-values are widely misinterpreted; peeking at results inflates false positives; novelty effects and network effects bias online experiments; statistical significance is not practical importance.
ref: book | Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments | Kohavi, Tang & Xu, 2020 |
ref: paper | Improving the Sensitivity of Online Controlled Experiments by Utilizing Pre-Experiment Data (CUPED) | Deng, Xu, Kohavi & Walker, WSDM 2013 |

@@ montecarlo
value: Monte Carlo methods estimate quantities by random sampling. They solve problems too complex for exact calculation: risk simulation, physical rendering, Bayesian inference, reinforcement learning, game-playing (Monte Carlo tree search in AlphaGo) and estimating the impact of system changes.
how: Draw many random samples, evaluate the quantity on each, and average; the error shrinks as 1/√n regardless of dimension, which is why it beats grid methods in high dimensions. Variance reduction (importance sampling, stratification) improves efficiency. Markov chain Monte Carlo (Metropolis–Hastings, Gibbs sampling, Hamiltonian Monte Carlo) samples from complex distributions known only up to a constant. Developed at Los Alamos in the 1940s by Ulam, von Neumann and Metropolis.
intuition: If you can't solve it, simulate it many times and count.
friction: Slow convergence for rare events; needs good random number generators; MCMC convergence is hard to diagnose.
ref: paper | The Monte Carlo Method | Metropolis & Ulam, JASA 1949 |
ref: book | Monte Carlo Statistical Methods, 2nd ed. | Robert & Casella, 2004 |

@@ kolmogorov
value: Kolmogorov complexity defines the information in a single object as the length of the shortest program that produces it. It formalizes Occam's razor, explains why random data can't be compressed, and gives the deep link between compression and prediction behind 'language modelling is compression'.
how: K(x) is the length of the shortest program (in a fixed universal language) that outputs x; the choice of language changes it only by a constant. Most strings are incompressible (counting argument). K is uncomputable (it reduces to the halting problem), so practical work uses compressors as approximations. Minimum description length and Solomonoff induction turn this into a principle for model selection and prediction.
intuition: A pattern is anything that lets you describe data more briefly than listing it.
friction: Uncomputable; constants depending on the language can be large; links to deep learning are suggestive rather than exact.
ref: book | An Introduction to Kolmogorov Complexity and Its Applications, 4th ed. | Li & Vitányi, 2019 |
ref: paper | Language Modeling Is Compression | Delétang et al. (DeepMind), ICLR 2024 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10668
