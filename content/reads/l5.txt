@@ maxwell
value: Maxwell's equations (1865) describe how electric and magnetic fields create each other and travel as waves at the speed of light. Every wire, chip interconnect, radio, Wi-Fi link and optical fibre is an application of them, so they set the physical limits on signalling.
how: Four equations: electric charges create electric fields; there are no magnetic charges; a changing magnetic field creates an electric field (induction, behind transformers and generators); currents and changing electric fields create magnetic fields. Together they predict self-sustaining electromagnetic waves. In circuits, a fast signal is a wave, so at high frequencies wires behave as transmission lines with impedance, reflections and crosstalk, which chip and board designers must manage.
intuition: Electric and magnetic fields keep pushing each other forward, like two dancers taking turns to lead, and that motion is light.
friction: High-speed signals suffer loss, reflection and interference; the energy to charge and discharge wires dominates chip power, which is why moving data costs more than computing on it.
ref: book | The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. II | Richard Feynman | https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/II_toc.html
ref: book | A Student's Guide to Maxwell's Equations | Daniel Fleisch, 2008 |

@@ lightspeed
value: The speed of light sets a hard floor on latency: about 5 µs per kilometre in fibre. No software can beat it, so it decides where data centres go, why CDNs and edge caches exist, why cross-region consensus is slow, and why high-frequency traders pay for straighter cables and microwave links.
how: Light travels about 300,000 km/s in vacuum and about 200,000 km/s in glass fibre (refractive index around 1.5). Mumbai to Bengaluru is roughly 850 km in a straight line, so at least about 4 ms one way in fibre, and real routes are longer. A Raft or Paxos commit across continents needs at least one round trip between a majority of replicas, often 100 ms or more. On a chip, signals travel only a few centimetres per nanosecond, so a 4 GHz clock cycle covers only a few centimetres.
intuition: Distance is time: every kilometre of cable is a fixed, unavoidable delay.
friction: Global systems must choose between consistency and latency (PACELC); hollow-core fibre and low-orbit satellites reduce but cannot remove the delay.
ref: blog | Latency numbers every programmer should know | Jeff Dean & Peter Norvig (popularized), interactive version by Colin Scott | https://colin-scott.github.io/personal_website/research/interactive_latency.html
ref: paper | The Internet at the Speed of Light | Singla, Chandrasekaran, Godfrey & Maggs, HotNets 2014 |

@@ relativity
value: Relativity of simultaneity says that observers in different places (or moving differently) can't agree on what happens 'at the same time' without exchanging signals. In distributed systems, the practical version is the same: there is no global clock, which is why we need logical clocks, consensus and careful reasoning about ordering.
how: Einstein (1905) showed simultaneity depends on the observer's frame; only causal order (A could have influenced B because a signal could travel between them) is absolute. Lamport (1978) explicitly borrowed this idea: happens-before is defined by message passing, like light cones, and events with no causal link are concurrent. GPS satellites must even correct for relativistic time dilation (about 38 µs per day) to keep positioning accurate.
intuition: 'Now' is local; anything far away is news that has already aged by the time it reaches you.
friction: Engineers still try to order events by wall-clock timestamps; clock-based designs (like Spanner's TrueTime) work only by measuring and waiting out uncertainty.
ref: paper | Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System | Leslie Lamport, CACM 1978 |
ref: book | Relativity: The Special and the General Theory | Albert Einstein, 1916 |

@@ noise
value: Thermal noise and signal-to-noise ratio set the limit on how much information any channel can carry. Shannon's capacity formula, C = B log₂(1 + S/N), ties bandwidth and noise directly to bits per second, which is why Wi-Fi, 5G, cable and fibre engineering is a battle against noise.
how: Electrons in any conductor move randomly with thermal energy, producing Johnson–Nyquist noise (1928) with power proportional to temperature and bandwidth (kTB). Signals weaken with distance, while noise stays, so SNR falls; amplifiers add their own noise. Modulation schemes pack more bits per symbol only at high SNR (from BPSK to 1024-QAM); error-correcting codes approach Shannon's limit. The same noise causes bit errors in memory and logic as voltages shrink.
intuition: Whispering in a noisy room: the louder the room, the slower you must speak to be understood.
friction: Shrinking voltages in chips reduce noise margins; wireless spectrum is limited; the Shannon limit can be approached but never exceeded.
ref: paper | Thermal Agitation of Electric Charge in Conductors | Harry Nyquist, Physical Review 1928 |
ref: paper | A Mathematical Theory of Communication | Claude Shannon, Bell System Technical Journal 1948 |

@@ quantum
value: Quantum mechanics explains why electrons in solids have discrete energy states and behave like waves. Without it there would be no understanding of semiconductors, so no transistors, lasers, flash memory or modern chips.
how: Particles are described by wavefunctions; measurable quantities like energy come in discrete levels; the Pauli exclusion principle stops electrons from sharing a state. In a crystal, atoms' energy levels merge into bands, which explains conductors, insulators and semiconductors. Effects like tunnelling and the uncertainty principle become important as transistors shrink to a few nanometres.
intuition: At atomic scale, energy comes in steps, and electrons behave like spread-out waves rather than tiny balls.
friction: Deeply counter-intuitive; quantum effects such as tunnelling become sources of leakage and variability in tiny transistors.
ref: book | The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. III | Richard Feynman | https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/III_toc.html
ref: book | QED: The Strange Theory of Light and Matter | Richard Feynman, 1985 |

@@ bandgap
value: The band gap of a semiconductor is the energy needed to free an electron to conduct. Silicon's gap (about 1.1 eV) is the sweet spot that lets a transistor switch between conducting and insulating with small voltages, at room temperature, reliably, and it is the physical root of digital logic.
how: In a solid, electron energies form a filled valence band and an empty conduction band separated by the gap. Metals have no gap (always conduct); insulators have a large gap (never conduct). Semiconductors have a small gap, so conductivity can be controlled by doping, electric fields, light or heat. Wider-gap materials (GaN, SiC) handle higher voltages for power electronics; materials with direct gaps (GaAs, InP) emit light, used in lasers for fibre optics.
intuition: A small step electrons can be helped over on command, but won't jump on their own.
friction: Silicon is a poor light emitter, complicating on-chip optics; heat creates unwanted carriers; band gap limits efficiency and leakage trade-offs.
ref: book | Semiconductor Device Fundamentals | Robert Pierret, 1996 |
ref: book | Physics of Semiconductor Devices, 3rd ed. | Sze & Ng, 2006 |

@@ pnjunction
value: The p–n junction, where p-type and n-type silicon meet, forms a barrier whose height is controlled by voltage. Diodes, solar cells, LEDs, bipolar transistors and the source and drain of every MOSFET are built from it.
how: Electrons from the n side and holes from the p side diffuse across and recombine, leaving a depletion region with a built-in electric field. Forward bias (positive on p) lowers the barrier and current flows; reverse bias raises it and current is blocked. Light absorbed in the depletion region creates current (solar cells, photodetectors); recombination can emit light (LEDs, lasers). Russell Ohl discovered it at Bell Labs in 1940.
intuition: A one-way valve for electric current, whose opening is set by voltage.
friction: Leakage current in reverse bias; junction capacitance limits speed; as devices shrink, junctions become harder to form sharply.
ref: book | Semiconductor Device Fundamentals | Robert Pierret, 1996 |
ref: book | Crystal Fire: The Invention of the Transistor and the Birth of the Information Age | Riordan & Hoddeson, 1997 |

@@ tunneling
value: Quantum tunnelling lets electrons pass through barriers too high for them classically. It is how flash memory is written and erased, and it is why thin transistor insulators leak, one of the physical limits on shrinking chips.
how: An electron's wavefunction doesn't stop sharply at a barrier; it decays exponentially inside it, so for a thin enough barrier there is a real chance of appearing on the other side. Flash cells store charge on a floating gate or charge-trap layer, pushed through a thin oxide by high voltage (Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling); the stored charge shifts the transistor's threshold, encoding bits. In logic transistors, gate oxides only a few atoms thick leaked heavily, which led to high-k dielectrics around 2007.
intuition: A ball that occasionally passes through a wall it could never climb, more often the thinner the wall.
friction: Repeated tunnelling damages the oxide, limiting flash write endurance; leakage wastes power in idle chips.
ref: book | Physics of Semiconductor Devices, 3rd ed. | Sze & Ng, 2006 |
ref: paper | Introduction to Flash Memory | Bez, Camerlenghi, Modelli & Visconti, Proceedings of the IEEE 2003 |

@@ thermo
value: Thermodynamics says energy is conserved and entropy (disorder) increases. For computing, it means every operation turns electrical energy into heat that must be removed, so power and cooling, not transistor count, now limit chips, data centres and AI clusters.
how: The first law: energy is conserved, so all power a chip draws becomes heat. The second law: entropy of an isolated system never decreases, so heat flows from hot to cold and can't be fully converted to work. Chip power is mostly dynamic (switching capacitance: P ≈ C·V²·f) plus leakage. Data centre efficiency is measured by power usage effectiveness (PUE); AI racks now need over 100 kW each and liquid cooling. Boltzmann connected entropy to the number of microscopic states (S = k log W), which links it to Shannon's information entropy.
intuition: Every computation is a tiny heater; the only question is how efficiently it computes per unit of heat.
friction: Power density limits clock speed ('dark silicon'); cooling and electricity supply limit AI data centre growth; water use for cooling.
ref: book | The Feynman Lectures on Physics, Vol. I, ch. 44–46 | Richard Feynman | https://www.feynmanlectures.caltech.edu/I_toc.html
ref: book | The Datacenter as a Computer, 3rd ed. | Barroso, Hölzle & Ranganathan, 2018 |

@@ landauer
value: Landauer's principle (1961) says erasing one bit of information must release at least kT·ln 2 of heat, about 3×10⁻²¹ joules at room temperature. It connects information and physics directly, and sets a theoretical floor on the energy of computation; today's chips use many orders of magnitude more.
how: Erasing a bit reduces the number of possible states of the memory, decreasing its entropy; by the second law, that entropy must go into the environment as heat. Logically reversible computation (where no information is lost) can in principle avoid this cost (Bennett, 1973). Bérut et al. confirmed the limit experimentally in 2012 with a single colloidal particle.
intuition: Forgetting has a physical price.
friction: Practical chips are far above the limit, dominated by wire charging and leakage; reversible computing is hard to build and slower.
ref: paper | Irreversibility and Heat Generation in the Computing Process | Rolf Landauer, IBM Journal 1961 |
ref: paper | Experimental verification of Landauer's principle linking information and thermodynamics | Bérut et al., Nature 2012 |

@@ magnetism
value: Magnetism stores most of the world's cold data: hard disks and tape record bits as the direction of tiny magnetic regions. It also powers motors, transformers, and newer memories like MRAM.
how: Ferromagnetic materials have domains whose magnetization points in a direction. A write head's magnetic field flips the domains under it; a read head senses the field using giant or tunnelling magnetoresistance (Nobel Prize 2007 for GMR), where resistance changes with magnetic alignment. Perpendicular recording, shingled recording (overlapping tracks) and heat- or microwave-assisted recording push density further. Tape remains the cheapest long-term archive.
intuition: Billions of tiny compass needles, each pointing up or down.
friction: Smaller domains become thermally unstable (the superparamagnetic limit); mechanical seeks make disks slow for random access; shingled disks need sequential writes.
ref: paper | The Emergence of Spin Electronics in Data Storage | Chappert, Fert & Nguyen Van Dau, Nature Materials 2007 |

@@ photonics
value: Optics and photonics move data as light. Fibre optics carry almost all long-distance internet traffic, and optical links are moving inside data centres and toward chips (co-packaged optics) because copper can't keep up with AI cluster bandwidth.
how: A laser (1960) produces coherent light; it is modulated to encode bits and sent through glass fibre, where total internal reflection guides it with very low loss (Charles Kao's 1966 insight, Nobel 2009). Erbium-doped fibre amplifiers boost signals without converting to electricity. Wavelength-division multiplexing sends many colours through one fibre, each carrying 100+ Gbit/s. Silicon photonics builds optical components with chip manufacturing.
intuition: Data riding on beams of light inside hair-thin glass.
friction: Converting between electrical and optical signals costs power and money; lasers fail and are temperature-sensitive; packaging optics next to chips is hard.
ref: paper | Dielectric-fibre surface waveguides for optical frequencies | Kao & Hockham, Proc. IEE 1966 |
ref: book | City of Light: The Story of Fiber Optics | Jeff Hecht, 1999 |

@@ crystal
value: Quartz crystal oscillators give every computer, phone and network card its steady tick. Their stability (and their drift) explains both how chips are clocked and why machines' clocks disagree, making clock synchronization a distributed systems problem.
how: Quartz is piezoelectric: voltage makes it flex, and flexing makes voltage. A crystal cut to a precise shape vibrates at a stable resonant frequency (commonly 32.768 kHz for real-time clocks, MHz for system clocks), and a circuit sustains the oscillation. Phase-locked loops multiply it to GHz. Frequency drifts with temperature and age, often 10–100 parts per million, so a clock can drift by seconds per day. Temperature-compensated and oven-controlled oscillators are more stable.
intuition: A tiny tuning fork that keeps time, but runs a little fast or slow depending on the weather.
friction: Drift requires NTP or PTP correction; virtual machines add more timing error; clock jumps after correction break naive code.
ref: paper | The Evolution of the Quartz Crystal Clock | Warren Marrison, Bell System Technical Journal 1948 |
ref: blog | There is No Now | Justin Sheehy, ACM Queue 2015 | https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=2745385

@@ atomicclock
value: Atomic clocks define the second and, through GPS, give the whole planet a common time accurate to nanoseconds. Google Spanner's TrueTime, telecom networks, financial timestamps and PTP time synchronization rely on them.
how: Atoms (caesium-133) absorb microwave radiation only at an exact frequency; since 1967, one second is defined as 9,192,631,770 cycles of that radiation. GPS satellites carry atomic clocks and broadcast their time; receivers compute both position and accurate time. Data centres use GPS receivers and atomic references as time masters; TrueTime exposes time as an interval [earliest, latest] with uncertainty of a few milliseconds or less.
intuition: Nature's most reliable metronome, broadcast from space.
friction: GPS signals can be jammed or spoofed; local distribution adds error; leap seconds complicated civil time (and are scheduled to be abolished by 2035).
ref: paper | Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database | Corbett et al., OSDI 2012 |
ref: docs | NIST Time and Frequency Division | NIST | https://www.nist.gov/pml/time-and-frequency-division

@@ qcomputing
value: Quantum computing uses qubits that can exist in superpositions and become entangled, letting some algorithms solve problems far faster than classical computers. Shor's algorithm (1994) could break RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography, which is why the world is migrating to post-quantum cryptography now.
how: A qubit's state is a combination of 0 and 1 with complex amplitudes; quantum gates rotate states, and interference amplifies correct answers and cancels wrong ones. Shor's algorithm finds periods (hence factors) exponentially faster; Grover's search gives a square-root speed-up. Physical qubits are noisy, so quantum error correction combines many physical qubits into one logical qubit; Google showed below-threshold error correction with its Willow chip in 2024. Platforms include superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms and photons.
intuition: Instead of checking answers one by one, arrange waves so wrong answers cancel out.
friction: Qubits lose coherence quickly; error correction needs very many physical qubits per logical qubit; useful advantage is proven only for specific problems; timelines for breaking RSA are uncertain.
ref: paper | Algorithms for quantum computation: discrete logarithms and factoring | Peter Shor, FOCS 1994 |
ref: book | Quantum Computation and Quantum Information | Nielsen & Chuang, 2000 |
ref: paper | Quantum error correction below the surface code threshold | Google Quantum AI, Nature 2024 | https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.13687

@@ doping
value: Doping adds tiny amounts of other elements to silicon to control whether it carries current by electrons (n-type) or holes (p-type). It turns an almost insulating crystal into the building material for diodes and transistors.
how: Silicon has four outer electrons. Adding phosphorus or arsenic (five outer electrons) contributes free electrons: n-type. Adding boron (three) creates holes, missing electrons that act as positive carriers: p-type. Concentrations of about one dopant per million silicon atoms change conductivity enormously. Dopants are placed by ion implantation and activated by annealing; precise doping profiles define transistor behaviour.
intuition: Seasoning silicon with a pinch of something else to give it extra or missing electrons.
friction: At nanometre scales, the random number of dopant atoms per transistor causes variability; modern devices rely more on structure (FinFETs, gate-all-around) than on doping alone.
ref: book | Semiconductor Device Fundamentals | Robert Pierret, 1996 |

@@ lithography
value: Photolithography prints chip patterns with light, and it decides how small transistors can be. Extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography, made only by ASML, is the most complex machine ever sold commercially and the gate to leading-edge chips.
how: A wafer is coated with a light-sensitive resist; light passes through a mask (reticle) and lenses that shrink the pattern; exposed resist is developed, and the pattern is etched or implanted into the layers below. This repeats for dozens of layers. Resolution depends on wavelength: deep-ultraviolet lithography uses 193 nm light (with immersion in water and multiple patterning), while EUV uses 13.5 nm light produced by hitting tin droplets with lasers, reflected by special mirrors in vacuum. High-NA EUV improves resolution further.
intuition: Photographic printing at the scale of a few dozen atoms.
friction: EUV tools cost hundreds of millions of dollars each; export controls restrict access; defects and overlay errors limit yield; 'nanometre' node names no longer match any physical dimension.
ref: book | Chip War: The Fight for the World's Most Critical Technology | Chris Miller, 2022 |
ref: book | Fundamental Principles of Optical Lithography | Chris Mack, 2007 |

@@ oxides
value: Thin oxide and dielectric layers insulate transistor gates from channels and store charge in flash cells. Their thickness and material set how well a transistor switches, how much it leaks and how long flash lasts.
how: Silicon dioxide (SiO₂) grows naturally on silicon and was the gate insulator for decades. When it thinned to about 1.2 nm, tunnelling leakage became too large, so Intel introduced hafnium-based high-k dielectrics with metal gates in 2007 (45 nm): a higher dielectric constant gives the same control with a physically thicker, less leaky layer. In flash, a tunnel oxide lets charge pass under high voltage but holds it for years otherwise.
intuition: A very thin wall that lets the gate's electric field through but must stop the electrons.
friction: Each atomic layer matters; defects trap charge and degrade reliability; flash oxides wear out with use.
ref: paper | High-k/Metal-Gate Stack and Its MOSFET Characteristics | Chau et al. (Intel), IEEE Electron Device Letters 2004 |

@@ copper
value: Copper interconnects are the metal wiring between transistors. As transistors shrank, wires became the main source of delay and energy use, so modern chip performance is often limited by wiring, not switching.
how: IBM introduced copper (replacing aluminium) in 1997 using the damascene process: etch trenches in insulator, fill with copper, and polish flat. Modern chips have over a dozen metal layers, thin local wires and thick global ones. Wire delay grows with resistance times capacitance (RC); narrower wires have higher resistance, partly because electrons scatter off surfaces. Low-k insulators reduce capacitance; new metals (ruthenium, cobalt) and backside power delivery (Intel's PowerVia, TSMC's Super Power Rail) relieve congestion.
intuition: Transistors got faster, but the roads between them got narrower and slower.
friction: Electromigration (atoms pushed by current) damages wires; resistance rises sharply at small widths; moving data costs far more energy than computing.
ref: paper | Full Copper Wiring in a Sub-0.25 µm CMOS ULSI Technology | Edelstein et al. (IBM), IEDM 1997 |
ref: paper | Computing's Energy Problem (and what we can do about it) | Mark Horowitz, ISSCC 2014 |

@@ tsv
value: Through-silicon vias and die stacking connect chips vertically, putting memory dies on top of each other and right next to processors. High-bandwidth memory (HBM), essential for AI accelerators, depends on it, as do chiplet designs.
how: Vertical holes are etched through thinned silicon dies and filled with copper, connecting stacked dies with thousands of short links. HBM stacks 8–16 DRAM dies on a base logic die; it sits beside the GPU on a silicon interposer (TSMC's CoWoS), giving terabytes per second of bandwidth through very wide, short connections. Hybrid bonding joins dies with direct copper-to-copper contacts at very fine pitch (AMD's 3D V-Cache).
intuition: Build up instead of out, so the memory is upstairs rather than across town.
friction: Heat is hard to remove from stacks; manufacturing yield multiplies across dies; advanced packaging capacity has been a bottleneck for AI chip supply.
ref: paper | High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) DRAM | JEDEC JESD235 standard, 2013 |
ref: book | Chip War | Chris Miller, 2022 |

@@ liion
value: Lithium-ion batteries power phones, laptops, electric vehicles and backup power in data centres. Their energy density limits how much computing mobile and edge devices can do, and drives energy-saving design in chips and software.
how: During discharge, lithium ions move from the anode (usually graphite) through an electrolyte to the cathode (a lithium metal oxide such as NMC, or lithium iron phosphate), while electrons flow through the external circuit; charging reverses this. Energy density has improved roughly 3× since commercialization by Sony in 1991. Goodenough, Whittingham and Yoshino won the 2019 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it.
intuition: Lithium ions shuttling back and forth like a rechargeable pendulum.
friction: Degradation with cycles and heat; fire risk; supply of lithium, cobalt and nickel; slow improvement compared with chips.
ref: docs | Scientific background: Lithium-ion batteries (Nobel Prize in Chemistry 2019) | The Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences | https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2019/advanced-information/

@@ neurons
value: Biological neurons and synapses inspired artificial neural networks: units that sum weighted inputs and fire past a threshold, with connection strengths that change with experience. Understanding the original explains both the inspiration and how far modern AI has moved from it.
how: A neuron receives signals at synapses on its dendrites; if the combined input raises its membrane potential past a threshold, it fires a spike along its axon. Synapses strengthen or weaken with activity (Hebb, 1949: 'cells that fire together wire together'; spike-timing-dependent plasticity). The human brain has about 86 billion neurons and runs on about 20 watts.
intuition: A voting cell: if enough neighbours shout, it shouts too, and it learns whose voices to trust.
friction: Real neurons are far more complex (dendritic computation, spike timing, chemical signalling); backpropagation has no clear biological equivalent; the brain's energy efficiency is still unmatched.
ref: book | The Organization of Behavior | Donald Hebb, 1949 |
ref: book | Principles of Neural Science, 6th ed. | Kandel et al., 2021 |

@@ mccullochpitts
value: McCulloch and Pitts (1943) modelled a neuron as a threshold logic unit and showed networks of such units can compute any logical function. It was the first bridge between brains, logic and computation, and the ancestor of the perceptron and today's neural networks.
how: Each unit receives binary inputs, sums them with weights (excitatory or inhibitory), and outputs 1 if the sum exceeds a threshold. Such units implement AND, OR and NOT, so networks of them can implement any finite logic circuit. Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) added learning of the weights.
intuition: A neuron as a tiny voting machine, and a brain as a logic circuit.
friction: Binary, timeless and without learning, a big simplification; single-layer perceptrons can't learn XOR (Minsky and Papert, 1969), which contributed to the first AI winter.
ref: paper | A Logical Calculus of the Ideas Immanent in Nervous Activity | McCulloch & Pitts, Bulletin of Mathematical Biophysics 1943 |
ref: paper | The Perceptron: A Probabilistic Model for Information Storage and Organization in the Brain | Frank Rosenblatt, Psychological Review 1958 |

@@ visualcortex
value: Hubel and Wiesel discovered (1959–1962) that the visual cortex processes images in layers: simple cells respond to oriented edges at specific places, complex cells to edges anywhere in a region. This hierarchy directly inspired Fukushima's Neocognitron and LeCun's convolutional networks.
how: Recording from single neurons in cat visual cortex, they found receptive fields: small regions of the visual field where a stimulus activates the cell. Simple cells detect edges of a particular orientation; complex cells pool over positions, giving some position invariance. Higher visual areas respond to more complex shapes, faces and objects. Convolution corresponds to the same detector applied everywhere; pooling corresponds to complex cells. They won the 1981 Nobel Prize.
intuition: Vision built up in stages: edges, then corners and textures, then parts, then objects.
friction: The brain uses heavy feedback and recurrence, unlike feed-forward CNNs; biological plausibility of learning rules remains unclear.
ref: paper | Receptive fields, binocular interaction and functional architecture in the cat's visual cortex | Hubel & Wiesel, Journal of Physiology 1962 |
ref: paper | Neocognitron: A Self-organizing Neural Network Model for a Mechanism of Pattern Recognition Unaffected by Shift in Position | Kunihiko Fukushima, Biological Cybernetics 1980 |

@@ dopamine
value: Dopamine neurons signal reward prediction error: the difference between the reward expected and the reward received. This matches temporal-difference learning in reinforcement learning so closely that it is one of the clearest links between brains and algorithms.
how: Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997) showed that dopamine neurons fire for unexpected rewards; once a cue predicts the reward, they fire at the cue instead; and if an expected reward doesn't arrive, their firing dips. TD learning updates value estimates with exactly this error: δ = reward + γ·V(next) − V(current). The same idea, at massive scale, trains game-playing agents and parts of RLHF.
intuition: Learning happens when reality surprises you, not when it matches your expectations.
friction: Dopamine does more than signal prediction error (motivation, movement); simplified models can mislead; reward prediction in brains is distributed.
ref: paper | A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward | Schultz, Dayan & Montague, Science 1997 |
ref: book | Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, 2nd ed., ch. 15 | Sutton & Barto, 2018 | http://incompleteideas.net/book/the-book-2nd.html

@@ evolution
value: Evolution by natural selection shows that variation plus selection plus inheritance can create complex, well-designed systems without a designer. It inspired genetic algorithms and evolution strategies, and it is a model for iterative product development, A/B testing and markets.
how: Individuals vary (mutation, recombination); variants that survive and reproduce more pass on their traits; over generations, populations adapt. Selection works on populations, is local and greedy (it can get stuck at local optima), and is very parallel. Darwin and Wallace (1858–1859) proposed it; the modern synthesis combined it with genetics.
intuition: Try many small variations, keep what works, repeat for a very long time.
friction: Slow and wasteful; can only improve on what exists (no foresight); optimizes for reproduction, not for what we might value, a parallel to reward hacking in AI.
ref: book | On the Origin of Species | Charles Darwin, 1859 |
ref: book | The Blind Watchmaker | Richard Dawkins, 1986 |

@@ dna
value: DNA is an information storage system: a four-letter code (about 2 bits per base) copied, read and error-corrected by molecular machines. It shows nature solved data encoding, replication and error correction billions of years ago, and DNA itself is being explored as dense archival storage.
how: Each strand is a sequence of bases (A, C, G, T); the two strands pair (A–T, C–G), so each strand is a template for the other, making copying natural. Three-base codons map to amino acids, a code with redundancy (64 codons for 20 amino acids plus stop). Polymerases copy DNA with proofreading, and repair systems fix damage, giving error rates around one in a billion per base per replication. The human genome is about 3.1 billion base pairs, around 750 MB raw.
intuition: A self-copying tape written in a four-letter alphabet.
friction: Reading and writing DNA for storage is slow and expensive; biological 'code' is not cleanly modular like software.
ref: paper | Molecular Structure of Nucleic Acids: A Structure for Deoxyribose Nucleic Acid | Watson & Crick, Nature 1953 |
ref: paper | Next-Generation Digital Information Storage in DNA | Church, Gao & Kosuri, Science 2012 |

@@ immune
value: The immune system learns to tell 'self' from 'non-self' and to remember past threats. It inspired anomaly detection, intrusion detection systems and artificial immune algorithms, and it is a model of distributed, decentralized defence.
how: Innate immunity reacts quickly to general danger signals. Adaptive immunity generates huge numbers of random receptors; cells that react to the body's own molecules are removed (negative selection), so the rest respond to foreign patterns. Cells that match a threat multiply (clonal selection) and leave memory cells for faster future responses (the basis of vaccines).
intuition: Generate many random detectors, delete the ones that fire on normal behaviour, and keep those that catch intruders.
friction: False positives (autoimmunity) and false negatives; artificial immune algorithms have rarely beaten standard machine learning; adversaries adapt.
ref: paper | Self-Nonself Discrimination in a Computer | Forrest, Perelson, Allen & Cherukuri, IEEE S&P 1994 |
ref: book | Immunobiology (Janeway's), 10th ed. | Murphy & Weaver |

@@ homeostasis
value: Homeostasis is how living bodies keep temperature, blood sugar and chemistry stable through negative feedback loops. It is the biological original of control systems, Kubernetes-style reconciliation and autoscaling.
how: Sensors detect a variable; a control centre compares it with a set point; effectors act to reduce the difference: sweating cools you, insulin lowers blood sugar. Multiple overlapping loops provide robustness. Walter Cannon named it in 1926; Norbert Wiener's cybernetics (1948) generalized feedback across animals and machines.
intuition: The body's thermostat, running hundreds of loops at once.
friction: Feedback loops with delays oscillate; loops can fight each other; set points can drift (chronic conditions), similar to badly tuned autoscalers.
ref: book | The Wisdom of the Body | Walter Cannon, 1932 |
ref: book | Cybernetics: Or Control and Communication in the Animal and the Machine | Norbert Wiener, 1948 |

@@ perception
value: Human vision and hearing have specific blind spots, and every lossy codec exploits them. JPEG, H.264, HEVC, AV1, MP3 and AAC throw away information people can't perceive, which is why video streaming and music compression work at practical bitrates.
how: The eye has far more brightness-sensitive rods than colour-sensitive cones and is less sensitive to fine colour detail, so codecs separate luma from chroma and store chroma at lower resolution (4:2:0 subsampling). Eyes are less sensitive to high spatial frequencies, so codecs quantize those coarsely. The ear has frequency masking (a loud tone hides nearby quieter tones) and temporal masking, so perceptual audio coders drop masked sounds. Perceptual metrics (SSIM, VMAF from Netflix) estimate quality as people see it.
intuition: Don't spend bits on what nobody can see or hear.
friction: Perception varies by person, screen and environment; metrics don't fully match human judgement; artifacts become visible at low bitrates or after repeated re-encoding.
ref: paper | Image Quality Assessment: From Error Visibility to Structural Similarity (SSIM) | Wang, Bovik, Sheikh & Simoncelli, IEEE TIP 2004 |
ref: blog | Toward a Practical Perceptual Video Quality Metric (VMAF) | Netflix Technology Blog, 2016 | https://netflixtechblog.com/toward-a-practical-perceptual-video-quality-metric-653f208b9652

@@ mooreslaw
value: Moore's law (transistor counts doubling about every two years) and Dennard scaling (power density staying constant as transistors shrink) drove 50 years of exponentially cheaper computing. The end of Dennard scaling around 2006 forced multicore chips, and the slowing of Moore's law drives today's specialized accelerators, chiplets and focus on software efficiency.
how: Gordon Moore observed in 1965 that components per chip were doubling yearly, revised to every two years in 1975. Dennard et al. (1974) showed that shrinking a transistor by a factor k lowers voltage and capacitance together, so it gets faster with constant power density. Around 2005–2006, voltage could no longer drop because of leakage, so clock speeds stalled near 3–5 GHz and designers used extra transistors for more cores and specialized units instead. Transistor density still increases, but the cost per transistor falls more slowly.
intuition: For decades, waiting two years made every program faster for free; that free lunch ended.
friction: Each new node is exponentially more expensive (Rock's law); power and heat dominate; software must become parallel and efficient to benefit.
ref: paper | Cramming More Components onto Integrated Circuits | Gordon Moore, Electronics 1965 |
ref: paper | Design of Ion-Implanted MOSFETs with Very Small Physical Dimensions | Dennard et al., IEEE JSSC 1974 |
ref: blog | The Free Lunch Is Over | Herb Sutter, 2005 | http://www.gotw.ca/publications/concurrency-ddj.htm

@@ aristotle
value: Aristotle created the first formal logic about 2,350 years ago: patterns of reasoning that are valid because of their form, not their content. The idea that correct reasoning can follow mechanical rules is the seed of all logic, programming and AI.
how: Syllogisms combine two premises into a conclusion: 'All men are mortal; Socrates is a man; therefore Socrates is mortal.' Aristotle classified which forms are valid regardless of what the terms mean. He also formulated the laws of non-contradiction and the excluded middle, which underlie Boolean logic. His logic dominated for 2,000 years until Boole and Frege extended it.
intuition: Some arguments are correct just because of their shape.
friction: Syllogistic logic can't express relations or nested quantifiers ('every user has some device'); classical two-valued logic struggles with uncertainty and vagueness.
ref: book | Prior Analytics | Aristotle, c. 350 BCE |
ref: docs | Aristotle's Logic | Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy | https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-logic/

@@ leibniz
value: Leibniz (1646–1716) dreamed of a universal symbolic language and a 'calculus of reasoning' that would settle arguments by calculation ('Let us calculate!'). He also developed binary arithmetic and built a mechanical calculator, foreseeing much of computer science.
how: The characteristica universalis was a planned language where every concept had a symbol; the calculus ratiocinator would mechanically derive truths from them. He described binary numbers in 1679 (published 1703). His stepped reckoner (1670s) could multiply and divide mechanically. He also co-invented calculus, independently of Newton.
intuition: If thoughts could be written as symbols, thinking could become calculating.
friction: The universal language was never completed; Gödel and Turing later showed mechanical reasoning has fundamental limits, though LLMs and proof assistants revive parts of the dream.
ref: book | The Universal Computer: The Road from Leibniz to Turing | Martin Davis, 2000 |
ref: docs | Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz | Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy | https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/leibniz/

@@ boole
value: George Boole's 'The Laws of Thought' (1854) turned logic into algebra, with variables taking two values. Eighty years later, Claude Shannon showed this algebra describes switching circuits, making Boole the grandfather of every digital chip.
how: Boole represented classes and propositions with symbols, and logical AND, OR and NOT as algebraic operations obeying laws like x·x = x (idempotence). This made reasoning computable by manipulating equations. Shannon's 1937 master's thesis mapped Boole's algebra onto relay circuits.
intuition: Reasoning as arithmetic on true and false.
friction: Boolean logic alone can't express quantifiers or relations (Frege added those); real-world reasoning needs degrees of belief.
ref: book | An Investigation of the Laws of Thought | George Boole, 1854 | https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/15114
ref: book | The Universal Computer: The Road from Leibniz to Turing, ch. 2 | Martin Davis, 2000 |

@@ frege
value: Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) created modern predicate logic, with variables and quantifiers ('for all', 'there exists'). It made it possible to formalize mathematics and, eventually, to express database queries, program specifications and type systems precisely.
how: Frege introduced quantified variables, functions and arguments, and a fully formal proof system where every step is checkable. 'Every user has a device' becomes ∀u ∃d owns(u, d). His attempt to derive arithmetic from logic was hit by Russell's paradox (1902), which led to type theory and axiomatic set theory.
intuition: A precise language where 'every', 'some' and 'not' can be nested without ambiguity.
friction: Naive comprehension led to paradox; full first-order logic is undecidable, so practical systems use restricted fragments.
ref: book | Begriffsschrift | Gottlob Frege, 1879 |
ref: docs | Gottlob Frege | Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy | https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/frege/

@@ hilbert
value: Hilbert's program (1920s) aimed to put all of mathematics on a complete, consistent, mechanically checkable foundation, and asked whether there was an algorithm to decide any mathematical statement (the Entscheidungsproblem). Answering that question in 1936 is what led Church and Turing to define computation itself.
how: Hilbert proposed formalizing mathematics in axiomatic systems and proving, with simple ('finitary') methods, that they are consistent and complete. Gödel (1931) showed completeness and self-proved consistency are impossible for systems rich enough for arithmetic; Church and Turing (1936) showed no general decision procedure exists.
intuition: The dream of a machine that could answer every mathematical question, and the discovery that it can't exist.
friction: The program failed in its original form, but its methods (formal systems, proof theory) survive in logic, verification and proof assistants.
ref: docs | Hilbert's Program | Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy | https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hilbert-program/
ref: book | Logicomix: An Epic Search for Truth | Doxiadis & Papadimitriou, 2009 |

@@ godel
value: Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) prove that any consistent formal system rich enough to express arithmetic contains true statements it cannot prove, and cannot prove its own consistency. They set permanent limits on formal reasoning, and Gödel's technique of encoding statements as numbers anticipated programs as data.
how: Gödel numbering encodes formulas and proofs as integers, so arithmetic can talk about its own proofs. He built a statement that effectively says 'this statement is not provable'. If the system is consistent, the statement is true but unprovable. The second theorem: such a system can't prove its own consistency. Turing's halting problem proof uses the same self-reference idea.
intuition: A system that can describe itself can always build a sentence it can't settle.
friction: Often misused in popular arguments (about minds or AI); its limits apply to formal systems, not to everyday reasoning, and most practical mathematics is unaffected.
ref: book | Gödel's Proof | Nagel & Newman, 1958 |
ref: book | Gödel, Escher, Bach | Douglas Hofstadter, 1979 |

@@ churchturing
value: The Church–Turing thesis says that anything computable by a step-by-step method can be computed by a Turing machine. It is why all general-purpose programming languages are equally powerful in principle, and why questions in computer science are about efficiency and practicality, not raw possibility.
how: In 1936, Church (lambda calculus) and Turing (Turing machines) independently defined 'effective computability', and the definitions turned out to be equivalent, as did Gödel's recursive functions. The thesis is not a theorem (it connects an informal notion to a formal one), but every model proposed since agrees with it. Quantum computers don't change what is computable, only what is efficiently computable.
intuition: There is one kind of computation; different machines are just different speeds and styles of it.
friction: Says nothing about efficiency; debates continue about physical versions (whether physics allows more computation); hypercomputation remains speculative.
ref: docs | The Church-Turing Thesis | Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy | https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/church-turing/
ref: paper | On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem | Alan Turing, 1936 |

@@ epistemology
value: Epistemology asks what justifies belief. Hume's problem of induction (1748), 'why expect the future to resemble the past?', is exactly the problem of generalization in machine learning, and the question of how we know a system works is the question behind testing, monitoring and evaluation.
how: Hume argued that no logical argument proves induction works; we rely on habit. Popper (1934) proposed that science proceeds by falsification: theories are tested and kept only while they survive. Bayesian epistemology treats beliefs as probabilities updated by evidence. ML models are inductive learners: they assume training and deployment data come from the same distribution, and fail when it shifts. The 'no free lunch' theorems formalize that no learner is best on all possible problems.
intuition: Seeing a million white swans doesn't prove all swans are white.
friction: Distribution shift and black swans; models confidently extrapolate; deciding how much evidence is enough is always a judgement.
ref: book | An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding | David Hume, 1748 | https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9662
ref: book | The Logic of Scientific Discovery | Karl Popper, 1934/1959 |
ref: paper | No Free Lunch Theorems for Optimization | Wolpert & Macready, IEEE Trans. Evolutionary Computation 1997 |

@@ mind
value: Philosophy of mind asks whether computation can be thinking. Turing's imitation game (1950) and Searle's Chinese Room (1980) frame today's debates about whether large language models understand, reason or are conscious.
how: Turing proposed replacing 'can machines think?' with a behavioural test: can a machine's conversation be told apart from a person's? Searle argued that manipulating symbols by rules (like a person following a rulebook in Chinese without knowing Chinese) isn't understanding. Functionalism holds that mental states are defined by their roles, so any suitable system could have them. Current debates include whether LLMs have world models, and how to study model internals (interpretability).
intuition: If something answers like it understands, does it understand?
friction: No agreed definition of understanding or consciousness; behavioural tests can be gamed; claims about AI minds influence policy and public trust.
ref: paper | Computing Machinery and Intelligence | Alan Turing, Mind 1950 | https://academic.oup.com/mind/article/LIX/236/433/986238
ref: paper | Minds, Brains, and Programs | John Searle, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 1980 |

@@ aiethics
value: The ethics of AI asks how systems that make or influence decisions should be fair, accountable, private, safe and aligned with human values. For engineers building ad targeting, identity graphs and ML models, these questions shape design choices and regulation.
how: Fairness: models trained on historical data can reproduce bias; different fairness definitions (equal accuracy, equal positive rates) can't all be satisfied at once. Accountability and transparency: who is responsible, and can decisions be explained? Privacy: data minimization, consent and techniques like differential privacy. Alignment and safety: making powerful systems pursue intended goals and avoid harmful ones. Regulation includes the EU AI Act (2024) and India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act (2023).
intuition: Every model encodes choices about who benefits and who bears the risk; ethics makes those choices explicit.
friction: Values differ across cultures and stakeholders; trade-offs between fairness definitions, accuracy and privacy are real; measurement is hard; rules lag the technology.
ref: book | Fairness and Machine Learning: Limitations and Opportunities | Barocas, Hardt & Narayanan, 2023 | https://fairmlbook.org/
ref: book | The Alignment Problem | Brian Christian, 2020 |
ref: paper | Concrete Problems in AI Safety | Amodei et al., 2016 | https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.06565

@@ occam
value: Occam's razor says to prefer the simplest explanation that fits the evidence. In computing it becomes regularization, minimum description length, simple architectures and the engineering rule that the simplest design that works is usually the most reliable.
how: Attributed to William of Ockham (14th century): don't multiply entities beyond necessity. Formal versions: minimum description length (choose the model that compresses data plus model best), Bayesian model comparison (simpler models spread less probability, so they win when they fit), and Solomonoff induction (weight hypotheses by the length of the programs that generate them). In ML, simpler models generalize better at equal fit, though huge networks complicate this.
intuition: Extra assumptions are extra ways to be wrong.
friction: 'Simple' depends on the description language; the truth may be complex; over-simplified models underfit.
ref: book | The Minimum Description Length Principle | Peter Grünwald, 2007 |
ref: book | Information Theory, Inference, and Learning Algorithms, ch. 28 | David MacKay, 2003 | https://www.inference.org.uk/mackay/itila/

@@ reductionism
value: Reductionism and abstraction explain complex wholes through layered parts, each layer hiding details of the one below. It is the organizing idea of this whole map: Kafka rests on logs, logs on file systems, file systems on storage devices, devices on physics, and you can understand each layer through a simple interface.
how: Abstraction gives a layer a contract (an API, an ISA, a protocol) and hides the implementation. Reductionism explains a layer's behaviour by the layers beneath it. Emergence is the counterpoint: some properties (consensus safety, market prices, model capabilities) appear only at the level of the whole. Joel Spolsky's 'law of leaky abstractions' notes that all non-trivial abstractions leak, which is why first-principles knowledge of lower layers pays off in debugging and design.
intuition: Zoom in to understand why; zoom out to understand what for.
friction: Abstractions leak (performance, failures); emergent behaviour can't always be predicted from parts; too many layers add cost and confusion.
ref: blog | The Law of Leaky Abstractions | Joel Spolsky, 2002 | https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/
ref: paper | More Is Different | P. W. Anderson, Science 1972 |
ref: book | The Sciences of the Artificial, 3rd ed. | Herbert Simon, 1996 |

@@ infophysical
value: 'Information is physical' (Landauer, 1991) means information always lives in some physical form (charge, magnetization, light, molecules), so it obeys physics: storing, moving and erasing it cost energy and time. It connects the bottom of this map (physics) to the top (data systems).
how: Every bit needs a physical carrier and some energy barrier to keep it stable. Moving bits costs energy proportional to distance and capacitance, which is why data movement dominates power in modern chips and why locality matters at every layer, from caches to data centres. Erasing bits produces heat (Landauer's principle). Signals travel at most at the speed of light. Noise limits channel capacity (Shannon). The holographic bound even limits how much information fits in a region of space.
intuition: There is no information without a thing that holds it.
friction: The theoretical limits are far from today's practice, so engineering, not fundamental physics, sets most current limits.
ref: paper | Information is Physical | Rolf Landauer, Physics Today 1991 |
ref: book | The Information: A History, a Theory, a Flood | James Gleick, 2011 |

@@ chomsky
value: Noam Chomsky's hierarchy of grammars (1956) classifies languages by the power of the machines needed to recognize them. It founded formal language theory, which gives us regular expressions, parsers and compilers, and it framed the long debate between rule-based and statistical models of human language.
how: Type 3 regular grammars (finite automata), Type 2 context-free grammars (pushdown automata, used for programming language syntax), Type 1 context-sensitive, Type 0 unrestricted (Turing machines). Each class strictly contains the one below. Chomsky also argued that human language has deep innate structure, and criticized purely statistical models; LLMs have reopened this debate.
intuition: A ladder of grammars, each able to describe more structure than the last.
friction: Natural language doesn't fit neatly into one class; the success of statistical language models challenges, but doesn't settle, questions about innate grammar.
ref: paper | Three Models for the Description of Language | Noam Chomsky, IRE Trans. Information Theory 1956 |
ref: book | Syntactic Structures | Noam Chomsky, 1957 |

@@ distsem
value: Distributional semantics says 'you shall know a word by the company it keeps' (J. R. Firth, 1957): words that appear in similar contexts have similar meanings. This one idea is the root of word2vec, embeddings, and ultimately language models that learn meaning from predicting text.
how: Count which words co-occur with which (Harris, 1954; Firth, 1957). Early methods reduced large co-occurrence matrices with SVD (latent semantic analysis, 1990). Word2vec (2013) and GloVe (2014) learned dense vectors by predicting context words. Transformers learn contextual embeddings, where a word's vector depends on the whole sentence, by predicting masked or next tokens.
intuition: You can guess what a strange word means from the sentences it appears in.
friction: Context captures association, not necessarily truth or meaning; it absorbs biases in text; grounding in the physical world is limited for text-only models.
ref: paper | Distributional Structure | Zellig Harris, Word 1954 |
ref: paper | GloVe: Global Vectors for Word Representation | Pennington, Socher & Manning, EMNLP 2014 |
ref: book | Speech and Language Processing, 3rd ed. draft, ch. 'Vector Semantics and Embeddings' | Jurafsky & Martin | https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/
