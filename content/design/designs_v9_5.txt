@@ similar-listings
title: Design similar listings for a rental marketplace
category: AI & ML
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: embeddings, ann, funnel, vectorsearch, featureeng, clfmetrics, contrastive, experimentation
summary: A guest opens a listing page for a flat in Lisbon, and under the photos there is a carousel: "Similar homes". It shows 12 other listings the guest might want instead. The carousel rescues guests whose chosen listing is booked, too expensive or not quite right, and it helps guests who are still comparing. A listing is not a document: it has a price, a place, photos, a calendar of free dates, a host with rules, and a booking history that is mostly empty. Two homes can look alike and still be wrong for this guest, for example because one is booked on the guest's dates. We design the system for a marketplace with 5 million active listings: how we learn a vector for each listing from booking behaviour, how we search it, how we filter and rank candidates, how we handle new listings, and how we prove with an experiment that the carousel makes more bookings.
requirements: Functional requirements. For a given listing and an optional context (check-in and check-out dates, number of guests, the signed-in guest), return 12 listings ranked by how likely the guest is to book them. Every returned listing must be bookable for the dates in the context: it must be free on those dates, allow that number of guests and be in a sensible area. When there are no dates, return listings that look good for a typical stay. The list must be diverse: no more than 2 listings from the same host, and not 12 copies of the same building. New listings (cold start) must be able to appear within a few hours of going live. Listings that are paused, deleted or banned must disappear within minutes.
Non-functional requirements. Latency: the carousel loads after the main page, so we allow 150 ms at p99 for the whole call, and 60 ms at p50. Availability: if the service fails, the page must still work. We hide the carousel and do not show an error. Freshness: embeddings can be one day old, but availability and price must be fresh within a minute. Quality: the main online metric is the booking rate of guests who saw the carousel. We also watch click-through rate, and guardrails such as host fairness and page latency.
Objective and metrics. The objective is to maximise bookings that come through the carousel, not clicks. A click is a weak signal: a guest can click on a lovely villa that costs five times the budget. Offline we measure recall at 12 and at 100 (is the listing the guest finally booked among our candidates?), NDCG at 12 for the final order, and coverage (what share of listings ever appears in a carousel). Online we measure the booking rate per carousel viewer, click-through rate, revenue per viewer, and the share of impressions that go to new listings.
Assumptions I choose. 5 million active listings in 200 thousand markets (a market is a small area such as a city centre or a ski village). 40 million listing page views per day. Guests in the signed-in group are 60 percent of views. About 1 listing in 5 is new in the last 90 days.
estimates: Request load. 40 million page views per day divided by 86,400 seconds is about 460 requests per second on average. Peak is about 4 times the average, so we plan for 1,850 requests per second. With a cache that serves part of the requests we need much less (see below), but we size the compute for the peak without the cache.
Embedding size. We use 64 numbers per listing, stored as 16-bit floats (2 bytes each). That is 128 bytes per listing. 5 million listings times 128 bytes is 640 MB. With 32-bit floats it is 1.3 GB. Both fit in the memory of one machine, so we do not need to split the vectors for size. We split by market only to filter easily and to spread load.
Index size. A graph index (HNSW) with 16 links per node at the base layer stores about 16 times 4 bytes = 64 bytes of links, plus upper layers, so about 100 bytes per node. The total is about 5 million times (128 + 100) bytes = 1.1 GB. A machine with 16 GB of RAM holds the whole index with room for the filter data.
Search cost. A graph search with ef = 100 touches about 1,000 to 2,000 vectors. Each distance on 64 dimensions is 64 multiplications, so one query is about 100,000 floating-point operations plus memory reads. That takes about 0.3 to 1 ms on one core. For 1,850 requests per second at 1 ms we need about 2 cores. The search is not the bottleneck.
Brute force per market. The average market has 5 million divided by 200 thousand = 25 listings, but the big ones are large. Paris might have 60,000 listings. A brute-force scan of 60,000 vectors with 64 numbers is 3.8 million multiply-adds, about 1 to 2 ms with vectorised code. So for most markets a plain scan is as fast as an index, and it is exact. We use the graph index only for markets above 100,000 listings and for cross-market search.
Availability data. Each listing keeps a bitmap of free nights for the next 365 days: 365 bits = 46 bytes. 5 million times 46 bytes = 230 MB. A check for 5 nights is one bit-mask operation, a few nanoseconds. If 60 percent of the nights are free on a given date range, about 30 percent of the listings are free for the whole stay of 3 nights. So we must fetch about 4 times more candidates than we need after filtering.
api: GET /v1/listings/{id}/similar?checkin=2026-12-20&checkout=2026-12-24&guests=2&limit=12 with the header Authorization (optional). Returns {"model_version": "v37", "items": [{"listing_id", "score", "reason"}]}. The reason field is a short code such as "same_area_cheaper" that the UI can turn into a label. If no good items exist the list is empty and the client hides the carousel.
POST /v1/events/impression and POST /v1/events/click with {listing_id, source: "similar", position, request_id, model_version}. The request_id ties the click and the later booking back to the exact list that we served. Without it we cannot train on our own output or measure the carousel properly.
Internal: POST /internal/retrieval/search with {vector or listing_id, market_ids[], filters, k}. This is the call from the ranking service to the vector search service. A second internal call, POST /internal/features/batch_get with {listing_ids[], guest_id}, returns the feature vectors for ranking.
datamodel: The listing tower reads from a listing table, but we only describe what this system adds. We keep three stores.
The embedding store. Key: (model_version, listing_id). Value: a 64-dimension vector in 16-bit floats, plus the market id, a content hash and the creation time. It is written in bulk by the training pipeline and read when the index is built. It is never updated in place, so every vector belongs to one model version. This matters, because vectors from two model versions cannot be compared.
The availability and price store. Key: listing_id. Value: a 365-bit free-night bitmap, the nightly price per day, the minimum stay, the maximum guests and a status flag. It is updated by the booking service when a booking or a host change happens. It lives in a key-value store such as Redis or DynamoDB, and the serving side keeps a local copy refreshed every 30 to 60 seconds.
The session and event tables. They live in the data lake. The click events look like this:
```sql
CREATE TABLE click_events (
  event_time   TIMESTAMP,
  session_id   STRING,
  guest_id     STRING,
  listing_id   BIGINT,
  source       STRING,    -- search, similar, wishlist
  position     INT,
  request_id   STRING,
  action       STRING,    -- impression, click, book, host_reject
  market_id    INT
) PARTITIONED BY (event_date DATE);
```
The partition by day keeps training scans cheap. The training job reads the last 90 days. A second table, training_pairs, stores (anchor_id, positive_id, weight, market_id, kind), where kind says if the pair came from a booking session or a click session.
design: The system has an offline part that learns and builds the index, and an online part that serves each request through a funnel. A funnel (multi-stage retrieval and ranking) is the right shape: the first stage is cheap and covers 5 million listings, and each later stage is more expensive and sees fewer.
Learning the vectors. We train a two-tower model with contrastive learning. Each listing goes through a listing tower, a small neural network, which turns its inputs into one 64-dimension embedding. The inputs are: an ID embedding (learned from behaviour), the listing's content features (type, price bucket, bedrooms, amenities, area, rating, text and photo embeddings from a frozen image model) and the market. The training pairs come from guests' sessions. If a guest clicked listing A and then listing B in the same session, and especially if the guest booked B, then A and B should be close. The loss pushes the pair together and pushes random other listings in the batch away. The ID part lets popular listings learn rich behaviour. The content part is what lets a brand-new listing, with no history, get a good vector on day one.
Retrieval. For a request with listing L, the retrieval stage takes L's vector and looks for the nearest vectors in L's market and the neighbouring markets. This is vector search. In a small market we scan all vectors. In a large one we use approximate nearest neighbour search with a graph index (HNSW). We ask for 400 candidates. We ask for many because the next stage will remove most of them.
Filtering. The filter stage uses the availability store. It removes listings that are not free on the requested dates, do not fit the guests, are the same as L, or are in a blocked state. We apply the filter after the search, with an over-fetch factor of 4, and this is why the candidate count is 400 and not 100. If fewer than 50 remain, the service widens the search to more markets and tries again once. For requests without dates we skip the date filter and use the pre-computed neighbours list, which also serves as a cache.
Ranking. A ranking model scores the remaining 50 to 150 candidates. It is a gradient-boosted tree model or a small neural network with these features: cosine similarity between the two embeddings; the price ratio between candidate and L; distance in kilometres; the difference in bedrooms; review score and number of reviews; the candidate's recent booking rate; whether the candidate is an instant-book listing; and, for signed-in guests, how close the candidate is to the guest's past searches and wishlists. This is feature engineering, and the quality of the ranker depends on it more than on the model type. The ranker predicts the probability of a booking after an impression.
Re-ranking and diversity. The last stage takes the top 40 by score and picks 12. It uses a greedy method (maximal marginal relevance): at each step it picks the candidate with the best score minus a penalty for being too similar to those already picked. Rules cap two listings per host and two per building. It also reserves one or two slots for newer listings with few impressions, so they can collect data.
The path of one request. The page asks the carousel service. The service looks up L's vector and market, runs the retrieval call, filters by availability, fetches features in one batch call, runs the ranker, runs the re-ranker, writes the served list to the log with a request_id, and returns 12 items. Every stage has its own timeout. If the ranker times out, the service returns the top 12 by similarity, which is slightly worse but still useful.

deepdive: Training the embedding. The loss is a softmax over positives and negatives:
\[ \mathcal{L} = -\log \frac{\exp(s(a,p)/\tau)}{\exp(s(a,p)/\tau) + \sum_{j=1}^{K} \exp(s(a,n_j)/\tau)} \]
Here a is the anchor listing, p a positive, n_j are K negatives, s is cosine similarity and the temperature tau (for example 0.05) controls how sharp the loss is. The negatives matter more than the model size. Random negatives from the whole catalogue are too easy: a flat in Lisbon is never confused with a cabin in Colorado, so the model learns only that country matters. We add hard negatives from the same market, and also listings that were shown near the clicked listing but were not booked. A well-known paper on this problem, from Airbnb (Grbovic and Cheng, KDD 2018), used the same idea with a skip-gram model: it treated a guest's click session as a sentence and listing ids as words, added the booked listing as a global context for every click in the session, and chose negatives from the same market. We use the same logic with a two-tower network because it also takes content features.
Positives: which pairs? Click pairs in one session are noisy: guests click on many things. Booking sessions are rare but clean. We weight pairs: a pair from a session that ended in a booking gets weight 5, a pair with a long dwell time gets weight 2, and a pair where the guest only skimmed gets weight 0.5. We also remove sessions with more than 50 clicks (likely bots or agents). Another important signal is the negative one: listings that a host rejected or that a guest left quickly are not positive for each other, and we do not use them as pairs.
Approximate search and filters. An HNSW graph is built without knowing the filters. If the filter removes 70 percent of the nodes, a normal search that walks the graph and drops the filtered nodes can end up with too few results, or get stuck in a region where all neighbours are removed. We have three tools. Over-fetch and post-filter, which is simple and fine when the filter passes 25 percent or more. Per-market indexes, so the geographic filter is built in. And, for date filters, a filter-aware search that visits removed nodes but does not return them, with a larger ef. We use the first two and keep the third as a fallback. Recall of the index is measured weekly: we compare 10,000 random queries with a brute-force result and expect recall at 100 above 0.95.
Cold start. A new listing has no clicks, so its ID embedding is empty. The content tower alone gives it a vector, trained to match the full vector of old listings that have both (a distillation loss). In the ranker, behaviour features get default values and a flag "is_new". The re-ranker's exploration slots show the listing to a small share of viewers. After 50 to 100 impressions we have a first estimate of its booking rate. Without reserved traffic the system would show only listings that were already popular, which is a feedback loop.
Position bias. Guests click item 1 more than item 12, whatever the quality, so a ranker trained on raw clicks learns "what was shown at the top". We log the position and use inverse propensity weights learned from a small random-order slice (about 1 percent of traffic). That slice is also the only unbiased data for offline evaluation.
Marketplace effects. This is a two-sided market. If the carousel sends many guests to the same 1,000 popular listings, those listings get booked out, other guests find them unavailable, and the host side gets unbalanced. The availability filter reduces the harm for guests, and the diversity rules and reserved slots reduce the concentration. We track the Gini coefficient of impressions across listings as a guardrail.
Experimentation. We run an A/B test (experimentation) with the guest as the unit of randomisation. Say the baseline booking rate per carousel viewer is 1.5 percent and we want to detect a relative gain of 2 percent, which is 0.03 percentage points. The variance of a yes/no outcome is p(1-p) = 0.0148. A rough sample size per arm at 80 percent power and 5 percent significance is 16 times variance divided by the squared difference: 16 times 0.0148 divided by (0.0003 squared = 9e-8) is about 2.6 million viewers per arm. At about 10 million carousel viewers per week this takes one week, but we run two weeks to cover weekday and weekend behaviour. Because the booking rate has a long delay (guests book days or weeks after the click), we count a booking only if it follows a carousel click within 7 days and wait that long before the final read. Interference matters: both arms compete for the same limited homes, so the measured gain can be larger than the real gain. We check that total bookings of the marketplace do not fall in the treatment group, and for large changes we randomise by market.
tradeoffs: It depends: item-to-item versus user-to-item. Choose item-to-item similarity (this design) when the entry point is a listing page and the guest has shown intent by looking at it; it works for anonymous guests and for new users. Choose a user-to-item model (a personal recommendation on the home page) when you know the guest's history and there is no anchor listing. The best carousel mixes both: the anchor listing sets the context, and the ranker uses the guest's history only to reorder. You pay with more features and a more complex cache.
It depends: precomputed neighbours versus online search. Choose the nightly table of 200 neighbours when dates are not given, traffic is high and you want a low and stable p99 of a few milliseconds. Choose online search when dates, price or party size change the answer or when listings change fast. Online search costs about 8 ms more and needs the index in memory on every serving node. Most real systems keep both: the table is the fallback and the cache.
It depends: exact scan versus ANN. Choose an exact scan for markets under about 100,000 listings: it is simple, always correct and fast enough with vectorised code. Choose ANN with HNSW for larger markets or for global search, accepting that recall will be 95 to 99 percent and that the index must be rebuilt when vectors change. IVF-based indexes use less memory than HNSW and suit billion-scale sets, but at 5 million vectors memory is not a problem.
It depends: one model or a funnel. Choose a single learned similarity for a quick launch: no ranker, just sort by cosine score after the filter. Choose the funnel when you have enough bookings to train a ranker (millions of examples) and need to use price, distance and quality signals that the embedding does not capture. The funnel adds two services and a feature store to run, and about 35 ms of latency, but it usually brings the largest gains in booking rate.
failures: The vector index is stale or missing. A bad build can produce a half-empty index. Serving loads a new index only when the manifest says complete, checks its size against the last version (a drop of more than 5 percent blocks the swap) and runs 1,000 test queries first. If the check fails, the old version stays. We keep the last two versions in memory for a fast rollback.
Version mismatch. A model version v38 index must not be queried with a v37 vector. The embedding store key includes the model version, the retrieval call sends the version, and the index rejects a different one. The ranker model is also tied to the embedding version, because its similarity feature changes meaning. A new model is released as a pair: embeddings, index and ranker together.
Stale availability. A listing can be booked in the last 30 seconds and still be in the carousel. The filter uses a local copy with a one-minute delay. When the guest clicks, the listing page does its own live check and shows "dates no longer available" with a link to similar ones. We accept a small rate of stale items, below 1 percent, rather than pay a live call for each of the 150 candidates.
Feature store down. The ranker cannot get features. The service falls back to ranking by embedding similarity and price distance only, which we can compute from the data already in the retrieval response. The p99 stays under 150 ms and the booking rate drops by a few percent, which is far better than an empty carousel.
Bad training data. A logging bug, or a bot that clicks on random listings, can pull unrelated listings together. Before each release we compare the new model with the old one on 1,000 fixed anchor listings, and a person reads the 50 with the largest change. Recall at 100 on a held-out week must not fall by more than 1 percent relative. The new model first goes to 1 percent of traffic.
evolution: At 50,000 listings, skip the ML: rank listings in the same area by price distance, bedroom match and rating. That is a day of work and a fair baseline. At 500,000 listings with some history, train item embeddings from click sessions (skip-gram on sequences), scan in memory, and filter by date. At 5 million, move to the two-tower model with content features, per-market indexes, a nightly neighbours table and a learned ranker. At 50 million listings in many countries, add a multilingual text model, regional index shards and a feature store with streaming updates, and add a second objective, stay quality (reviews and cancellations), so the system does not push guests into bad stays.
followups: Why not use item-based collaborative filtering with co-click counts? || Co-click counts need many sessions per pair. In a marketplace most listings have few clicks, and a listing has only one set of dates, so the matrix is very sparse and new listings have no row. Embeddings generalise: similar content and similar behaviour give nearby vectors, even for pairs that never co-occurred.
How would you evaluate a new embedding offline before an experiment? || Hold out the last week of sessions. For each booking, take the previous clicks as anchors and check if the booked listing is in the top 100 candidates (recall at 100) and its rank (MRR). Also check by segments: new listings, small markets and large markets. Offline gains guide the choice but do not replace an A/B test, because offline data comes from the old system's lists.
How do you handle a new listing with no data? || Use a content tower trained to match the behaviour vector of old listings, give the ranker an "is_new" flag with default behaviour features, and reserve one or two carousel slots for exploration. Measure the booking rate of new listings after about 100 impressions and let them move into the normal ranking.
How do you know the carousel causes more bookings and does not just move them from search? || Run a randomised A/B test with a holdout group that sees no carousel, and measure total bookings per guest and not only bookings attributed to the carousel. Use a window of 7 days after the click. For marketplace interference, check the sum of bookings in the whole market and consider randomising at market level.

@@ street-view-blurring
title: Design face and licence-plate blurring for street imagery
category: AI & ML
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: objdetect, cv, batchproc, objectstore, clfmetrics, evals, gpucomp, ray, sampling
summary: A mapping company drives cars with 360-degree cameras and publishes the images as a street-level map. Before any image goes public, every readable face and every licence plate in it must be blurred. This is a privacy duty and, in many countries, a legal one. The task looks like a standard computer vision problem, but the goals are unusual. We do not want the best accuracy on average. We want almost no misses, because one unblurred face is a harm and one extra blurred poster is not. The images are huge (33 megapixels each), the faces can be 15 pixels tall, and the work must run for tens of millions of new images per month and for a backlog of one billion old ones. We design the full pipeline: the detector, the batch system on GPUs, the blur step, the quality measurement with sampling and human audit, the publishing gate that fails closed, and the feedback loop that finds the misses.
requirements: Functional requirements. Take a panorama (an equirectangular 360-degree image) and produce a blurred copy in which every face and every licence plate above a minimum size is made unreadable. Save the detections (boxes with scores) so that we can re-blur later without running the model again. Support blur requests from people ("blur my house, my car, me"), which add a manual region to a panorama. Never publish an image that has not passed the blur step with an approved model version. Re-run old images when a better model arrives.
Non-functional requirements. Recall: at least 99.5 percent of faces that are 20 pixels tall or larger, and at least 99 percent of plates that are readable by a person, measured on a labelled test set that we trust. Precision is a secondary goal: we accept that up to 10 percent of blur boxes cover something that is not a face or plate (a poster, a mannequin, a wheel). Throughput: 50 million new panoramas per month, with a delay of under 48 hours from upload to publication. Backlog: one billion panoramas re-processed within 30 days when a major model upgrade needs it. Security: the raw images are more sensitive than the blurred ones. Only the pipeline and a small group of staff may read them, and every read is logged. Cost: GPU hours are the main cost and we want to keep the whole pipeline near full utilisation.
Assumptions I choose. Panoramas are 8192 by 4096 pixels, JPEG, about 6 MB each. An average panorama has 4 faces and 1 licence plate that pass the size limit. Roads in cities have far more.
estimates: New load. 50 million panoramas per month divided by 30 days is 1.67 million per day, and divided by 86,400 seconds is about 19 per second. Capture is bursty (cars drive in the daytime and upload in batches), so peak is about 3 times the average: 60 per second for a few hours.
Raw storage. 50 million times 6 MB is 300 TB per month for new images, or 3.6 PB per year. The backlog of one billion images is 6 PB. In an object store at about 2 cents per GB per month, 6 PB costs roughly 120,000 dollars per month. This is why raw images go to a cheaper cold tier after processing, and the blurred copy is what we serve. The blurred copy is about the same size as the raw, so we pay for both.
Tiles for the detector. A detector works well at about 1,280 pixels on the long side. A face 20 pixels tall in an 8192-wide panorama would be only 3 pixels if we shrink the whole image to 1,280. So we cut the image into tiles at full resolution. We first project the panorama into 6 cube faces of 2,048 by 2,048 pixels, to remove the strong stretching near the top and bottom poles. We then cut each face into four overlapping tiles of 1,280 by 1,280 (the overlap is 512 pixels, so a face on a tile border is whole in at least one tile). This gives 24 tiles per panorama.
Compute per panorama. A mid-size detector costs about 80 GFLOPs at 640 by 640. A tile of 1,280 by 1,280 has 4 times more pixels, so about 320 GFLOPs. 24 tiles give about 7.7 TFLOPs per panorama. A data-centre GPU for inference sustains about 20 TFLOPs in half precision at realistic utilisation (a card with a peak of 65 TFLOPs usually reaches 25 to 40 percent). So one panorama costs 7.7 / 20 = 0.38 s of GPU time, or 2.6 panoramas per second per GPU.
GPU count for new data. 19 per second divided by 2.6 is 7.3 GPUs at the average. At peak, 60 / 2.6 is 23 GPUs. Because we have 48 hours to finish, we do not have to size for the peak. We queue and run at a steady rate: with 12 GPUs we process 31 per second, a safe margin over 19.
GPU count for the backlog. One billion divided by 2.6 per second is 385 million GPU-seconds, which is 107,000 GPU-hours. In 30 days (720 hours) we need 107,000 / 720 = about 150 GPUs. At a spot price of about 0.50 dollars per GPU-hour the bill is about 53,000 dollars. That is small compared with the 120,000 dollars per month for storing the images, so we can afford to re-run the backlog when the model improves and not live with an old model for years.
CPU work. Decoding a 33-megapixel JPEG takes about 150 ms on one core and encoding the blurred result about 200 ms. Projecting to cube faces adds about 100 ms. That is 0.45 s of CPU per panorama, as much as the GPU time. For the backlog this is 450 million core-seconds, 125,000 core-hours, or 175 cores for 30 days. If the CPU stage is too slow the GPUs wait, so the CPU side must be sized together with the GPU side. GPU JPEG decoding (nvJPEG) can shift part of this work.
Labels. To estimate a 99.5 percent recall with a confidence interval of plus or minus 0.1 percentage points, we need about 20,000 labelled face instances (see the deep dive). At 4 faces per image this is 5,000 images, and a labeller needs about 90 seconds per image to box faces, plates and mark hard cases. That is 125 hours of labelling for one evaluation set. We keep several such sets, one per region and camera, so the total is a few thousand hours per year.
api: Pipeline API (internal). POST /v1/blur-jobs with {input_prefix or pano_ids[], model_version, mode: "new" | "reprocess", priority}. Returns {job_id}. GET /v1/blur-jobs/{job_id} returns counts of queued, running, done and failed panoramas and the p95 age of the oldest item. Jobs are idempotent: the output key contains the pano_id and the model_version, so a repeated job overwrites the same objects.
Detection record. GET /v1/panos/{pano_id}/detections?model_version=v12 returns a list {class: "face"|"plate", box: [x, y, w, h] in panorama pixels, score, source: "model"|"propagated"|"human"|"request"}. Only the blur service and the audit tool can call this.
Public tile API. GET /tiles/{pano_id}/{zoom}/{x}/{y}.jpg serves only blurred tiles. There is no public path to the raw image. A tile request first checks that the panorama's status is "published".
Blur requests. POST /v1/blur-requests with {pano_id, region: polygon in panorama coordinates, kind: "face"|"plate"|"house"|"person", contact}. The response is a ticket id. An approved request writes a record with source "request"; the tile renderer applies it on the next build and the change is permanent.
datamodel: The raw and the blurred images live in an object store, not in a database. Keys: raw/{capture_date}/{pano_id}.jpg and blurred/{model_version}/{pano_id}/{zoom}/{x}/{y}.jpg. The raw bucket has a strict access policy, server-side encryption, access logs, and a lifecycle rule that moves objects to a cold tier after 30 days. The blurred bucket is the one behind the CDN. The two buckets are in different accounts, so a mistake in the public side cannot expose raw images.
The metadata lives in a table store:
```sql
CREATE TABLE panos (
  pano_id        STRING PRIMARY KEY,
  capture_time   TIMESTAMP,
  camera_id      STRING,
  country        STRING,
  status         STRING,   -- uploaded, processing, blurred, published
  blur_version   STRING,   -- model that produced the live blur
  updated_at     TIMESTAMP
);
CREATE TABLE detections (
  pano_id        STRING,
  model_version  STRING,
  det_id         INT,
  class          STRING,   -- face, plate
  x INT, y INT, w INT, h INT,
  score          FLOAT,
  source         STRING,   -- model, propagated, human, request
  PRIMARY KEY (pano_id, model_version, det_id)
);
```
The detections table is partitioned by pano_id. It holds the boxes for the final blur and also for every earlier model version, so we can compare versions on the same images. A third table, audit_labels, holds the human reviews: (pano_id, reviewer, boxes, reviewed_at, sample_kind). The sample_kind column says whether the image came from the random sample, a low-confidence queue or a user report, because each kind needs a different weight when we compute the miss rate.
design: The pipeline has five stages, run as a batch process on a cluster. Batch processing fits because there is no user waiting for a single image, and the work is large, parallel and can be repeated.
Stage 1: ingest. Cars upload panoramas to the raw bucket. An event adds a row in the panos table with status "uploaded" and puts the pano_id into a work queue. A quick check rejects broken files and records the camera, the time and the country.
Stage 2: prepare. A CPU worker decodes the JPEG, projects the equirectangular image into 6 cube faces and cuts 24 tiles of 1,280 by 1,280. Tiles are not written to disk. They are passed in memory to the next stage as a batch. Decoding is the slowest CPU step, so each node runs many decode workers.
Stage 3: detect. The GPU workers hold the object detection model in memory. A detector is a convolutional or transformer network that outputs boxes, a class (face or plate) and a score for each. We use a single-stage detector in the YOLO family, fine-tuned on our own labelled panoramas, because it is fast and we need to scan 24 tiles per image. The model runs in half precision, compiled with TensorRT, with a batch of 16 to 32 tiles so the GPU stays busy (GPU computing is efficient only with large batches). A low score threshold keeps recall high.
Stage 4: merge and refine. The boxes from tiles are mapped back to panorama coordinates. Duplicates from tile overlaps are merged with non-maximum suppression. Boxes are made larger by 30 percent in each direction, because blur must cover hair, ears and the edge of the plate. A second pass adds three safety rules: (a) temporal propagation: panoramas are captured every 5 to 10 metres, so a face found in the frames before and after, at a consistent place, is added in the middle frame if the model missed it; (b) a lower threshold in regions of the image where the neighbours found something; (c) a second, different model (for example a face-specific detector) runs on crops of persons and cars, and its boxes are added by union. The union of detectors raises recall and lowers precision, which is the direction we want.
Stage 5: blur and render. The service draws the blur on the full-resolution panorama, not on a shrunken copy. The blur is irreversible: we average the pixels in each box down to a coarse grid with a large kernel and add mild noise, and we do not use light blurs that a deconvolution model could partly undo. The result is encoded and cut into the tile pyramid for the viewer. The blurred copy goes to the public bucket. The status changes to "blurred" and, after the gate checks, to "published".
Orchestration. We run the stages as a streaming pipeline on Ray (Ray Data), with CPU actors for the prepare and render stages and a pool of GPU actors for the detect stage. Streaming means the CPU decode of the next batch overlaps with the GPU work on the current one, so the GPUs do not wait. Ray's object store passes tiles in memory between stages on the same node, and its backpressure stops the decode workers from running too far ahead. If a node is lost, Ray re-runs the lost tasks. Since each task writes outputs under deterministic keys, a re-run is harmless.
The publishing gate. A panorama becomes "published" only when five conditions hold: all 24 tiles have been processed; the detections were made by a model version on the approved list; the blurred output exists; the checksum of the blurred image differs from the raw one; and no pending blur request exists for it. If any condition is false, the image stays private. The system fails closed.

deepdive: Measuring recall honestly. We evaluate on a labelled set, but the set must represent the data. We use stratified sampling: we split the data into strata by country, camera type, time of day, and face size, and we draw a fixed number from each stratum, then we weight the results by the real stratum sizes. A random sample would hold too few night images in snowy regions, and these are exactly the cases where models miss. Each face is one trial: the model finds it or not. If the true recall is 99.5 percent, the standard error with n = 20,000 faces is the square root of 0.995 times 0.005 divided by 20,000, which is 0.05 percentage points. A 95 percent confidence interval is about plus or minus 0.1 points. To prove only that the miss rate is below 0.5 percent when we saw no misses at all, the rule of three says we need about 600 faces. But a model never has zero misses, so we use the 20,000-face set for the real figure. We report the lower bound of the interval, not the mean, in the release decision.
Choosing the threshold. A detector gives a score for each box. As we lower the threshold, recall goes up and precision goes down. We plot the precision-recall curve per class and per size bucket and pick the lowest threshold at which the precision is still acceptable (for instance, at least 90 percent), then check that recall passes the target. We do this per class, because plates are easier than faces. Faces are smaller, more varied and include profiles, hats, masks, mirrors and reflections. For tiny faces under 20 pixels, the person is not identifiable, so we exclude them from the target but still blur them when found.
Audit sampling in production. The labelled set shows how the model did on yesterday's data. Production data changes: a new camera, a new city, a new season. So every day we send a random 0.1 percent of the processed panoramas (about 1,700 images a day at 1.67 million) to human reviewers who mark any face or plate that was left unblurred. Each audited image gives a small number of trials, so the daily estimate is noisy; we pool a week (about 12,000 images and 48,000 faces) to track the miss rate to about plus or minus 0.06 points. We also send images from a second queue, the low-confidence queue: panoramas where the model's best boxes have scores just below the threshold, or where the count of detections is far lower than the average for the area. These images have more misses per review hour, but they are not a random sample, so we keep them in a separate stratum and do not mix them into the miss-rate estimate. They train the next model.
Training and the data loop. The training set grows from three sources: the first big labelled set, the audit labels from production and hard negatives (posters, statues, wheels). Each new model is evaluated on a frozen test set and on the slices, then runs in shadow mode over 1 million recent panoramas. We send the images where it disagrees most with the production model to human review. A model must beat the current one on every slice. Then comes a canary: 1 percent of new panoramas are blurred with the new version, and the audit sample estimates its real miss rate. Only then is the version approved for the gate and the backlog re-run.
tradeoffs: It depends: one detector versus several. Choose a single model when the cost is the issue and when the audit shows a miss rate under the target. Choose a union of two or three detectors (a general detector, a face-specialised model, a text or plate model) when the cost of a miss is high, as it is here: recall improves a lot, because the models miss different things. You pay about twice the compute and more over-blur. Our GPU budget is small compared with storage, so we choose the union for faces.
It depends: tile at full resolution versus downscale. Choose tiling when objects are small relative to the image, as here: it costs 24 times the compute of one 1,280-pixel pass, but it is the only way to see a 20-pixel face. Choose a downscaled single pass for a first rough filter, for instance to skip panoramas that are mostly sky or the interior of a tunnel. A multi-scale scheme mixes both: a cheap pass on the whole image, then tiles only where the cheap pass sees people or cars. It can cut compute by a third, but adds complexity and a new way to miss things.
It depends: blur type. Choose a strong blur or pixelation with a large cell for faces and plates in public images. Choose a solid fill when legal teams ask for no trace at all, even though it looks worse. Avoid light Gaussian blur and low-resolution mosaics: research on deblurring and face recovery shows that mild blurs can leak identity. We blur at full resolution and never ship the unblurred pixels to the public tile service.
It depends: keep the raw images or delete them. Keep them when you plan to re-run better models and honour requests later, in a cold, locked bucket. Delete them when the law requires it or when the leak risk is larger than the value of re-processing. Some operators keep only the blurred copy and the box list after a retention period of months. This is a policy and an engineering decision together.
failures: A GPU node is pre-empted (spot instances are reclaimed with a two-minute warning). Ray retries the tasks on another node. Tasks write to a deterministic output key, so a partial result never becomes a final one: the status row changes only after all outputs are complete. Cost is a few seconds of repeated work.
A bad model version ships. The model passes the offline test but misses faces at night in one country. The canary stage with the audit sample catches it within a day at 1 percent of traffic, and the version is removed from the approved list. Panoramas blurred with it are marked "needs reprocessing" by the gate and re-run with the previous version within hours, because the raw images and the pipeline are ready.
A corrupted or very unusual image. A truncated JPEG, a black frame, a panorama with a wrong size, or one that crashes the decoder. The task fails after 3 attempts and the image goes to a dead-letter queue. The panorama stays private. A dashboard shows the dead-letter count, and an on-call alert fires if it passes 0.1 percent of the daily volume.
Leak of raw data. The raw bucket is in a separate account with its own keys, logs every read and allows a small list of service identities. The public CDN has no route to it. A scheduled test tries to fetch a raw key through the public path and must fail.
Silent drift. Nothing crashes, but a new camera model produces images with a different colour profile and the detection rate drops. We track the number of boxes per 1,000 panoramas, by camera and by region, and the histogram of scores. An alert fires if the box rate for a camera drops by more than 20 percent against its 4-week average. The audit sample then confirms if it is a real drop in recall.
evolution: At 100,000 panoramas, a single GPU box and a script are enough, with a spreadsheet of audit results. At 10 million, add a queue, a few GPU workers, the metadata tables and a daily audit. At 1 billion, add the Ray pipeline, a spot-instance fleet, the union of detectors, temporal propagation and the strict publishing gate. Later, move a first blur to the vehicle, so raw faces never leave the car. The edge model is small and misses more, so the cloud step stays as a second check.
followups: Why is recall the main metric and not mean average precision? || Mean average precision treats misses and false alarms as equally bad. Here a miss exposes a person and a false alarm only blurs a poster. We fix a recall target per class and size bucket, then choose the highest precision that reaches it. We report the lower confidence bound of recall, and slice-level recall as well as the average.
How do you estimate the miss rate in production, where you have no labels? || Draw a random sample (for instance 0.1 percent of panoramas per day), have people mark unblurred faces and plates, and count the misses as a fraction of true faces. Pool a week for a narrow confidence interval. Keep low-confidence samples as a separate stratum for training and do not mix them into the estimate.
Why not run the detector on the whole image shrunk to 1,280 pixels? || A 20-pixel face in an 8,192-pixel panorama shrinks to 3 pixels, which no detector can find. We tile at full resolution, with overlap so no face is cut at a border, and use cube-face projection to avoid stretching near the poles. The cost is 24 detector passes per panorama.
A new model is better on average but worse in one country. Do you ship it? || No. The release gate checks recall on every slice (country, camera, light, size). A slice that fails blocks the model. We either fix the gap with more labelled data from that slice, or run the old model there until the new one catches up.
How would you re-process a billion old panoramas safely? || Work in priority order (busy city streets first), use deterministic output keys so tasks are idempotent, run on spot GPUs with retries, keep the old blurred version live until the new one passes the gate, and swap per panorama. The job needs about 107,000 GPU-hours, so use the spare capacity and watch the audit sample for the new version.

title: Design training of a chat assistant (pre-training → SFT → RLHF)
category: AI & ML
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: llm, scalinglaws, parallelism, allreduce, mixedprec, flashattn, moe, bpe, rlhf, lora, evals
summary: We want to train a chat assistant from scratch: a large language model (an LLM, a transformer that predicts the next token) that follows instructions, answers in a helpful tone and refuses harmful requests. Training happens in three stages. Pre-training teaches the model language and world knowledge by predicting the next token on trillions of tokens of text. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) teaches it the chat format with a few hundred thousand good example conversations. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) then pushes the model toward answers that people prefer. The model has 70 billion parameters. The hard parts are the size of the compute (millions of GPU-hours), keeping thousands of GPUs busy and alive for weeks, and measuring whether a new model is really better. We design the plan, the data, the parallel training system, the post-training loop and the evaluation.
requirements: Functional requirements. Produce a model that (a) writes fluent text in at least 8 major languages and code in the common languages, (b) follows multi-turn instructions with a system prompt, (c) can use a context of 128 thousand tokens, (d) refuses clearly harmful requests but does not refuse harmless ones, and (e) can be served at a reasonable cost. The output of the project is a set of weights, a tokenizer, a chat template and an evaluation report.
Non-functional requirements. Compute budget: about 4 times 10^24 floating-point operations for pre-training, a cluster of about 8,000 GPUs, and a wall-clock limit of about 3 weeks for the main run. Efficiency: at least 40 percent of the GPUs' peak speed (model FLOPs utilisation, MFU). Reliability: the run must survive daily hardware failures and lose less than 10 percent of time to them. Reproducibility: every checkpoint can be traced to a data version, a code version and a config. Safety: the final model passes a fixed safety and red-team suite.
Objectives and metrics. Pre-training minimises the next-token cross-entropy loss on held-out data per domain. Post-training maximises human preference. Offline we track benchmark scores (knowledge, maths, code, reasoning, long context, multilingual), loss on held-out sets, and safety violation rates. Online (before launch) we use human side-by-side comparisons: the win rate against the previous model and against a reference model.
Assumptions I choose. Dense transformer, 70 billion parameters, 80 layers, hidden size 8,192, grouped-query attention. 10 trillion training tokens. H100-class GPUs, 8 per node with NVLink inside the node and a 400 Gbit/s network link per GPU between nodes. Sequence length 8,192 in the main phase.
estimates: Compute for pre-training. A standard rule: training needs about 6 floating-point operations per parameter per token (2 for the forward pass, 4 for the backward pass). With N = 70 billion parameters and D = 10 trillion tokens: C = 6 x 70e9 x 10e12 = 4.2e24 FLOPs.
GPU time. An H100 does about 990 TFLOPs in bf16 at peak. At 40 percent MFU we get about 400 TFLOPs. 4.2e24 / 4e14 = 1.05e10 GPU-seconds = 2.9 million GPU-hours. On 8,192 GPUs: 1.05e10 / 8,192 = 1.28 million seconds = 14.8 days. We plan 3 weeks to allow for failures and slow starts. At about 2 dollars per GPU-hour the cost is 2.9M x 2 = about 5.8 million dollars for the main run, plus small test runs (about 10 to 15 percent more).
Why this size? The scaling laws of the Chinchilla paper say that, for a fixed compute budget, the best loss comes when the tokens are about 20 times the parameters. For C = 4.2e24, N x D with D = 20 N gives 6 x 20 x N^2 = 4.2e24, so N = sqrt(3.5e22) = 187 billion parameters and D = 3.7 trillion tokens. We choose a smaller model (70B) and more tokens (10T, 143 per parameter) on purpose: the model is trained once but served billions of times, and a 70B model costs about 2.7 times less to serve than a 187B model. We accept a slightly higher training loss for the same compute.
Memory per parameter. Mixed precision training with Adam keeps: bf16 weights (2 bytes), bf16 gradients (2), fp32 master weights (4), and Adam's two moment vectors in fp32 (8). That is 16 bytes per parameter, so 70e9 x 16 = 1.12 TB for the states. One GPU has 80 GB, so the states alone need at least 14 GPUs, and activations need more. This is why one model must be split across many GPUs.
Parallel layout. We use 8-way tensor parallelism (inside one node, over NVLink), 4-way pipeline parallelism, and 256-way data parallelism: 8 x 4 x 256 = 8,192 GPUs. One model replica spans 32 GPUs, so each GPU holds 70e9 / 32 = 2.2 billion parameters, with 35 GB of states. The rest of the 80 GB is for activations and buffers.
Batch and steps. Global batch size 16 million tokens, which is 2,048 sequences of 8,192 tokens. Each data-parallel replica processes 2,048 / 256 = 8 sequences per step, split into micro-batches for the pipeline. Steps = 10e12 / 16e6 = 625,000. Step time = 1.28e6 s / 625,000 = about 2.05 s.
Communication. Per step, each GPU holds 2.2 billion bf16 gradients = 4.4 GB. A ring all-reduce over the 256 data-parallel replicas moves 2 x 4.4 = 8.8 GB per GPU. At 50 GB/s (400 Gbit/s) this is 0.18 s, and most of it overlaps with the backward pass. Tensor parallelism needs two all-reduces per layer in each direction, over NVLink at several hundred GB/s, so it is cheap inside the node but would be slow across nodes.
Attention memory. With a sequence of 8,192 tokens, one attention matrix has 8,192 x 8,192 = 67 million entries, 134 MB in bf16, per head. With 64 heads that is 8.6 GB per layer for one sequence. Written to GPU memory, this is a bottleneck in memory traffic. FlashAttention avoids writing it.
Failures. A cluster of 8,192 GPUs fails often. A public report for a 16,000-GPU run says roughly one interruption every 3 hours. At half the size, plan on one every 6 hours: about 4 per day. If we save a checkpoint every 30 minutes and a restart takes 10 minutes, each failure costs on average 15 + 10 = 25 minutes. 4 failures per day x 25 min = 100 min = 7 percent of the day. That meets our limit of 10 percent.
Post-training compute. SFT with 1 million examples of about 800 tokens for 3 epochs is 2.4 billion tokens: 6 x 70e9 x 2.4e9 = 1.0e21 FLOPs, or about 700 GPU-hours, less than 0.03 percent of pre-training. Reward model training and RLHF cost more, mainly because of text generation (see the deep dive), but still stay at a few percent of the pre-training cost.
api: This is a training system, so its interfaces are for engineers. Job submit: POST /v1/runs with {name, config_ref, data_version, code_sha, nodes: 1024, resume_from: optional checkpoint id}. Returns a run id and a state machine: queued, starting, running, restarting, stopped.
Checkpoint interface. save(step, shard_states) writes an atomic, sharded checkpoint with a manifest listing every shard and its checksum. load(checkpoint_id, layout) restores it, even into a different parallel layout (for example 8x4x256 to 8x4x128), because a checkpoint stores the full tensors by name and not by rank.
Data loader. next_batch(rank, step) returns the tokens for that data-parallel rank. It is deterministic: the same (seed, step, rank) always gives the same tokens, so a restart resumes exactly where it stopped, with no repeated or skipped data.
Eval service. POST /v1/evals with {checkpoint_id, suite: "pretrain-fast" | "post-full" | "safety", compare_to}. Returns scores per task, confidence intervals and a pass or fail against thresholds.
Serving contract (the final product). POST /v1/chat with {messages: [{role, content}], max_tokens, temperature}. The chat template adds special tokens such as start and end of turn. The template is part of the model release, because the model learned it in SFT.
datamodel: Pre-training data. Text is cleaned, deduplicated, tokenized and stored as shards of token ids in an object store. Each shard has about 1 billion tokens (4 GB as 32-bit ids, since a 128,000-entry vocabulary needs 17 bits). 10 trillion tokens = 10,000 shards = 40 TB. An index file lists each shard with its source (web, code, books, maths, multilingual), language, quality score bucket and token count, so the sampler can build the mixture by weights.

Preference records hold one prompt and two answers with a choice: {prompt_id, prompt, chosen, rejected, margin (1 to 4), annotator_id, model_versions, rubric_scores}. We keep both model versions that wrote the answers, because the reward model must see answers from the current policy to stay accurate.
Run metadata is a table keyed by (run_id, step): loss, learning rate, gradient norm, tokens seen, MFU, data shard ids, and the hardware ids of any node that was replaced. When the loss goes wrong we can find what data and what machine were involved.
design: The project is a sequence of stages with a gate after each one.
Stage 0: scaling-law experiments. Before spending 5 million dollars, we train 20 to 30 small models (from 50 million to 3 billion parameters) on the same data pipeline, using about 1 percent of the final compute. We fit a curve that predicts the final loss from model size N and tokens D:
\[ L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}} \]
E is the irreducible loss of the data, and A, B, alpha and beta are fitted. The same runs pick the learning rate, the batch size and the data mixture. They show if a design change (for instance a mixture of experts layer) helps. Scaling laws turn a 6 million dollar gamble into a forecast.
Stage 1: data and tokenizer. We crawl and filter the web, add code, books, papers and maths, remove duplicates (MinHash on documents, exact match on lines), remove personal data and toxic pages, and score quality with a small classifier. We train a tokenizer with byte-pair encoding (BPE) on a sample: it starts from bytes and merges the most frequent pairs until the vocabulary has 128,000 entries. A larger vocabulary means fewer tokens per text (about 15 percent fewer than a 32,000 vocabulary on English, more on other languages) and so cheaper training and serving. The cost is a bigger embedding matrix: 128,000 x 8,192 = 1.05 billion parameters.
Stage 2: pre-training. The transformer is a stack of 80 blocks, each with self-attention and a feed-forward network. We use the AdamW optimiser, a warm-up of 2,000 steps, a peak learning rate around 1.5e-4 and a cosine decay to 10 percent. We train on bf16 with fp32 master weights (mixed precision). The three parallel dimensions, tensor, pipeline and data, are described in the estimates. Inside each block, FlashAttention computes exact attention in tiles that stay in the GPU's fast on-chip memory, so the 8.6 GB attention matrix is never written out, and attention becomes up to 2 to 4 times faster. In the last 5 percent of the run the learning rate decays to near zero while the mixture is shifted toward high-quality data (an "annealing" phase). A final stage extends the context from 8,192 to 128,000 tokens over about 800 billion tokens with long documents, in steps.
Stage 3: SFT. Starting from the pre-trained weights, we train on 1 million conversations (half written or edited by contractors, half synthetic and filtered) with a small learning rate (1e-5) for 2 to 3 epochs. The loss is the same next-token loss, only on assistant tokens. SFT teaches format, tone and instruction following. It does not add much knowledge. We also use LoRA here: instead of updating all 70 billion weights, we freeze them and learn two small matrices (rank 16) next to each attention projection. That is about 84 million trainable parameters, 0.12 percent of the model, and it lets us run dozens of SFT experiments on 64 GPUs in a few hours. The main release is a full fine-tune, and the LoRA runs find the best data mix.
Stage 4: RLHF. We collect 500,000 preference pairs. A reward model (the SFT model with a scalar head) is trained so that it scores the chosen answer higher than the rejected one. Then the policy is optimised against the reward model, with a penalty that keeps it near the SFT model. We run several rounds: each round, the current model writes answers, people compare them, the reward model is retrained, and the policy is updated.
Stage 5: evaluate and release. A frozen suite covers capabilities, long context, multilingual, safety and human side-by-side comparison. A model is released only if it passes every gate.

deepdive: Parallelism in detail. Data parallelism copies the model on many replicas, gives each a different part of the batch and averages the gradients with an all-reduce. It is simple, but each replica needs the whole model. Sharding methods (ZeRO and FSDP) cut the memory by splitting the optimiser states, then the gradients, then the weights across the data-parallel group, at the cost of more communication. Tensor parallelism splits each matrix multiplication across the GPUs of one node: each GPU computes part of the columns and a collective operation sums the result. It needs very fast links, so it stays inside the NVLink domain of 8 GPUs. Pipeline parallelism puts different layers on different GPUs and passes activations forward. Its problem is the bubble: while the first micro-batch travels, later GPUs are idle. With p stages and m micro-batches the idle fraction is about (p - 1) / (m + p - 1), which is 3 / 11 = 27 percent for p = 4 and m = 8. That is too much. An interleaved schedule gives each GPU v = 4 smaller chunks of layers and cuts the bubble to about (p - 1) / (v m) = 3 / 32 = 9 percent. Combining all three is called 3D parallelism, and the layout puts the chattiest communication on the fastest link.
All-reduce and the network. The gradient all-reduce is the main traffic between nodes. A ring all-reduce over p workers sends 2 (p - 1) / p times the data size per worker, which hardly depends on p, so it scales well in bandwidth, but its latency grows with p. Production systems use hierarchical schemes: reduce inside the node over NVLink, then across nodes over the network, then broadcast. One slow link or one bad GPU slows the whole step, because all workers wait. Before the job we run a pairwise all-reduce benchmark and replace the slowest nodes. During the run a loss spike (from bad data or numerical overflow) is handled by clipping the gradient norm to 1.0, rolling back to the last good checkpoint and skipping about 200 steps of data, which the deterministic loader makes exact.
Mixture of experts as an alternative. A dense model uses all 70 billion parameters for each token. A mixture of experts (MoE) replaces the feed-forward network of each block with, say, 64 experts and a router that picks 2 per token. The model can hold 400 billion parameters while only about 40 billion are active per token, so compute per token is like a 40B model and capacity is like a 400B model. DeepSeek-V3, for example, has 671 billion total and 37 billion active parameters. The costs: all experts must sit in memory, tokens travel to their experts by all-to-all communication across nodes, the router needs load balancing, and serving is harder. We stay dense for the first release for simplicity and stability.
Reward model and the RLHF objective. The reward model is trained on pairs with the Bradley-Terry loss:
\[ \mathcal{L}_{RM} = -\log \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big) \]
where y_w is the chosen answer, y_l the rejected one and sigma the logistic function. The policy then maximises the reward with a KL penalty to the reference (SFT) model:
\[ \max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{x, y \sim \pi}\big[r(x,y)\big] - \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot|x) \,\|\, \pi_{ref}(\cdot|x)\big) \]
Without the KL term the policy finds answers that fool the reward model (reward hacking): very long answers, flattery or odd phrases that the reward model likes. PPO needs four large models in memory: policy, reference, reward model and a value network. At 70B this is 4 x 140 GB in bf16 for weights alone, plus optimiser states for the policy and the value network. The time goes mostly to generation: for each prompt we generate 4 to 8 answers of 500 tokens with an inference engine (KV cache, continuous batching), because generation is memory-bound and slow compared with training. Simpler methods are often preferred: rejection sampling (sample 8 answers, keep the best by reward model, fine-tune on them) and direct preference optimisation (DPO), which trains on the preference pairs with a classification-style loss and needs no reward model during training and no generation loop.
Evaluating the assistant. Pre-training evals are cheap: held-out loss per domain after every 1,000 steps, and a fast suite of 10 benchmarks every 10,000 steps. Benchmarks are in the data pool of the whole internet, so we run a contamination check (n-gram overlap of 13 tokens between train and test) and keep a private set of new questions. Post-training evals add human side-by-side comparison on 2,000 prompts, with a win-rate confidence interval: at a true win rate of 55 percent, the standard error with n = 2,000 is sqrt(0.55 x 0.45 / 2,000) = 1.1 points, so a 5-point edge is detectable and a 1-point edge is not. An LLM used as a judge is cheap and fast, but it prefers long answers and its own style, so we calibrate it against humans on a sample. The safety suite checks refusals on 5,000 harmful prompts and over-refusals on 5,000 harmless but edgy prompts, since both errors matter.
tradeoffs: It depends: bigger model versus more tokens. Choose the compute-optimal size (about 20 tokens per parameter) when you want the lowest loss for a fixed training budget and the model will be used rarely. Choose a smaller model trained on far more tokens (over 100 per parameter) when serving cost dominates, which is true for a chat product. The price is a higher training cost for the same quality as a larger model, and diminishing returns after the data runs out of good tokens.
It depends: dense versus mixture of experts. Choose dense when the team is new to training at scale, when serving on few GPUs matters, and when stability matters more than efficiency. Choose MoE when you need the quality of a very large model at the compute cost of a smaller one and you have a strong network and a team that can handle routing, load balance and all-to-all traffic. You give up simplicity and memory efficiency.
It depends: PPO versus DPO versus rejection sampling. Choose PPO when you have a good reward model, need online learning from the current policy's own outputs, and can pay for four models and a generation loop. Choose DPO when you have a fixed set of preference pairs and want a stable, cheap run. Choose rejection sampling plus SFT for the first rounds, since it is simple and robust. Many teams mix them: rejection sampling to improve, DPO to polish.
It depends: full fine-tune versus LoRA. Choose full fine-tuning for the main release, where quality counts and you have the GPUs: it changes all weights and learns more. Choose LoRA for fast experiments, for many task or customer adapters on one base model, and when memory is small: the adapter is about 0.12 percent of the model, and the base model is shared. The quality of LoRA on hard new skills, such as learning a new language, is usually lower.
failures: A node or GPU dies (the most common event, a few times per day). The job controller detects a missing heartbeat or a hang through a watchdog timeout on collectives (all workers wait in an all-reduce and none returns), stops the job, swaps in a spare node from a pool of 2 to 3 percent extra, and restarts from the last checkpoint. The deterministic data loader resumes at the right step. Cost: about 25 minutes each time.
Silent data corruption. A GPU returns a wrong number without an error. The loss gets worse or a gradient norm is odd on one rank. We compare per-rank gradient norms and checksums of the weights across data-parallel replicas every few hundred steps, and drain the machine that disagrees.
Loss spikes and divergence. Described above: roll back, skip data, lower the learning rate. We keep checkpoints at 12-hour spacing so that we can go back more than once if the bad data was already inside an earlier one.
Reward hacking and over-optimisation. The reward goes up but humans like the answers less. We monitor the KL to the reference, the average answer length and a held-out set of human comparisons, and we stop when the human win rate peaks. The reward model is retrained each round with fresh samples.
evolution: First test the pipeline with a 1 billion parameter model on 20 billion tokens on 8 GPUs for a day: data, tokenizer, eval harness. Next, a 7 billion model on 1 trillion tokens on 256 GPUs for a week, with sharded data parallelism only, to test the recipe. Then the 70B run with 3D parallelism. Later versions add longer context, multilingual data, tool use, a mixture of experts, and reinforcement learning against verifiable rewards (unit tests for code, exact answers for maths), which uses the same infrastructure with a bigger share of generation.
followups: Why is the cost of training about 6 N D? || Each parameter takes part in one multiply-add (2 FLOPs) per token in the forward pass. The backward pass computes gradients with respect to activations and weights, about twice the forward cost, so 4 FLOPs. Total 6 per parameter per token. It ignores attention over long contexts, which adds a few percent.
What happens when a GPU fails in the middle of a run? || Detect through heartbeats and collective timeouts, stop the job, replace the node, reload the latest complete checkpoint, and resume with a deterministic data loader. With checkpoints every 30 minutes, the loss is about 15 minutes of work plus restart time. Store checkpoints by tensor name so the layout can change.
What is reward hacking and how do you reduce it? || The policy finds outputs with a high reward-model score but low real quality, such as very long or flattering answers. Reduce it with the KL penalty to the reference model, early stopping on a held-out human comparison, a reward model retrained on fresh samples, and length or format controls.
How do you know the new model is better and not just lucky? || Use many tasks, report confidence intervals, and compare on the same prompts. For human win rate with n = 2,000 the standard error is about 1.1 points, so only differences above 2 to 3 points count. Check contamination, run a private test set, and check slices such as languages and safety.

title: Design email smart compose
category: AI & ML
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: llm, decoding, bpe, kvcache, quant, distill, llmserving, specdecode, evals, privacytech
summary: While a person writes an email, the editor shows a short grey suggestion after the cursor, for example "Thank you for your" followed by "quick reply". The person presses Tab to accept it, or keeps typing to ignore it. This is "smart compose". It saves a few keystrokes at a time, and over millions of emails this adds up. What makes it hard is the combination of three limits. The suggestion must appear in about 100 ms, before the next keystroke, so the model must be small and the serving path very lean. It must be right most of the time, because a wrong suggestion is noise that people turn off. And it is trained on and run over private text, so privacy is a first-class requirement and not an add-on. We design the model, the training and distillation pipeline, the serving system with KV-cache reuse and speculative decoding, the evaluation, and the privacy controls for a webmail product with 100 million daily active writers.
requirements: Functional requirements. As the user types the body of an email, return at most one suggestion of up to about 8 tokens (around 5 words) that continues the text at the cursor. The suggestion uses the context: the text so far, the subject line, the previous message in the thread, the recipient's name, the time of day and the language. Tab accepts it. Any other key either confirms it (if the user types the same characters) or dismisses it. The system never suggests across a sentence end, never suggests an email address, a phone number or a number that is not already in the context, and never completes sensitive topics from a blocklist. Users can turn the feature off, and it is off for accounts in regions or plans that exclude it.
Non-functional requirements. Latency: the suggestion appears within 100 ms of the keystroke at p95 as seen by the user, which leaves about 30 ms at p99 for the server after the network round trip and the client debounce. Quality: precision matters more than coverage. We show a suggestion only if the model is confident, and target an acceptance rate (accepted divided by shown) of at least 20 percent. Availability: if the service is slow or down, the editor works as usual, with no suggestions. Privacy: no human reads the email text, the text is not stored in logs, the training procedure limits memorisation, and the model cannot show one person's private phrases to another. Cost: the feature must cost less than the value of the saved time and the engagement it brings, so we aim for under 0.1 cent per active user per day.
Metrics. Offline: perplexity, and the exact-match rate of the first N tokens of the suggestion against what the user really wrote (ExactMatch@N), computed on held-out emails by replaying typing. Online: acceptance rate, coverage (the share of keystroke opportunities where we show a suggestion), characters saved per email, and guardrails: the share of users who switch the feature off, the share of accepted suggestions that the user deletes within 3 seconds, and the latency. The product number is expected characters saved = coverage x acceptance rate x average accepted length.
Assumptions I choose. 100 million daily active users write emails. Each writes 5 emails a day with 400 characters on average. The model is a transformer with about 120 million parameters. The context given to the model is about 64 tokens.
estimates: Request volume. A client sends a request after a pause in typing, roughly every 3 characters once the user has typed at least 3 words. Per email: 400 characters / 3 = about 130 opportunities, but the client skips the ones where the previous suggestion still matches what is typed, or where the cursor is in the middle of a sentence. Say 60 requests per email. 5 emails x 60 = 300 requests per user per day. 100 million x 300 = 3e10 requests per day. Divided by 86,400 s is 347,000 per second on average. Writing happens in office hours, so the peak is about 3 times the average: roughly 1 million requests per second. The client-side reuse and the server cache (below) remove about 40 percent, so the model sees about 600,000 requests per second at peak.
Compute per request. A transformer needs about 2 x N floating-point operations per token. With N = 120 million, that is 0.24 GFLOPs per token. Prefill of 64 context tokens: 15 GFLOPs. Decoding 8 tokens: 2 GFLOPs. The total is about 17 GFLOPs per request, but with the KV cache we prefill only the 3 new tokens since the last request: 3 x 0.24 = 0.7 GFLOPs plus 2 GFLOPs of decoding, about 2.7 GFLOPs.
GPU count. Decoding is limited by memory reads, not by arithmetic: each decode step reads all the weights once (120 MB in int8), and a batch of requests shares that read. A mid-range inference GPU has about 300 GB/s of memory bandwidth, so a step costs 0.4 ms for the weights, about 1.5 ms with overhead. A batch of 32 requests needs 8 steps = 12 ms, so one GPU serves 32 / 0.012 = 2,700 requests per second. For 600,000 requests per second we need 600,000 / 2,700 = 222 GPUs. We add 50 percent for headroom and zone failure: about 330 GPUs. Without the KV cache the compute per request would be 17 / 2.7 = 6 times higher.
Memory. Weights: 120 million x 1 byte (int8) = 120 MB. The KV cache per token for a model with 12 layers, hidden size 768, in fp16: 2 (K and V) x 12 x 768 x 2 bytes = 37 KB. A 72-token session holds 2.7 MB. At the peak, about 500,000 users are typing at the same moment. 500,000 x 2.7 MB = 1.35 TB, which is about 4 GB per GPU on 330 GPUs. A 24 GB card holds this easily.
Training. The teacher model has 2 billion parameters. Pre-training on 300 billion tokens costs 6 x 2e9 x 3e11 = 3.6e21 FLOPs. At 400 TFLOPs per GPU, this is 9e6 GPU-seconds = 2,500 GPU-hours, about 5 days on 20 GPUs. Distillation of the 120M student on 100 billion tokens, with the teacher run forward-only (2 x 2e9 x 1e11 = 4e20 FLOPs plus the student's cost): about 300 GPU-hours. The training bill is small next to the serving fleet.
api: POST /v1/compose/suggest. Body: {compose_id, seq, prefix, subject, thread_summary_ids, recipient_first_name, locale, client_time}. Returns {seq, suggestion, confidence, model_version} or 204 No Content when there is nothing to show. The seq number (increasing per compose) lets the client drop an answer that arrives after the user has typed more. The client sends the last 300 characters only, not the whole email.
POST /v1/compose/events. Body: {compose_id, event: "shown" | "accepted" | "dismissed" | "typed_through", suggestion_length, latency_ms, model_version}. No text. It trains nothing by itself, but feeds the online metrics and the guardrails.
Client behaviour. The client waits 40 ms after the last keystroke (debounce), cancels any in-flight request when a new key arrives, and keeps the last suggestion. If the next typed characters match the start of the suggestion, the client removes those characters from the grey text and shows the rest with no new request ("typed-through"). It stores nothing about the email on disk for this feature.
datamodel: There is little durable state in the serving path. The serving replica keeps an in-memory table: key (compose_id), value {token ids of the prefix so far, the KV cache blocks for those tokens, last_used}. Entries expire after 60 seconds of no use and are evicted least-recently-used when memory is full. The load balancer routes by compose_id (consistent hashing), so the same replica sees all requests of one email and finds its cache.
Event logs hold only numbers and ids, never the text. A suggestion event is a row:

```sql
  compose_id     STRING,    -- random per email, not linked to content

  locale         STRING,
  event          STRING,    -- shown, accepted, dismissed, typed_through

  confidence     FLOAT,
  latency_ms     INT

```
Training data is kept apart. The pre-training corpus is made of public and licensed text. If we also train on users' emails, they are processed inside a secure environment with automated access only, de-identified (names, numbers and addresses replaced by placeholders), deduplicated, and stored with a short retention. A table of "canary" strings, secret random sequences that we insert on purpose into a small part of the training data, supports the memorisation tests described later.
design: The system has an offline path that builds a small, fast, private model, and an online path that answers each keystroke within a few milliseconds.
Model. We use a transformer decoder (a small LLM) that reads a prompt and predicts the next tokens. The prompt has a fixed layout: the subject, a short summary of the previous message in the thread, the recipient's first name, and the text typed so far. The text is cut into tokens by byte-pair encoding (BPE), with a vocabulary of 32,000 pieces trained on email text, so that common phrases such as "Thank you for" are 3 tokens. A smaller vocabulary than a general chat model keeps the embedding matrix small: 32,000 x 768 = 25 million parameters, one fifth of the model.
Training and distillation. Step 1: pre-train a 2-billion-parameter teacher on 300 billion tokens of text, with a mix of web text, public mailing lists and, under the privacy rules, email-style data. Step 2: fine-tune on the exact task: given the text so far, predict the continuation up to the end of the sentence, with the subject and thread as context. Step 3: distill. The small student (120 million parameters, 12 layers, hidden size 768) is trained to match the teacher's full probability distribution over the next token, using a KL loss, on a larger set of text than the teacher saw labels for. Distillation transfers much of the teacher's quality into the student, so the student beats a 120M model trained directly by a clear margin. We also use sequence-level distillation: the teacher writes continuations with beam search, and the student learns from them. Step 4: quantization. We convert the student's weights and activations to 8-bit integers using calibration data (post-training quantization), and then run a short quantization-aware fine-tune if the offline metrics drop by more than 0.5 percent. Int8 halves the memory traffic compared to fp16 and doubles the arithmetic speed on tensor cores.
Serving. A request enters at the edge, near the user, and the load balancer sends it to the replica that owns the compose_id. On the replica, an inference engine (built like an LLM serving stack) keeps the model on the GPU and applies continuous batching: requests join the next decode step, and wait at most 2 ms for a batch to fill. If the cached prefix is a prefix of the new prompt, the engine reuses its KV cache and prefills only the new tokens. The engine then decodes up to 8 tokens, stops at a sentence end or a low-confidence token, and returns the text.
Decoding rules. Quality of the suggestion depends on how we pick tokens. Sampling would give diverse but risky text. We want the single most likely phrase, so we use beam search with beam width 3 to 4, which finds a continuation with a higher total probability than greedy decoding. We score the finished candidates by the average log-probability per token and show the suggestion only if the score passes a threshold that we tune on held-out data for the target acceptance rate. Constraints are applied during decoding: banned tokens (digits, "@", URLs), a stop at punctuation that ends a sentence, and a minimum of 2 tokens.
The token boundary problem. When the user has typed "Thank yo", the BPE tokenizer may cut this into "Thank" and " yo", but the token that the model saw in training for the full word is " you". The prompt ends in the middle of a token, which hurts the model. We use token healing: remove the last token from the prompt, and force the first generated token to start with the removed text (" yo" prefix). The model then picks " you" naturally. This is a small detail with a large effect on accepted suggestions, because users are most often in the middle of a word.
Filtering. After decoding, a cheap filter checks the suggestion against the blocklist, the rules on pronouns and names, and a classifier for toxic or sensitive content. A suggestion that fails is dropped.

deepdive: Making decoding fast. A decoder generates one token per step, and each step is slow on a GPU because it reads all the weights and does little arithmetic. For a suggestion of 8 tokens, 8 steps of 1.5 ms is 12 ms. Speculative decoding cuts the number of steps. A tiny draft model (10 million parameters) proposes k = 4 tokens, and the student checks all 4 in a single forward pass, accepting the longest prefix that matches what it would have chosen itself. The output is exactly the same as normal decoding, only faster. If each draft token is accepted with probability alpha = 0.7, the expected number of tokens per student pass is (1 - alpha^(k+1)) / (1 - alpha) = (1 - 0.7^5) / 0.3 = 2.77. So 8 tokens need about 3 student passes, plus a small draft cost. Email text is repetitive ("looking forward to hearing from you"), so alpha is often higher than for open text. For a model as small as ours the gain is smaller than for a big chat model, so we test it, and drop it if the draft costs more than it saves.
KV cache reuse. Without a cache, every keystroke would recompute the attention keys and values for the whole 64-token prompt. The KV cache stores them. When the user types 2 more characters, the new prompt is the old prompt plus 1 or 2 tokens, so only those are processed. Reuse works only if the next request reaches the same replica. We route by compose_id with consistent hashing. If a replica dies, the next request misses the cache and pays a full prefill of 15 GFLOPs, which takes about 5 ms more. That is acceptable, so we do not replicate the cache. The cache lives in GPU memory in fixed-size blocks (16 tokens per block), as in paged attention, to avoid fragmentation. The beam search forks the cache: the beams share the prefix blocks and only copy the last block.
Privacy. Email is among the most private data a person has. Four layers protect it. First, data handling: training and serving run inside a secure boundary, no human reads the text, logs contain no text, and training data has a short retention. Second, limiting memorisation: a language model trained on emails can repeat a rare string, such as a phone number, to someone else. Research on extraction attacks (Carlini et al.) shows this is real, and it grows with repeated text. So we deduplicate the training data, scrub known personal-data patterns, and fine-tune on user text with differentially private SGD where affordable: the gradient of each example is clipped to norm C and Gaussian noise is added, which gives a formal bound (epsilon) on what one example can change. It costs quality and compute, so we measure the loss against the non-private model. Third, we test: we insert canary strings, secret random sequences, into the training data and measure how much more likely the trained model is to produce them than random strings (the exposure metric). Release is blocked above a threshold. Fourth, output rules: we never output digits, addresses or names that are not in the user's own context, so even a memorised phone number could not appear.
Personalisation without leaking. Users want their own names and phrases to complete. We do not train a model per user. A small personal lexicon is built from the user's own sent mail (frequent contacts' first names, closing lines) and offered to the decoder as extra candidates for the first token. It is never shared across users.
Evaluating without peeking at private data. Offline we replay held-out emails from licensed or de-identified secure data: after 3 words we ask for a suggestion and compare it with the true text. We compute ExactMatch@N, coverage under the confidence threshold and characters saved per email. Human raters see only synthetic or public emails. We choose the confidence threshold by sweeping it on the replay and picking the point that maximises expected characters saved subject to an acceptance floor of 20 percent. An A/B test, randomised by user for at least 2 weeks, measures the real result. A model must not worsen the guardrails (switch-off rate, deletions after accept) even if acceptance goes up, since long, risky text can raise acceptance among a few users and annoy the others. The latency budget is: network 20 ms, queue and batch wait 5 ms, prefill 2 ms, decoding with speculation 6 ms, beam and filter 2 ms, about 35 ms at the median and a 70 ms budget at p99. The client drops any answer later than 150 ms.
tradeoffs: It depends: server-side versus on-device model. Choose the server when the model needs 100 million parameters or more, when you want to update it daily, and when your devices are weak. Choose on-device (a 20 to 30 million parameter int8 model, about 25 MB, running on the phone's NPU) when privacy is the headline, when latency below 20 ms matters or when the user is offline. On-device has no network cost and no server cost, but the model is smaller and weaker, updates ship with the app, and battery is used. Many products use both: a small model on the device for common phrases and a server model for the rest.
It depends: beam search versus greedy versus sampling. Choose greedy for the fastest path and the simplest code when suggestions are 2 to 3 tokens. Choose beam search of width 3 to 4 when suggestions are longer, since greedy can commit to a poor first token. Choose sampling only for creative features such as "rewrite my sentence". Beam search costs about 2 to 3 times more compute per step, but it improves the total probability and the acceptance rate.
It depends: precision versus coverage. Choose a high confidence threshold, with coverage of 15 to 25 percent of opportunities and an acceptance rate of 25 percent or more, if users find wrong suggestions distracting, which is the usual finding for typing aids. Choose a low threshold if the aim is to teach new users or to maximise the data for training. The right operating point is found with an A/B test that includes switch-off rate as a guardrail, because the damage of a noisy feature appears slowly, as users disable it.
failures: The service is slow or unavailable. The client has a 150 ms timeout and shows nothing. A circuit breaker on the client stops sending requests for 30 seconds after 5 timeouts in a row, so a broken backend does not get a flood of retries. The editor is not affected.
A replica or zone fails. The load balancer re-hashes the compose_ids of the lost replica to others. Sessions lose their KV cache, and the first request costs a full prefill. A zone loss removes a third of capacity; with 50 percent headroom, the fleet runs hotter and the p99 rises, but stays in the budget. If the queue length passes a limit, the replica sheds the oldest requests, since the user has already typed more.
A bad model is released. The offline gates (quality, safety suite, canary exposure test) catch most problems. The rest is caught by the canary: 1 percent of users for 24 hours, with automatic rollback if the acceptance rate drops by 10 percent, the switch-off rate rises by 20 percent or the latency passes the budget. Rollback is a config change (PUT /v1/rollout) and takes under a minute.
A harmful or embarrassing suggestion is reported. Users can report a suggestion with one click. The text is not logged, so the report includes the visible context only with the user's consent. An operator adds a rule to the blocklist, and replicas apply it within a minute. The incident is added to the safety evaluation set so that it never returns. Because the model completes common phrases and is rarely creative, severe cases are rare, but bias in pronouns and stereotyped completions need active tests.
Data drift. People write differently after a holiday or a new product launch, and new names, products and phrases appear. We track the confidence histogram and the acceptance rate per locale daily, and we retrain the student every quarter (and the teacher yearly) on fresh data.
evolution: At 100,000 users, a phrase table is enough: a trie of the 50,000 most common phrases built from public text, giving suggestions in under 1 ms on a CPU. At 10 million users, train an LSTM or small transformer on a few GPUs, serve with batching and a cache. At 100 million, add the teacher, distillation, quantization, KV-cache reuse by session, speculative decoding and the full privacy controls. Beyond that, add more languages with a shared multilingual model, extend from phrases to full sentence drafts triggered by an explicit key (a different product, with a larger model and a longer latency allowance), and move a first small model to the device.
followups: Why not call a large chat model from the editor? || Latency and cost. A large model has 100 times more parameters, so each decode step reads far more weights and takes tens of milliseconds, and the cost per request is 100 times higher. At 600,000 requests per second a large model would need tens of thousands of GPUs. A distilled 120M model reaches most of the accuracy on this narrow task.
How does the KV cache help, and what is its limit? || It stores the attention keys and values of tokens already processed, so each keystroke prefills only the new tokens. It needs session affinity (same replica for one email) and GPU memory: about 2.7 MB per session. If the replica changes, we pay one full prefill, which is acceptable, so we do not replicate the cache.
How does speculative decoding keep the same output? || A small draft model proposes k tokens, the main model verifies them in one pass and accepts the longest prefix that agrees with its own choice, then continues from there. The result is identical to decoding with the main model alone. With acceptance probability 0.7 and k = 4 you get about 2.8 tokens per main-model pass.
How do you stop the model from leaking private text? || Deduplicate and scrub training data, fine-tune with differential privacy where possible, test with canary strings and the exposure metric, block digits, addresses and names that are not in the user's own context, and show only high-probability phrases. Keep logs free of text and limit human access.
What do you do when the user is typing in the middle of a word? || Use token healing: remove the last token from the prompt and force the first generated token to start with the removed characters. Without it, the model sees an unusual token split and suggests poorly. Mid-word is the most common position, so it matters.
How would you test a new model before launch? || Offline: perplexity, ExactMatch@N, the safety and blocklist suite, canary exposure test, latency on the real hardware. Then shadow traffic (run but do not show), then a 1 percent canary with automatic rollback, then a ramp with an A/B holdout of at least 2 weeks.

category: AI & ML
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: diffusion, denoising, contrastive, vae, attention, multimodal, quant, llmserving, evals, clfmetrics
summary: A user types "a red fox reading a newspaper in a rainy Paris cafe, watercolor" and gets four images back in a few seconds. We design this product end to end: the model, the training data and training run, the serving fleet that turns a prompt into pixels, the safety layer and the way we measure quality. The model is a latent diffusion model. A variational autoencoder (VAE) compresses an image into a small grid of numbers. A transformer learns to remove noise from that grid step by step, guided by the text. Text and image are two modalities that the model must align, and it learns this from hundreds of millions of image-caption pairs. The hard parts are the cost (each image needs 20 to 30 passes through a multi-billion-parameter network), the quality of the captions in the training data, the lack of a single metric for "a good picture", and safety, because the output is open-ended and some of it is harmful.
requirements: Functional requirements. Given a text prompt (up to about 300 tokens), an optional negative prompt, a size (up to 1024 by 1024 pixels), a number of images (1 to 4), a seed and a style preset, return images that match the prompt: the right objects, counts, colours, positions and, when asked, readable text. The same prompt and seed must give the same image for the same model version. Show a rough preview while the image is being made. Prompts and outputs are checked for harmful content. Every image carries provenance information (a signed metadata record and an invisible watermark).
Non-functional requirements. Latency: p50 of 6 seconds and p95 of 15 seconds for 4 images at 1024 by 1024, including queueing. Throughput: 10 million images per day. Availability: 99.9 percent for the generation API. Cost: we want a serving cost under half a cent per image. Quality: our images should win at least 50 percent of side-by-side human comparisons against the previous model, with no loss in text alignment. Safety: block clearly harmful prompts and images, with a false block rate on normal prompts under 1 percent.
Metrics. Offline: FID (a distance between the distribution of generated and real images, computed on features of a pretrained network; lower is better), CLIP score (the cosine similarity between the prompt and the image embedding), and compositional tests that count objects, check colours and positions (GenEval-style). Online and human: side-by-side preference win rate, the share of generations that users keep or download, and the rate of re-tries. Safety: precision and recall of the classifiers on labelled red-team sets.
Assumptions I choose. A latent diffusion transformer with 3 billion parameters. Images up to 1024 by 1024. The training set has 1 billion candidate image-caption pairs from licensed and public sources, of which about 500 million pass the filters. Serving uses H100-class GPUs.
estimates: Latent size. A 1024 by 1024 RGB image has 1024 x 1024 x 3 = 3.1 million values. The VAE downsamples by 8 in each direction and uses 16 channels: 128 x 128 x 16 = 262,000 values, 12 times fewer. The transformer cuts the latent into 2 by 2 patches, giving 64 x 64 = 4,096 image tokens (each of 64 input numbers). A 512 pixel image has 1,024 tokens and a 256 pixel image has 256 tokens. Attention cost grows with the square of this number, so resolution is expensive.
Training compute. A transformer needs about 2 x N FLOPs per token for the forward pass, and 3 times that for forward plus backward: 6 N per token. N = 3e9. Progressive training in three stages: (1) 256 px, 256 tokens, 1.5 billion samples: 6 x 3e9 x 256 x 1.5e9 = 6.9e21 FLOPs. (2) 512 px, 1,024 tokens, 500 million samples: 6 x 3e9 x 1,024 x 5e8 = 9.2e21. (3) 1024 px, 4,096 tokens, 200 million samples: 6 x 3e9 x 4,096 x 2e8 = 1.5e22. Total 3.1e22 FLOPs. Add 50 percent for the attention part and text encoders: about 4.7e22. At 400 TFLOPs per GPU (40 percent of peak): 4.7e22 / 4e14 = 1.2e8 GPU-seconds = 32,000 GPU-hours. On 512 GPUs this is 2.6 days. Test runs, ablations and restarts usually cost 3 times the final run, so we budget about 100,000 GPU-hours, roughly 200,000 dollars at 2 dollars per GPU-hour.
Training data size. 500 million images at about 300 KB each is 150 TB of JPEG. We precompute the VAE latents once: 128 x 128 x 16 x 2 bytes (bf16) = 524 KB per 1024 px image, so 260 TB for the high-resolution stage. A T5-XXL caption embedding is 77 x 4,096 x 2 bytes = 630 KB, more than the image latent, so we compute it on the fly on separate GPUs. The loader must feed about 8,000 samples per second (4 batches of 2,048), which is 2.4 GB/s of JPEG or 4 GB/s of latents. This data engineering task is as large as the model. Scoring 1 billion images for filtering costs about 2e19 FLOPs, small, but JPEG decoding at 20 ms per image is 5,500 CPU core-hours.
Serving compute. One denoising step is one forward pass over 4,096 image tokens plus about 300 text tokens: 2 x 3e9 x 4,400 = 2.6e13 FLOPs. Classifier-free guidance runs the model twice (with and without the prompt), so 5.3e13 per step. With 28 steps: 1.5e15 FLOPs. The VAE decoder adds about 2 to 5e12, small. At 400 TFLOPs, one image takes 1.5e15 / 4e14 = 3.7 s of GPU time. With FP8 weights and activations (quantization), the speed rises by about 1.6 times on H100s: 2.3 s. We also use fewer steps, 20 instead of 28, with a good solver: 1.6 s.
Fleet size. 10 million images per day / 86,400 = 116 per second on average. Peak is 3 times: 350 per second. At 1.6 GPU-seconds per image the peak needs 350 x 1.6 = 560 GPUs at full load. We plan for 65 percent utilisation at peak, so 560 / 0.65 = about 860 GPUs. Cost: 860 x 24 h x 2 dollars = 41,000 dollars per day, which is 0.41 cents per image. This meets the target, and the idle capacity at night can be given to training or batch work.
Memory per GPU. DiT weights 3e9 x 2 bytes = 6 GB in bf16 (3 GB in FP8). T5-XXL encoder (4.7 billion parameters) 9.4 GB in bf16. CLIP text encoders 0.5 GB. VAE 0.2 GB. Total below 20 GB, so one 80 GB GPU holds everything and has space for a batch of 8 images. Activations for 4,096 tokens at batch 8 take a few GB with FlashAttention.

design: The system has a training side and a serving side. They share one set of model artifacts: the VAE, the text encoders, the diffusion transformer and the safety classifiers.
How the model works. Training images are encoded by the VAE into latents x0. We pick a random time t between 0 and 1, draw Gaussian noise e, and make a noisy latent x_t = (1 - t) x0 + t e. The transformer takes x_t, t and the text embeddings, and learns to predict the direction from the image to the noise, which is the target v = e - x0. This is rectified-flow training, a close relative of the classic denoising objective where the network predicts the noise itself:
\[ \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, \epsilon, t}\, \big\| v_\theta(x_t, t, c) - (\epsilon - x_0) \big\|^2 \]
Here c is the text conditioning. At generation time we start from pure noise at t = 1 and take about 20 to 30 small steps along the predicted direction toward t = 0. Each step removes a little noise (this is the denoising process of a diffusion model), and after the last step the VAE decoder turns the clean latent into pixels.
The text path. The prompt goes through two or three text encoders. A CLIP text encoder, trained with contrastive learning on image-text pairs, gives a pooled vector that carries the overall meaning and the style. A larger language model encoder (T5-XXL) gives a sequence of token vectors that carry details such as counts, spatial words and spelling. Contrastive pretraining is what makes the CLIP vector live in the same space as images, which is why it also serves as the quality filter and as a judge of prompt match (CLIP score).
The transformer. The image tokens and text tokens are processed together in a stack of blocks. Each block applies self-attention over the concatenation of both: every image patch can look at every other patch and at every word, and words can look at the patches. The two modalities have separate weights for their projections (a multimodal transformer, as in MMDiT), which helps because text and image tokens have different statistics. The time step enters through adaptive layer normalisation. Self-attention costs the square of the number of tokens, so we use FlashAttention.
Classifier-free guidance. During training we replace the caption by an empty one in 10 percent of the samples. At inference we run the network with the prompt and without it, and move away from the unconditional prediction: v = v_uncond + w (v_cond - v_uncond), with w around 4 to 7. A higher w gives images that follow the prompt closely but look less natural and less diverse. This doubles the compute, and it is the main reason why step count and guidance tricks matter for cost.
Data. Raw captions on the web are short and noisy ("IMG_2231"). We filter the images (remove duplicates by perceptual hash, low resolution, watermarks, unsafe content, and pairs with a low CLIP score), and then re-caption most images with a vision-language model, which writes dense, accurate descriptions of objects, colours, counts and layout. Training on a mix, 80 percent synthetic captions and 20 percent original ones, improves prompt following a lot and keeps the model robust to short user prompts.
Serving path. The API gateway authenticates the user, applies rate limits and sends the prompt to the safety service. If it is allowed, an LLM-based prompt expander may rewrite a short prompt into a richer one (users often write 5 words, and the model was trained on 60-word captions). The job goes to a queue. A scheduler assigns it to a GPU worker with the right model version. The worker encodes the text, runs the sampler, decodes with the VAE, and sends each image to the output classifier. Images that pass are encoded as WebP, watermarked, written to the object store and returned.

deepdive: The VAE and why latents. Running diffusion in pixel space at 1024 by 1024 would need 3.1 million values per image and a huge network. The VAE learns a compact code. The encoder maps the image to a mean and a variance in a latent space of 128 x 128 x 16, the decoder rebuilds the image, and the loss combines a reconstruction term, a perceptual term (distance in the features of another network), an adversarial term (a discriminator that makes details sharp) and a small KL term toward a standard normal distribution. The KL weight is kept small: if it is too large the latents are smooth but the images are blurry, and if too small the latent space is irregular and hard for diffusion. The number of channels matters: 4 channels (as in early open models) lose fine details such as small text and faces; 16 channels keep them, at the cost of a bigger latent. The VAE is trained first and then frozen.
Sampling and the number of steps. The sampler solves an ordinary differential equation from noise to image. A simple Euler solver needs 25 to 50 steps; better solvers get similar quality in 15 to 25. For rectified flow the schedule shifts time steps toward the noisy end at higher resolutions, because a 1024 px image needs more noise to destroy its information than a 256 px one. To go below 10 steps we distil the model: a student learns to jump several steps at once (consistency or adversarial distillation), reaching 4 to 8 steps at a small quality loss. Going from 28 steps to 6 is 4.7 times cheaper. We keep the full model for a high-quality tier.
Quantization. The transformer is the main cost, so we quantize it to FP8 (or INT8 with SmoothQuant-style scaling). Diffusion models are sensitive: the activations change a lot across time steps, and small errors add up over 28 steps, so we calibrate the scales per time step and keep the first and last layers, the normalisation layers and the time embedding in bf16. We check that FID and the CLIP score change by less than the noise level, and we compare 1,000 pairs by eye. The gain is 1.5 to 2 times faster and half the memory.
Serving like an LLM, but different. Diffusion serving shares ideas with LLM serving: a queue, a scheduler, batching and GPU memory management. The differences matter. There is no KV cache and no growing sequence: each request runs a fixed number of identical steps, each one compute-bound (a 4,400-token forward pass keeps the GPU busy even at batch 1). So batching gives much less benefit than for LLM decoding, which is memory-bound. We batch mainly to fill gaps and to group requests of the same resolution. Step-level (iteration-level) scheduling still helps: a request that arrives while others are at step 12 of 28 can join the batch at step 0, with no need to wait for them to finish. The latency of one image is the number of steps times the time per step, and cannot be hidden by adding GPUs, so the p50 depends on step count, resolution and quantization. We separate pools by size (1024 px, 512 px) so that small jobs are not stuck behind large ones, and we keep a small pool of idle GPUs for bursts. Text encoders can run on cheaper GPUs in a separate pool, because T5 is 9 GB and used once per prompt.
Evaluation in depth. No single number measures image quality. FID needs 10,000 to 30,000 images to be stable and measures realism and diversity against a reference set, not prompt following. CLIP score measures prompt match, but CLIP is weak at counts and spatial relations. Compositional benchmarks ask for "two red cubes on a blue sphere" and check with detectors. Human evaluation is the ground truth: raters see two images for the same prompt and choose, over prompts that cover categories (people, animals, text, scenes, styles). With 2,000 prompts and a true win rate of 55 percent, the standard error is sqrt(0.55 x 0.45 / 2000) = 1.1 points, so an edge of 3 points is clear. We report by category and watch text rendering, hands and faces. A preference model trained on human votes ranks candidates in daily checks and is validated against humans each month.
Safety as a classification problem. The prompt filter and the image classifiers are binary or multi-label classifiers, and their quality is measured with precision, recall and the precision-recall curve, per category (sexual content, violence, hate, self-harm, minors, real people, copyright marks). The costs are asymmetric: a missed harmful image is much worse than a blocked harmless one, so we pick a low threshold for the worst categories (recall above 99 percent) and accept a false block rate of 1 to 2 percent there. For rare categories the test set must contain enough positives: to see a recall of 99 percent with a margin of 0.5 points we need about 1,500 positive examples. Defence in depth: the prompt filter, the training-data filter (the model cannot draw what it has never seen), the image classifier on the output, and a blocklist of names. We also run a red team that tries to bypass the filters with misspellings, other languages and indirect descriptions, and we put each success into the test set.
tradeoffs: It depends: pixel space versus latent space. Choose latent diffusion when you need high resolution at a sensible cost: it is 12 to 50 times fewer values, and it is the standard choice. Choose pixel-space diffusion for small images (up to 64 px) or in a cascade where the last stage upsamples, if the VAE's loss of detail is not acceptable. Latent models inherit the VAE's limits, such as small text and fine textures.
It depends: U-Net versus transformer backbone. Choose a U-Net when you have limited compute and want a proven design with strong inductive bias for images; it works well up to about 2 to 3 billion parameters. Choose a diffusion transformer when you want to scale to many billions of parameters, joint attention with text, and the same tooling as LLMs (tensor parallelism, FlashAttention, quantization). You give up some efficiency at small scale for better scaling.
It depends: few steps versus many steps. Choose the full model with 25 to 30 steps for the highest quality and a "pro" tier. Choose a distilled model with 4 to 8 steps for the default tier, for interactive editing and for cost: the cost per image is 4 to 6 times lower, but diversity drops and some fine detail is lost. Many products ship both and let a router choose by tier and load.
It depends: prompt rewriting. Choose rewriting by an LLM when users write short prompts and the model was trained on long captions: it raises quality noticeably. Choose no rewriting when the user needs full control, as in professional tools, or when determinism matters. Rewriting adds 300 to 800 ms and can change the user's intent, so we show the rewritten prompt and allow turning it off.
failures: A GPU worker crashes in the middle of a job. The scheduler sees the missing heartbeat in under 5 seconds and re-queues the job at the front. Because the seed is fixed, the retry gives the same image. The client sees a short pause in progress.
A queue builds up at peak. The scheduler first drops quality settings for free users (fewer steps with the distilled model) and then rate limits them, and keeps paid users' latency stable. The autoscaler adds GPUs, but this takes minutes, so we keep a warm pool of 10 percent. A job older than 60 seconds returns "busy" instead of a late image.
The safety service is down. We fail closed for generation: no safety check, no image. A cached allow-list of the last 24 hours of approved prompts' hashes lets repeat prompts through, and a lighter local classifier handles the rest at lower recall for a short time.
A bad model release. Images become blurry, or text rendering gets worse. Pre-release gates (FID, CLIP score, compositional suite, human win rate against the old model, safety suite) catch most issues. We ship to 1 percent of traffic, watch the keep rate, the retry rate and the safety block rate, and roll back by changing the model version pointer. Because quantized and distilled variants are separate artifacts, each passes the same gates.
Out-of-memory or numeric failures. Some seeds with FP8 give NaN values or black images. The worker checks the latent for NaN and extreme values, and retries once in bf16 with a different noise. A rate above 0.1 percent triggers an alert and a calibration check.
Abuse. Users try to generate harmful or deceptive content, or to extract the training data. We rate limit by account and by prompt similarity, ban repeat violators, keep the watermark and provenance data, and monitor for near-copies of training images using embedding search, as diffusion models can reproduce images that were duplicated many times in the training set.
evolution: A first version can be built on an open model (for example a public latent diffusion checkpoint) with a single GPU server, a queue and a basic safety filter. It serves a few thousand images a day. At 1 million images a day, add the scheduler, separate pools, quantization and a prompt expander, and fine-tune the model on your own curated data. At 10 million, train your own VAE and transformer with synthetic captions, add step distillation and a router between quality tiers. At 100 million, add region-level fleets, spot capacity for batch jobs and caches, and extend to editing, inpainting and video, which reuse the same parts with longer token sequences and much higher cost.
followups: Why diffusion in latent space and not in pixels? || The VAE reduces the 1024 x 1024 x 3 image to 128 x 128 x 16, a 12 times smaller tensor (and 48 times smaller with 4 channels), and the transformer sees 4,096 tokens and not millions. The cost of each step is proportional to this size, and attention is quadratic, so the saving is large. The price is the VAE's reconstruction limit.
What does classifier-free guidance do and what does it cost? || It trains the model with and without the caption, and at sampling time extrapolates from the unconditional prediction toward the conditional one with a scale w. It improves prompt following and image sharpness but doubles the compute per step and, at high w, reduces diversity and makes colours oversaturated.
How would you cut the cost per image by a factor of 5? || Combine distillation to 6 to 8 steps (about 4 times), FP8 quantization (1.5 to 2 times), guidance distillation so that one pass replaces two, and good batching and bin-packing by resolution. Verify with FID, CLIP score, human win rate and the safety suite that quality did not drop.
How do you evaluate a new model if FID is not enough? || Use several signals: FID for realism and diversity, CLIP score and compositional tests for prompt following, human side-by-side preference on a fixed prompt set with category breakdown, and a preference model for daily checks. Report confidence intervals: with 2,000 prompts the win rate has a standard error of about 1.1 points.
How would you design the safety system? || Layers: a prompt classifier, filtered training data, an output image classifier, a name and mark blocklist, rate limits and account enforcement, plus provenance (watermark and signed metadata). Tune thresholds per category on the precision-recall curve with high recall for the worst harms, build test sets with enough positives, and add every red-team success to the test set.

summary: Build a translation service for 100 languages, used by a web page translator, a mobile app and a public API. A user sends text in one language and gets text in another, in under half a second for a sentence. This was the first large product of neural sequence models, and it still shows every hard point of natural language processing. The input is open-ended, languages differ in script, word order and how much data exists (English has billions of sentence pairs, Wolof has thousands), and the output must be faithful. A fluent but wrong translation is worse than a clumsy correct one. We design the data, the tokenizer, the encoder-decoder model, distillation and quantization for cheap serving, the decoding algorithm, a second path that uses a large language model for hard cases, and the evaluation with automatic metrics and human review.
requirements: Functional requirements. Translate plain text and HTML between any of 100 languages (about 10,000 directions), with automatic detection of the source language. Keep tags, numbers, URLs, names and line breaks intact. Support glossaries (a customer says "Blue Book" must always become "Livre Bleu"). Translate documents of many pages as an asynchronous job. Offer a "formal or informal" setting where the target language has such a choice. Return a quality estimate with each result so that the client can flag doubtful output.
Non-functional requirements. Latency: p50 of 80 ms and p95 of 250 ms server time for a sentence of 30 words, so that live "as you type" translation feels instant. Throughput: 200 million sentence-level requests per day. Quality: on a held-out test set, the system should be within a small margin of the best published systems for every language, and must not regress for any language when we ship a new model. Fairness across languages: the quality gap between high-resource and low-resource languages should shrink with each release. Privacy: user text is not stored beyond the request, and not used for training unless the customer opts in. Cost: under 0.01 cent per sentence for the common path.
Assumptions I choose. 200 million translated segments per day, each averaging 30 words, which is about 45 subword tokens in and 50 tokens out. A teacher model of 3 billion parameters and a serving model of about 270 million. GPUs of the H100 class.
estimates: Request rate. 200 million / 86,400 = 2,300 per second on average. Peak is 3 times: about 6,900 per second.
Cache. Many segments repeat (menus, boilerplate on web pages, common phrases). With a 35 percent exact-match hit rate, the model sees 65 percent: 4,500 per second at peak. The cache key is the hash of (source language, target language, model version, normalised text); the value is the translation, about 250 bytes with key. Unique entries per day: 200 million x 0.65 = 130 million x 250 bytes = 33 GB. Keeping 7 days is 230 GB, which fits in a small cluster of in-memory or SSD key-value stores.
Compute per sentence for the serving model. The student has a 12-layer encoder (about 150 million parameters), a 3-layer decoder (50 million) and a 64,000-piece embedding table (66 million): about 270 million parameters in total. The encoder processes 45 tokens at once: 2 x 1.5e8 x 45 = 13.5 GFLOPs. The decoder (with the output layer, about 116 million parameters) produces 50 tokens one by one, each costing 2 x 1.16e8 = 0.23 GFLOPs, and beam search with 4 beams multiplies this by 4: 50 x 0.23 x 4 = 46 GFLOPs. Total about 60 GFLOPs per sentence.
GPU count. 4,500 per second x 60 GFLOPs = 2.7e14 FLOPs per second at peak. Decoding is memory-bound and runs far below peak speed. Say an effective 60 TFLOPs per GPU, 15 percent of an H100's bf16 peak. Then 2.7e14 / 6e13 = 4.5 GPUs. We run 12 to cover zone loss, tail latency and growth. The common path is cheap.
The large-model path. An 8 billion parameter LLM costs 2 x 8e9 = 16 GFLOPs per token. For a prompt of 120 tokens (instructions plus source) and 50 output tokens, the cost is 16e9 x 170 = 2.7e12 FLOPs per sentence, about 45 times the cost of the NMT model. If 5 percent of the traffic uses it, that is 225 per second x 2.7e12 = 6e14 FLOPs per second, more than twice the whole NMT fleet. We reserve the LLM for cases where it pays off (see the router).
Memory. The serving model in int8 is 270 MB. The decoder keys and values (3 layers x 2 x 1,024 x 1 byte = 6 KB per token) for 50 tokens x 4 beams take 1.2 MB, and the cross-attention keys and values of the encoder output take 0.3 MB. About 1.5 MB per request, so a batch of 64 is 100 MB. GPU memory is mostly free, so we run several replicas per GPU.
Training data and compute. We aim for 10 billion sentence pairs in total across languages, with the heavy skew: 70 percent involve English. At 30 tokens per side, that is 6e11 tokens. Training the 3 billion parameter teacher for about 1.5 passes over this (1e12 tokens seen) costs 6 x 3e9 x 1e12 = 1.8e22 FLOPs. At 400 TFLOPs per GPU, this is 4.5e7 GPU-seconds = 12,500 GPU-hours, about 2 days on 256 GPUs. Distillation: the teacher translates 3 billion monolingual sentences with beam search: each costs about 800 GFLOPs (beam of 5), 2.4e21 FLOPs in total, but at 15 percent efficiency this equals about 11,000 GPU-hours. The student then trains on 150 billion tokens: 6 x 2.7e8 x 1.5e11 = 2.4e20 FLOPs, small.
Tokenizer. With a shared vocabulary of 256,000 pieces and a model width of 1,024, the embedding matrix has 262 million parameters. It is shared between the encoder, the decoder and the output layer in the teacher, which saves memory. For the 270M student we use a smaller vocabulary of 64,000 (66 million parameters), which makes the final softmax 4 times cheaper.

datamodel: The training manifest has one row per sentence pair; it is the table behind data filtering and the sampling temperature:
```sql
CREATE TABLE bitext (
  pair_id      BIGINT,
  src_lang     STRING,
  tgt_lang     STRING,
  src_text     STRING,
  tgt_text     STRING,
  origin       STRING,    -- human, mined, back_translated, distilled
  domain       STRING,    -- news, web, subtitles, legal, tech
  lid_src      FLOAT,     -- language-id confidence of the source
  lid_tgt      FLOAT,
  len_ratio    FLOAT,     -- tgt tokens / src tokens
  sim_score    FLOAT,     -- cross-lingual embedding similarity
  split        STRING     -- train, dev, test
) PARTITIONED BY (src_lang, tgt_lang);
```
Serving data is small. The translation cache is a key-value store keyed by the hash described above. Glossaries are stored by customer id and language pair, loaded into memory by the serving replica and compiled into a trie for constrained decoding. A translation memory (optional, per customer) maps exact source segments to approved human translations, and takes priority over the model. Models are versioned artifacts in an object store: tokenizer, weights (fp16 and int8), language list and an evaluation report, and one manifest says which version is live in each region.
Quality logs hold, per request: model version, language pair, token counts, latency, quality-estimate score and cache hit, but not the text.
design: There are three layers: data and training, model compression, and serving.
Data. Human-made parallel text exists mainly for English and a few dozen languages (government and parliament texts, subtitles, books, websites that are translated by humans). To get more, we mine the web: we embed sentences from different languages into one shared vector space with a multilingual encoder trained by contrastive learning, and pair sentences whose vectors are close. For each language we also use monolingual text with back-translation: a reverse model translates real target-language sentences into the source language, and the synthetic pair trains the forward model. We clean everything: language identification on both sides, a length ratio between 0.5 and 2, removal of duplicates and of pairs whose similarity is low, and removal of toxic or misaligned lines. Low-resource languages have too little data to learn alone. We sample languages with a temperature: the share of language i is p_i = n_i^(1/T) / sum_j n_j^(1/T), where n_i is its data size. T = 1 follows the data and ignores small languages. T = 5 flattens the distribution, so Wolof is seen far more often than its size suggests. We choose T near 5 and check that high-resource languages lose little.
Tokenization. We train a SentencePiece byte-pair encoding (BPE) vocabulary on a sample that is balanced across languages, with byte fallback so that any character can be encoded. A word in English averages about 1.3 tokens, but in languages with less training text and complex scripts it may be 3 to 5 tokens. More tokens mean more compute, longer sequences, and worse quality. This is a fairness cost, so we measure tokens per word per language and rebalance the vocabulary when a language is far above the average.
Model. The teacher is a Transformer encoder-decoder with 24 layers in the encoder and 24 in the decoder (3 billion parameters). The encoder reads the source tokens with bidirectional self-attention. The decoder generates the target left to right with masked self-attention and cross-attention to the encoder output, which is how it looks at the right source words for each target word. The source gets a language tag, and the decoder starts with the target-language tag, so one model serves all directions, including pairs never seen together in training (zero-shot). Attention replaced the earlier recurrent (RNN, LSTM) sequence models, which read tokens one at a time, compressed the whole sentence into a few vectors and trained slowly. With attention, all positions are processed in parallel in training and the decoder can look back at any source word.
Distillation and quantization. The teacher is too slow and costly for 200 million requests a day. We train a student with a deep encoder and a shallow decoder (12 encoder layers, 3 decoder layers, 270 million parameters), because the decoder runs once per output token and the encoder once per sentence. The student learns from the teacher's translations (sequence-level distillation): the teacher translates monolingual text with beam search, and the student trains on these outputs as if they were references. The outputs are cleaner and more consistent than human text, which makes the learning easier. We also match the teacher's probability distribution over the next token (word-level distillation). Then we quantize the student to int8 with calibration data, giving about 2 times the speed and half the memory, at a loss under 0.2 COMET points if the sensitive parts (embeddings, output layer, layer norms) stay at higher precision.
Serving path. A request reaches the API, which checks the cache. On a miss, the language detector (a small model) finds the source language if needed. The segmenter splits long text into sentences (a very long input is chunked). Segments are put into a queue by length bucket. A batcher takes up to 64 segments of similar length, or whatever has arrived after 3 ms, and sends them to a GPU replica. The replica runs the encoder, then beam search for the decoder with the glossary constraints, detokenizes, restores tags and numbers, and returns. The result goes to the cache and back to the client.
The router. Some requests are better served by the large model: very long or document-level text where context matters, rare directions where the student is weak, domains such as legal or medical, creative text, and cases where the NMT output has low estimated quality. A quality-estimation model scores each (source, translation) pair without a reference. If the score is below a threshold, the router sends the segment to the LLM path, which runs on a separate fleet with continuous batching and a paged KV cache (the LLM serving stack). The prompt gives the languages, the glossary and the previous two sentences for context. The result is checked by the same quality estimator and by a language-id check on the output.

deepdive: Decoding. The decoder gives a probability distribution over the vocabulary at each step, and we want the sequence with the highest total probability. Greedy decoding takes the top token each step; it is fast but can commit to a bad word early. Beam search keeps the best B partial translations (B = 4 here) at each step and expands each; it finds a better overall sequence at B times the decoder cost. Raw probabilities favour short outputs, because every extra token multiplies by a number below 1, so we divide the log-probability by a length penalty ((5 + length) / 6) ^ alpha, and stop when the best finished hypothesis beats all open ones. We do not sample for translation: unlike in a chat, there is mostly one right answer. Constraints are added in the search: forced terms from the glossary (a hypothesis must contain the term, with a trie guiding the tokens), a ban on repeating a trigram, and a rule that the output must be in the target script. A known failure is the model stopping too early (omission) or looping. We watch the ratio of output length to input length and compare it to the language pair's usual ratio. Speed: decoding is sequential, so latency is the number of output tokens times the time per step. With the shallow decoder, one step takes about 0.7 ms at batch 32 in int8, so 50 tokens take 35 ms, plus 8 ms for the encoder, which fits the 80 ms budget. Batching sentences of similar length avoids wasted padding.
Multilingual training. One model for 100 languages shares what is common (syntax patterns, named entities, world knowledge) and helps low-resource languages, which gain from related high-resource ones (for example Galician from Spanish). But capacity is shared too, and high-resource languages can lose a little (the curse of multilinguality). Fixes: more parameters, language-specific layers or experts for groups of languages, and the temperature sampling above. A known danger is off-target translation, where the model answers in the wrong language, mostly in zero-shot directions. We train with direct data for the top 1,000 directions that are not English pivoted, and check the output language with a language-id model.
Evaluation. BLEU counts how many n-grams of the output appear in a reference translation:
\[ \mathrm{BLEU} = \mathrm{BP} \cdot \exp\Big(\tfrac{1}{4}\sum_{n=1}^{4} \log p_n\Big) \]
where p_n is the n-gram precision and BP is a penalty for outputs shorter than the reference. It is cheap and standard but punishes valid paraphrases and is poor for languages with rich morphology, where chrF (an F-score on character n-grams) works better. Learned metrics such as COMET are trained on human judgements and correlate much better with people, but they can be gamed and are weaker for low-resource languages. The final judge is human: professional translators mark errors in a sample using MQM (a scheme with error types and severity), and the score is a weighted error count per 1,000 words. Test sets: FLORES-200 for broad language coverage, the yearly WMT sets for the main pairs, and our own in-domain sets and stress sets (numbers, names, negation, very short and very noisy text, gender-ambiguous sentences). To see if a gain is real, we use paired bootstrap resampling: resample the test sentences thousands of times and count how often the new system wins. A 1,000-sentence test set typically gives a confidence interval of about plus or minus one BLEU point, so smaller gains need bigger sets.
Quality estimation and hallucination. Fluent output makes errors hard to see. A quality estimation model predicts a score from source and translation alone. We also run cheap checks: output-to-input length ratio, language id of the output, copy rate (the output copies the source), repetition, and, for suspicious cases, a round trip (translate back and compare with the source using a cross-lingual embedding similarity). Failing segments go to the router or are marked as low confidence for the client to show.
tradeoffs: It depends: one multilingual model versus many bilingual models. Choose one multilingual model when you support many languages, have low-resource ones that gain from sharing, and want a simple release process: one artifact, one test pipeline. Choose bilingual models (or small groups) for the few pairs that carry most of the traffic, such as English to Spanish, where a dedicated model can reach slightly higher quality and run faster. You pay with more models to train, ship and keep warm. Many products do both: a big multilingual model for the long tail and special models for the top 20 directions.
It depends: NMT encoder-decoder versus a general LLM. Choose the dedicated NMT model when volume is high, latency is tight and cost matters: it is 20 to 50 times cheaper per sentence and has fewer hallucinations on plain text. Choose the LLM when the text needs context beyond a sentence, an instruction (formal tone, a glossary, a style guide), domain knowledge, or when a language pair is high-resource but unusual. The LLM is more fluent and flexible but costlier, slower, and at risk of following instructions that are hidden in the text being translated (prompt injection), so we wrap the source in delimiters and tell the model to treat it as data. The router above gets the benefit of both.
It depends: pivot through English versus direct. Choose English as pivot when data between two languages is scarce, since English has the most data: two hops, but both are good. The costs are doubled latency and compounding errors, and loss of information such as gender or formality, which English does not mark. Choose direct translation, with a multilingual model trained on mined data for the pair, when the pair is large (Hindi to Bengali, French to Arabic) or quality matters more. A multilingual model does both: zero-shot for the tail, direct data for the head.
It depends: Transformer decoder versus recurrent decoder. Choose the Transformer decoder on servers: training is parallel, quality is the best known, and the KV cache is cheap. Choose a recurrent decoder (a GRU or LSTM on top of a Transformer encoder, a hybrid shown to work well in published work) for on-device models and streaming, where the memory per step is constant and no cache grows with length. Newer state-space models follow the same idea. You give up some quality on long outputs.
failures: A GPU replica fails. The load balancer retries the batch on another replica; requests that have waited more than 150 ms are served from a second region or get a cached or lower-quality answer. The model is small, so replicas start in under a minute.
The output is in the wrong language, empty, or a loop of repeated words. The checks after decoding (language id of the output, length ratio, repetition) catch these. The segment is retried with a different beam setting, then sent to the LLM path, and if it still fails, the original text is returned with a flag. Every failure is logged without text and counted per language pair.
Hallucination on noisy or short input. A short, odd string such as "..." or a list of numbers may produce a fluent unrelated sentence. We add a rule-based pass for non-linguistic input (copy numbers and symbols as they are), the quality estimator, and a training set of such cases with a copy target.
Overload at a sudden peak (a news event, a viral page). The queue is bounded. We shed load in this order: lower the beam from 4 to 2 (about a 40 percent cost saving with a small quality drop), serve more from the cache, stop the LLM path, then return a retry-after for batch jobs. Autoscaling adds replicas, but we also keep a 2 times buffer.
A bad model release. A new student improves the average but breaks one language (for instance Thai, which has no spaces between words and can break with a tokenizer change). The per-language gates in the release pipeline catch it. Rollout goes by language group and region, 1 percent first, and comparison against the old model on a daily sample using QE scores and complaint rates. Rollback is a version pointer change.
evolution: A first version can be a bilingual Transformer for the top 5 languages using public data, served on CPUs with a cache. At 10 million requests per day, move to a multilingual model with a shared vocabulary, add language detection, glossaries and a GPU fleet with dynamic batching. At 200 million, add the teacher-student pipeline, int8, the router with quality estimation and an LLM path, and per-language release gates. Next steps: document-level context, speech-to-text-to-speech translation with streaming (a wait-k policy that starts to write after reading k words), on-device models for offline use, and customer-specific adaptation with small LoRA-style adapters or translation memories.
followups: Why did attention beat RNN sequence models for translation? || A recurrent encoder compresses the sentence into a fixed vector, which loses information on long sentences, and it must run step by step, so training is slow. Attention lets the decoder look at every source token directly and lets all positions train in parallel. That raised quality and cut training time by a large factor.
How do you handle a language with only 10,000 sentence pairs? || Share a multilingual model with related languages, upsample the language by temperature sampling, mine more pairs from the web with cross-lingual embeddings, use back-translation of monolingual text, and add human-made test and tuning data. Measure with chrF and human review, and mark the quality honestly in the product.
What does distillation give and what does it cost? || A small student trained on the large teacher's outputs reaches most of the teacher's quality at a fraction of the serving cost; outputs are more consistent and so easier to learn. It costs a pipeline run: translating billions of monolingual sentences with the teacher, and a risk that the student copies the teacher's mistakes and loses diversity.
How would you evaluate a new model for 100 languages? || Run automatic metrics (COMET, chrF) on FLORES-200 and in-domain sets for every direction, with bootstrap confidence intervals and per-language gates. Add stress sets for numbers, names and gender. Then professional human review with MQM on a sample of the languages at highest risk. Ship to 1 percent of traffic and watch QE scores and complaints.
When do you route to an LLM and when not? || Route when context, instruction following or domain knowledge matter, or when the NMT output has a low estimated quality. Do not route plain, short, high-volume text: the LLM costs 20 to 50 times more and may add content or follow hidden instructions. Always check the output language and the quality score of the LLM result too.
