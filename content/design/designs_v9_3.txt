@@ video-recs
title: Design video recommendations like YouTube
category: AI & ML
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: funnel, recsys, embeddings, ann, matfact, featureeng, featurestore, clfmetrics, experimentation, bandits, mlops
summary: A video platform has a catalogue of hundreds of millions of videos and a few hundred million people who open the app every day. The home page and the "up next" panel must show about 25 videos that each person is likely to enjoy, in under 200 ms. We cannot score every video for every person, so the system is a funnel: a cheap stage finds about a thousand candidates, a heavier model orders them, and a final step applies diversity and policy rules. The hard parts are not the model architecture. They are choosing a label that does not reward clickbait, keeping training and serving features identical, serving embeddings at 100,000 requests per second, giving new videos a fair chance, and proving with experiments that a change really made people happier. We design the recommender for a platform with 300 million daily users.
requirements: Functional requirements. For a user and a surface (home feed, watch-next, shorts feed) the system returns a ranked page of videos, with a page token to continue. It must work for new users with no history (cold start) and for new videos with no views. Users can say "not interested" or "do not recommend this channel", and the system must obey within the same session. Creators, safety and legal teams can remove or demote videos, and this must take effect within minutes.
Objective. The business goal is long-term user satisfaction, which shows up as people coming back. A single click is a poor proxy, because thumbnails and titles can pull clicks for videos that disappoint. We therefore optimise a blend of predicted outcomes: expected watch time, probability of a "like" or share, probability of a survey answer of "worth my time", and negative signals such as "not interested" and quick abandon. A weighted sum of these predictions is the final score, and the weights are tuned by online experiments.
Offline metrics. For retrieval: recall at K (does the true next-watched video appear in the top 500 candidates?). For ranking: AUC and log loss per task, NDCG at 10 on logged sessions, and calibration (when the model says 20 percent, about 20 percent of those impressions should convert). Online metrics: watch time per user per day, number of sessions, survey satisfaction, long-term retention at 28 days, and guardrails such as complaint rate and share of recommendations that come from a few channels.
Non-functional requirements. Latency: p99 under 200 ms per feed request. Availability: 99.95 percent; if the personalised path fails, the user still gets popular videos for the region. Freshness: a new upload can be recommended within 10 minutes. Assumptions: 300 million daily users, 12 recommendation requests per user per day, 800 million videos of which 100 million are eligible, 5 million uploads per day.
estimates: Request rate. 300 million users times 12 requests = 3.6 billion requests per day. Divide by 86,400 seconds: about 41,700 requests per second on average. Peak is about 2.5 times the average: 41,700 times 2.5 is roughly 100,000 requests per second. We design for 100,000 QPS.
Funnel sizes. One request starts from 100 million eligible videos and ends with 25 shown. Candidate generation returns about 1,500 candidates from 5 sources (300 each) and, after removing duplicates and videos already watched, about 1,000 remain. A light ranker scores 1,000 and keeps 200. The heavy ranker scores 200 and keeps 50. The final re-ranker picks 25. So the heavy model sees 200 items, not 100 million: a reduction of 500,000 times.
Embedding storage. A video embedding has 128 numbers. As 4-byte floats this is 512 bytes. 800 million videos times 512 bytes is about 410 GB for the full catalogue. We only index eligible videos: 100 million times 512 bytes = 51 GB as floats, or about 13 GB with 1-byte quantisation (128 bytes each). With the graph links of an HNSW index (about 32 links times 4 bytes = 128 bytes per video) the index is roughly 100 million times 256 bytes = 26 GB. We split it into 8 shards of about 3.3 GB and replicate each shard 8 times for throughput.
Retrieval compute. One request runs the user tower once (about 1 ms) and about 5 nearest-neighbour searches. Each search must visit all 8 index shards, and costs about 1 ms of one CPU core. So one request costs 5 times 8 = 40 shard searches. At 100,000 QPS that is 4 million searches per second. A 32-core machine at 50 percent load does about 16,000 per second. 4 million divided by 16,000 = 250 machines.
Ranking compute. The light ranker costs about 2 MFLOP per video times 1,000 videos = 2 GFLOP. The heavy ranker costs about 30 MFLOP times 200 videos = 6 GFLOP. Total 8 GFLOP per request, or 800 TFLOP per second at 100,000 QPS. A GPU gives maybe 15 TFLOP per second on these small, memory-bound models. 800 divided by 15 = about 53 GPUs; with headroom and zone redundancy we plan for about 120.
Feature reads. The heavy ranker needs features for 200 videos per request: 100,000 times 200 = 20 million reads per second. A network key-value store cannot take that. Static video features live in the ranker's local cache (the hottest 5 million videos at 1 KB = 5 GB, hit rate about 85 percent). Only fast counters come from the key-value store, in batches of 100 keys: 15 percent of 20 million = 3 million reads per second.
Event logging. Each request shows 25 impressions: 3.6 billion times 25 = 90 billion impressions per day. A raw impression log with ids, position and the served feature vector is about 1 KB, so 90 TB per day. We keep all positive events (about 5 percent, 4.5 billion) and 10 percent of the negatives (8.5 billion) for training, with a weight of 10 on the sampled negatives. That is about 13 billion rows, 13 TB per day. We keep 14 days: about 180 TB.
Latency budget (p99, 200 ms). Routing and user lookup 15 ms; candidate generation 35 ms (sources in parallel); feature fetch 20 ms; light ranker 20 ms; heavy ranker 45 ms; re-ranking 15 ms; response and slack 50 ms.
api: GET /v1/recs/home?user_id=U&limit=25&page_token=T returns {items: [{video_id, reason, rec_id}], next_page_token}. The rec_id identifies this impression slot and the model version that produced it. It is passed back in every event, so we can join outcomes to what was served.
GET /v1/recs/watch-next?user_id=U&video_id=V&limit=20 returns the same shape for the panel next to a playing video. The current video is an input feature, which makes this a different model task from the home feed.
POST /v1/events takes a batch of {rec_id, video_id, type: impression | click | watch_progress | like | dislike | not_interested, position, watch_ms, client_ts}. The client batches events and sends them every 10 seconds or on app background, to save battery and radio time. The server de-duplicates on (rec_id, type, video_id).
POST /v1/feedback/block with {user_id, channel_id or video_id} writes to a per-user suppression list that the re-ranker reads in the same session.
datamodel: Events are the source of truth. Raw events go to a log (Kafka) and then to columnar files partitioned by day.
```sql
CREATE TABLE impressions (
  rec_id        STRING,      -- one per served slot
  user_id       BIGINT,
  video_id      BIGINT,
  surface       STRING,      -- home | watch_next | shorts
  position      INT,
  model_version STRING,
  served_ts     TIMESTAMP,
  feature_blob  BINARY,      -- features exactly as the ranker saw them
  event_date    DATE
) PARTITIONED BY (event_date);

CREATE TABLE outcomes (
  rec_id STRING, event_type STRING, watch_ms INT, event_ts TIMESTAMP
);
```
The online feature store has two logical tables. user_features is keyed by user_id (history embedding, last 50 watched ids, language, device, counters for 1 hour, 1 day and 28 days). video_features is keyed by video_id (static metadata, content embedding, rolling counters). Both are read by key, so a key-value store partitioned by id fits. The offline store keeps the same features with timestamps, so we can build training rows "as of" the impression time. The model registry keeps, for each version, the weights, feature list, index build and experiment.
design: The request path. The client calls the recommendation API. The service loads the user's features (one key-value read) and builds the context: surface, device, time, current video. It then calls the candidate generators in parallel. Each generator returns video ids with a source tag. The union is de-duplicated and filtered (already watched, blocked channels, not available in the region). The light ranker scores about 1,000 candidates, the heavy ranker scores 200, and the re-ranker builds the final page of 25. The service logs the full served feature vectors under a rec_id and returns the page.
The funnel. This is multi-stage retrieval and ranking. Each stage trades accuracy for cost: a stage that sees N items can afford a time per item of budget divided by N. Each stage must have high recall for the next one, because a video dropped early cannot come back.
Candidate generation. Several sources run side by side. (1) A two-tower retrieval model: one neural network turns the user context into an embedding, another turns each video into an embedding, and the dot product is the score. Video embeddings are computed offline and stored in an approximate nearest neighbour index, so the query is one user-tower pass plus an index search. The two-tower model is the neural version of matrix factorization: classic collaborative filtering learns a vector per user and per video from the watch matrix, and the two-tower model computes the vectors from features so it also works for new videos and users. (2) Item-to-item co-watch lists: for the current video, the videos most often watched in the same session, computed offline by an ALS matrix factorization job. (3) Subscriptions and channels the user follows, newest first. (4) Trending and fresh videos in the region, with a boost for new uploads. (5) A topic source built from the user's recent interests (clusters of video embeddings).
Ranking. The light ranker is a small network on cheap features, trained to imitate the heavy model. The heavy ranker is a multi-task network with a shared bottom and one head per outcome (click, watch fraction, like, not-interested, survey). The position the video had is a training-only feature that models position bias; at serving time it is set to a default. A final re-ranker enforces rules the model cannot learn: at most 2 videos per channel, topic diversity, safety demotions and the "not interested" list. Feature engineering matters more than depth here: the strongest features are the user's recent history as a pooled embedding, the video's rolling quality counters, the cross of user topic and video topic, the time since upload, and the position the video would have (used in training only, to model position bias). The final score is a weighted sum of the head outputs.
Exploration. New videos have no history, so the models know little about them. A multi-armed bandit layer reserves about 2 percent of slots for exploration. For each new video we keep a posterior on its engagement rate (a Beta distribution) and use Thompson sampling: sample a rate from the posterior and rank by the sample. Uncertain videos get shown until the data narrows the posterior. Exploration is targeted to users whose history suggests they like the video's topic, so the cost to satisfaction is low.
Training and deployment. A pipeline joins impressions with outcomes, builds labels and trains the retrieval and ranking models daily. It exports the video tower, embeds all eligible videos, builds the index and publishes the user tower and the index as one versioned bundle. The MLOps layer (registry, canary, rollback, monitoring) controls how a bundle reaches users.

deepdive: Labels and the objective. The label is the model's whole view of what "good" means. Training on clicks teaches the model to prefer exciting thumbnails. The YouTube ranking paper (Covington and others, 2016) used weighted logistic regression: clicked impressions are positive examples weighted by the watch time, and non-clicked impressions have weight 1. The learned odds then approximate expected watch time. We use a multi-task version. Each head has its own label: click, watch fraction, like, not-interested. Watch fraction (seconds watched divided by duration) is better than raw watch time because it does not favour only long videos. We also drop positives where the user left within 10 seconds, and we add a survey head trained on the 0.1 percent of impressions followed by a "was this worth your time" question. The final score mixes heads with weights that experiments tune.
Training-serving skew and the feature store. The most common reason a good offline model fails online is that the features at training time differ from those at serving time. Examples: a rolling counter computed with a different window, a join that sees the future, a null handled differently. The cure has three parts. First, the ranker logs the exact feature vector it used for each impression (feature_blob), so training reads what was served. Second, features are defined once in a feature store and computed by one code path for both batch and online use. Third, when new features are tested offline, they are rebuilt "as of" the impression time (point-in-time correct joins) so the model never sees information from after the event. Streaming jobs keep the online counters fresh within a minute.
Embeddings and index versions. A user tower and the video index must come from the same training run. If the tower is from Tuesday's model and the index from Monday's, the dot products compare vectors in two different spaces and recall collapses silently. We publish the tower and the index as one bundle with a version number, load the new bundle next to the old one, and switch in one atomic step per replica. During rollout a request carries its bundle version so that all shards answer from the same version, or the shard rejects the query. The index is built for fresh videos continuously: new videos go to a small in-memory delta index (searched in parallel) and are merged into the main index at the next full build.
Approximate nearest neighbour search. Exact search over 100 million vectors costs about 26 GFLOP per query, impossible at millions of queries per second. HNSW (a layered graph walk) or IVF-PQ (clusters plus compressed codes) answer in about 1 ms with 95 to 98 percent recall. We accept the loss because the ranker makes the final call. Filters (region, age rating) are applied during the search, not after, or a strict filter leaves too few results.
Cold start. New user: no history, so we use country, language, device, time of day, the first clicks and an onboarding topic choice; the bandit explores topics fast in the first 10 videos. New video: a content embedding built from title, transcript and frames puts it near similar videos with zero views, and the uploader's history gives a prior. The video-age feature is set to zero at serving time, so all videos are scored as if shown now.
Evaluation. Offline: replay logged sessions and compute recall at K for the retriever and NDCG for the ranker, but remember the log only has the videos the old system showed (selection bias). Use inverse propensity weights where the show probability is known, and treat offline gains under 1 percent as noise. Online: randomise by user. For watch time with a standard deviation of about 80 minutes around a mean of 60 minutes per day, to detect a 0.2 percent change (0.12 minutes) with 80 percent power at 5 percent significance we need n = 2 x (1.96 + 0.84)^2 x 80^2 / 0.12^2 = 2 x 7.84 x 6,400 / 0.0144 = about 7 million users per arm. That is 2.3 percent of daily users per arm. Variance reduction with a pre-experiment covariate (CUPED) cuts the variance by 30 to 50 percent. A 1 percent long-term holdout, kept for months, measures effects that need weeks to appear, such as retention.
tradeoffs: Two-tower retrieval versus a heavy joint model. Choose the two-tower model for stage one because it lets us precompute video vectors and use an index: the cost per query does not grow with the catalogue. Choose a joint model (user and video features crossed inside the network) only in the ranking stage, where we score 200 items. You give up feature interactions in retrieval (the dot product is a weak interaction) and gain a 100,000-times cheaper search.
Matrix factorization versus neural towers. Choose ALS matrix factorization when you need a robust baseline in days, have mostly id-based signals and want a model that trains on one machine group in an hour. Choose neural towers when you have rich features (text, audio, context), need to handle new videos, and can pay for GPU training. In practice we keep both: ALS co-watch lists are cheap, stable and explainable, and they add candidates the neural model misses.
Exact versus approximate search. Choose exact (brute force on GPU) when the catalogue is under about 1 million items; 1 million times 128 numbers is 0.25 GFLOP per query, fine. Choose HNSW when you want the best recall per millisecond and can hold the graph in memory. Choose IVF-PQ when memory is the limit, because it stores 16 to 64 bytes per vector instead of 512, at a cost of a few points of recall that a re-ranking step on exact vectors can win back.
Optimise watch time versus satisfaction. Choose watch time as the main reward when you need a stable signal with plenty of data. Choose survey and "worth my time" labels when you see signs of regret, like rising watch time but falling retention. You give up some short-term minutes for long-term trust. A good compromise is to mix several heads and to keep a long holdout that checks the mix.
failures: The feature store is slow or down. The ranker cannot get counters or user history. Per-feature timeouts (10 ms) trigger defaults: the ranker uses the last cached values or the global averages, and the log marks the request as degraded. If the user vector is missing, we use the non-personalised path: popular videos in the region, ranked by a small model on context only. The page is worse but present.
An ANN shard is down or slow. The retrieval stage uses a deadline of 35 ms and takes whatever shards answered. With one of 8 shards missing we lose about 12 percent of candidates, which the ranker rarely notices. Replicas absorb the traffic. A circuit breaker removes a slow replica from rotation.
A bad model is deployed. A new bundle may be wrong in ways offline tests do not show (a feature that is always zero, a tower and index mismatch). The release goes through shadow scoring (the new model scores real traffic, no one sees it), a 1 percent canary and an automatic comparison of score distributions, click-through and "not interested" rate against the old model. A drop beyond a threshold rolls back in minutes. Keeping the old bundle loaded makes rollback a pointer change.
Feedback loops and filter bubbles. The model learns from what it showed, so it can narrow a feed to one topic. We counter with diversity rules, exploration slots, a survey head and a check of the topic entropy of pages per user.
Drift. User taste, catalogue and device mix change. We monitor the population stability of key features, the calibration of each head (predicted versus observed rate per decile), and the weekly retrain gap between old and new models on fresh data. A calibration shift greater than a few percent pages the on-call engineer.
evolution: At 1,000 users and 10,000 videos, rules are enough: most popular in the last day, by topic, plus the channels the user follows. At 1 million users, add an item-to-item co-watch table (ALS or simple counts) and a gradient-boosted ranker over a dozen features, trained nightly with logged impressions. At 100 million users, the funnel appears: two-tower retrieval with an ANN index, a feature store to remove skew, a multi-task deep ranker and a real experimentation platform. At 1 billion users and hundreds of millions of videos, the work moves to cost and scale: GPU serving with request batching, quantised embeddings, sharded embedding tables for user ids, regional indexes, sequence models over watch history, long-term reward estimation and a team that owns the experiment platform, label quality and the creator ecosystem as much as the model.
followups: Why not rank all 100 million videos with the heavy model? || The cost is 100 million times 30 MFLOP = 3 PFLOP per request, which is impossible at 100,000 QPS. The funnel keeps the cost per request at about 8 GFLOP by making each stage cheaper per item and wider. The price is that stage one must have high recall, which we check with recall at K.
Why optimise expected watch time instead of clicks, and what can go wrong with watch time? || Clicks reward misleading thumbnails. Watch time rewards content that holds attention. But it also favours long videos and can reward compulsive content, so we use watch fraction, add survey and like heads, penalise quick abandons and watch long-term retention in a holdout.
How do you stop training-serving skew? || Log the exact feature vector at serving time and train on it, define features once in a feature store for both batch and online, and build any new historical feature with point-in-time joins. Also monitor the live feature distribution against the training distribution.
How do you know a ranker change is really better? || Run a randomised experiment by user with a pre-registered main metric and guardrails. For a 0.2 percent change in watch time we need about 7 million users per arm, fewer with variance reduction. Check for novelty effects over at least 1 to 2 weeks and keep a long-term holdout.
What happens if the user tower and the video index come from different models? || The vectors live in different spaces, so dot products are meaningless and recall drops without any error. Ship the tower and index as one versioned bundle, load both together, switch atomically, and have shards reject queries from a different version.

@@ bot-detection
title: Design bot and fake-account detection
category: AI & ML
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: anomaly, featureeng, gnn, idgraph, clfmetrics, streamproc, boosting, featurestore, sampling, ratelimit
summary: A large social platform is attacked every day by people who create fake accounts and run bots. They send spam, scrape data, take over accounts with stolen passwords, and sell fake likes. The defender must stop them without annoying real users, and the attackers see every decision and adapt. We design a layered system. Cheap rate limits and rules stop the crude traffic at the edge. A real-time model scores each signup, login and sensitive action in under 100 ms. A stream job keeps behaviour counters fresh. A daily graph job finds rings of accounts that share devices, networks and phones. Human reviewers label a small, carefully sampled set of cases, and these labels train the next model. What makes the problem hard is not the algorithm. It is the rare positive class, labels that arrive late and are biased by earlier decisions, an adversary who changes behaviour, and the high price of blocking a real person.
requirements: Functional requirements. The system produces a risk score and a decision for events: signup, login, password reset, post, message, follow, like, and API calls. The decision is one of: allow, allow with friction (stricter rate limits, delayed visibility), challenge (CAPTCHA, phone code, ID check) or block and suspend. Every decision has machine-readable reasons, so analysts can debug and support can explain. Suspended users can appeal, and the appeal outcome becomes a label. Analysts can write rules that go live in minutes, and can search for clusters of related accounts.
Objectives and metrics. The target is to keep the share of bot activity that real users see as low as possible while the share of real users wrongly challenged or blocked stays tiny. Because bots are a small part of the population, accuracy is a useless metric: a model that says "real" for everyone is 96 percent accurate. We use precision, recall, the precision-recall curve (PR-AUC), recall at a fixed false positive rate, and, most important, the estimated prevalence of bots among active accounts, measured by random audits. Operational metrics: time to detect a new attack wave, challenge solve rate for real users, appeal overturn rate (a proxy for false positives) and reviewer cost per catch.
Non-functional requirements. Latency: the signup and login score returns in under 100 ms at p99 because the user is waiting. Scoring of ongoing activity can take minutes. Availability: if the scorer fails, signups continue with stricter static limits; existing users are not blocked. Explainability: each block shows the top reasons. Privacy: features use behaviour and device signals, not message content, unless a separate policy allows it; data retention follows law.
Assumptions. 150 million daily active users, 500 million total accounts, 2 million signups per day of which about 25 percent are attacker-made. About 100 sensitive actions per active user per day.
estimates: Event volume. 150 million users times 100 actions = 15 billion events per day. Divide by 86,400 seconds: about 173,000 events per second on average. Peak is about 2.5 times the average: roughly 430,000 events per second. At 200 bytes per event this is 86 MB per second, or 3 TB per day.
Scoring volume. We do not score every action. We score at signup (2 million per day), at login (150 million), at the first 20 actions of a new account, and then whenever a stream window shows a change (a spike in actions, a new device, a new country). Assume about 6 scores per active user per day: 150 million times 6 = 900 million scores per day, or 10,400 per second on average and about 30,000 at peak. A boosted tree with 400 trees of depth 6 runs in about 50 microseconds on one core, so scoring itself needs only a few cores. The cost is in fetching the features: 2 to 3 ms from the online store.
Stream state. The stream job keeps per-account windows (1 minute, 1 hour, 1 day) of counts and distinct counts. At about 1 KB per active account, 150 million times 1 KB = 150 GB of state, plus per-device and per-IP state of about 100 GB. Spread over 64 workers this is about 4 GB each, which fits in local state stores on SSD. Ingest of 86 MB per second is easy for a Kafka topic with 64 partitions.
Class imbalance and the arithmetic of precision. Of 2 million daily signups, 500,000 are fake and 1.5 million are real. Suppose the model catches 90 percent of the fake accounts (recall 0.90) at a false positive rate of 0.1 percent. True positives: 450,000. False positives: 0.001 times 1.5 million = 1,500. Precision = 450,000 / 451,500 = 99.7 percent. Now take a quiet day when only 5 percent of signups are fake (100,000) and the same rates apply: true positives 90,000, false positives 1,900, precision 97.9 percent. The same model looks different when the base rate changes, so we track precision with fresh audits and never trust old numbers.
Prevalence audit. To estimate the share of bots among 150 million active accounts, sample 20,000 accounts at random and have experts label them. If 4 percent are bots, the standard error is the square root of 0.04 times 0.96 divided by 20,000, which is 0.00139, and the 95 percent interval is plus or minus 0.27 percent. So we can say prevalence is between 3.7 and 4.3 percent with 20,000 labels.
Review capacity. 200 reviewers times 150 cases per day = 30,000 labels per day. This is tiny compared with 150 million accounts, so the sampling plan decides the quality of our training data.
Graph size. 500 million accounts, 300 million devices, 200 million network blocks, 100 million phones and emails. About 3 billion edges ("account used device", "account logged in from IP /24", "account verified phone"). Stored as adjacency arrays (both directions, 4-byte ids), this is 6 billion times 4 bytes = 24 GB plus offsets: it fits in the memory of one large machine, or in a few sharded ones.
api: Internal scoring call: POST /v1/risk/score with {event_type, account_id, device_id, ip, ts, context{user_agent, tls_fingerprint, attestation_token, form_fill_ms}} returns {score, decision: allow | friction | challenge | block, reasons: [{feature, contribution}], model_version, ttl_s}. The caller caches the decision for ttl_s.
POST /v1/risk/events is the asynchronous path. Services publish activity events to a Kafka topic; the same shape is used for the stream job.
POST /v1/labels takes {account_id, label: bot | human | uncertain, source: reviewer | appeal | honeypot | user_report, ts, evidence_ref}. Labels are append-only.
GET /v1/accounts/{id}/risk returns the score history, reasons and cluster id, for analysts.
PUT /v1/rules/{rule_id} with {condition, action, ttl, mode: shadow | enforce} deploys a rule. New rules always start in shadow mode, which logs what the rule would have done.
datamodel: The decision log and the label store are the base of everything.
```sql
CREATE TABLE decisions (
  decision_id   BIGINT,
  account_id    BIGINT,
  event_type    STRING,
  ts            TIMESTAMP,
  score         DOUBLE,
  action        STRING,
  model_version STRING,
  feature_blob  BINARY,       -- features exactly as scored
  sample_prob   DOUBLE,       -- chance this case was picked for review
  event_date    DATE
) PARTITIONED BY (event_date);

CREATE TABLE labels (
  account_id BIGINT, label STRING, source STRING,
  labeled_ts TIMESTAMP, weight DOUBLE   -- weight = 1 / sample_prob
);
```
Online features are in a key-value store keyed by account_id, device_id and ip_block. Each value is a compact struct: window counts, distinct counts, first-seen time, last attestation result, graph cluster id, graph embedding (64 numbers), cluster size and cluster flagged ratio. The offline copy keeps all versions with timestamps.
Graph storage. Edges are in columnar files: (src_type, src_id, dst_type, dst_id, first_seen, last_seen, weight). The batch job builds adjacency arrays from them each night. Cluster results go to cluster(cluster_id, member_count, created_span_minutes, flagged_ratio).
design: Layer 1: the edge. Before any model, requests pass through rate limits and static rules at the gateway. Limits are applied per IP, per device and per phone number: for example 5 signups per device per day, 20 signup attempts per /24 network per hour, 10 failed logins per account per hour. A token bucket gives each key a burst and a steady rate. Rate limiting is also an action in our ladder: a suspicious account is moved to a lower limit tier rather than blocked. This layer is cheap, has no false-positive learning cost, and removes the crude 80 percent of traffic.
Layer 2: the real-time scorer. For signup, login and risky actions, the risk service gathers features, runs the model and returns a decision in under 100 ms. Feature engineering is where most of the gain is: raw counts are turned into ratios, rates, entropies and time-since-first-seen. Features come from three places: the request itself (IP type, user agent, TLS fingerprint, device attestation, form-fill time), the online feature store (counters, graph cluster, embedding) and quick lookups (email domain reputation, phone type). The model is a gradient-boosted tree ensemble. Trees handle mixed numeric and categorical data and missing values, need little tuning, train fast on tens of millions of rows and run in microseconds. On tabular data like this they usually beat deep networks. We use monotonic constraints (more user reports can only raise the risk) so that small changes cannot flip the answer in odd directions, and we compute per-feature contributions (SHAP values) to produce reasons.
Layer 3: stream features. Every sensitive event goes to Kafka. A stream processing job (Flink) groups by account, device and IP block, and keeps window features: actions per minute, distinct targets (followed 300 different accounts in an hour), the entropy of the gaps between events (humans are irregular, scripts are regular), share of actions at night in the account's time zone, ratio of outgoing messages to replies. Results are written to the online store with a timestamp and also to the offline store by the same code, so training and serving read identical definitions. Checkpointed state gives exactly-once counts after a restart.
Layer 4: graph. A nightly batch job builds the identity graph and finds rings. It removes nodes with too many neighbours (a shared university network or a mobile carrier gateway links thousands of real people), finds connected components and communities, and computes cluster features: size, how tightly signup times are packed, similarity of names and emails, share of already-flagged members. A graph neural network (two layers of neighbour sampling, as in GraphSAGE) learns a 64-number embedding for each account from its own features and those of its 2-hop neighbourhood. The embeddings and the cluster ids go to the feature store and become inputs to the tree model. A real account next to a ring of flagged accounts gets a high risk, even when its own behaviour looks clean.
Layer 5: anomaly detection for the unknown. Supervised models know yesterday's attacks. Unsupervised detectors look for what is new: seasonal time-series baselines on signup counts per ASN, country and email domain per hour (alert at 4 standard deviations), and an isolation forest or autoencoder on session behaviour. Alerts go to analysts, and the accounts they confirm become labels.
Layer 6: decisions and humans. A policy layer maps score and context to actions. Reviewers see the account, the reasons and the cluster. Their labels, appeal results and honeypot hits flow back to the label store.

deepdive: Labels are the hard part. We never see ground truth. We see four weak sources: reviewer decisions (accurate but few), enforcement outcomes (the model's own past decisions, so they are biased), user reports (noisy, and used by abusers against real people) and honeypots (accounts or links that only a bot would touch, precise but narrow). Bot labels also arrive late: an account that looks fine on day 1 may be banned on day 20. So we train on accounts at least 14 days old, add the fast labels (honeypot hits, failed challenges) for younger accounts, and treat "not yet banned" as unknown, not as human.
Sampling for labels. The 30,000 daily review slots are split by design. About 10,000 are drawn uniformly at random from all active accounts. This gives an unbiased estimate of prevalence and of what the model misses, and these cases have weight 1 over the probability of being picked. About 12,000 are drawn from the uncertain band (score between 0.3 and 0.7) because labels there teach the model the most. About 8,000 come from the top scores, to measure precision of blocks. Each case stores its sample probability, so the training job uses inverse-probability weights and the metrics are corrected for the unequal sampling. Without the random slice the model learns only from what it already catches and never finds out about the bots it misses.
Choosing thresholds. Let \( C_{FP} \) be the cost of a wrong action on a real person and \( C_{FN} \) the cost of letting a bot act. We act when the probability of bot is above
\[ t = \frac{C_{FP}}{C_{FP} + C_{FN}} \]
For a hard block, a lost real user is expensive. Suppose \( C_{FP} = 100 \) and \( C_{FN} = 5 \) in the same units; then \( t = 100/105 = 0.95 \). For a CAPTCHA, the cost of friction is small, say 0.5, so \( t = 0.5/5.5 = 0.09 \). This is why the decision is a ladder: a low score gets light friction, a medium one a challenge, and only very high scores a block. The costs are estimates and must be revisited with data, but the structure holds.
Adversaries. Every signal that is cheap to fake loses value over time. Usernames, profile text and user agents are free for the attacker to change. Signals that cost money or time keep value: device attestation (Play Integrity on Android, App Attest on iOS), a verified real phone number, account age, and membership in a graph neighbourhood. Defences: do not tell the attacker which feature triggered a block; delay enforcement and ban in waves, so attackers cannot learn from single trials; plant honeypots; and retrain often. A red team runs attacks against staging models and the findings become features and tests.
Training and release. A daily job builds the training set with point-in-time features: for each labelled account, features are taken as they were at the time of the event, not as they are today. Using today's graph embedding for a bot caught last week would leak the answer. Models are checked on a time-based split (train on days 1 to 60, test on days 61 to 75), never a random split, because attackers change over time. A release runs in shadow mode first: the new model scores live traffic, nothing is enforced, and we compare score distributions, flagged rates per cohort and agreement with the old model. Then it goes to 5 percent of traffic and is watched for block rate, challenge solve rate and appeals.
Monitoring and drift. We watch the score distribution (population stability index per feature and for the score), the daily block rate, the challenge solve rate (a drop means real people are being challenged or solvers have improved), appeal overturn rate, and estimated prevalence from the random audit. A sudden rise in blocks may be an attack or a bug, so alerts show a breakdown by country, ASN, app version and cohort. A guardrail automatically falls back to the previous model if block rate is more than 3 times the 7-day baseline for 15 minutes.
Shared infrastructure and false positives. A campus network, a mobile carrier with carrier-grade NAT or an office can show hundreds of real users from one IP. Features that count accounts per IP would punish them. We use IP features as soft inputs weighted by the type of network, cap the graph degree, and rely more on device attestation and behaviour for these networks.
tradeoffs: Rules versus a model. Choose rules for attacks you understand and must stop now (a known spam text, a known bad ASN): they are exact, explainable and deployable in minutes. Choose a model when the pattern is a combination of dozens of weak signals. You give up explainability with a model, and you gain coverage. Run both: rules catch the known, the model generalises, and rules are retired when the model covers them.
Gradient-boosted trees versus deep learning. Choose trees for the real-time scorer: tabular features, tens of millions of examples, microsecond inference and easy debugging. Choose deep sequence models (over the sequence of user actions) when you have billions of events and the ordering of actions carries signal that counters miss. Their price is GPU serving and harder explanations. A common path is to feed the sequence model's output as one feature into the tree model.
Real-time versus batch graph. Choose a nightly graph job when rings live for days and weekly rotation is slow; it is simple and cheap. Choose an incremental graph (update the cluster when a new account arrives, using a graph database or in-memory index) when attackers burn accounts within hours. You pay with state, complexity and harder replays. A hybrid works: nightly full rebuild plus a small online lookup of "accounts seen on this device in the last 24 hours".
Block versus challenge versus shadow-limit. Choose a block only for very high confidence cases. Choose a challenge when the model is unsure and real users can solve it; remember that paid solving farms cost attackers a few dollars per thousand, so a challenge raises cost but does not stop a funded attacker. Choose a silent limit ("shadow ban", limited reach) when you want to keep the attacker busy and not alert them, but note the ethics and the appeal needs for real users.
Fail open versus fail closed. Choose fail open for logged-in actions of established accounts, because an outage of the scorer must not lock people out. Choose fail closed (a challenge or a hard limit) for signup and password reset, where the attacker would use the outage window to create thousands of accounts.
failures: The feature store is down. The scorer uses only request-level features and static limits, flags the decision as degraded and tightens signup limits. Existing users are not touched.
A bad model release. The shadow and canary stages catch most problems. A monitor on block rate and appeals triggers rollback to the previous model, which stays loaded.
Label leakage and feedback loops. If a feature is computed after the ban (for example "number of reports received") the model looks perfect offline and useless online. Feature definitions are reviewed for time: every feature has an "available at" timestamp. If the model only learns from bots it already catches, it gets worse at new ones; the random audit slice breaks this loop.
A false-positive wave. A viral event brings a surge of real new users from one country, and the anomaly detectors see a spike. Alerts go to humans first, and new automated blocks from anomaly signals are capped to a fraction of traffic per hour until a person confirms the attack.
Attackers poison the labels. They can mass-report real users to get them blocked, or mass-appeal to flood the queue. Reports are weighted by the reporter's trust and are never the only reason for a block. Appeals have per-account limits.
evolution: At 100,000 users, a handful of rules and rate limits per IP and per email domain are enough, with manual review of reports. At 10 million users, add a tree model on signup features, a basic feature table and a labelled review queue. At 100 million users, add the stream job, the feature store, the nightly graph job with community detection, the sampling plan for labels and shadow releases. At 1 billion users, add GNN embeddings, sequence models, a regional split of the stores, a dedicated red team, cross-product signals (the same attacker on messaging and ads) and an investigation tool for clusters. The effort shifts from algorithm to labels, speed of response and the cost of each reviewer minute.
followups: Why not just use accuracy to evaluate the model? || With 4 percent bots, a model that says "human" for everyone is 96 percent accurate and useless. Use precision, recall, PR-AUC and recall at a fixed false positive rate, and estimate prevalence from a random audit with a confidence interval.
How do you get labels for training, and what is wrong with using bans as labels? || Bans come from the old model and rules, so they teach the new model to copy the old one and miss what it misses; they also arrive late. Mix reviewer labels, honeypots and appeals, hold out a random sample for unbiased estimates, and use inverse-probability weights for the unequal sampling.
How do you detect a ring of 5,000 accounts created from a rented farm? || Build an identity graph of accounts, devices, IP blocks and phones, drop high-degree nodes, find communities, and score the cluster by size, signup-time tightness, name and email similarity and flagged share. A GNN embedding passes neighbourhood information to each account, so a clean-looking member of a flagged ring is still scored high.
How do you avoid blocking real users on a shared network? || Treat IP features as soft signals weighted by network type, cap graph degree, rely on device attestation and behaviour, and prefer a challenge over a block when the evidence is mostly network-based. Track appeal overturn rate per ASN to find systematic errors.
How do you ship a new model safely against an adaptive adversary? || Time-based validation, shadow mode on live traffic, a 5 percent canary with guardrails on block rate, challenge solve rate and appeals, and a fast rollback. Keep a red team, retrain often, and do not reveal which feature triggered a block.
How would you set the decision thresholds? || Compare the cost of a wrong action on a real person with the cost of letting a bot act: act when the probability is above C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN). Different actions have different costs, so use a ladder (friction, challenge, block) and revisit the costs with data.

title: Design a nearby-friends feature
category: Web & product
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: spatialidx, realtime, websocket, pubsub, fanout, cachelayer, partitioning, conshash, redis, mobilearch
summary: A social app shows a user which of their friends are close by, for example "Asha is about 2 miles away, seen 1 minute ago", and the list updates while the user watches. Users who opt in send their location from the phone every 30 seconds. The server must tell each of their friends who are also online and nearby. This is not a search problem: the friend list is known, so we do not need to find strangers in a map. It is a real-time fan-out problem. Every location update goes to dozens of friends, and there are hundreds of thousands of updates per second. It is also a privacy and battery problem, because the data is sensitive and the phone must send it all day. We design the system with persistent connections, a publish-subscribe layer sharded by user, a small cache for the latest location, and careful rules on the phone.
requirements: Functional requirements. A user can turn the feature on or off at any time. When on, the app shows friends who also have it on, are within a radius (default 5 miles, user can choose 1 to 10), and were active in the last 10 minutes. Each entry has a name, an approximate distance ("about 2 miles") and a "last seen" time. The list updates without refresh. A user can hide from selected friends and can pause sharing. Location history is not shown to anyone.
Non-functional requirements. Freshness: a friend's move appears within 5 seconds, end to end, at p95. Scale: tens of millions of users online at peak. Availability: if the feature is down the rest of the app works. Cost and battery: the phone should spend little energy and data. Privacy: opt-in, no storage of precise history by default, distances are rounded, and the server never sends a friend's exact coordinates to a client when only a distance is displayed. Consistency is relaxed: a missed update is repaired by the next one 30 seconds later, so we choose speed and availability over exact delivery.
Assumptions. 100 million daily users. At peak, 10 million users have the feature on and the app open. Each has about 400 friends; about 100 of them have the feature enabled, and about 10 percent of friends are online at any moment, so a user has about 40 online friends.
estimates: Update rate. 10 million connected users, each sending a location every 30 seconds: 10,000,000 / 30 = 333,000 updates per second. A phone that has not moved more than 50 metres does not send; if 60 percent of users are stationary, the real rate is about 133,000 per second. We design for the full 333,000.
Fan-out. Each update goes to the online friends of the sender, about 40. 333,000 times 40 = 13.3 million deliveries per second. This is the cost driver. For comparison, a polling design where each client reads the locations of its 100 enabled friends every 30 seconds would make 10,000,000 / 30 = 333,000 requests per second, each reading 100 keys: 33 million key reads per second, about 2.5 times more work, and it would send data even when nothing changed.
Subscription count. Each connected user is subscribed to the channels of its 100 enabled friends: 10 million times 100 = 1 billion subscriptions. If each subscription costs about 100 bytes of memory in the pub/sub server (a channel entry and a pointer to the subscriber), the total is 100 GB. Spread over 200 nodes, 0.5 GB each.
Pub/sub nodes. A single Redis pub/sub node can push roughly 100,000 small messages per second before its one thread saturates (a figure we confirm by load test). 13.3 million divided by 100,000 = 133 nodes. With 30 percent headroom: 133 times 1.3 = about 175; we round to 200 nodes.
Connection servers. One WebSocket server holds about 50,000 idle connections. Memory per connection is about 20 KB (buffers and state): 50,000 times 20 KB = 1 GB. 10 million divided by 50,000 = 200 servers. Each server receives 13.3 million / 200 = 66,000 messages per second from pub/sub and must filter them, which is light work.
Connect storm. With an average session of 30 minutes, 10 million sessions turn over at 10,000,000 / 1,800 = 5,500 connects per second. Each connect subscribes 100 channels and reads the 100 friends' latest locations: 550,000 subscribe operations and 550,000 key reads per second, spread over the cluster.
Location cache. The latest location of each active user is about 60 bytes (id, latitude, longitude, timestamp, sequence) plus about 100 bytes of key and overhead: 160 bytes. 10 million times 160 bytes = 1.6 GB. Writes are 333,000 per second. Four cache shards of 100,000 writes per second each are enough, with replicas.
Client bandwidth. A friend update to a client is about 80 bytes. After the server-side radius filter, only friends within the radius are sent. If 5 percent of the 13.3 million deliveries pass the filter, that is 665,000 messages per second, or about 53 MB per second across the fleet: 5 bytes per second per client. Data use on the phone is small.
api: WebSocket connection: GET wss://nearby.example.com/v1/stream with a bearer token. The connection is the only API. Client to server messages are small JSON or protobuf frames.
Client sends {type: "location", lat, lon, accuracy_m, ts, seq} every 30 seconds while the app is open and the user moved. The server replies {type: "ack", seq}.
Client sends {type: "subscribe_settings", radius_miles} to change the radius, and {type: "pause"} or {type: "resume"}.
Server sends {type: "friend_update", friend_id, distance_bucket, last_seen_ts, seq} when a nearby friend moves, and {type: "friend_gone", friend_id} when the friend goes out of range, pauses or times out. A first {type: "snapshot", friends: [...]} follows the connect.
HTTP endpoints for settings: PUT /v1/nearby/settings {enabled, radius_miles, hidden_from: [user_id]} and GET /v1/nearby/settings. The friend graph comes from the existing friends service.
datamodel: Durable data is small and lives in a relational database sharded by user_id.

The friend list is read from the friends service and cached for each connection. Hot, temporary data is in Redis. Latest location: key "loc:{user_id}", a hash with lat, lon, ts and seq, expiry 600 seconds, so a user who goes quiet vanishes by itself. Pub/sub channel per user: "pos:{user_id}". Connection registry: "ws:{user_id}" holds the id of the connection server, expiry 90 seconds, renewed by heartbeats. It is used for push fallback and for support tools. No location history is stored by default. If analytics needs it, it is written to a separate store with coarse cells (geohash length 6, cells of about 1.2 km by 0.6 km) and a retention limit.
design: The path of one update. A phone sends its location over the WebSocket to its connection server. The server (1) checks the user's settings in memory, (2) writes the location to the cache with a 10-minute expiry, and (3) publishes the update to the user's channel "pos:{user_id}" on the pub/sub cluster. Every connection server that has a connected friend of this user is subscribed to that channel and receives the message. For each local subscriber, it reads the receiver's own latest location from a local memory copy, computes the distance, and sends a friend update to the receiver only if the friend is inside the radius. The phone shows it.
Connect. When a user opens the app, the load balancer assigns a connection server. The server loads the friend list and the settings, subscribes to the channel of every friend with the feature enabled (about 100 subscriptions), fetches those friends' latest locations from the cache in one batch, filters by radius, and sends the first snapshot. Then it accepts updates.
Persistent connection. Real-time delivery needs a channel from server to phone. Long polling would repeat headers on every request and server-sent events only go one way; the phone also uploads a position every 30 seconds. A WebSocket carries both directions on one connection, with heartbeat frames every 30 seconds to keep mobile carrier NAT mappings alive and to detect dead peers. The connection server is stateful: it holds the socket, the friend list and the subscriptions. Load balancing is by least connections at connect time, and later messages do not need routing because the pub/sub layer finds the right server through subscriptions.
Pub/sub layer. Each user channel lives on exactly one pub/sub node, picked by consistent hashing of the user id. A ring of 200 nodes is kept in a service registry. A connection server computes the node for a channel locally and keeps connections to all 200 nodes (or the subset it needs). Consistent hashing matters because when we add 20 nodes only about 10 percent of the channels move, not all of them, so a resize causes a small burst of resubscriptions and not a storm. Redis Pub/Sub is "fire and forget": a message goes to the clients subscribed at that moment, nothing is stored and there are no acknowledgements. For location this is right, since the next update comes in 30 seconds.
Distance filter. The exact distance between two points on the Earth is given by the haversine formula
\[ d = 2R \arcsin\sqrt{\sin^2\frac{\Delta\varphi}{2} + \cos\varphi_1 \cos\varphi_2 \sin^2\frac{\Delta\lambda}{2}} \]
where \( R \approx 6{,}371 \) km, \( \varphi \) is latitude and \( \lambda \) is longitude. It takes about 100 nanoseconds. At 66,000 messages per second per server, even the plain formula on every message costs 66,000 times 100 ns = 6.6 ms of CPU per second, under 1 percent of a core. So no spatial index is needed on the delivery path. We keep a geohash of length 5 (a cell of about 4.9 km by 4.9 km) with each location only as a cheap first check and for the optional "people near me" feature.
Fan-out on write. Here the update is pushed to the subscribers at the moment it happens. The alternative, fan-out on read, is that every client polls for its friends' positions. Push costs one delivery per online friend per update: 13.3 million per second. Pull would cost 33 million key reads per second. Push also gives lower delay.

deepdive: Why per-user channels and not map cells. In "find strangers near me" you must store everyone in a spatial index (geohash, quadtree, S2 or H3) and query it. Here the audience of an update is known: the sender's friends. A per-user channel reaches exactly them: about 40 deliveries per update. A channel per map cell (everyone in the cell subscribes) would reach all users in that cell. In a city centre at lunch a cell of 1 km square can hold 20,000 online users, so one update would be delivered 20,000 times and filtered by the friend list, 500 times worse than 40. So the spatial index is used only to filter and in the feature "people near me", not as the delivery path. Cell channels become the better choice only for non-friend discovery.
Heavy users. A user with 5,000 friends and 500 online has 500 deliveries per update, 12 times the average. This is a hot key problem for the pub/sub node holding that channel. We cap the nearby feature to the 1,000 most recent or closest friends, and for users above the cap we use a coarser update interval (60 seconds). We also place channels by consistent hashing with virtual nodes, so no node holds a large cluster of heavy channels, and we monitor per-channel message counts.
Resizing the ring. When the pub/sub ring changes, some channels move to a new node. Publishers must publish to the new owner and subscribers must subscribe there. A safe approach: the registry has two versions of the ring for 60 seconds, a connection server publishes to the new owner and subscribes to both owners during the change, and then drops the old subscription. Duplicates are harmless because every message has a sequence number per sender, and the receiver drops anything older or equal to the last one seen. A short gap is also harmless since the next update comes in 30 seconds. Redis 7 has sharded pub/sub (SPUBLISH, SSUBSCRIBE) for cluster mode, which routes by hash slot, so the same idea can be run with the built-in slot mapping instead of our own ring.
Phone side. The phone is the weakest part of the system. GPS is the most expensive sensor. We use the platform's location service with a "balanced accuracy" setting (cell and Wi-Fi positioning) for the 30-second updates, which costs much less energy than continuous GPS, and ask for GPS only when the user opens the radar screen. The app sends nothing if the user moved less than 50 metres, so a phone on a desk makes no radio calls. Updates are small, and the same connection is reused so there is no new TLS handshake. In the background, mobile operating systems suspend the app and close its socket within seconds, so real-time delivery is a foreground feature. For the background we use the OS "significant location change" feature, which wakes the app on a move of about a few hundred metres, and the server sends a push notification ("Asha is near you") through APNs or FCM, with a limit of one per friend per day. Reconnects use exponential backoff with random jitter to avoid a thundering herd after a server restart.
Privacy and abuse. The feature is opt-in, with a clear indicator and a pause button. The server returns distance buckets ("about 2 miles") and never the coordinates. A malicious friend could still move around, collect distances and triangulate a person, so distances are rounded to coarse steps (for example 0.5 miles), updates to the friend are rate-limited to one per 30 seconds and the nearby list cannot be queried on demand. Hidden friends are filtered at subscription time, not at display time, so the data never leaves the server. Location data is encrypted in transit and the cache entries expire. Account deletion removes the cache entry at once.
Idempotence and ordering. Messages from one sender carry a sequence number set by the phone and a server timestamp. A receiver ignores messages with a sequence not higher than the last seen. This makes duplicates and reordering during failover harmless. Delivery is at most once in the normal case and at least once during ring changes, and both are fine for state that is overwritten every 30 seconds.
tradeoffs: Pub/sub versus polling. Choose push through pub/sub when updates are frequent and the audience is bounded (friends): 13.3 million deliveries per second against 33 million reads, and lower delay. Choose polling when updates are rare, clients are mostly in the background, or you cannot keep a million connections open; polling is simpler and works with any HTTP infrastructure. You pay with stateful connection servers in the first case and with wasted requests in the second.
Redis Pub/Sub versus Kafka. Choose Redis Pub/Sub when messages are tiny, short-lived and loss is acceptable: sub-millisecond latency, millions of ephemeral channels, no storage. Choose Kafka (or another log) when you need replay, ordering per key and durable history, for example for analytics or abuse investigation, and accept higher delay and a limit of thousands, not millions, of partitions. A common split: live delivery on Redis, and a sampled copy of events into Kafka for offline use.
Per-user channels versus geohash channels. Choose per-user channels when the audience is a friend list of tens to hundreds. Choose geohash cell channels when the audience is "everyone nearby", as in a "people around me" feature or a local-events feed, and when users are sparse enough that a cell holds few of them. You give up exact audience (you must filter) and risk hot cells in dense areas; use finer cells and quadtree-style splitting where density is high.
Update interval. Choose 15 seconds when users walk to meet and the product value is a live map; the cost is twice the messages and more battery. Choose 30 to 60 seconds for a radar that answers "who is around"; this is our default. Choose adaptive intervals (shorter when moving fast, none when still) to get most of both, at the cost of client complexity.
Centralised versus regional deployment. Choose a region-local deployment, where each region has its own ring and cache and friends in other regions are not shown, because "nearby" means physically close and cross-region friends are rarely within 5 miles. This cuts cross-ocean traffic and the blast radius of a failure. Choose a global ring only if a large share of nearby pairs would cross a region border, as in a small country split between two data centres.
failures: A connection server crashes. Its 50,000 users reconnect within seconds. With jittered backoff over 10 seconds that is 5,000 connects per second, each subscribing 100 channels, so 500,000 subscription calls per second spread over the pub/sub ring. This is within capacity because the ring is sized for 550,000 per second in normal operation. The pub/sub nodes see unsubscribe events from the closed connections.
A pub/sub node crashes. All channels on that node lose their subscribers. Connection servers see the TCP connection break, mark the node as down in the registry, and subscribe to the new owner of each affected channel. During the 5 to 10 seconds of the change, friends of about 0.5 percent of users miss an update. The next update fixes it. The node comes back empty (no state), so recovery is a matter of resubscribing.
Cache node failure. The latest-location entries on that shard are gone. Users keep sending every 30 seconds, so the cache is complete again in 30 seconds. In the meantime new connects see fewer friends in their snapshot, and the live updates fill the rest.
Network partition between a connection server and the pub/sub ring. Its users stop receiving updates. The server notices missed heartbeats on the pub/sub connections, tells clients with a "degraded" frame, and clients show "last updated 2 minutes ago". When the network returns, a re-snapshot repairs the view.
Overload and slow consumers. A phone on a poor network cannot read its socket fast enough. The connection server keeps a bounded queue per client, drops the oldest updates when the queue is full (only the latest position per friend matters), and closes the socket if the queue stays full for 30 seconds. This stops one slow client from using all the memory.
Stale or wrong positions. GPS can be wrong by hundreds of metres indoors. The client sends the accuracy, and the server discards fixes with accuracy worse than 500 metres. Friends not updated for 10 minutes disappear because the cache entry expires and a "friend_gone" frame is sent by a timer.
evolution: At 10,000 users, one server with an in-memory map of latest positions is enough, and clients poll every 30 seconds. At 1 million users, add WebSockets, one Redis for cache and pub/sub and a few connection servers. At 100 million daily users, as designed: sharded pub/sub with consistent hashing, 200 connection servers, sharded cache and region-local rings. At 1 billion users, split by region and by city size, move hot channels into dedicated nodes, add adaptive update rates and aggressive client-side suppression of still positions, and keep the product simple: the cost of each connected user per month is what decides whether the feature survives.
followups: Why not store everyone's location in a geospatial index and query nearby users? || It solves the wrong problem. We already know who the audience is (the friends), and an index query per user per 30 seconds would cost 333,000 queries per second plus the writes. Pushing through per-user channels costs about 40 deliveries per update and returns only the relevant people.
Why a WebSocket and not HTTP polling or SSE? || The feature needs two-way traffic (upload position, receive updates), low delay and no repeated headers. Polling wastes 90 percent of requests when nothing changes; SSE is one-way. WebSocket keeps one connection per phone with a 30-second heartbeat to keep carrier NAT entries open.
What happens when a pub/sub node dies? || The channels on it lose their subscribers and some updates are lost. Connection servers detect the broken connection, update the ring and resubscribe to the new owners. Because each user sends a new position every 30 seconds and messages carry sequence numbers, the state converges without any repair job.
How do you size the pub/sub layer? || Count deliveries: 333,000 updates per second times 40 online friends = 13.3 million deliveries per second. At about 100,000 per node, that is 133 nodes; add 30 percent headroom and use 200. Memory is not the limit: 1 billion subscriptions at 100 bytes is 100 GB.
How do you protect privacy and prevent triangulation? || Opt-in, pause, per-friend hiding applied at subscription time, rounded distance buckets instead of coordinates, no on-demand queries, rate limits, short expiry for cached locations and no history by default. Rounding and rate limits make it hard to find a person by moving around and reading distances.
How do you keep the battery cost low? || Use cell and Wi-Fi positioning for routine updates, send nothing when the phone has not moved 50 metres, reuse one connection, keep updates small, and use the OS significant-change service plus push in the background instead of a live socket.
How would you add the feature "tell me when a friend comes within 1 mile" while the app is closed? || Keep the app in background with significant-change updates, store the last coarse cell per user on the server, and when an update arrives look up friends in the same or neighbouring cells with a spatial index, compute distances, and send a rate-limited push notification. This is where the geohash index is useful.

title: Design Google Maps (routing, ETA, map tiles)
category: Web & product
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: roadrouting, graphsalgo, spatialidx, cdn, objectstore, streamproc, mobilearch, cachelayer, partitioning, httpcaching
summary: A maps product does three jobs. It draws the map on a phone at any zoom level, it finds the best route between two places, and it tells you how long the trip will take, taking live traffic into account. These jobs have very different shapes. Drawing the map is a read-heavy content delivery problem: hundreds of thousands of small, static, cacheable files per second. Routing is a graph algorithm problem on a road network with hundreds of millions of nodes, which must answer in tens of milliseconds, so plain Dijkstra is far too slow and we need precomputation. ETA is a streaming and machine learning problem: millions of moving phones report their speed, and the system must turn that into a travel time for every road segment each minute. We design all three parts and the phone app that uses them, including offline maps and battery limits.
requirements: Functional requirements. (1) Map display: pan and zoom a world map from level 0 (whole Earth) to level 20 (single buildings), with roads, labels, places and optional satellite or traffic layers. (2) Routing: given an origin, a destination and a mode (car, walk, bike, transit), return one or more routes with geometry, distance, turn-by-turn steps and ETA. (3) Navigation: while the user drives, show position, detect when they leave the route, and compute a new one. (4) Live traffic and incidents on the map and inside ETA. (5) Search and geocoding: turn text into coordinates and back. We treat search as a separate service and focus on tiles, routing and ETA. (6) Offline: download a region and use it with no network.
Non-functional requirements. Map tile latency: p95 under 100 ms from the CDN. Routing latency: p99 under 300 ms for a cross-country route, under 100 ms for a city route. Freshness: traffic speeds no older than 2 minutes; base map changes (a new road) visible within days, closures within minutes. Availability 99.99 percent for navigation, because a driver depends on it. ETA accuracy: median absolute error of a few percent of the trip time.
Assumptions. 200 million daily users, each with 3 sessions per day. At peak 10 million users are navigating. The road network of the world has about 500 million nodes (intersections and shape points that matter for routing) and 1 billion directed edges (road segments). This is an order-of-magnitude assumption for the design; real counts depend on the data source.
estimates: Tile traffic. A phone loads about 40 tiles per session after its own cache. 200 million users times 3 sessions times 40 tiles = 24 billion tile requests per day. Divide by 86,400: about 278,000 per second on average; with a peak factor of 3, about 830,000 per second. A compressed vector tile averages about 20 KB. 830,000 times 20 KB = 16.6 GB per second, or about 133 Gbit per second at the edge. Maps are cache-friendly: the same city tiles are asked for again and again. With a CDN hit rate of 95 percent, the origin sees 5 percent: 42,000 requests per second, 830 MB per second, about 6.6 Gbit per second.
Tile count. At zoom level z the world is divided into 2^z by 2^z tiles, so 4^z tiles. Level 14 has 4^14 = 268 million tiles. All levels from 0 to 14 together hold (4^15 - 1) / 3 = 358 million tiles. If 30 percent of them have content (the sea and empty land are the same few blank tiles), that is 107 million tiles times 20 KB = about 2.1 TB. Going to level 18 would give 4^18 = 68.7 billion tiles at the finest level alone, which at even 10 KB each would be 687 TB for populated areas only. So we do not pre-build deep levels. Vector tiles can be over-zoomed: the client draws a level-14 tile at levels 15 to 20 with the same geometry, scaled. We pre-build to level 14, build levels 15 and 16 on demand for populated areas (where there is detail to show) and cache them.
Routing requests. 200 million users times 0.5 route requests per day = 100 million per day, about 1,160 per second on average and 4,600 at peak (factor 4). Navigation adds re-route requests: 30 million navigating sessions per day, each with about 2.5 re-routes: 75 million per day, 870 per second. Peak total about 8,000 routes per second. At about 15 ms of CPU per route (search, traffic weights, path unpacking, ETA model), 8,000 times 15 ms = 120 CPU-seconds per second, so 120 cores at full load and 240 at 50 percent. With replication in three zones and several regions, about 100 machines of 32 cores. Routing is cheap if the algorithm is right.
Graph memory. An edge record has the target node (4 bytes), a travel time (4), a length (4) and flags such as road class and one-way (4): 16 bytes. 1 billion edges times 16 bytes = 16 GB. Node coordinates: 500 million times 8 bytes = 4 GB. Offsets of the adjacency arrays: 500 million times 4 = 2 GB. Total about 22 GB for one travel mode. A contraction hierarchy adds about as many shortcut edges as there are original edges: about 16 GB more. So the graph for one mode is about 40 GB and fits in one large machine. We still split it by region for locality, rollouts and failure isolation.
Probe traffic. 10 million navigating phones, each sends a GPS point every 10 seconds in batches of 3: 1 million points per second at about 60 bytes: 60 MB per second, 5 TB per day. A stream processing cluster handles this easily when partitioned by location.
api: Tiles: GET /tiles/{version}/{z}/{x}/{y}.mvt returns a vector tile (Mapbox Vector Tile format, protobuf, gzip). The version is part of the URL. GET /style.json returns the style and the current tile version. GET /traffic/{z}/{x}/{y}.mvt returns the traffic layer with a one-minute lifetime.
Routing: POST /v1/routes:compute with {origin: {lat, lon}, destination, waypoints[], mode: drive|walk|bike|transit, departure_time, alternatives: true, avoid: [tolls, ferries]} returns {routes: [{polyline, distance_m, duration_s, duration_in_traffic_s, steps[], legs[]}]}. The polyline is an encoded sequence of points.
Navigation: POST /v1/routes:reroute with {route_id, position, heading, speed} returns a new route that starts at the snapped position. Position updates for traffic go to POST /v1/probes with a batch of {device_hash, ts, lat, lon, speed, heading, accuracy}. Device ids are rotated and the data is anonymised.
Geocoding: GET /v1/geocode?q=...&bias=lat,lon and GET /v1/reverse?lat=...&lon=....
datamodel: Base map data. The source of truth is a versioned set of features in an object store: roads, buildings, land use, water, places, each with geometry and attributes. A weekly (or more frequent) build job cuts them into tiles.
The tile address is (z, x, y). For a point with latitude \( \varphi \) and longitude \( \lambda \) in the web Mercator projection, the tile column and row at zoom \( z \) are
\[ x = \left\lfloor \frac{\lambda + 180}{360}\, 2^z \right\rfloor, \quad y = \left\lfloor \frac{1 - \ln(\tan\varphi + \sec\varphi)/\pi}{2}\, 2^z \right\rfloor \]
The quadkey (the digits of x and y interleaved, two bits per level) puts neighbouring tiles next to each other in storage order. This is a space-filling curve used as a key.
Routing graph. Stored as arrays for fast loading, not as database rows. Logically:
```sql
CREATE TABLE edge (
  edge_id      BIGINT PRIMARY KEY,
  from_node    BIGINT,
  to_node      BIGINT,
  length_m     INT,
  road_class   SMALLINT,    -- motorway ... residential
  speed_limit  SMALLINT,
  flags        INT,         -- oneway, toll, ferry, access per mode
  profile_id   SMALLINT     -- index into typical-speed profiles
);
CREATE TABLE turn_restriction (
  via_node BIGINT, from_edge BIGINT, to_edge BIGINT, kind SMALLINT
);
```
Typical-speed profiles. One value per 5-minute slot of the week is 7 times 24 times 12 = 2,016 slots. Storing 2,016 bytes for each of 1 billion edges would be 2 TB, so we cluster edges into a few thousand profiles (shape of the daily curve) and each edge stores a profile id. Live speed layer: a map from edge_id to current speed and a timestamp, held in memory by every router and replaced as a whole every minute.
design: The tile path. The phone computes which tiles cover the screen at the current zoom and asks for them from the CDN edge. On a hit the edge answers in a few milliseconds. On a miss, the edge asks a regional shield cache and then the tile origin. The origin is a stateless tile server that reads the tile from packed archives in an object store: one large file per region and zoom range, with an index from tile id to offset and length, read with HTTP range requests. We avoid storing 107 million separate small objects, which is costly in requests and slow to list and publish. Level 15 and 16 tiles that are missing are generated from the feature data on demand and cached. The phone draws the vector data with its GPU using a style, so one tile serves day and night styles, several languages and any rotation.
The routing path. A request goes through the API gateway to the routing service. The service snaps the origin and destination to the road network by finding the nearest edges with a spatial index (a grid or an R-tree over edge geometry; the cell keys come from an S2 or quadkey scheme). It chooses the regional graph. For a route inside a region the search runs on that shard. For a route across regions, a small "overlay" graph of border nodes connects the regions: the router runs the search to the borders on each side and the overlay in the middle. The result is unpacked into a polyline and steps, and each edge gets its travel time from the live layer or the typical profile for the expected time of travel. The ETA service then corrects the sum with a model.
The traffic path. Phones in navigation mode batch their positions and send them to the probe API, which writes them to Kafka. A stream processing job map-matches each trace to roads. A raw GPS point can be 10 to 30 metres off, so the job uses a hidden Markov model that scores the candidate roads near each point (found with the spatial index) by distance to the point and by how plausible the move from the previous candidate is along the network. From matched traces we get, for each edge, the speeds of the vehicles that crossed it. A windowed aggregation per edge computes a robust speed (the median over the last minutes, with a minimum number of vehicles). Every minute a new speed layer is published, and routers load it. Edges with too few probes fall back to the typical profile, adjusted by the speed on neighbouring edges.
The navigation path. While driving, the app samples its position about once per second and snaps it to the route locally, so the blue dot moves without a network. If the user stays more than about 50 metres off the route for a few seconds, the app asks for a re-route. The app also asks for a fresh ETA every few minutes, and the server can push a better route when traffic worsens ahead.

deepdive: Why Dijkstra is too slow. Dijkstra's algorithm explores nodes in order of distance from the start. For a 500 km trip it visits about half of the nodes of the region. If a region has 100 million nodes and each visit costs 100 ns, one query costs about 5 seconds. A* uses a straight-line estimate to aim at the goal, but on roads the search area is still a wide ellipse, and bidirectional search only helps by about a factor of two. At 8,000 queries per second this is impossible. The key fact is that the road network barely changes between queries, so we can pay once for precomputation.
Contraction hierarchies. We order the nodes by importance (a driveway is less important than a motorway junction). We then "contract" nodes one by one from the least important: when a node is removed, we add a shortcut edge between its neighbours if the path through the node was the only shortest path, with the weight of the path. After all nodes are processed, a query runs two small Dijkstra searches, one forward from the start and one backward from the goal, and each only goes "up" to more important nodes. They meet at a high-level node on the shortest path. Each side visits only hundreds to a few thousand nodes, so the query takes well under a millisecond to a few milliseconds even on a continent. Preprocessing for a continental graph takes minutes to an hour, and the shortcuts about double the edge count. This is why a graph of 1 billion edges needs 16 GB for edges plus roughly 16 GB for shortcuts.
Live traffic breaks plain contraction hierarchies, because the shortcuts hold fixed weights. If one highway edge slows down, every shortcut that includes it is wrong. There are two answers. The first is to split the work in two. A multi-level partition of the graph into cells (the idea behind Customizable Route Planning) is computed once from the road topology and does not depend on travel times. The weights of the shortcuts between cell borders are computed in a second phase called customization, which for a new traffic snapshot takes seconds and can run in parallel on each region. The second answer is to keep the hierarchy but to run a corrected search that checks traffic on the final path. We use the first for the live layer: the graph topology is built nightly, and the weights are customized every minute.
Time-dependent travel times. A trip of 90 minutes should use the traffic of 90 minutes from now when the car reaches the far part of the route, not the current traffic. For the first 15 to 30 minutes we use the live speeds. After that we blend toward the typical profile for that time of week. A time-dependent edge weight function costs more in the search, so some systems search with current weights and then recompute the ETA of the top few candidates along the path with the time-dependent function.
Turn restrictions and costs. Roads have "no left turn", one-way streets and long waits at signals. A node-based graph cannot express "you cannot go from edge A to edge C through this node". We use an edge-based graph in which the nodes are road segments and the edges are the turns between them, each with a turn cost. This doubles the graph size but gives correct routes.
ETA with machine learning. Summing edge times is a good start but systematic errors add up: signals, turns, parking, a slow-down that begins after you pass. A learned model takes the route as a sequence of segments with features (length, class, live speed, typical speed, time of day, weather, events) and predicts the actual duration. Training data is the ground truth from finished trips: predicted ETA at departure versus actual arrival. Google and DeepMind described a graph neural network over "supersegments", groups of adjacent road segments, which improved the accuracy of real-time ETAs in several cities (by up to 50 percent in some of them) in 2020. We train the model on the recent weeks of trips, evaluate by median and 90th percentile relative error, and compare each release to the current one on held-out trips and by an A/B test on route choice and trip outcome.
Caching in several places. Tiles are cached on the device, the CDN edge, the shield and the origin. Routes are cached for popular pairs: the key is the start and end cell (about 100 metres), mode and a 5-minute time bucket. Hit rates are low for random pairs but high for airports, stations and big venues.
tradeoffs: Vector tiles versus raster tiles. Choose vector tiles for road maps: 20 KB or less, style changes without a rebuild, smooth zoom, rotation and tilt, crisp labels and languages chosen on the device. Choose raster tiles for satellite and aerial images and for very old devices; they are heavier (30 to 100 KB each for imagery) and fixed in style. You pay the GPU and battery cost on the phone for vector rendering.
Pre-built tiles versus tiles on demand. Choose pre-built packs for levels 0 to 14, where the request distribution is wide and a cold miss costs hundreds of milliseconds. Choose on-demand generation with caching for deep levels where 99 percent of tiles are never requested. You give up a small latency on the first request for a rare area.
Contraction hierarchies versus customizable route planning. Choose plain contraction hierarchies for fixed weights (walking, a static routing profile, offline use on the phone): the fastest queries and simple code. Choose a customizable approach when weights change every minute with traffic: queries are a few times slower, but a new weight set is ready in seconds, not hours. The price is a more complex pipeline.
Live ETA versus predicted ETA. Choose live speeds for the first part of the trip, where the current state is the best forecast. Choose profiles and learned prediction for the part more than 30 minutes ahead. If you use only live speeds, long trips show a morning jam that will have cleared; if you use only profiles, the system misses accidents.
Single global graph versus regional shards. Choose regional shards, with an overlay for border crossings, to keep memory per machine small, roll out data by region, and keep a failure local. Choose one global graph only if cross-region routes are a large share of traffic, which they are not.
failures: The traffic feed is late or broken. Routers keep using the last snapshot for up to 5 minutes, then slowly blend the speeds toward typical profiles, and the response marks "traffic data delayed". The route stays valid but less precise.
A router shard fails. Each region has at least three replicas in different zones behind a load balancer. If all replicas of a region fail, the gateway sends the request to a standby region that holds the same graph, and the extra delay is acceptable for a rare event.
A bad map build. A bug can reverse a one-way street or close a motorway. Builds are versioned and go through automatic checks: compare the number of edges by class with the last build, route thousands of known origin-destination pairs and compare the durations (a change over 10 percent triggers review), and roll out by region with a canary. Rollback is a pointer change to the previous version of tiles and graph.
GPS errors. In tunnels the signal is lost and in city canyons it jumps. The app uses dead reckoning from the last speed and heading, and snaps to the route only when accuracy is good. The stream job discards points with accuracy over 50 metres and sudden jumps that are impossible for a vehicle.
Probe bias and privacy. Probes come mostly from phones in navigation mode on major roads, so small streets have no data. We fall back to profiles there. Device ids are rotated frequently, traces are cut near home and work, and data is aggregated per edge, with a minimum number of vehicles before a speed is published. This also reduces the risk of identifying a single driver.
evolution: At 10,000 users and one country, a single machine can hold the road graph, run A* or contraction hierarchies and serve tiles from disk behind a small cache. At 1 million users, put tiles on a CDN, run two or three routing replicas and add a nightly build. At 100 million users, split the graph by region, add the live probe pipeline with the stream job, build the customizable weight pipeline and add offline maps. At 1 billion users, the work moves to data: more precise maps through imagery and vehicle data, learned ETA models per city, multimodal routing across transit feeds, lane-level guidance, and the cost of the CDN, which is the largest line in the bill.
followups: Why not run Dijkstra or A* for each route? || A search over a continental road network visits millions of nodes and takes seconds per query. Contraction hierarchies precompute shortcuts so that a query visits hundreds to thousands of nodes and answers in about a millisecond. At 8,000 routes per second only the precomputed approach fits.
How do you include live traffic if the shortcuts are precomputed? || Separate the topology from the weights. Build the partition and the hierarchy structure from the road network, and recompute the shortcut weights for each new traffic snapshot (customization), which takes seconds. Routers load the new weights every minute.
Why vector tiles and why can you over-zoom them? || A vector tile holds geometry and attributes, not pixels. The client draws it at any scale and style, so level 14 data can serve levels 15 to 20 and day and night modes. Raster tiles would need a separate image for every level and style.
How do you make tiles cache well at the CDN? || Put the version in the URL and mark the tile immutable with a very long max-age, so a changed map has a new URL and no purge is needed. Keep the small style file short-lived so it points to the current version. Traffic tiles use a 60-second lifetime with stale-while-revalidate.
How do you turn phone GPS points into road speeds? || Map-match each trace to the network with a hidden Markov model that uses the distance to candidate roads and the plausibility of moves along the graph, then aggregate speeds per edge per minute with a minimum number of vehicles, and publish a speed layer. Edges without data use typical profiles.
How do you support offline navigation on the phone? || Download vector tiles up to level 14 or 16 for the chosen region, a compact routing graph with a static hierarchy and typical speeds, and a local search index. The phone runs contraction-hierarchy queries on its own, without live traffic. Storage is in the hundreds of megabytes for a large region.

title: Design a hotel reservation system
category: Web & product
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: occ, isolation, acid, idempotence, ccontrol, replication, cachelayer, outbox, partitioning, oltp, apidesign
summary: A hotel reservation system lets guests search for rooms on given dates, hold a room while they pay, and confirm the booking. Hotels use it to manage how many rooms of each type they have for each night. The traffic is small compared with a social network: a chain of 5,000 hotels sees only a few bookings per second. What makes it hard is correctness. Two guests must never get the last room, a retry must never charge a card twice, a payment that succeeds after the hold expired must be handled, and a crash in the middle of a booking must not leave a room blocked for ever. The core of the design is a short database transaction on an inventory table, protected by concurrency control, plus a small state machine and events for everything that must happen after the commit (emails, search index, analytics). We keep search fast with caches that are allowed to be a little stale, and we make the final booking check exact.
requirements: Functional requirements. (1) Search: by city or map area, dates and number of guests; show hotels with available room types and prices. (2) Hotel and room detail pages. (3) Reserve: choose a room type, dates and number of rooms; the system holds the rooms for 10 minutes while the guest pays. (4) Pay and confirm; the guest gets a confirmation. (5) View, modify and cancel a reservation, with the hotel's refund policy. (6) Hotel staff manage room types, inventory, prices and blocked dates. (7) Overbooking: hotels may sell up to 110 percent of their rooms for a room type, because some guests cancel or do not show up.
Non-functional requirements. Correctness first: no overbooking beyond the allowed limit, no double charge, no lost confirmation. Availability: the search and browse parts should be up at 99.99 percent; the booking path at 99.9 percent, and a short failover pause is acceptable. Latency: search p95 under 300 ms; booking p95 under 1 second excluding the payment provider. Durability: a confirmed reservation survives the loss of one data centre zone (zero data loss). Consistency: booking is strongly consistent; search can be stale by up to a minute.
Assumptions. 5,000 hotels with an average of 200 rooms, so 1 million rooms. Average occupancy 70 percent and average stay 3 nights. Each hotel has about 20 room types. The calendar is open for 2 years (730 days). A guest looks at about 10 hotel pages for each booking page, and 10 booking pages for each confirmed booking (a 100 to 10 to 1 funnel).
estimates: Booking rate. The system must handle 1,000,000 rooms times 0.70 occupancy = 700,000 occupied rooms each night. With a stay of 3 nights, new bookings per day = 700,000 / 3 = 233,000. Per second: 233,000 / 86,400 = 2.7 bookings per second. In a holiday sale the peak is 10 times higher: 27 per second.
Funnel. With a 100 to 10 to 1 funnel: 27 bookings per second at peak means 270 booking-page views (holds created) per second and 2,700 hotel-detail views per second. Search queries are more frequent still: assume 5,000 per second at peak. So the read load is about 200 times the write load, and reads are the part that needs caches.
Inventory size. One row per hotel, room type and date: 5,000 hotels times 20 room types times 730 days = 73 million rows. At about 48 bytes per row (three key fields, two counters, a version) this is 3.5 GB. A single database server holds it in memory. We still shard by hotel to scale and to isolate failures, but the size is not the problem.
Contention. Take a popular 300-room hotel on New Year's Eve with a sale. Suppose 100 booking attempts per second hit the same inventory rows (same hotel, room type and night). If a transaction holds the row lock for 2 ms, the row can serve 1 / 0.002 = 500 updates per second, so 100 per second is safe. At 1,000 per second the requests would queue. This is the worst case that sizes our design.
api: The API is REST over HTTPS, versioned in the path, with JSON bodies. Errors use standard HTTP codes and a stable error code in the body.
GET /v1/hotels?city=...&check_in=2026-12-30&check_out=2027-01-02&guests=2&page_token=... returns a page of hotels with the cheapest available price and a cursor for the next page. Cursor pagination, not offset, so the list does not shift between pages.
GET /v1/hotels/{hotel_id}/rooms?check_in=...&check_out=... returns room types with availability and a price quote: {room_type_id, rooms_left, price_total, quote_token}. The quote_token is signed and valid for 15 minutes, so the price shown is the price charged.
POST /v1/reservations creates a hold. The header Idempotency-Key (or the client-generated reservation_id in the body) makes retries safe. Body: {reservation_id, hotel_id, room_type_id, check_in, check_out, rooms, guest, quote_token}. Response 201 {reservation_id, status: "PENDING", hold_expires_at, amount}. If the rooms are gone: 409 with code "sold_out". If the quote is old: 409 "price_changed" with the new price. A repeat of the same request returns the same 201 with the same body.
POST /v1/reservations/{id}/payment with {payment_method_token} starts the payment. GET /v1/reservations/{id} returns the state. POST /v1/reservations/{id}:cancel cancels and returns the refund amount.
POST /v1/webhooks/payments receives asynchronous results from the payment provider. It is verified by a signature and handled idempotently.
PUT /v1/hotels/{hotel_id}/room-types/{type_id}/inventory with {from_date, to_date, total_inventory} is for hotel staff.
datamodel: The booking path uses a relational database because it needs multi-row transactions with constraints.
```sql
CREATE TABLE room_type_inventory (
  hotel_id         BIGINT,
  room_type_id     INT,
  stay_date        DATE,            -- the hotel's local night
  total_inventory  INT NOT NULL,
  total_reserved   INT NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
  PRIMARY KEY (hotel_id, room_type_id, stay_date),
  CHECK (total_reserved >= 0),
  CHECK (total_reserved * 10 <= total_inventory * 11)  -- 110 percent
);

CREATE TABLE reservation (
  reservation_id   UUID PRIMARY KEY,     -- chosen by the client
  hotel_id         BIGINT NOT NULL,
  room_type_id     INT NOT NULL,
  guest_id         BIGINT NOT NULL,
  check_in         DATE NOT NULL,
  check_out        DATE NOT NULL,        -- that night is free
  rooms            INT NOT NULL,
  status           TEXT NOT NULL,        -- PENDING, CONFIRMED, ...
  amount_cents     BIGINT NOT NULL,
  currency         CHAR(3) NOT NULL,
  hold_expires_at  TIMESTAMPTZ,
  payment_ref      TEXT,
  created_at       TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now()
);
CREATE INDEX ON reservation (hotel_id, check_in);
CREATE INDEX ON reservation (status, hold_expires_at);

CREATE TABLE outbox (
  event_id BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY, aggregate_id UUID,
  type TEXT, payload JSONB, created_at TIMESTAMPTZ DEFAULT now()
);
```
Dates are half-open: a stay from 30 December to 2 January occupies the nights 30, 31 and 1, and the room is free again on the night of 2 January. This lets one guest check out and another check in on the same day without a conflict. The 110 percent limit is written as an integer check (total_reserved times 10 is at most total_inventory times 11) so no floating point is involved. Money is stored as integer cents with a currency code.
Sharding key: hotel_id. All rows of one hotel (inventory, reservations) live on one shard, so a booking is a single-shard transaction. A global lookup "reservations of this guest" is served by a second table keyed by guest_id, filled from the outbox events (so it can lag by a second), or by a query to all shards for rare support cases.
Search data is a separate read model: an index with hotel location, amenities, rating and a compact availability summary per hotel per date, built from events.
design: The search path. The guest sends city, dates and party size. The search service queries a geographic index (hotels in the area, ranked by relevance) and joins an availability summary: for each hotel and date range, the minimum number of rooms left for the cheapest room types. The summary is held in a cache (Redis) and a search index, rebuilt from change events and refreshed with a time to live of 30 to 60 seconds. It may say "2 rooms left" when the real number is 1. Static content (photos, descriptions, reviews) is served from a CDN and an object store. The key point is that search is approximate and the booking is exact.
The booking path. When the guest chooses a room and clicks reserve, the client generates a reservation_id (UUID) and calls POST /v1/reservations. The reservation service runs one transaction on the hotel's shard:
```sql
BEGIN;
INSERT INTO reservation (reservation_id, hotel_id, room_type_id,
   guest_id, check_in, check_out, rooms, status, amount_cents,
   currency, hold_expires_at)
VALUES (:id, :h, :t, :g, :ci, :co, :n, 'PENDING', :amt, :cur,
   now() + interval '10 minutes')
ON CONFLICT (reservation_id) DO NOTHING;
-- no row inserted: a retry, return the stored reservation

UPDATE room_type_inventory
   SET total_reserved = total_reserved + :n
 WHERE hotel_id = :h AND room_type_id = :t
   AND stay_date >= :ci AND stay_date < :co
   AND (total_reserved + :n) * 10 <= total_inventory * 11;
-- if rows updated < number of nights: ROLLBACK and return 409 sold_out

INSERT INTO outbox (aggregate_id, type, payload)
VALUES (:id, 'HoldCreated', :json);
COMMIT;
```
Why this is correct. The UPDATE tests availability and takes the room in one statement, so no other transaction can slip in between the check and the change. It locks the inventory rows of the nights it changes. A second transaction that wants the same rows waits for the first to commit, then re-evaluates the condition against the new value, and fails if the room is gone. The result is checked: if fewer rows were updated than nights, at least one night was full and the whole transaction is rolled back, which undoes the nights it already took. The CHECK constraint on the table is a second line of defence: even a buggy code path cannot push the counter above the limit.
Payment. The client calls the payment endpoint with a token from the payment provider. The service calls the provider using the reservation_id as the provider's idempotency key, so a retry cannot charge twice. When the provider reports success, directly or by webhook, a handler runs a transaction: if the reservation is PENDING and not expired, set CONFIRMED and add a 'ReservationConfirmed' outbox row. If it is already CONFIRMED, do nothing (a duplicate webhook). If it has EXPIRED, we try to take the rooms again; if they are gone, we refund and tell the guest. This is a saga: reserve inventory, charge the card, confirm, with a compensation (release inventory, refund) for each step that can fail. We do not use a distributed transaction (two-phase commit) because the card network cannot take part in one.
Expiry. A worker runs every 30 seconds. It selects reservations with status PENDING and hold_expires_at in the past, using FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED so several workers do not collide, marks them EXPIRED, subtracts the rooms from the inventory and writes an outbox row, all in one transaction. Time is always taken from the database clock, not from application servers, which avoids clock-skew bugs.
Events after the commit. A relay process reads new outbox rows in order and publishes them to Kafka. Consumers send emails, update the search index and the cache, fill the guest-keyed table, feed analytics and notify the hotel. Because the outbox row is written in the same transaction as the state change, there is no case where the booking exists but no event was ever produced. The relay delivers at least once, so consumers de-duplicate with the event id.

deepdive: Concurrency control: three ways to protect the last room. The problem is a race. Two guests, A and B, both see one room left. If the application reads the counter, checks it and writes it back in separate steps, both can read 99 of 100, both write 100, and 101 rooms are sold for 100 (or the second write overwrites the first and one booking is lost). This is a lost update.
Pessimistic locking. Run SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on the inventory rows, check the counts in the application, then update. The lock makes other transactions wait. It is correct and easy to reason about, but holds the lock across a network round trip to the application (about 1 ms or more), and under a surge the wait queue grows. A fixed order of locking (by date ascending) is needed to avoid deadlocks between bookings with overlapping dates.
Optimistic concurrency. Read the row with a version number, compute the new counter in the application, then write with UPDATE ... SET total_reserved = :new, version = version + 1 WHERE ... AND version = :old. If zero rows are updated, someone else changed the row: retry from the read. No lock is held during thinking time, which is excellent when conflicts are rare. For a hot hotel on a sale night, most attempts conflict, and the retries waste work and increase load exactly when it is already high.
Conditional update (our choice). Put the check inside the UPDATE statement, so the database does the check and the change as one atomic step on the latest committed value. The lock is held only for the statement and commit, about 2 ms, and there is no read-modify-write loop in the application. With a conflict rate of 100 per second on one row, requests simply serialise at 500 per second capacity. A CHECK constraint adds a guarantee that no code path can break.
Isolation level. Read Committed is enough for this transaction in databases like PostgreSQL, because after waiting for a row lock the UPDATE re-checks its WHERE clause on the new row version. Under Repeatable Read (snapshot isolation), the second transaction fails with a serialization error when the row was changed after its snapshot, and the application must retry the whole transaction. Serializable would also work but is more expensive and also needs retries. The risk to watch is write skew: two transactions each read different rows and then write rows that together break a rule. Our rule is on a single counter row, so the conditional update avoids it. A rule that spans rows, for example "a guest may hold at most 3 reservations", would need either a lock on a guest row or Serializable isolation. In all cases the client code retries on deadlock (error 40P01) and serialization failure (40001) up to three times with jitter.
Idempotence. Mobile networks fail after the server has done the work but before the client sees the reply. The client then retries. With the reservation_id as the primary key and ON CONFLICT DO NOTHING, the second call finds the existing row and returns it, with no second hold. The same key is used with the payment provider. Webhooks are keyed by the provider's event id. Every step of the saga is safe to repeat, which is what lets us use at-least-once delivery everywhere.
Unknown payment outcome. If the call to the provider times out, we do not know if the card was charged. We set the status to PAYMENT_UNKNOWN, keep the hold, and ask the provider for the result by the idempotency key (or wait for the webhook). We never release the inventory or issue a second charge until the answer is known, up to a limit (for example 30 minutes), after which a reconciliation job decides.
Replication and reads. The primary replicates synchronously to a standby in another zone, so a committed reservation exists in two zones (no data loss if one zone is lost). Read replicas are asynchronous and may lag. Search and hotel pages read from replicas and caches. "My bookings" right after payment must show the new booking, so for a short time after a write we read from the primary, or we pass the log position of the write and read only from replicas that have caught up (read-your-writes).
tradeoffs: Pessimistic locks versus optimistic versioning versus conditional update. Choose conditional updates for counters with a simple rule (our case): atomic, short and no retry loop. Choose optimistic versioning when the rule needs application logic that cannot be written in one statement and conflicts are rare (editing a booking's guest list). Choose pessimistic locks for long multi-step flows with high conflict, such as a staff tool that reallocates rooms across many dates. You pay with waiting in the third case and with wasted retries in the second.
Hold inventory at "reserve" versus at "pay". Choose to take inventory when the guest enters the payment page (our design): no guest pays for a room that is gone, and the experience is smooth. The cost is abandoned holds that block rooms for up to 10 minutes. Choose a soft hold (no decrement, check again at payment) when inventory is plentiful and hold abandonment is high; some guests will pay and then be told the room is gone, and you must refund. A good hybrid is a short hold (5 minutes) that can be extended once.
Single database versus sharding by hotel. Choose a single primary with a standby while the data is under a few hundred GB and the writes under 1,000 per second: it is simplest and gives ACID transactions everywhere. Choose sharding by hotel_id when you need isolation, regional data placement or 10 to 100 times more scale. The booking stays a single-shard transaction. What you give up is cheap global queries by guest or by city, which move to read models fed by events.
Synchronous versus asynchronous replication. Choose synchronous replication to a second zone for the booking database: a confirmed booking must not be lost, and the cost is about 1 to 2 ms more per commit. Choose asynchronous replication for read replicas and for the disaster recovery region, and accept a recovery point of a few seconds there.
failures: The primary database fails. The standby is promoted in 20 to 60 seconds, because replication was synchronous, no committed booking is lost. During the pause, booking calls fail with a retryable 503; search continues from caches. Clients retry with the same reservation_id, so there are no duplicates.
The payment provider is slow or down. New payments fail fast with a circuit breaker; holds are kept but the 10-minute timer runs, so many guests lose their hold. A banner asks guests to retry. For payments in doubt, the PAYMENT_UNKNOWN state and the reconciliation job protect the guest and the hotel.
Duplicate or late webhook. A success webhook arrives twice, or after the hold expired. The state machine makes the first idempotent. For the second, the handler tries to re-take the rooms; if it cannot, it starts a refund and notifies the guest. Both outcomes are tested.
The outbox relay stops. Events pile up in the outbox table, search and emails lag, but bookings are correct. An alert watches the age of the oldest unpublished event. When the relay restarts it continues from its last position, and consumers ignore duplicates.
The cache is stale or lost. After a flush, search shows wrong availability until it is rebuilt. We protect the database with request coalescing (one rebuild per key), a warm-up for the top hotels and a fallback to read replicas. The booking transaction never depends on the cache.
evolution: At 1 hotel group with 50 hotels, a single PostgreSQL primary and a monolith serve search and booking. At 5,000 hotels, as designed: a primary with a synchronous standby, read replicas, a Redis availability cache, an outbox and Kafka, and a payment saga. At 500,000 properties (a marketplace), shard by hotel_id, put a dedicated search system in front of the availability summaries, add per-region deployments with the data kept near the property, and accept channel managers: hotels also sell through other sites, so inventory changes arrive from outside and the system must handle conflicts with those feeds. At the largest scale, add dynamic pricing and a waiting room for events, and keep the same core: a short conditional update on an inventory row.
followups: How do you guarantee two guests never get the last room? || Do the availability check and the decrement in one conditional UPDATE inside a transaction on the hotel's shard, check that the number of updated rows equals the number of nights, and keep a CHECK constraint on the counter. The row lock makes the second transaction wait and then fail the condition.
Why not use a distributed lock in Redis around the booking? || A lock outside the database adds a second system that can fail, expire early (a paused process holds a lock that has already expired) or be lost on failover. The database already provides atomic row updates with durable commit, so we use it as the single place where inventory is decided.
How do you make retries safe? || The client creates the reservation_id, which is the primary key. A repeat insert is ignored and the stored result is returned. The same id is the payment provider's idempotency key, and webhook handlers key on the provider event id.
What if the card is charged but the hold expired? || The success handler checks the state. If the reservation is EXPIRED it tries to take the rooms again; if that fails it refunds the card and informs the guest. The compensation is part of the saga and is tested.
How do you keep search fast and still correct? || Search reads an availability summary from a cache and index refreshed from events and with a short time to live, so it may be a minute stale. The booking transaction does the exact check and returns sold-out with alternatives. Never decide a booking from the cache.
How would you shard this system, and what becomes hard? || Shard by hotel_id so a booking is a single-shard ACID transaction. Queries by guest or by city need read models built from outbox events, with a small delay. A booking that spans hotels becomes a saga with compensation.

title: Design a digital wallet
category: Web & product
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: ledger, eventsourcing, exactlyonce, idempotence, raft, twopc, outbox, partitioning, acid, kafka, hotkeys
summary: A digital wallet holds money for its users. People top up from a bank or card, send money to friends, pay merchants and withdraw to a bank. The rules are strict. Money is never created or destroyed, no balance goes below zero, a payment is applied once even if the phone retries three times, and every cent can be traced and reconciled with the bank. The load is large: tens of thousands of transfers per second at peak, and almost every transfer touches two accounts that live on different database shards. The design rests on a double-entry ledger where entries are never changed, a balance that is updated in the same local transaction as its entries, a transfer split into two idempotent legs joined through an in-flight account and an outbox, shards replicated with Raft, and special handling for accounts that receive thousands of credits per second.
requirements: Functional requirements. (1) Create a wallet account per user (one currency per account; we assume one currency for the numbers). (2) Top up from a bank or card. (3) Transfer between wallets (person to person and person to merchant). (4) Withdraw to a bank. (5) Show balance and the list of transactions. (6) Refund and reverse a payment. (7) Daily statements and reconciliation with banks and payment providers. (8) Freeze an account for fraud or compliance.
Non-functional requirements. Correctness: the sum of all entries of every transfer is zero; customer balances never go below zero; no transfer is applied twice; no transfer is lost. Durability: a confirmed transfer survives the loss of a machine or an availability zone (recovery point zero). Latency: p99 under 1 second for a transfer, balance reads under 100 ms. Availability: 99.99 percent for transfers; if we must choose, we refuse a payment rather than apply it wrongly. Auditability: every change is an immutable record, kept for the period the law requires (often 5 to 10 years). Scale: 100,000 transfers per second at peak.
Assumptions. 100 million daily users, 200 million wallets, 5 transfers per user per day. Money is stored as integers in the smallest unit (cents or paise), never as floating point. A few merchants receive thousands of payments per second.
estimates: Transfer rate. 100 million users times 5 = 500 million transfers per day. Divide by 86,400: 5,800 per second on average. On a festival night or at salary time the peak is about 10 times higher: 58,000 per second. We design for 100,000 per second, which leaves 70 percent headroom.
Cross-shard share. With N shards chosen by hash of the account id, the chance that both accounts of a transfer are on the same shard is 1/N. For N = 128 it is 0.8 percent. So 99.2 percent of transfers touch two shards. A design that needs a distributed transaction for every transfer would pay that cost almost every time.
Local transactions. Each transfer is two local transactions (leg 1 on the source shard, leg 2 on the destination shard): 200,000 local transactions per second at peak. One shard primary can do about 3,000 such transactions per second (each updates two balance rows, inserts two entries, one dedup row and one outbox row, with group commit on NVMe). We plan for 60 percent load: 1,800 per second. 200,000 / 1,800 = 111, rounded up to 128 shards. Each shard is a Raft group of 3 replicas in 3 zones: 128 times 3 = 384 database nodes.
Ledger growth. Each transfer writes 4 entries (debit source, credit in-flight account, debit in-flight account, credit destination). 500 million times 4 = 2 billion entries per day. At about 200 bytes per entry with indexes, 400 GB per day, 146 TB per year, 440 TB with three replicas. Per shard: 146 TB / 128 = 1.1 TB per year. We keep 90 days online (about 280 GB per shard) and move older entries to columnar files in an object store, where they stay queryable for audit.
Balance reads. 100 million users check the balance 10 times per day: 1 billion per day, 11,600 per second on average, about 100,000 at peak. They are served from the shard replicas or a short cache, not from the leaders.
Latency budget. Request checks and idempotency lookup 5 ms. Leg 1 commit: the Raft leader appends and fsyncs (about 1 ms), copies to two followers in other zones (round trip 1 to 2 ms), so a commit takes 3 to 5 ms, and the whole transaction 6 to 10 ms. Leg 2 the same. Add the network hops and service time: about 50 to 100 ms in the median, under 500 ms at p99.
Hot account. A large merchant receives 5,000 credits per second. If a balance update holds the row lock for 2 ms, one row can take 500 updates per second. The demand is 10 times the capacity of the row. We come back to this in the deep dive.
api: All calls use HTTPS, signed requests and an idempotency key. Money is {currency: "INR", minor_units: 12500}, an integer.
POST /v1/transfers with header Idempotency-Key and body {from_account_id, to_account_id, amount, reference, note}. Response 201 {transfer_id, status: "COMPLETED" | "PENDING"} or 409 with code "insufficient_funds", "account_frozen" or "duplicate_with_different_body". A repeat with the same key and the same body returns the stored result.
GET /v1/transfers/{transfer_id} returns the state: PENDING, COMPLETED, FAILED or REVERSED.
GET /v1/accounts/{account_id}/balance returns {available, pending, as_of}. GET /v1/accounts/{account_id}/entries?cursor=...&limit=50 returns a page of immutable entries, newest first.
POST /v1/topups with {account_id, source_token, amount} starts a top-up; the wallet is credited only when the bank confirms. POST /v1/withdrawals with {account_id, destination_id, amount} reserves the money and sends it out.
POST /v1/transfers/{transfer_id}:reverse with {reason} creates a new transfer in the opposite direction; the original is never edited.
datamodel: Each shard has the same tables. The shard key is the account id; a transfer record and its first leg live on the source account's shard.
```sql
CREATE TABLE account (
  account_id   BIGINT PRIMARY KEY,
  kind         TEXT NOT NULL,   -- WALLET, MERCHANT, INFLIGHT, FEE, BANK
  currency     CHAR(3) NOT NULL,
  status       TEXT NOT NULL,   -- ACTIVE, FROZEN, CLOSED
  balance      BIGINT NOT NULL, -- minor units
  CHECK (kind <> 'WALLET' OR balance >= 0)
);
CREATE TABLE ledger_entry (
  account_id   BIGINT NOT NULL,
  entry_id     BIGINT NOT NULL,      -- per-account sequence
  transfer_id  UUID NOT NULL,
  leg          SMALLINT NOT NULL,
  amount       BIGINT NOT NULL,      -- signed: debit < 0, credit > 0
  balance_after BIGINT NOT NULL,
  created_at   TIMESTAMPTZ NOT NULL DEFAULT now(),
  PRIMARY KEY (account_id, entry_id)
);
CREATE TABLE applied_leg (
  transfer_id UUID, leg SMALLINT, PRIMARY KEY (transfer_id, leg)
);
CREATE TABLE transfer (
  transfer_id UUID PRIMARY KEY, idem_key TEXT UNIQUE,
  from_account BIGINT, to_account BIGINT, amount BIGINT,
  status TEXT, created_at TIMESTAMPTZ DEFAULT now()
);
CREATE TABLE outbox (
  seq BIGSERIAL PRIMARY KEY, topic TEXT, key TEXT, payload JSONB
);
```
Rules the database enforces: the two entries of one leg add up to zero (checked by the service and by a nightly audit query); a wallet balance cannot be negative (CHECK); one (transfer_id, leg) is applied once (primary key of applied_leg). The balance column is a cache of the sum of entries: balance equals the sum of amount over the account's entries. Entries are never updated or deleted. A mistake is corrected by a new, opposite transfer.
design: The model: a double-entry ledger. Every movement of money is a transfer made of legs, and each leg is a set of entries that add up to zero: whatever is debited from one account is credited to another. Bank accounts and fees are accounts in the same ledger, so nothing appears from nowhere. The ledger is the source of truth. The balance is a projection of it, updated in the same transaction as the entry. Rebuilding balances from entries and comparing is our main audit. This is the event sourcing idea: store facts as an append-only log and derive state from it.
The transfer path. (1) The client calls POST /v1/transfers with an idempotency key. (2) The transfer service finds the shard of the source account and starts a local transaction there: insert the transfer row (a unique idem_key makes a retry return the old row), insert the applied_leg row (transfer_id, 1), debit the source (conditional update, below), credit the in-flight account of this shard, write two entries, and write an outbox row "credit leg due". Commit. The money is now in the in-flight account, and the sum over all accounts is unchanged. (An in-flight account of one shard may be negative; only the total over all of them is the money in transit.) (3) The service calls the destination shard for leg 2: insert applied_leg (transfer_id, 2), debit that shard's in-flight account, credit the destination, write two entries, commit. (4) The service marks the transfer COMPLETED on the source shard and answers the client. If the process dies after step 2, the outbox row ensures leg 2 is still done.
The debit is a conditional update:
```sql
UPDATE account SET balance = balance - :amt
 WHERE account_id = :a AND status = 'ACTIVE' AND balance >= :amt;
-- zero rows updated: insufficient funds or frozen -> abort leg 1
```
The check and the change happen in one atomic statement, as in any ACID transaction. The balance cannot go below zero even if two payments race for the last rupee.
Why two legs and not one distributed transaction. The destination is almost always on another shard. A two-phase commit would lock both rows, ask both shards to prepare (a durable write on each), and then commit both. The cost is two network round trips and two durable writes while locks are held, and a failure of the coordinator between prepare and commit leaves the participants blocked with locks held until it recovers. With 99 percent of transfers crossing shards, contention would rise and hot accounts would suffer. Splitting into two local transactions removes the cross-shard locks. The price is a short window (milliseconds, up to seconds on failure) when the money sits in the in-flight account and the receiver has not yet been credited. The state is explicit and the recovery is mechanical.
Outbox and events. Every state change writes an outbox row in the same transaction. A relay reads outbox rows in order and publishes them to Kafka, keyed by account id so that events of one account stay in order. Consumers are the notification service, the fraud engine, the transaction-history read model (a separate store optimised for "list my transactions"), analytics and the reconciliation job. This is the read side of a CQRS design: writes go to the ledger shards, reads of history go to the read model. A consumer that fails reads again from its offset.
Replication with Raft. Each shard is a Raft group: one leader and two followers in three zones. The leader appends a transaction to its log and replies to the client only after a majority (itself and one follower) has stored it durably. If the leader dies, the followers hold an election (typically a few seconds with the usual timeouts) and a follower with the full log becomes leader. No committed transaction is lost, and no two leaders can both commit, because a commit needs a majority. We use a database that implements Raft per range of data (CockroachDB, YugabyteDB and TiDB are examples) or run PostgreSQL with a consensus-based failover layer. Balance reads for the user's own account right after a transfer go to the leader; other reads may use followers.

deepdive: Exactly-once effect. The network gives at-least-once behaviour: phones retry, the transfer service retries leg calls, the relay re-publishes after a crash, Kafka consumers re-read after a restart. We cannot get exactly-once delivery, but we can get an exactly-once effect by making every operation idempotent. The client sends an idempotency key, stored in a unique column of the transfer table. Each leg inserts (transfer_id, leg) into applied_leg in the same transaction as the balance change: a second attempt hits the primary key, the transaction fails, and the service reads the stored result. Kafka offers idempotent producers and transactions that give exactly-once processing inside Kafka (read, process and write back to Kafka). They do not extend to a database write, so a consumer that updates the history store uses a deduplication key (event id) with an upsert. The rule is the same everywhere: deliver at least once, apply at most once.
Recovery of half-done transfers. A sweeper job looks, on every shard, for transfers in state DEBITED older than 30 seconds, and re-drives leg 2 using the same ids. Because the leg is idempotent this is always safe. If leg 2 cannot succeed, for example the destination account is closed or frozen, the sweeper runs a compensating leg: debit the in-flight account and credit the source back, and sets the transfer to FAILED. A leg is never rolled back by deleting entries; we write opposite entries. Transfers stuck for more than a configured time go to an operations queue.
Hot accounts. Two places have rows that every transaction touches. The first is the in-flight account: if each shard had one, all 1,800 local transactions per second would update the same row, but a row can take only about 500 updates per second (2 ms lock time). We therefore keep 64 in-flight accounts per shard and choose one by hash of the transfer id: 1,800 / 64 = 28 updates per second per row. The second is the large merchant, fee or bank-settlement account with 5,000 credits per second. There are two remedies, and we use both. (1) Sub-accounts: the merchant has 32 buckets; each credit goes to a bucket chosen by hash of the transfer id; the balance shown to the merchant is the sum of the buckets, and a payout drains buckets one by one. Now each row gets 5,000 / 32 = 156 updates per second, about 30 percent of its capacity. (2) Micro-batching of credits: the leg-2 consumer groups credits for the same account that arrive within 100 ms and applies them in one transaction: many entries but one balance update. 5,000 credits per second become 10 transactions per second with 500 entries each. Credits need no balance check, so batching is safe. Debits from the hot account are rare (payouts) and keep the normal path.
Top-ups and withdrawals. Money from outside enters through a BANK account in the ledger. A top-up is a transfer from BANK_SETTLEMENT to the wallet. The wallet is credited only when the bank or card network confirms, or optimistically with a risk limit and a later reversal. A withdrawal first moves money from the wallet to a "withdrawal pending" account (so it cannot be spent), sends the instruction to the bank with an idempotency reference, and on confirmation moves it from pending to BANK_SETTLEMENT. If the bank rejects it, it moves back. Bank files and provider reports arrive hours later and are matched with the ledger by reference.
Reconciliation. Three checks run continuously. (1) For each account, the balance equals the sum of its entries. (2) For each transfer, the sum of all entries over all legs is zero, and every transfer reaches a final state within a time limit. (3) The ledger's total of BANK accounts equals the external statements, matched by reference and amount. Any mismatch is an alert with the transfer ids. Because entries are immutable and the stream is replayable, an investigator can reconstruct the state of any account at any time.
Multi-region. Raft across three zones of one region gives zero data loss for zone failures, but a loss of the whole region would lose the last few seconds if the DR copy is asynchronous. For money, we can place the third replica in a second region 20 to 40 km away (metro distance, about 1 ms), or run five replicas across three regions at the cost of 30 to 80 ms per commit. This is a business decision on recovery point against latency.
tradeoffs: Two-phase commit versus legs with an in-flight account. Choose two-phase commit when both sides must become visible together, the transaction rate per account is low, and the database offers it built in (Spanner-style systems run it over Paxos groups). Choose two local legs with an in-flight account when 99 percent of transfers are cross-shard and the rate is 100,000 per second: no locks held across shards, and a coordinator crash does not block anyone. You give up instant atomic visibility and take on recovery code.
Balance column plus entries versus pure event sourcing. Choose a balance column updated with the entries in one local transaction when you need a fast, strict balance check ("balance at least the amount") and standard SQL tooling. Choose a pure event-sourced state machine (commands go to a Raft log, state is rebuilt by replay, as in some in-house designs) when you want one writer per shard with no row locking, snapshots, and very high throughput per node; you pay with custom code and harder ad hoc queries. Both keep the entries as the truth.
Synchronous leg 2 versus asynchronous through Kafka. Choose a direct call for leg 2 when the user waits for a "payment successful" and the latency is under 100 ms; use the outbox and Kafka only as the safety net. Choose the asynchronous path when you accept a "processing" screen, for example for bulk payouts, because it decouples the shards and smooths peaks.
failures: A shard leader fails. The Raft group elects a new leader in a few seconds. Calls to that shard time out and the transfer service retries with the same ids. Transfers whose leg 1 was committed (and replicated, so it is in the new leader's log) continue; others are retried and applied once. Affected users see a delay, not an error in their balance.
A whole availability zone fails. Each Raft group has one replica per zone, so two of three remain and the groups keep a majority. Capacity falls by a third, which the 40 percent headroom absorbs. Kafka, the services and the read model are also spread over three zones.
Crash between leg 1 and leg 2. The money is in the in-flight account. The outbox row and the sweeper make leg 2 happen. The user sees "pending" for seconds. If the process died before leg 1 committed, nothing happened, and the client retries with the same key.
Duplicate requests and replays. A phone with a bad network sends the same transfer three times. The idem_key makes the second and third calls return the first result. If the same key arrives with a different amount or recipient, we return an error and alert, because it signals a client bug or an attack.
Bank or provider outage. Top-ups and withdrawals stay pending. A circuit breaker rejects new ones with a clear message. Wallet-to-wallet transfers continue because they do not depend on the bank. When the bank returns, the pending operations are re-sent with the same references.
evolution: At 10,000 users, one PostgreSQL primary with a standby holds accounts, entries and the transfer table, and a transfer is one local ACID transaction with two debit and credit statements. At 1 million users, add read replicas for history and the outbox with Kafka for notifications. At 100 million users, shard by account id, split transfers into two idempotent legs with in-flight accounts, run each shard as a Raft group, handle hot accounts with sub-accounts and batching, and build the reconciliation pipeline. At 1 billion users and many currencies and countries, add regional ledgers with one ledger per currency and region, cross-border transfers as legs through a foreign-exchange account, a dedicated settlement system for banks, and a data platform that stores all entries in an open columnar format for audit and analytics.
followups: How do you apply a transfer exactly once when the client, the network and Kafka all retry? || Make every step idempotent. The client sends an idempotency key stored in a unique column; each leg inserts (transfer_id, leg) into a dedup table in the same transaction as the balance change; consumers use event ids. Delivery is at least once, and the effect is once.
Why not use two-phase commit for the cross-shard transfer? || Two-phase commit holds locks on both shards across two round trips and two durable writes, and a coordinator crash leaves participants blocked. At 99 percent cross-shard traffic this raises latency and contention. Two local legs through an in-flight account avoid cross-shard locks, and recovery is a simple re-drive of an idempotent step.
What happens if the process crashes after the debit commits and before the credit? || The money sits in the in-flight account, which keeps the global sum correct. The outbox row and a sweeper re-drive leg 2 with the same ids. If the destination cannot accept the credit, a compensating leg returns the money to the source and the transfer is marked FAILED.
How do you handle a merchant that receives 5,000 payments per second? || A single row can take about 500 updates per second. Spread credits over 32 sub-accounts chosen by hash of the transfer id, and group credits that arrive within 100 ms into one transaction. The merchant sees the sum of the buckets.
How do you know the ledger is correct? || Entries are immutable; the balance must equal the sum of entries; every transfer sums to zero; the bank accounts in the ledger must match external statements. Continuous reconciliation jobs check these and alert on any difference. Errors are fixed with reversing entries.
What do you do about disaster recovery for money? || Within a region, Raft across three zones gives zero data loss for a zone failure. For a region loss, place one replica in a nearby second site, or accept a few seconds of loss with asynchronous replication and rebuild the gap from bank records. The choice trades commit latency against recovery point.
