@@ live-comments
title: Design live comments for a live video stream
category: Web & product
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: realtime, websocket, pubsub, fanout, partitioning, hotkeys, loadshed, cachelayer, kafka, redis, sampling
summary: Live comments are the scrolling text next to a live video: a sports final, a product launch, a creator stream. Viewers post short messages, and everybody who watches the same stream sees them within about one second. The problem is not storage. The problem is the shape of the traffic. One popular stream can have 10 million viewers and 20,000 new comments per second in a goal moment. If every comment were sent to every viewer, the system would have to deliver \(2 \times 10^{11}\) messages per second, which no one can build and no human can read. The key insight of the design is that a screen can show only about 10 comments per second, so the system sends each viewer a small, sampled, batched view of the stream. We design the write path, the fan-out tree to millions of long-lived connections, the late-joiner path, and the protections for the hot stream.
requirements: Functional requirements. A signed-in viewer can post a comment of up to 200 characters to a live stream. Every viewer of that stream sees new comments in near real time, in roughly the same order. A viewer who joins late sees the most recent 50 comments at once and then the live flow. The poster sees his own comment at once. Moderators can delete comments and ban users, and deletions must reach viewers within a few seconds. Comments are stored so that the replay of the stream can show them later. Creators can turn on "slow mode" (one comment per user per N seconds) or "followers only".
Non-functional requirements. Delivery latency: p50 under 1 second and p99 under 3 seconds from post to screen. This is a live conversation, but nobody needs millisecond precision, so we use that slack for batching. Availability: a failure of the comment system must never stop the video. Comments are best effort for viewers, but accepted posts must not be lost silently for the poster, and the stored log must be complete for replay. Ordering: all viewers see comments in roughly the same order (a small difference of a few hundred milliseconds is fine). Scale: the biggest stream has 10 million concurrent viewers, and the platform has 15 million concurrent viewers at peak. Cost: comment traffic must stay a tiny fraction of video traffic.
Assumptions. At peak 2 percent of viewers post one comment in a given minute, and goal moments raise the rate six times. A comment is 100 bytes of text, and with ids, user name and flags about 300 bytes. A screen shows 8 comments per second at most. One gateway machine holds 100,000 open connections, a number that LinkedIn also reported for its live video frontends. The video itself is delivered by a CDN and is outside this design.
estimates: Posting rate. 10 million viewers times 2 percent per minute is 200,000 comments per minute, which is about 3,300 per second on average. A goal moment is 6 times higher: 3,300 times 6 is about 20,000 per second. The write side is small: 20,000 times 300 bytes is 6 MB per second, so one Kafka topic with a few partitions carries it.
Naive fan-out. If each comment went to each viewer, the system would send 20,000 times 10 million, which is \(2 \times 10^{11}\) messages per second. Even at 100 bytes this is 20 TB per second, which is 160 Tbit per second, more than the video itself. This is the number that kills the naive design.
Sampled fan-out. A viewer cannot read 20,000 comments per second. We cap the view at 8 comments per second per viewer, and we send them as one frame per second per connection. A frame has 8 comments of about 150 bytes (text, short user name, id) which is 1.2 KB, or about 0.4 KB after compression. Total egress for the top stream is 10 million times 0.4 KB = 4 GB per second = 32 Gbit per second. The video for the same viewers at 3 Mbit per second is 30 Tbit per second, so comments are about 0.1 percent of the video bytes.
Gateways. 15 million connections divided by 100,000 per machine is 150. We want about 70 percent utilization so that a zone loss does not break the system: 15 million divided by 70,000 is about 215. We plan 220 gateway machines in 3 zones. Per machine the load is 70,000 frames per second at 0.4 KB, which is 28 MB per second, or 0.22 Gbit per second. The CPU cost is TLS encryption and one write per frame, which is easy. Memory is 10 to 20 KB per connection for buffers and TLS state: 70,000 times 15 KB is about 1 GB.
Internal fan-out. A stream is published to every gateway that has at least one viewer of it. In the worst case that is all 220 gateways. Aggregators publish one batch every 250 ms, so the internal traffic is 4 batches per second times 220 gateways = 880 deliveries per second. Compare this with 20,000 comments per second times 220 gateways = 4.4 million deliveries if each comment were published alone. Batching cuts internal traffic by 5,000 times.
Join storm. A big match starts and 10 million viewers join in 10 minutes. That is 17,000 joins per second on average, and the last minutes are 5 times higher: about 83,000 per second. Each join needs one TLS handshake (about 0.5 ms of CPU) and one fetch of the recent comments. The handshakes need 83,000 times 0.5 ms = 41 CPU cores in total, spread over 220 machines. The recent-comments fetch must come from a cache, not from a database.
api: Post a comment: POST /v1/streams/{stream_id}/comments with body {client_id, text}. The client_id is a random id made on the device. The server returns 202 Accepted with {comment_id, seq} or an error: 401 (not signed in), 403 (banned or followers-only), 429 (slow mode or rate limit, with Retry-After), 503 (shedding). The call is idempotent on client_id, so a retry does not create a second comment.
Subscribe: GET /v1/streams/{stream_id}/live with header Accept: text/event-stream (Server-Sent Events). The server keeps the connection open and sends events: "comments" (a batch with an array of comments and a cursor), "delete" (ids to remove), "meta" (viewer count, slow mode setting) and "ping". On reconnect the browser sends Last-Event-ID with the last cursor. Mobile clients may use a WebSocket instead with the same event types.
Recent comments: GET /v1/streams/{stream_id}/comments?limit=50&before=cursor returns the newest comments and a cursor. It is served from the caching layer with a short TTL of 1 second and can be put behind the CDN.
datamodel: Durable store (wide-column, for example Cassandra or DynamoDB). Table comments: partition key (stream_id, time_bucket), clustering key (seq). The time bucket is 1 minute of stream time. Columns: comment_id, user_id, display_name, text, created_at_ms, flags (deleted, pinned, from_mod). Why this key: a replay reads a time range of one stream, which is a few partitions. A bucket keeps one partition from growing without limit. For a hot stream the key becomes (stream_id, bucket, shard) with 4 to 8 shards, written round-robin and merged by created_at on read.
Kafka log. Topic comments.raw, key = stream_id, 64 partitions in total. The key keeps all comments of one stream in order inside one partition. A hot stream is split: key = stream_id plus a number from 0 to 7 chosen by the producer, so it spreads over 8 partitions, and the aggregator merges them by sequence time. Topic comments.moderation carries deletes and bans.
Redis. Key live:{stream_id}:recent is a capped list or stream of the last 200 comments (about 60 KB). Key live:{stream_id}:meta is a hash with slow mode, followers-only flag, viewer count and the current sampling rate. Key ban:{stream_id} is a set of user ids with TTL. Key slow:{stream_id}:{user_id} is a string with TTL equal to the slow-mode period, used with SET NX to allow one comment per period. Pub/sub channel stream:{stream_id} carries batches from aggregators to gateways.
design: The write path. The viewer's app sends POST to the comment API behind a load balancer. The service checks the session, then runs cheap checks in this order: ban set, followers-only, slow-mode key (SET NX with TTL in Redis), and a per-user rate limit. Then it runs the fast text filter (blocked words, links, repeated text), which takes under 1 ms. A comment that passes is written to Kafka with acks=all, and the client gets 202. Expensive checks, for example a machine-learning toxicity model, run later in a moderation consumer, and a deletion event is sent if the comment is bad. The poster's app shows his own comment immediately (local echo) without waiting for the stream.
The aggregator. One aggregator task per live stream reads the stream's partitions of comments.raw. Every 250 ms it forms a batch: it takes the new comments, applies the sampling policy, and publishes one message with the selected comments to the channel stream:{stream_id}. It also writes the selected and the unselected comments to the durable store (the full log is kept, only the live view is sampled), and pushes the selected ones into the Redis recent list. Aggregator tasks are spread over a pool of workers by partitioning on stream_id with a lease, so a crashed worker's streams move to other workers in seconds.
The gateway tier. Each gateway holds the long-lived connections. When the first viewer of a stream connects, the gateway subscribes to the stream's channel in Redis pub/sub (or Redis sharded pub/sub, available since Redis 7.0), and when the last viewer leaves it unsubscribes. When a batch arrives, the gateway encodes the frame once, compresses it once, and writes the same bytes to each of its connections for that stream. This is the second level of the fan-out tree: one aggregator, up to 220 gateways, about 70,000 connections each. The tree has only two levels, which keeps latency low and the failure of a node easy to understand.
Late joiners. A new viewer calls GET recent comments, which reads live:{stream_id}:recent from the caching layer. The API server keeps a copy of the 50 newest comments in process memory for 1 second, so 17,000 joins per second become a few reads per second against Redis per API server. Then the client opens the live connection with the cursor of the last comment it has, and the gateway sends only newer batches.

deepdive: Sampling is the core idea. A human reads about 3 to 5 lines per second with effort. A product shows up to 8. When the incoming rate \(r_{in}\) is below the budget \(r_{out}\), everything is shown. Above it, the aggregator keeps each comment with probability \(p = r_{out}/r_{in}\), so every comment has the same chance and the view stays honest. To make it useful, the policy first takes "must show" items: comments from the creator, from moderators, pinned comments, and the sender's own comments (these are done on the client). Then it fills the remaining slots with a weighted random choice: a small boost for longer text and for users with a good history, a penalty for repeated text. The view must still look like a conversation, so the policy avoids showing 8 copies of "GOAL!!!" by collapsing repeats into one line with a count. The full log is kept, so a sampling bug never loses data. Per-viewer personalization is possible (show my friends first) but costs memory and CPU per connection, so we do it only on the client: a gateway sends 12 comments instead of 8 and the client picks which 8 to play.
Ordering. Perfect global order is not needed, but two viewers should not see a reply before the original. The Kafka partition gives a total order within one partition, and each comment gets a sequence number from the aggregator, so every viewer receives batches in sequence order. A hot stream uses 8 partitions, so the aggregator merges them by the producer timestamp inside a small reorder window of 250 ms, and the sequence number is assigned after the merge. This costs 250 ms of latency and gives the same order for all viewers. Clock skew between API servers is a few milliseconds with NTP and does not matter at this scale.
The hot stream is a hot key. Most streams have 50 viewers, and one stream has 10 million. Hashing by stream_id sends the whole top stream to one Kafka partition and one aggregator. We handle it in four ways. First, write sharding: the producer adds a number 0 to 7 to the key. Second, the aggregator for a hot stream can run as a small group, where each member samples a part of the partitions and one leader merges the output. Third, the Redis channel carries only batches (4 per second), not comments, so one Redis shard is never overloaded. Fourth, the join storm hits the caching layer, which holds a 1-second copy, so the hot stream is read from memory in each API server. A hot key is found by counting: the aggregator reports viewer counts and comment rates for each stream, and above a threshold the stream switches to the "big stream" mode with more partitions and a fan-out tree of 3 levels (aggregator, regional relays, gateways).
Connections and delivery. Server-Sent Events fit well because traffic is one-way: the server pushes comments, and the client posts with normal HTTP. SSE gives automatic reconnect and Last-Event-ID resume. It uses plain HTTP, so it passes proxies and works with the same load balancers and authentication as other calls. WebSocket is better for mobile apps that already keep a socket for chat and for polls. Over HTTP/2 each SSE stream is one stream of a multiplexed connection, but browsers on HTTP/1.1 allow only 6 connections per host, which a page must not waste. Delivery is at-most-once for the live view and nothing is queued per connection: if a client is too slow, the gateway drops frames for that client instead of queueing them (the cursor lets the client ask for a gap fill later). This keeps memory fixed. LinkedIn described the same trade: the live path favors speed over a delivery guarantee.
Reconnect storms. If a gateway dies, its 70,000 viewers reconnect at the same time. Clients wait a random time between 0 and 5 seconds with exponential backoff and jitter, and the load balancer sends them to the least-loaded gateway. Each reconnect carries a cursor, and the gateway replays the batches it has in the ring buffer, or tells the client to fetch the gap from the recent list. We also limit new connections per second per gateway, so the surviving gateways do not melt while taking the traffic.
tradeoffs: SSE versus WebSocket. Choose SSE when the traffic is mainly server to client, when you want plain HTTP, easy proxy support and automatic resume. Choose WebSocket when clients also send a lot (typing, reactions, bidirectional chat) or when you need binary frames and lower per-message overhead. You give up simple infrastructure with WebSocket (sticky routing, custom ping, no automatic resume), and you give up bidirectional use with SSE.
Sampling versus sending everything. Choose sampling when the stream is bigger than about 10 comments per second, because a human cannot read more and the fan-out cost grows with viewers times rate. Choose full delivery for small streams (under 8 comments per second) and for channels where every message matters, such as a Q&A session, a paid chat or a moderator channel. Sampling gives up completeness in the live view, so the full log must always be stored, and the replay should show all comments.
Redis pub/sub versus Kafka for the fan-out leg. Choose Redis pub/sub (or a custom relay) between the aggregator and the gateways when you want low latency, fire-and-forget delivery and many dynamic subscribers: gateways come and go and subscribe per stream. Choose Kafka for the leg before the aggregator and for any consumer that needs replay, because a log gives order, durability and re-reading. Kafka is a poor fit for the gateway leg: a consumer group per gateway and thousands of topics or partitions add cost and rebalance delays, and the live view does not need durability. With pub/sub you give up delivery guarantees, so the cursor and gap fill on the client cover the loss.
Push to all versus pull. Choose push over long-lived connections when latency under 2 seconds matters and the same data goes to many viewers. Choose polling with a CDN-cached response every 2 to 3 seconds when the audience is huge, the cost of connections is too high, and a latency of 3 to 6 seconds is acceptable. Polling at 10 million viewers each 3 seconds is 3.3 million requests per second, which a CDN can serve from cache because everyone asks for the same URL. You give up latency and smoothness, and you gain simple operations and the use of existing CDN capacity.
failures: A gateway fails. Its connections drop. Clients reconnect to other gateways with their cursor, with jitter. The stream's channel is still subscribed by the other gateways, so nothing else changes. Viewers lose at most a few seconds.
Redis pub/sub node fails. Gateways notice the broken subscription and re-subscribe on the replica or the new primary. During the gap, batches are lost (pub/sub does not store messages). The cursor in the next frame shows the gap, and the client calls the recent list to fill it. The cost is a visible pause of a few seconds in the comment flow, and the video continues.
Aggregator crash. Its lease expires after 5 seconds, and another worker takes the stream. It starts from the last committed Kafka offset and the last sequence number kept in Redis. Comments are not lost because they are in Kafka. Some batches may be published twice, and gateways ignore batches with a sequence number they have already sent.
Kafka unavailable or slow. The comment API cannot write, so it returns 503 with Retry-After for posts, and the video and the live view of existing comments continue. We do not buffer posts for long in the API, because a comment that arrives 2 minutes late has no value in a live conversation.
Overload and load shedding. When the system is stressed, we shed in this order: first lower the number of comments per frame from 8 to 4, then increase the frame period from 1 to 2 seconds, then stop sending personalized extras and viewer counts, then reject new posts from low-trust accounts, then new posts from everybody. The last step keeps the connections alive and the video watchable. Gateways also drop frames for slow clients and refuse new connections above a limit, which protects the people who are already connected.
evolution: At 1,000 viewers, one server with WebSocket connections and an in-memory room is enough: it keeps a list of sockets per stream and writes every comment to each. At 100,000 viewers, add a Redis pub/sub channel and a few gateways, and send comments in batches. At 1 million, add Kafka for the write log, the aggregator, sampling and the recent-comments cache. At 10 million and more, add the hot-stream mode with write sharding, a 3-level fan-out tree with regional relays, per-region gateways so that the batch crosses an ocean once per region, and a degradation plan that is tested in drills before big events. At 100 million concurrent viewers across a few streams the only change is more regions and relays, because the design already cuts the work from viewers times comments to viewers times a fixed small budget.
followups: Why do you not send every comment to every viewer? || The cost is viewers times comment rate, here \(2 \times 10^{11}\) messages per second, and a person can read only a few lines per second. We sample to a fixed budget per viewer, so the cost grows only with the number of viewers. The full log is stored for replay and moderation.
How do you keep the order the same for everyone? || Kafka orders comments within one partition, and the aggregator assigns a sequence number after merging partitions of a hot stream with a small reorder window. All gateways forward batches in sequence order. We accept a few hundred milliseconds of difference in arrival time.
What happens when a gateway holding 70,000 connections crashes? || Clients reconnect with exponential backoff and jitter to other gateways and send their cursor. The gateway sends missed batches from a ring buffer or the client fetches the gap from the recent list. We limit new connections per second per gateway and keep 30 percent headroom so the survivors can take the load.
How do you handle the 17,000 to 83,000 joins per second when a match starts? || Joiners read the recent 50 comments from a 1-second cache in each API server, backed by Redis, so the database sees almost nothing. TLS handshake cost is spread over many gateways, and we can stagger the start of the live connection with a random delay. The CDN can cache the recent-comments response too.
Why SSE and not WebSocket, and when would you switch? || Traffic is one-way, SSE is plain HTTP with automatic reconnect and Last-Event-ID resume, and it works with common proxies. Switch to WebSocket when clients send many events (reactions, typing, polls) or for a mobile app that already keeps a socket.
How do you delete a toxic comment that is already on 10 million screens? || The moderation event travels the same path as comments but bypasses sampling, as a "delete" frame with the comment id. Clients remove it within a second or two. The row in the store is flagged so replay and late joiners do not show it, and the recent cache is updated first.

@@ flash-sale
title: Design a flash sale
category: Web & product
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: hotkeys, loadshed, ratelimit, cachelayer, occ, ccontrol, mqueue, idempotence, cdn, queueing, redis
summary: A flash sale sells a small, fixed stock (say 10,000 phones at half price) that starts at an exact time and sells out in seconds. Two million people wait for the start. At 12:00:00 they all click "Buy" in the same second. All the traffic goes to one product and one stock number, so it is the hardest kind of hot key: one counter that two million people want to change. Three promises must hold under this storm. We never sell more than we have (no overselling). We do not sell the same unit twice because of a retry or a double click. And the rest of the shop stays up. The design uses the CDN for everything that is static, a virtual waiting room to admit only the load the back end can take, an atomic split counter in Redis as the fast gate, a message queue to create orders at a calm pace, and a database as the final truth.
requirements: Functional requirements. A shopper sees a product page with a countdown. At the start time the Buy button becomes active. A shopper can buy at most 2 units of the item. Buying means: reserve the units, then pay within 10 minutes; if the payment fails or times out, the units go back to stock and can be sold again. The shopper sees a clear answer within a few seconds: "You got it, pay now", "You are in line, position N", or "Sold out". The operator can load stock, schedule the start, pause the sale and see live numbers.
Non-functional requirements. Correctness first: the number of confirmed orders never exceeds the stock, even with crashes and retries. Fairness: a person on a slow phone connection should have a fair chance, and bots should not take the stock; we prefer a random order among the people who arrive in the first minute over "fastest connection wins". Availability: the rest of the site (search, carts of other products) must work during the sale. Latency: the buy call answers in under 300 ms at p99 once admitted. Scale: 2 million shoppers at the start, 10,000 units, sold out in under 30 seconds.
Assumptions. Each waiting shopper has the page open and the app calls our API about once per second until the sale starts, then keeps trying. The sale page is small, 100 KB in total. The shop has a normal payment provider, a relational database for orders and a Redis cluster. A commercial waiting-room product could be used, but we design our own to show the mechanism.
estimates: Static traffic. 2 million shoppers load a 100 KB page: 2 million times 100 KB = 200 GB. If most of them load it in the last 60 seconds, the average rate is 200 GB divided by 60 = 3.3 GB per second = 27 Gbit per second. A normal origin cannot do this, a CDN can, because the page is the same for everyone. With a 500 KB page it would be 5 times more, so we keep the page small and cache it at the edge.
Naive buy traffic. 2 million shoppers each press Buy about once per second in the first 10 seconds: 2 million requests per second. One Redis primary runs about 100,000 simple commands per second, so this needs at least 20 primaries even without headroom, and the app servers need 2 million divided by about 2,000 requests per second per server = 1,000 servers. All of it is needed for 10,000 units. This is the waste we remove.
Admission control. The back end only needs to process enough people to sell out. Say a third of the people who get through will actually pay, so we need about 30,000 buyers through the gate. We admit 5,000 shoppers per second: the 30,000 are done in 6 seconds, and the rest are told the sale is over as soon as the stock is 0. The buy path sees 5,000 requests per second, not 2 million: 400 times less.
Little's law. In-flight requests L = arrival rate times time in the system. With 5,000 requests per second and 50 ms each, L = 5,000 times 0.05 = 250 requests in flight. Eight app servers with 64 worker threads each hold 512, so the buy path has about 2 times headroom. Without admission control, 2 million per second at the same 50 ms would need 100,000 in flight; the servers would queue, latency would grow, clients would retry and the arrival rate would grow even more.
Waiting-room checks. Every waiting client polls its status once every 5 seconds with a signed token. 2 million divided by 5 = 400,000 checks per second. This is verified at the edge by checking a signature (an HMAC takes about 5 to 20 microseconds), with no database read. 400,000 times 20 microseconds = 8 CPU cores of work. We add a 50 percent margin.
Orders. 10,000 units, at most 2 per person, so at most 10,000 reservations. If they are all created in 30 seconds the order service sees 333 writes per second. This is easy for one database, even with the unlucky case that all shoppers pay in the same minute.
api: Product page: GET /sale/{sale_id} returns static HTML and JSON {product, price, starts_at, server_now}. It is cached at the CDN. The client uses server_now to compute its clock offset, so the countdown is correct even when the phone clock is wrong.
Join the queue: POST /sale/{sale_id}/queue returns {ticket, position_hint, poll_after_ms}. The ticket is a signed token with the random rank. A second call by the same user returns the same ticket.
Poll status: GET /sale/{sale_id}/queue/status with the ticket returns {state: waiting | admitted | sold_out, eta_s, purchase_token}. An admitted shopper gets a purchase_token valid for 5 minutes.
Reserve: POST /sale/{sale_id}/reserve with headers Authorization, Purchase-Token and Idempotency-Key, body {qty}. It returns 201 {reservation_id, expires_at} or 409 {reason: sold_out | limit_reached}. The same Idempotency-Key always returns the same answer.
Pay: POST /orders/{reservation_id}/pay with {payment_method} confirms the order. Admin: POST /admin/sales (create), PUT /admin/sales/{id}/stock, POST /admin/sales/{id}/pause.
datamodel: Relational database (source of truth).
```sql
CREATE TABLE sale_stock (
  sale_id     BIGINT PRIMARY KEY,
  total       INT NOT NULL,
  sold        INT NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,   -- confirmed + held
  CHECK (sold <= total)
);
CREATE TABLE reservation (
  reservation_id BIGINT PRIMARY KEY,
  sale_id        BIGINT NOT NULL,
  user_id        BIGINT NOT NULL,
  qty            INT NOT NULL,
  status         TEXT NOT NULL,         -- held, paid, expired
  idem_key       TEXT NOT NULL,
  expires_at     TIMESTAMP NOT NULL,
  UNIQUE (sale_id, user_id),
  UNIQUE (idem_key)
);
```
The CHECK constraint is the last guard: even if all the code above is wrong, the database refuses an update that would make sold larger than total. The unique key on (sale_id, user_id) is the one-per-person rule and the unique idem_key makes a retried request return the first result. Index on (status, expires_at) lets the sweeper find expired holds.
Redis (fast gate). The stock of one sale is split into K buckets: keys stock:{sale_id}:{b} for b = 0 to K-1, each an integer. With K = 20 and 10,000 units each bucket starts at 500. Key bought:{sale_id} is a set (or per-user keys with TTL) of user ids that already got units. Key soldout:{sale_id} is a flag. Key queue:{sale_id} is a sorted set of tickets scored by their random rank. The buckets live on different Redis shards, because a single key always lives on one shard.
Message queue. Topic reservations, partitioned by sale_id and user_id. Messages: {reservation_id, user_id, qty, idem_key}. The consumer inserts rows in the database.
design: Before the sale. The product page, images and scripts are published to the CDN with a long cache time. Only the sale-start time and the server time are dynamic, and the client fetches a tiny JSON that is also cached for 1 second. This caching layer has one rule: the page has no user data and no stock count, so every shopper gets the same bytes and the origin sees about one request per second per edge location. The operator loads the stock into the database (sale_stock.total = 10,000) and then into Redis as 20 buckets of 500. A few minutes before the start, shoppers who open the page join the waiting room.
The waiting room. When a shopper joins, the queue service gives him a random rank. If you arrive in the 60 seconds before and after the start, you get a random number, not the order of arrival. People who come earlier or a little later all have the same chance. This removes the race of milliseconds that a bot or a fast connection would win. The ticket is a signed token with the sale id, the user id and the rank. At the start time, an admission worker opens the gate at a rate of 5,000 per second: it takes the next ranks and marks them admitted. Clients poll the status endpoint, which is verified at the edge and answered from a small cached value (the current admission watermark, for example "ranks up to 83,000 are in"). A client with rank below the watermark gets a purchase token; the rest see their approximate position.
The buy path. An admitted shopper calls reserve. The API checks the purchase token (a signature, no database call), the rate limit for the user, and the idempotency key. Then it runs a Lua script in Redis that does three things atomically: it checks that the user is not in bought:{sale_id}, picks a bucket, and decrements it if it is greater than 0. If the bucket is empty it tries the next buckets in a random order until it has tried them all; if all are empty it sets the soldout flag and returns "sold out". If the decrement succeeds it adds the user to the bought set and returns the bucket. Because Redis runs one script at a time on a shard, the check and the decrement cannot interleave. Redis is the fast gate and decides in under 1 ms.
Creating the order. After a successful decrement the API writes a message to the queue and returns 201 with the reservation id and a 10-minute expiry. The consumer reads the queue and inserts the reservation in the database in a single transaction that also does UPDATE sale_stock SET sold = sold + qty. Consumers batch up to 50 messages into one transaction, so the single hot row in sale_stock is updated 200 times per second rather than 10,000. If the database check fails (it should never fail), the reservation is cancelled and the shopper is told.
Payment and expiry. The shopper pays. The payment service calls the provider with an idempotency key, and on success marks the reservation paid. A sweeper job looks for held reservations past expires_at, marks them expired, subtracts from sold and returns the units to a Redis bucket. The returned units are offered to the people still in the waiting room.
Sold out. When the soldout flag is set, API servers keep a copy in memory for 1 second, and the CDN serves a "sold out" version of the page. From this moment almost no request reaches Redis. The waiting room tells all clients "sold out" on their next poll and closes the queue.

deepdive: The hot key and the split counter. One product is one counter, and one counter lives on one Redis shard with one thread. Even 100,000 decrements per second is not enough for 2 million attempts. The fix is to split the counter into K buckets on K shards, so the attempts are spread randomly. Each bucket is a small independent stock. A buyer picks a random bucket, and if it is empty tries another. This is the same trick as sharded counters in other systems. The cost is the end game: when the stock is nearly gone, many buckets are empty and a buyer may need several tries, so the last units are sold slower, and a unit that is "in a bucket whose shard is down" cannot be sold until the shard returns. For the last 5 percent of the stock we can merge the buckets into one, since by then the admission gate has cut the traffic. Another way to avoid the end game is to pre-assign each user a bucket by user id hash and let him try only that one, but then some buckets sell out before others and shoppers see "sold out" while stock still exists elsewhere.
Concurrency control: why not the database row lock or OCC? The simple way is SELECT stock FROM item FOR UPDATE, then UPDATE. This is pessimistic concurrency control with two-phase locking: the first transaction holds the row lock, and all others wait in line. One row can be updated roughly 500 to 2,000 times per second, depending on the disk and the lock handling. The waiting transactions hold connections, the pool is exhausted, and the whole database stalls. The optimistic way reads a version number and runs UPDATE ... WHERE version = v. With 5,000 concurrent buyers on one row, only one write wins per round and 4,999 fail and retry, so the work is quadratic and the retries add load. Optimistic control wins when conflicts are rare, and here every attempt conflicts. The better tool is a commutative operation: "subtract 1 if greater than 0" is an atomic conditional decrement that never needs a read first. We use it in Redis (fast, in memory) and in the database in batches (the constraint is the guard). The idea is to move the contention to the cheapest place and make the operation one step.
Admission control and queueing theory. A server is a queue. When the arrival rate approaches the service rate, the delay grows without limit. For a simple M/M/1 queue the mean time in the system is \( W = \frac{1}{\mu - \lambda} \), where \( \mu \) is the service rate and \( \lambda \) the arrival rate. At 90 percent utilization the delay is 10 times the service time, at 99 percent it is 100 times. Worse, the clients that wait time out and retry, so \( \lambda \) rises: a collapse. Load shedding says: when we are at capacity, reject early and cheaply, so that the accepted work still finishes quickly. The waiting room is a polite form of this: we keep the arrival rate at the buy path at 5,000 per second, 60 percent of what it can do, and the other people are told to wait with a progress hint. Rejecting at the edge costs microseconds, and processing a request until it fails deep in the back end costs milliseconds.
Idempotence. Mobile networks fail, users double-click and clients retry. Every state-changing call carries an Idempotency-Key. The API stores the key and the response in Redis (SET NX, TTL 24 hours) and in the database unique index. A repeated request returns the stored response and does not touch the stock. The payment call to the provider uses the reservation id as its own key, so a retry cannot charge twice. The consumer that reads the queue is also idempotent: it uses the reservation id as the primary key, and an insert that finds an existing row is ignored. The queue delivers at least once, so this is needed.
Keeping Redis and the database in agreement. Redis is a fast opinion; the database is the truth. Rule: Redis may only be more strict than the database, never looser. At load time the database total is the maximum. If Redis loses a shard (the bucket values vanish), we rebuild the bucket as \( \text{total} - \text{reservations held or paid} \) divided by the number of buckets, but only after the consumer has drained the queue, so that no reservation is missing. The alternative is to rebuild to zero and sell the unsold units in a second round. It is better to sell too few than too many. After the sale, a reconciliation job compares Redis, the queue and the database, and fixes the differences.
tradeoffs: Waiting room versus plain rate limiting. Choose a waiting room when the demand is 100 times the capacity, when you want to give the user an honest position and when fairness matters. Choose a plain rate limit (a token bucket per user and a global limit that returns 429) when the spike is small, only 2 to 5 times the capacity, and a few failed clicks are acceptable. A waiting room costs engineering work and adds one more state machine to run, and the plain limiter gives users a dice roll with retries that make the spike worse.
Redis counter versus database stock. Choose Redis (or another in-memory store) for the decision when you need tens of thousands of conditional decrements per second on a few keys. Choose the database alone when you sell fewer than 500 units per second in total, since one transaction with a conditional UPDATE and a CHECK constraint is simpler and fully durable. Redis adds a second source of state, which can differ from the database, and a replica failover can lose the last writes; the database adds latency and a hard ceiling for one row.
Reserve then pay versus pay then confirm. Choose reserve-then-pay (a hold for 10 minutes) when payment can take a minute, because the shopper does not lose the item while typing his card. You give up stock to people who may not pay; the sweeper returns it after the timeout and the sale feels as if it is "not sold out" for some minutes. Choose pay-first (charge, then confirm stock) when units are very scarce and cheap to resell, or when holds are abused; you give up the experience and you risk charging people for stock that is gone, which needs refunds.
Random lottery versus first-come first-served. Choose a random window or lottery when bots are a problem and you want a fair chance for everyone. Choose first-come first-served when the customer expects it (tickets with a published start time) and you have strong bot defense. FCFS rewards the best connection and the best script. A lottery rewards patience but needs registration and a longer process.
failures: A Redis shard fails. The buckets on it vanish, and its replica may have stale values. Because Redis is only allowed to be stricter, we promote the replica and lower its buckets to the safe value from the database, or take the shard out and let the other buckets serve. We may sell a bit less. We never sell more, since the database constraint is the last guard.
The database is down. The gate and the queue keep accepting reservations for a while, but the shopper cannot pay because the order cannot be confirmed. We pause the sale if the lag passes a limit, and show "we will be back in a minute" while the queue holds reservations. After recovery the consumer replays them.
The payment provider is slow or down. Holds stay in place until they expire. The sweeper does not release a hold when a payment attempt is in progress (a payment state "pending" extends the hold by 5 minutes). If the provider is down, we pause the sale, because selling that cannot be paid for is worse than waiting.
Overload anyway. If the admission worker admits too much (a bug), the buy API sheds with 503 and Retry-After, based on the number of in-flight requests (an adaptive concurrency limit). The edge answers 429 for requests without a valid purchase token. Because the user sees a message, not a timeout, he does not hammer.
evolution: At 1,000 shoppers, a single web server and a transaction with SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on the stock row is enough. At 100,000 shoppers, put the page on a CDN, add a conditional UPDATE with a CHECK constraint and a rate limit, and queue order creation. At 2 million shoppers, add the waiting room, the split Redis counter, the idempotency keys and the separate deployment for hero sales. At 20 million and more, the edge does more: it holds the queue and the admission logic in edge workers, the stock is split by region (each region sells its own part of the stock), and a global rebalancer moves unsold units between regions in the last seconds. The principle stays the same: cut the load before it reaches the one thing that cannot scale.
followups: How do you avoid selling more than you have? || Three layers. The Redis Lua script does an atomic check-and-decrement per bucket, so it never goes below zero. The database has a CHECK (sold <= total) constraint and a conditional update. Redis is allowed to be stricter than the database but never looser, and a reconciliation job compares both after the sale.
Why not just use SELECT FOR UPDATE on the stock row? || It serializes all buyers on one row lock at maybe 500 to 2,000 updates per second, and waiting transactions hold connections, so the pool is exhausted. A conditional decrement in memory handles 100,000 per second per shard, and splitting the counter into buckets multiplies that.
What happens if a user clicks Buy twice or the network retries? || Every reserve call carries an Idempotency-Key. The server stores the first response under that key and returns it for repeats without touching the stock. The queue consumer is idempotent too, because the reservation id is the primary key, and payment uses the reservation id as its key.
How do you make the sale fair against bots? || Give a random rank to everyone who joins in a time window instead of first-come first-served, require sign-in and one ticket per account, challenge suspicious clients at join time, rate limit by user, IP and device, and enforce one-per-person in the database. A pre-registered lottery is the extreme version.
How do you handle the last 100 units when many buckets are empty? || Buyers may need to scan several buckets, which costs extra Redis calls. By then the waiting room has cut the traffic, so merging the remaining units into one or two buckets or scanning all buckets in one script is cheap. The alternative is to keep a small reserve bucket for the end.

@@ online-auction
title: Design an online auction like eBay
category: Web & product
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: occ, ccontrol, isolation, realtime, websocket, idempotence, outbox, durableexec, auction, ntp, mqueue
summary: An online auction lets a seller list an item for some days, lets buyers place bids, and sells the item to the highest bidder when the clock runs out. It looks simple, but it has three hard parts. First, all bids for one item must be applied one after another, with exact rules (proxy bidding, bid steps, a hidden maximum), even when 200 people bid in the last 10 seconds. Second, time is part of the correctness: a bid at 12:00:00.9 is valid and a bid at 12:00:01.1 is not, and the answer must be the same for everybody. Third, the end of an auction triggers money and notifications, which must happen exactly once even if a server dies in the middle. We design the bid engine with one owner per item and optimistic concurrency, the push of price changes to watchers, and a durable workflow that closes each auction.
requirements: Functional requirements. A seller lists an item with a title, photos, a start price, an optional hidden reserve price and an end time (1 to 10 days). A buyer places a bid by giving the maximum he is willing to pay. The system then bids for him automatically (proxy bidding) in fixed steps, so that he leads with the lowest price that keeps him ahead. The current price is public; maximum bids are secret. A buyer is told at once if he is outbid. Watchers see the price change live. When the auction ends, the highest bidder wins if the reserve is met, an order is created, and both sides are notified. Sellers can end early only if there are no bids. Bids cannot be retracted after the last 12 hours (a simple rule to avoid fraud).
Non-functional requirements. Correctness: no lost bids, no two winners, no price that goes down, and a result that depends only on the order of accepted bids. Fairness in time: the end time is decided by one authoritative clock. Latency: a bid is accepted or rejected within 300 ms at p99, and watchers see the new price within 1 to 2 seconds. Availability: browsing never depends on the bid engine. Bidding for one item can pause for a few seconds during a failover but must not give wrong answers. Durability: an accepted bid survives any single machine or zone loss.
Assumptions. 30 million auction items are live. Each lasts 7 days on average. Most items get zero to two bids, and popular items get hundreds. Half of all bids arrive in the last 5 minutes of an auction. A hot item (a rare collectible) can get 200 bids per second and 100,000 watchers in its last 10 seconds.
estimates: Auctions per day. 30 million live items divided by 7 days of average life is about 4.3 million new listings and 4.3 million endings per day, which is 50 per second on average.
Bids. Assume 3 bids per ended item on average, including the many with none: 4.3 million times 3 = 13 million bids per day, about 150 per second. Half arrive in the last 5 minutes: 6.5 million bids. Auctions end in the evening more than at night, so the peak hour has about 3 times the average. The system peak is then about 150 times 3 times 2 (extra for the last-minute clustering) = 900 per second. We design for 5,000 bids per second globally to have more than 5 times headroom. A single item is a different problem: a hot item takes 200 per second, which one machine handles easily if bids are processed one by one in memory, and costs 5 ms of database time each if it must be done through a lock on one row.
Per-bid cost. A bid reads and writes one state row and inserts one bid row and one outbox row: 3 writes in one transaction, about 3 to 5 ms in a database with synchronous replication in the same region. One database primary does 2,000 to 5,000 such transactions per second. With 5,000 bids per second we shard the item state by item_id over 8 shards, and each shard sees about 600 per second.
Watchers. Each item page has a live connection while open. Assume 2 million concurrent viewers across the site, each on an item page. One gateway holds 100,000 connections, so 20 gateways, or 30 with headroom. For a hot item with 100,000 watchers and 2 price updates per second after coalescing, the push is 200,000 messages per second of 100 bytes = 20 MB per second, which is shared by about 3 gateways holding these watchers.
api: Place a bid: POST /v1/items/{item_id}/bids with header Idempotency-Key (or the field bid_id, a UUID made by the client) and body {max_amount, currency}. It returns 200 {bid_id, status: leading | outbid | rejected, current_price, your_max, ends_at, reason?}. Rejection reasons: below_minimum (needs at least current price plus one step), auction_ended, seller_cannot_bid, bidder_not_verified.
Read item: GET /v1/items/{item_id} returns {title, current_price, bid_count, high_bidder_alias, ends_at, server_now, min_next_bid}. It is cacheable for 1 second for anonymous viewers. Bid history: GET /v1/items/{item_id}/bids returns public rows (aliases, amounts that were the price at the time, times), never the hidden maximum of the leader.
Watch: GET /v1/items/{item_id}/live (Server-Sent Events) or a WebSocket message {subscribe: item_id}. Events: price {current_price, bid_count, ends_at}, outbid {item_id} (only for the affected user's own channel), ended {winner_alias, final_price}.
datamodel: The central table has one row of state per item, and it is the point of serialization.

Tables outbox (id, item_id, type, payload, created_at, sent_at) holds events written in the same transaction as the bid; watch(item_id, user_id) is kept in the watch service. The shard key is item_id for item_state, bid and outbox, so that one bid is a single-shard transaction. Users, payment methods and orders live in other services. Photos are objects in a blob store, behind a CDN.
design: The bid path. The client sends POST bids to a stateless bid API through a load balancer. The API checks the session, that the bidder is verified and not the seller, and the idempotency key. It routes the call to the shard that owns the item (hash of item_id on a ring). On the shard one transaction does the following: read item_state, check status = live and now < ends_at using the shard's own clock, apply the proxy-bidding rule to compute the new leader, price and version, update item_state with WHERE version = v, insert the bid row and the outbox rows (price changed, outbid), and commit. If the update touches zero rows, another bid won the race; the API reads again and retries up to 3 times. The answer to the client is built from the committed result.
The proxy bidding rule. Let the leader have maximum \( M \), the price be \( p \), and the bid step be \( s \). A new bid has maximum \( m \). If \( m < p + s \), reject it. If the bidder is the leader, raise \( M \) to \( \max(M, m) \) and keep the price. If \( m > M \), the new bidder leads and the price becomes \( \min(m, M + s) \). Otherwise the leader stays and the price becomes \( \min(M, m + s) \), and the new bidder is told "outbid" at once. When \( m = M \), the earlier bid wins. This is a second-price rule, the same idea as the Vickrey auction, which is also the base of many ad auctions: the winner pays about the second highest maximum plus one step, so the best strategy is to bid your true maximum once.
Telling people. The bid transaction writes events into the outbox table in the same commit. A relay reads new rows by order and publishes them to a message queue, then marks them sent. Consumers do four things: send the new price to the live gateways, send the "you were outbid" notification (push, e-mail) to the old leader, update the search index and the item cache, and update the seller's page. Because the event is written in the same transaction as the bid, we never have a bid without an event, or an event without a bid.
Live prices. Watchers hold a WebSocket or SSE connection to a gateway, which subscribes to a pub/sub channel item:{id}. When a price event arrives the gateway sends it to its watchers of this item. For a hot item the gateway coalesces: it keeps only the latest price and sends at most two updates per second per item. The full bid history is available by a normal call. Every update carries ends_at and server_now so that clients can correct their countdown.
Closing the auction. When a listing is created the system starts a durable workflow for it. The first step is a durable timer that sleeps until ends_at. When it fires, the workflow runs steps: set status = closing under the same version check (this fences out late bids), re-read ends_at (a soft close may have moved it, in which case the workflow sleeps again), decide the winner (leader above the reserve), create the order, ask the payment service to authorize the winner's saved payment method with an idempotency key, publish "ended" events, and mark the item ended. Each step is recorded by the workflow engine, so after a crash the workflow resumes at the last completed step, and activities that may run twice are idempotent.
Clocks and the countdown. The authoritative time of an auction is the clock of the shard that processes the bid. Servers sync with NTP (typical skew a few milliseconds, with a monitor that takes a host out if it is more than 50 ms off). The browser countdown is only a hint: the page gets server_now, computes an offset and shows a timer, and the server decides. A bid that arrives after ends_at is rejected with auction_ended, even if the user's timer showed one second left.

deepdive: One writer per item: lock, version or actor? All bids of one item are a sequence, and the rule for the next bid depends on the result of the last. Three ways serialize them. Pessimistic: SELECT ... FOR UPDATE on the item row, so a second bid waits. It is simple and correct, but on a hot item 200 waiting transactions hold connections and each waits for all earlier ones. Optimistic: read the version, compute, then UPDATE ... WHERE version = v, and retry on zero rows. It does not hold locks during the compute, and for normal items (one bid per minute) conflicts are rare. For a hot item with 200 bids per second, most attempts conflict and retry, so we cap retries at 3 and add a small random delay. Actor or single-writer: all bids of an item go to one process (the shard owner), which keeps the item state in memory and applies bids in a queue, writing the result with a version check as a safety net. This is the fastest on hot items (one memory update, a batch of bids per commit), but it needs routing, leases and a careful failover. We start with the optimistic version and move to the single-writer queue for items flagged hot.
Isolation level. With READ COMMITTED, two transactions can both read price 20, both decide they lead, and the second overwrites the first: a lost update. The version column prevents this at any level, because the second UPDATE finds version already changed. REPEATABLE READ with first-committer-wins (as in PostgreSQL) aborts the second transaction with a serialization error, and the code retries. SERIALIZABLE gives the same safety without relying on the version column, at the cost of more aborts and predicate-lock memory. We use the explicit version, because it is visible in the code, works on any database and costs one column. Write skew is not a problem here since all bids of an item write the same row.
Idempotence. A mobile client sends a bid, the network drops the answer, the client retries. Without protection the buyer can lose 25 and 26 in two bids, or see "below minimum" on a bid that actually succeeded. Each bid carries a bid_id. The table has a UNIQUE constraint on it, and the transaction first checks for it. A repeated call returns the stored result. The same key is passed to the payment provider at closing. The queue consumers dedupe by event id because the queue delivers at least once, and an update to a price display is naturally idempotent if it carries the version: a gateway ignores a price with a version lower than the one it has sent.
The outbox and the dual-write problem. If the API writes the bid and then publishes an event, a crash between the two leaves a bid with no notification, and the old leader never learns he lost. If it publishes first, a crash leaves a notification for a bid that does not exist. The outbox pattern writes the event in the same transaction as the bid. A relay (a poller that reads rows with sent_at IS NULL in order, or a change-data-capture stream from the database log) publishes and marks them. Delivery is at least once, and consumers are idempotent. The order of events per item is kept by the primary key (item_id, seq) and by partitioning the queue on item_id.
Time, soft close and sniping. In a hard-close auction, people bid in the last seconds so that others cannot react ("sniping"); the rule "a bid is valid if the shard receives it before ends_at" makes network speed a part of the game. A soft close extends the end by 2 minutes when a bid arrives in the last 2 minutes; the auction ends only after 2 quiet minutes. This changes the system: ends_at is mutable, the close timer must be rescheduled (the workflow reads ends_at again after waking), and the load pattern changes from a spike at the deadline to a longer fight. The product choice is not ours alone, but the design supports both with one flag per item.
tradeoffs: Optimistic version check versus a row lock versus a single writer. Choose optimistic control when most items see little contention (the normal case) and you want simple stateless API servers. Choose a row lock for a small system with low bid rates where the simplest correct code wins. Choose a single writer per item (an actor or a partition owner) when a few items get hundreds of bids per second and the retries would waste capacity. You give up simple routing and fast failover with the third, you give up throughput on hot items with the first two.
Hard close versus soft close. Choose a hard close when buyers expect an exact end time, the items are cheap and the platform wants predictable load for support and payments. Choose a soft close for high-value items, where the seller wants the final price to reflect real demand and not a lucky last second. A soft close can make an auction run long, and operations like payments and shipping then cannot be planned by the hour.
One database per shard versus a log-based design. Choose a sharded relational database with the transaction above when you want simple strong guarantees, ad hoc queries and a team that knows SQL. Choose an event log per item (an append-only stream where the order of events is the truth, and the state is derived) when you need to replay history, audit every decision or support many consumers. The log makes failover and rebuilds clean, but readers must handle eventual consistency, and the team must run a stream platform.
Push to watchers versus polling. Choose push (SSE or WebSocket) when an item is in its last minutes and the price matters every second. Choose polling every 5 to 10 seconds with a CDN-cached response for the long tail of items that have no bids or end in days, because 30 million items times a live connection each would cost far more than it is worth. The client opens the live connection only when the end is less than about 10 minutes away.
failures: A shard primary fails. Bids for items on that shard fail or wait during the failover, which takes 10 to 30 seconds with a synchronous replica. Because the replica is synchronous, no accepted bid is lost. The client shows "try again", and idempotent retries are safe. Auctions that end during the failover are closed late, and the soft-close rule or a grace period (accept bids with placed_at before ends_at that arrived late because of the outage, only if we have a trustworthy timestamp) is a product decision made before it happens.
Outbox relay stops. Events pile up in the outbox table. Outbid notifications and live prices are late, but bids and closing are correct. An alert on the age of the oldest unsent row fires after 30 seconds. After restart the relay sends the backlog in order. Gateways also poll the item state when a watcher has not seen a price for 10 seconds, as a safety net.
Workflow engine or timer is late or lost. The durable timer is stored in the database, so a restart resumes it. A reconciler scans for items with status = live and ends_at in the past and starts the close step; the close step is idempotent, so a duplicate is harmless. Late closing is better than none, and the bid path already rejects bids after ends_at, so the winner does not change.
Payment fails for the winner. The workflow marks the order unpaid, retries with backoff for 24 hours, then offers the item to the second-highest bidder at his maximum price or relists it. A seller's score and the buyer's strike count are updated. The bid history keeps the second bidder's maximum for this purpose.
Clock skew on one host. A host with a clock 5 seconds ahead would reject valid bids; one 5 seconds behind would accept late ones. NTP monitoring removes drifting hosts from the pool, and the bid rule uses the time of the shard primary only. After a failover the new primary's clock is checked against the old one's last bid time, and it refuses to accept a bid with placed_at lower than the last accepted one.
evolution: At 1,000 items, a single database with a row lock and a cron job that closes auctions is enough. At 1 million items, add the version check, the outbox and a message queue for notifications, and move the closing to a workflow with durable timers. At 30 million items, shard by item_id, add the live gateways with coalescing and the watcher channel, and add the hot-item single-writer mode. At several hundred million items in many countries, run each region's items in its own region (a seller and buyers mostly share a region), keep one authoritative shard per item, and let cross-region buyers bid through the item's home region with an extra 100 to 200 ms. We do not use multi-primary writes for one item, because conflicting bids would need a merge that the rules of an auction do not allow.
followups: Why can two people not both win when they bid at the same time? || All bids for one item are applied to one state row by one transaction at a time. The version check (or the lock) makes the second transaction re-read the state, which includes the first bid, and apply the proxy rule again. The result depends only on the order of commits, and the order is unique.
How does proxy bidding work and why is it second-price? || The system keeps each bidder's hidden maximum and raises the price only as far as needed to stay ahead of the second best, by one step. The winner pays about the second highest maximum plus one step. It is the Vickrey idea, which makes bidding your true value the best strategy.
What clock decides if a bid came in time? || The clock of the shard that owns the item, synchronized by NTP, compared with ends_at inside the transaction. The user's device clock is never trusted, and the page only shows a countdown from server_now. Network delay counts against the bidder, which is the stated rule.
What if the server crashes after saving a bid but before sending the outbid notification? || The event is in the outbox table, written in the same transaction. After restart the relay finds unsent rows and publishes them. Delivery is at least once, so consumers dedupe by event id. This is why we do not publish directly from the API.
How do you make sure the auction closes exactly once with one winner? || A durable workflow runs the steps and records progress. The first close step sets status = closing with a version check, so late bids are fenced out and a second worker cannot close it again. Order creation and payment use idempotency keys so a replayed step has no second effect.
How do you handle one item that gets a huge number of bids and watchers? || Flag it hot and route its bids to a single writer that applies them in memory in order and commits in small batches. Shed bids that cannot win (below the current price plus step) at the API with a cached price. Push to watchers through pub/sub and gateways with coalescing, and cache the read endpoint for 1 second.

title: Design a stock brokerage app like Robinhood
category: Web & product
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: realtime, websocket, pubsub, fanout, outbox, idempotence, ledger, acid, durableexec, cachelayer, mqueue
summary: A brokerage app lets people watch live prices, buy and sell stocks and options, and move money in and out. It has two very different halves. The first half is a firehose: market data arrives at hundreds of thousands of messages per second, and millions of phones want the numbers for the few symbols on their screen, live. This half is about fan-out, conflation and connection management, and a lost message costs nothing. The second half is the opposite: orders and money. Every order must be checked, sent to a market exactly once, and every fill must change cash and positions exactly once, in a ledger that always balances. A lost or doubled message here means real money and a regulator. We design both halves, the order lifecycle as a state machine driven by outbox and durable workflows, and the double-entry ledger that is the truth of the account.
requirements: Functional requirements. A customer sees a watchlist and a stock page with a live price, a chart, and his position with profit and loss. He can place market and limit orders (buy and sell, whole and fractional shares), cancel or change an open order, see order status and fills, and see his cash, buying power and holdings. He can link a bank account, deposit and withdraw by ACH, and receive an instant buying power while the deposit is still settling. The system checks pre-trade rules for every order: enough buying power or shares, market hours, price limits, restricted symbols, day-trading rules, and account status. The system sends push notifications for fills and big price moves, and produces statements, tax documents and regulatory reports.
Non-functional requirements. Correctness of money: cash and positions are exact, every change is a pair of ledger entries, and the sum of all entries is zero. No order is sent to the market twice, and no fill is applied twice. Latency: an order reaches the market link within 100 ms of the tap, and the app shows live prices within 1 second of the market. Availability: order entry and cancel should work during market hours with a target of 99.99 percent; the quotes can degrade first. Peak: the first 30 minutes after the open, and days with news or a meme stock, run 3 to 10 times above normal. Auditability: every state change is stored and kept for years (regulators require several years of records; we plan for 7).
Assumptions. 20 million funded accounts, 6 million daily active users, and 2 million connected at the peak minute after the 9:30 a.m. New York open. A user views 5 symbols at a time. We receive a market data feed for about 10,000 US symbols; the peak is 500,000 messages per second after we remove symbols we do not offer. Orders go to market makers and exchanges through a routing partner or our own router over FIX sessions. Since 28 May 2024 US stocks settle one business day after the trade (T+1), and this affects cash and buying power.
estimates: Market data in. 500,000 messages per second at peak. Per symbol, 10,000 symbols, an average of 50 messages per second, but the busy ones get thousands. Phones need the latest price, not every tick. We conflate: keep only the latest quote per symbol and publish it every 250 ms. That is at most 10,000 symbols times 4 = 40,000 updates per second, a reduction of 12 times, and in practice fewer, since many symbols are quiet.
Quote delivery. 2 million connected users times 5 symbols each, one update per second at most: 10 million symbol updates per second to deliver. Each connection receives one frame per second with up to 5 updates of about 60 bytes, so 2 million frames per second and 600 MB per second = 4.8 Gbit per second in total. One gateway holds about 50,000 connections (busy with TLS and frames), so 2 million divided by 50,000 = 40, and with headroom for a zone loss and a spike we run 60.
Internal fan-out. Each gateway subscribes to the symbols its users watch, which is nearly all active symbols. 40,000 updates per second times 60 gateways = 2.4 million internal deliveries per second, about 150 MB per second. This is fine for a pub/sub layer with a few brokers.
Orders. 6 million daily active users, one quarter place orders, 2 orders each on average: 3 million orders per day. Assume 30 percent come in the first 30 minutes: 900,000 divided by 1,800 s = 500 per second on average in that window, and a burst factor of 3 gives 1,500 per second. We design for 5,000 per second to allow a meme-stock day.
Writes per order. One order creates about 20 rows over its life: the order row, 5 state changes, 2 hold entries in the ledger, 4 to 6 fill entries, 5 outbox rows, and the execution rows. At 5,000 orders per second that is 100,000 row writes per second. We shard by account_id into 16 database shards, so each takes about 6,000 writes per second, within the range of a well-tuned relational primary with synchronous replication.
Ledger size. About 8 ledger entries per order times 3 million orders = 24 million entries per day. At 150 bytes each that is 3.6 GB per day, 1.3 TB per year, and about 9 TB for 7 years. Deposits, withdrawals, dividends and fees add a little. This is a modest size for a partitioned relational store with cold storage for old years.
api: Orders: POST /v1/orders with header Idempotency-Key and body {account_id, symbol, side, type (market | limit | stop), qty or notional, limit_price, time_in_force (day | gtc)}. It returns 201 {order_id, status: received} or 4xx with a reason (insufficient_buying_power, market_closed, restricted_symbol, price_out_of_collar). GET /v1/orders/{order_id} returns the state and fills. DELETE /v1/orders/{order_id} requests a cancel and returns 202, because the cancel is a request to the market that may lose a race with a fill. PATCH /v1/orders/{order_id} replaces price or quantity.
Account: GET /v1/accounts/{id}/portfolio returns positions, cash, buying power and a snapshot time. GET /v1/accounts/{id}/ledger?from=... lists entries. Money movement: POST /v1/transfers {bank_account_id, direction, amount} with an Idempotency-Key returns 202 {transfer_id, status: pending}.
Market data: WebSocket wss://stream/v1 with messages {action: subscribe, symbols: [AAPL, MSFT]} and server frames {t: "q", s: "AAPL", bid, ask, last, ts} batched once per second. GET /v1/quotes?symbols=... is the polling fallback and returns the snapshot from the cache. GET /v1/charts/{symbol}?range=1D returns candles.
datamodel: Everything about money is in a relational database partitioned by account_id, because ACID transactions are needed inside one account.
```sql
CREATE TABLE ledger_entry (
  entry_id    BIGINT PRIMARY KEY,
  txn_id      BIGINT NOT NULL,        -- groups the balanced entries
  account_id  BIGINT NOT NULL,        -- customer or house account
  ledger_acct TEXT NOT NULL,          -- cash, hold, pos:AAPL
  amount      NUMERIC(20,6) NOT NULL, -- signed: debit + / credit -
  currency_or_symbol TEXT NOT NULL,
  ref_type    TEXT NOT NULL,          -- order, fill, transfer
  ref_id      TEXT NOT NULL,          -- order_id, exec_id
  created_at  TIMESTAMP NOT NULL,
  UNIQUE (ref_type, ref_id, ledger_acct, txn_id)
);
```
Entries are only inserted, never updated. A balance is the sum of entries, and a table balance(account_id, ledger_acct, amount, version) is a cached running total, updated in the same transaction. Table order(order_id, account_id, symbol, side, qty, limit, status, version, client_key UNIQUE). Table execution(exec_id UNIQUE, order_id, qty, price, venue, ts). exec_id is the market's own identifier, and its UNIQUE key makes fills idempotent. Table outbox(id, account_id, type, payload, sent_at). Table transfer(transfer_id, state, bank_ref, amount).
Market data is not in the database. The latest quote per symbol is in memory in the conflation service and in a small cache (key quote:{symbol}); candles are in a time-series store or in files; the previous close and instrument details are in a reference-data cache.
design: The quote path. Market data arrives from a vendor or directly from the exchanges' feeds into a few feed handlers in a data center near the exchanges. They decode, drop symbols we do not offer, and keep the latest quote per symbol. Every 250 ms a conflation step publishes the changed symbols to the pub/sub layer, keyed by symbol. Gateways subscribe to symbols; they keep a map from symbol to the connections that watch it. Each second, each connection gets one frame with its symbols' latest values. A phone opening a stock page sends a subscribe message and first receives a snapshot from the cache, so the screen is never empty. A symbol with 500,000 watchers is not special for the gateway: the cost is one entry per connection, not per update.
The order path. The app sends POST orders with an idempotency key. The order service, in one transaction on the shard of the account, does: reads the account balance row, checks the pre-trade rules, writes the order as received, creates the hold (ledger entries that move cash to a hold account, or reserve the shares), and writes an outbox row "order accepted". Then it commits and returns 201. The customer sees "order received" in about 50 ms. A relay reads the outbox and puts the message on a queue partitioned by account_id (so that all events for one account are in order). The order router consumes it, runs the second set of risk checks (price collars, notional limit, venue status), picks a venue, and sends a FIX NewOrderSingle with the order id as ClOrdID. The router stores the sequence numbers of the FIX session, as the protocol requires, so that after a disconnect it can ask for missing messages.
Fills. The venue sends execution reports. The market link converts each report into an event with the venue's ExecID and puts it on a queue partitioned by account_id. The execution handler runs one transaction: insert the execution row (UNIQUE on exec_id, so a repeated report is ignored), update the order state machine (accepted, partially filled, filled), release the hold, post the ledger entries for the fill (cash out, shares in, fees, with a counter-entry on a house account for each), and add outbox rows for the push notification and the live order status. If the transaction hits the unique key, the fill was already applied, and the handler just acknowledges the message. This is idempotence at the database, which is the only place where exactly-once effects can be enforced.
Money movement as a workflow. A deposit by ACH takes days and can fail late. A durable workflow models it: create the transfer, request the debit from the bank, wait (a durable timer and a signal for the bank's callback), give instant buying power up to a limit if risk allows, settle, or, if the bank returns the payment, reverse the entries and, if needed, close out positions. The workflow engine keeps each step and its input, so the wait of three days survives deploys and crashes, and every external call has an idempotency key. The same engine runs the end-of-day jobs, dividend payments and corporate actions (splits).
The cache and read models. The portfolio screen reads positions and cash from a cache, which is filled by consumers of the outbox events, and keeps a version number. The phone computes the total value itself by multiplying positions by live quotes, so the server is not asked once per second. A read that must be exact (before an order, or in the statement) goes to the database. Charts use precomputed candles.

deepdive: The ledger is the truth, and it is double-entry. Every business event is a transaction with entries that add up to zero: a fill takes cash from the customer and gives it to the counterparty account of the firm, and takes shares from the firm's inventory and gives them to the customer. The customer's balance is a sum of immutable entries, so no code can quietly overwrite a number, and an auditor can replay the history. A running balance table is a cache of this sum and is updated in the same ACID transaction, so atomicity guarantees that entries and balance move together, consistency is enforced by a CHECK on non-negative cash (or buying power rules in code), and durability comes from synchronous replication. Daily reconciliation compares our ledger with the clearing firm's and the bank's files: positions with the clearing firm, cash with the bank, and our own totals (the sum of all customer cash must equal the cash we hold at the bank, less house funds). A break is an alert, not a fix-by-hand: corrections are new entries with a reason.
Buying power and the race between two orders. Two orders on the same account, sent at the same moment, must not both spend the same cash. The order transaction reads and updates the balance row of the account, with a version check or a row lock. Because the account is the partition key, the lock is local and the transaction is a single-shard one, so there is no distributed transaction. The cost: an account serializes its own orders, which at a human speed is nothing. Trading firms and API users with hundreds of orders per second per account need a different path: an in-memory risk engine that owns the account and applies holds in order, with the database updated in batches. We keep the interface the same.
Exactly-once on an unreliable network. Between the phone and the order API: the idempotency key. Between the order API and the router: the outbox and a queue with at-least-once delivery, and the router dedupes by order id before sending, plus the venue itself rejects a duplicate ClOrdID. Between the venue and the ledger: the venue's ExecID in a unique key. When a FIX session breaks, the router does not know if the last order arrived. It must not resend blindly (the order could be doubled) and it must not assume it was rejected. It sends an OrderStatusRequest, or uses the session recovery of FIX (sequence numbers and ResendRequest), and the order stays in a state "unknown" until the venue answers. This is a state machine with explicit states for "I do not know", and the customer sees "pending".
Order state machine. States: received, accepted (risk passed), sent, working (acknowledged by venue), partially filled, filled, cancel requested, canceled, rejected, expired. A cancel is a request: a fill can arrive after the customer tapped cancel, and the system must apply the fill and tell the customer it was too late. Each transition is written with a version check, so two events about one order (a fill and a cancel acknowledgment) are serialized. Events for one account travel on one queue partition, so they are applied in the order of arrival; reports out of order, which happen after reconnects, are handled by a rule per transition (a fill after "canceled" is still valid).
Surviving the open. At 9:30 the orders that customers queued before the open are released at once, and the market is busy. The order router sends through FIX sessions with a rate limit that venues set, so a queue builds in front of it. We use priorities: cancels first, then market orders, then limit orders; orders of the same account stay in order. When load goes beyond the limit, load is shed in layers: charts and news first, then the live quote frame rate (1 per 2 s), then new account opening and non-urgent transfers, and last order entry, which is protected by a separate pool of servers, separate database connections and its own queue. A circuit breaker on the venue stops sending to a halted symbol (a trading halt is a normal event) and rejects the order with a clear message.
tradeoffs: Push versus pull for quotes. Choose a WebSocket stream with conflation when users stare at live prices and 1 second matters, as for a watchlist during the open. Choose polling a cached snapshot every 5 seconds for screens that the user rarely watches (portfolio overview, a stock he does not follow) because a connection costs memory and a pub/sub subscription. You give up smoothness with polling, and simplicity with sockets.
Conflation versus every tick. Choose conflation to 250 ms or 1 second for retail apps: it cuts the internal traffic by 10 times and matches what a human can see. Choose a full tick stream for charts with order flow, for professional users and for the order router's own price checks, since a stale price in a price collar is dangerous. The pro feed is a separate, smaller path with a few subscribers.
Single relational database per account shard versus event sourcing for orders. Choose a relational database with the order, the ledger and the outbox in one transaction when you want simple guarantees, ad hoc queries for support, and regulatory reporting from SQL. Choose an event-sourced log, where each order is a stream of events and the state is derived, when you need a replay of every decision for audit and you can run a log platform. Event sourcing makes audits and rebuilding natural, and it makes reads, fixes and schema changes harder.
Instant buying power versus waiting for settlement. Choose to credit buying power at once when the deposit is small, the customer has a history and the bank's risk signal is good; it is what customers expect and what drives engagement. Choose to wait for settlement for new accounts, large amounts and risky bank accounts. The firm takes the loss when an ACH return arrives after the customer has bought and sold, so limits and a loss budget are risk decisions, not engineering ones.
failures: A database shard fails. Orders for the accounts on that shard fail with a clear error, and other accounts continue. With a synchronous replica the failover takes 10 to 30 seconds and loses no committed transaction. Open orders already at the venue continue, and their fills wait in the queue until the shard is back, then they are applied in order. Nobody loses a fill, because the queue is durable and the handler is idempotent.
The venue link or a FIX session drops. The router reconnects, uses sequence numbers to recover missing messages, and asks for the status of every order in state "sent" or "unknown". During the outage, new orders to this venue are routed to another one if the order type allows; otherwise they wait in the queue with a timeout and the customer sees "pending". Orders are never marked rejected on a guess.
Market data feed fails. The feed handlers run in two sites and merge the streams by sequence number. If both fail, the gateways keep the last quote with a "delayed" flag, and the order path blocks market orders (they need a price) while limit orders still go. A wrong price is dangerous in the app, so the router checks every order against the last good price and a collar.
Overload on a volatile day. Layered load shedding as above, the autoscaling of gateways one hour before the open on schedule (not after the load arrives), and the protected pool for orders. Rate limits per user stop one API script from hurting everybody.
Bad deploy or a bad ledger bug. Ledger code is released slowly, first to internal accounts. The invariants are checked continuously: the sum of entries per transaction is zero, no negative cash without a margin flag, the balance table equals the sum of entries. A violation pages the team and can freeze the affected accounts. The fix is made with new entries, never by editing history.
evolution: At 1,000 customers, one database, orders sent through a broker-dealer partner's API, quotes polled from a vendor, and a nightly reconciliation file. At 100,000, add the WebSocket quote stream with a conflation service, the outbox and the queue, and a ledger with double entries. At 1 million, shard by account_id, add the durable workflows for money movement, the layered load shedding and a separate pool for order entry. At 20 million, add the own router and risk engine, second site for feed handlers and order routing, entitlement and delayed-data buffers, options, and the controls for margin and day-trading rules. At 100 million and more, add regional gateways, per-region caches of portfolio snapshots, and event streams for the audit and the risk systems, while the ledger stays one logical truth per account.
followups: How do you make sure a fill is applied only once? || Use the market's ExecID as a UNIQUE key on the execution table in the same transaction as the ledger entries. A redelivered report hits the key and is ignored. Queue delivery is at least once, so the dedupe must be in the database, which is the place that can enforce it.
Why double-entry and an append-only ledger? || Every transaction has balanced entries, so money cannot appear or disappear, and a bug shows up as a non-zero sum. Entries are never edited, so history is auditable and balances can be rebuilt. Corrections are new entries with a reason.
What do you do when you lose the connection to the exchange and do not know whether an order arrived? || Treat it as unknown. Do not resend, because it could be doubled, and do not mark it rejected. Use FIX sequence numbers and resend requests, or an order status request, and keep the order in a pending state until the venue answers. The venue also rejects a duplicate ClOrdID as a safety net.
How do you serve live prices to 2 million phones? || Conflate the feed to 4 updates per second per symbol, publish by symbol through pub/sub to about 60 gateways, and send each connection one frame per second with its symbols. Cache the encoded frames, send snapshots on subscribe, and drop frames for slow clients. Internal traffic is tens of thousands of messages per second, not millions.
How does the system behave on a day when traffic is ten times normal? || Shed in layers: charts and news first, then quote frequency, then non-urgent features, and protect order entry with its own capacity. Scale gateways before the open, queue orders with priorities (cancels first), and respect the venue rate limits. The user sees "pending" rather than errors.
How do you prevent two orders from spending the same cash? || Both orders update the same balance row in transactions on the account's shard, using a version check or a row lock, so one sees the hold of the other. The account is the partition key, so no distributed transaction is needed. High-frequency users get an in-memory risk engine per account.
How do you handle an ACH deposit that is reversed after the customer traded? || Model the transfer as a durable workflow with states and a long timer. If the bank returns the payment, post reversal entries; if cash goes negative, the account is restricted and positions may be liquidated by the rules. The firm limits the instant buying power it gives, so the loss is bounded.

summary: A local delivery service keeps household goods, snacks and drinks in many small warehouses (micro-fulfillment centers, MFCs) inside cities, and promises delivery in about 30 minutes. A customer enters an address, sees what is in stock near him, pays, and a picker and a driver bring the order. Unlike a restaurant marketplace, the company owns the inventory, so the system must know the real stock of about 5,000 products in each of hundreds of warehouses, and it must not sell what is not on the shelf. The traffic is modest by internet standards (tens of orders per second), so the hard part is not raw scale. It is correctness in the physical world: which warehouse serves this door, whether the item is really there, which driver goes where, and how long it takes by road. We design the serviceability lookup with a spatial index, per-warehouse inventory with safe reservation, dispatch, and tracking.
requirements: Functional requirements. A customer sets a delivery address or uses GPS. The app shows only the products available at the warehouse that serves this address, with prices, and an honest delivery estimate. The customer adds items to a cart, checks out with a saved payment method, and tracks the order on a map. Warehouse staff see new orders on a picking screen, pick and pack them, and mark them ready. Drivers receive offers, pick up the order and deliver with proof. The customer can cancel before picking starts. If an item is missing at picking, the system offers a substitute or refunds that line. Operations can close a warehouse, change its delivery area or prices, and see live KPIs.
Non-functional requirements. Correctness: never accept an order that the warehouse cannot fill with high probability (target: under 1 percent of order lines short at picking). Never charge twice or lose an order. Latency: serviceability and catalog reads in under 100 ms, checkout in under 500 ms. Promise: the estimated delivery time shown at checkout should be met in 90 percent of orders. Availability: browsing and checkout work 99.9 percent of the time; if one warehouse fails, only its area is affected. Cost: warehouses and drivers are the main cost, so the software must keep drivers busy without breaking the promise.
Assumptions. 600 warehouses, each with 5,000 products on hand out of a catalog of 20,000. 400,000 orders per day and 3 million app sessions per day. The peak hour has 10 percent of the day's orders, and a promotion or bad weather can bring 4 times more. Each order has 4 lines on average. At the peak hour 14,000 drivers are active. Maps and a road-network routing engine are available.
estimates: Orders. 400,000 per day divided by 86,400 = 4.6 per second on average. The peak hour has 40,000 orders: 40,000 divided by 3,600 = 11 per second. With a 4.5 times promotion factor we design for 50 orders per second. This is small, and one well-tuned relational database could take it. We still shard, but for isolation and for the shape of the problem, not for throughput.
Inventory size and writes. 600 warehouses times 5,000 products = 3 million inventory rows. At 100 bytes per row this is 300 MB, small enough to keep fully in memory. Writes: each order reserves 4 lines, so 50 orders per second times 4 = 200 conditional updates per second at design peak. Receiving stock, cycle counts and picks add about the same. A hot product in a hot warehouse (ice cream on a hot night) may get 5 reservations per second on one row, which a row lock handles easily if the transaction is short.
Browse. 3 million sessions times 20 pages = 60 million page views per day. The peak hour is 12 percent: 7.2 million per hour = 2,000 per second. Each page shows 30 products, so up to 60,000 product lookups per second, all keyed by warehouse. These come from a cache, and the database sees only misses.
Serviceability map. Use H3 hexagonal cells of resolution 9 (an edge of about 174 m and an area of about 0.1 km²). A warehouse serves a drive-time area of about 80 km², which is 800 cells. 600 warehouses times 800 = 480,000 cells. Even with 100 bytes per cell this is 48 MB, which fits in every API server's memory.
Drivers. Little's law: the number of busy drivers equals the arrival rate of trips times the trip duration. At the peak hour 11 orders per second, with 1.2 orders per trip, gives 9 trips per second. A trip (wait for pack, drive, hand over, return) takes about 25 minutes = 1,500 seconds. Busy drivers = 9 times 1,500 = 13,500, which matches our 14,000. Per warehouse this is 23 drivers.
Latency numbers for checkout. A read from an in-memory cache in the same zone takes about 0.5 ms, a database round trip in the same region about 1 ms, a commit with a synchronous replica 2 to 5 ms, a call to the payment provider 100 to 300 ms, and a cross-region round trip 70 ms or more. So checkout time is dominated by payment, and the inventory transaction (about 10 ms) is not a problem.
api: Serviceability: GET /v1/serviceability?lat=..&lng=.. returns {mfc_id, eta_min_minutes, eta_max_minutes, fee, open} or {serviceable: false}. The app calls it when the address changes and caches the result for the session.
Catalog: GET /v1/mfcs/{mfc_id}/products?category=..&cursor=.. returns products with price and availability state (in_stock | low_stock | out). The availability is a state, not an exact number.
Cart and checkout: PUT /v1/carts/{cart_id}/items. POST /v1/carts/{cart_id}/checkout with headers Idempotency-Key and body {payment_method_id, address_id, tip}. It returns 201 {order_id, status: placed, eta} or 409 {unavailable_lines: [...]} when some line could not be reserved; the app lets the customer change the cart.
Order: GET /v1/orders/{order_id} returns status, lines, substitutions and ETA. POST /v1/orders/{order_id}/cancel works while the status is placed or accepted. WebSocket or SSE /v1/orders/{order_id}/track pushes {status, driver_lat, driver_lng, eta_s}.
Warehouse and driver: POST /v1/mfcs/{id}/picks/{order_id}/lines/{line_id} {picked_qty, substitute_sku?}. POST /v1/drivers/{id}/location {lat, lng, ts} (batched), POST /v1/dispatch/offers/{offer_id}/accept.
datamodel: Core tables are in a relational database partitioned by mfc_id (all rows of one warehouse live together), because the main transaction touches one warehouse.
```sql
CREATE TABLE inventory (
  mfc_id     INT NOT NULL,
  sku_id     INT NOT NULL,
  on_hand    INT NOT NULL,        -- physical units counted
  reserved   INT NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
  safety     INT NOT NULL DEFAULT 0,
  version    BIGINT NOT NULL,
  PRIMARY KEY (mfc_id, sku_id),
  CHECK (reserved >= 0 AND on_hand - reserved >= 0)
);
CREATE TABLE orders (
  order_id   BIGINT PRIMARY KEY,
  mfc_id     INT NOT NULL,
  customer_id BIGINT NOT NULL,
  status     TEXT NOT NULL,
  idem_key   TEXT NOT NULL UNIQUE,
  version    BIGINT NOT NULL
);
```
Available units are on_hand minus reserved minus safety. Table order_line(order_id, line_no, sku_id, qty, price, state). Table dispatch_offer(offer_id, order_id, driver_id, state, expires_at). Table outbox(id, mfc_id, type, payload, sent_at) is written in the same transaction as the order changes.
Spatial data. A table cell_map(h3_cell, mfc_id, drive_minutes, rank) is built offline: for each warehouse, compute the roads reachable within the target drive time and mark the cells; for a cell covered by several warehouses keep a ranked list. The table is small, loaded in memory in each API server, and refreshed by a version number. Driver positions are in an in-memory store keyed by (mfc_id, driver_id) with the last point and a 10-second TTL; the history is appended to a time-series store.
Cache keys. catalog:{mfc_id}:{category} with a 30-second TTL, avail:{mfc_id}:{sku_id} holding the availability state, serviceability results in the app. Messages in the queue use mfc_id as the partition key so that all events of one warehouse are in order.
design: Finding the warehouse. When the customer sets an address, the app sends the coordinates. The API converts them into an H3 cell id (a pure function, no network), looks up the cell in the in-memory cell_map, and gets a ranked list of warehouses with drive minutes. It picks the first one that is open and has capacity (a small status table that updates every few seconds), and returns it with an ETA. The cell map gives O(1) lookup and avoids running a polygon test for every request. The map itself is built from the road network: for each warehouse a routing engine computes all points reachable in, say, 12 minutes of driving, which gives a shape that follows rivers, highways and one-way streets, and not a circle. An address across a river may be 400 m away and 25 minutes by road.
Browsing. Once the warehouse is known, every list request is keyed by mfc_id. A caching layer holds catalog pages and the availability state per product for 30 seconds. Availability is cached as a state (in stock, low stock, out), and it is recomputed only when the state changes, so the cache is invalidated a few times per hour per product and not on every order. Inventory change events flow through the message queue and update the cache entries. Reads are served by cache first, then by read replicas of the database. A stale "in stock" costs a failed reservation at checkout, which the app handles.
Checkout. The API runs one transaction on the warehouse's shard. It sorts the cart lines by sku_id, and for each line runs a conditional update: reserve qty if available is at least qty. If any line fails, the whole transaction rolls back and the API returns 409 with the missing lines. Sorting the lines gives all transactions the same lock order, which avoids deadlocks. If all lines succeed, the transaction inserts the order (status placed), the lines, and an outbox row, and commits. Only after the commit does the API authorize the payment (a hold on the card, not a charge) with the order id as the payment idempotency key. If the authorization fails, a compensation step releases the reservation. We reserve first and pay second because a reservation is cheap and local, while a payment is slow and external.
From order to doorstep. The relay publishes "order placed" to the queue, partitioned by warehouse. The warehouse service shows the order on the picking screen. Pickers pick by a route through the shelves. If a line is short, the picker reports it, and the system either substitutes (customer's rules) or refunds the line, and sets on_hand to the corrected number. When the order is packed, a dispatch step starts. A dispatcher for the warehouse looks at ready orders and free drivers, computes the travel time for each pair with the routing engine, and solves a small assignment problem: minimize total pickup plus delivery time, with a bonus for batching two orders that go in the same direction. It runs every few seconds as a single loop per warehouse, so there are no races between two dispatchers for the same driver. The driver gets an offer, accepts, and the order status moves forward as the driver scans pickup and delivery.
Tracking. Driver apps send locations in small batches to a location service, which writes the latest point in memory. For a customer who has the tracking screen open, a gateway holds a WebSocket and pushes the driver's position and a new ETA every few seconds. The ETA is recomputed by the routing service from the driver's position to the door, plus a margin.

deepdive: Inventory is a belief, not a fact. The database holds a number, and the shelf holds boxes, and they differ because of picking errors, theft, damaged goods and wrong receiving counts. Reserving in the database protects against selling the same unit twice, but not against selling a unit that is not there. The design handles it in layers. A safety stock per product and warehouse hides the last units from the customer: if the system says 2 and the average error for this product is 1, we show "out" at 2. Each pick short is data: it triggers a cycle count of that product, and the safety stock rises for products with frequent errors. A cheap daily sample of counts estimates the error rate for each warehouse. The target is not zero shorts but a low rate with a good recovery (substitute or refund).
Atomic reservation across several lines: OCC or locks? A checkout touches 1 to 20 inventory rows of one warehouse. Optimistic control would read rows with versions, compute, and write with checks; if any row changed, redo everything. For popular rows, this repeats often. Pessimistic control with row locks takes the rows in a fixed order (sorted by sku_id), holds them for a few milliseconds, and commits, so it waits instead of retrying. Because transactions are short and one warehouse sees at most a few dozen checkouts per second, waiting is cheap and deadlocks cannot happen with a global lock order. A single conditional UPDATE per line (WHERE on_hand - reserved - safety >= qty) is atomic by itself, so even without an explicit read the check and the change cannot be separated. Our rule: use conditional updates inside one short OLTP transaction, in sorted order; use the version column only for state machines such as the order status.
Idempotence everywhere. A phone on a weak connection sends checkout, never sees the answer and sends again. The Idempotency-Key makes the second call return the first order, and the unique key on orders.idem_key enforces it even when two requests arrive at the same time. The payment provider call carries the order id as its own key. The driver app can send "delivered" twice; the status update is a conditional update (set delivered where status = picked_up), so the second does nothing. Every consumer of the queue dedupes by event id, because the queue delivers at least once.
Partitioning by warehouse. Almost everything in the system has a natural key, the warehouse: inventory, orders, picking, dispatch and driver positions. Partitioning by mfc_id gives single-shard transactions, and a failure or a hot spot stays inside a city. It also matches the operational reality: each warehouse has its own staff, its own stock and its own delivery area. The costs are that a customer near the border of two areas might see different catalogs depending on the assigned warehouse, and that moving a driver between warehouses is a cross-shard event, which is rare and handled by the dispatcher. Large cities with many warehouses can be grouped into a "market", which is the unit for pricing and capacity decisions.
Road routing and the ETA promise. Straight-line distance is a poor estimate. The routing engine works on the road graph (hundreds of millions of edges for a continent) with preprocessing such as contraction hierarchies, which makes a point-to-point query take well under a millisecond to a few milliseconds, with travel times that vary by time of day. We use it in three places: building the cell_map offline (many queries, once a night), computing the ETA at checkout (a few queries per order), and dispatch (a matrix of drivers times orders per warehouse every few seconds, with a few dozen of each). The ETA shown to the customer is the sum of the expected picking time (it depends on the queue in the warehouse), the wait for a driver, the drive time and a margin. The picking queue, not the road, is often the largest part at the peak, so the serviceability check includes a capacity signal: when a warehouse has more open orders than the pickers can finish in 15 minutes, the ETA grows and then new orders are paused.
Caching availability without lying. If the cache is 30 seconds old, a customer can see "in stock" for a sold-out product. We accept this, because the reservation is the real check and a failed reservation shows a polite message. We reduce the damage by caching a coarse state, not a count, and by invalidating when the state changes. For the few seconds after an out-of-stock event, an event updates the cache entry directly. The aim is that most browse requests never touch the database, and that the price of a stale read is one retry.
tradeoffs: Reserve at add-to-cart versus at checkout. Choose reservation at checkout when carts are often abandoned (most of them) and stock is limited, since early holds block other customers. Choose a short soft hold (3 to 5 minutes) at the start of checkout when the stock is scarce and a failure at the last step is costly for the customer. Reserving at add-to-cart would lock stock for people who never buy.
Warehouse assignment: fixed by address or decided at order time. Choose assignment by address when the catalog differs per warehouse and you want the app to show consistent stock and prices; this is the simple model we use. Choose dynamic assignment at checkout (try the best warehouse for the cart, and fall back to the second) when you have many overlapping warehouses and the catalogs are almost the same. Dynamic assignment raises the fulfillment rate, but ETAs and prices can change at the last step, and splitting a cart over two warehouses doubles the delivery cost.
H3 or S2 cells versus polygons with an R-tree. Choose a precomputed cell map when the areas change rarely, the lookup must be extremely fast and simple, and you want to cache or ship the map to every server. Choose polygons with a spatial index (R-tree, or PostGIS) when areas are drawn by hand, have exact borders that matter legally (a tax zone, an alcohol license) or change many times a day. Cells give an approximation at the border, and cell size sets the accuracy; polygons are exact but slower and harder to cache.
Sharded SQL versus a key-value store for inventory. Choose a relational database for inventory and orders when you need multi-row atomic reservation, constraints and ad hoc queries for support and finance, which is our case at 50 orders per second. Choose a key-value store with conditional writes when the item count is huge, rows are independent and each order touches one key; multi-line orders then need a saga with compensations, and you give up cross-row constraints.
failures: A warehouse shard primary fails. Its replica is promoted in 10 to 30 seconds. Orders in that area fail for that time, with a clear message, while other areas work. No committed reservation is lost because of synchronous replication. The area's cache keeps serving browse requests, and the orders resume after failover.
The payment provider is slow or down. The checkout transaction has already reserved stock. If the authorization times out, the order stays in "payment pending" and a worker retries with the same idempotency key; after 2 minutes the reservation is released and the customer is told. We avoid the danger of charging without an order (the key prevents a double charge) and of an order without payment (only a captured authorization completes the order).
The queue or the outbox relay stalls. Orders are in the database and are correct, but the picking screen does not see them. A pull fallback lets the warehouse app poll its own orders every 10 seconds from the database. Alerts fire when the oldest unsent outbox row is older than 30 seconds.
Warehouse operational failure. A warehouse loses power, or has no pickers. Operations flip a flag, the status table marks it closed, the serviceability check skips it, and customers see the next warehouse with a longer ETA or an honest "unavailable". Open orders are canceled or moved to another warehouse by a runbook.
Demand spike. A storm raises orders by 4 times. We protect the promise, not the order count: ETAs grow as the picking queue grows, then the delivery area shrinks (cells farther than 8 minutes are dropped), then new orders are paused with a "busy" message. This is load shedding in physical space, since the bottleneck is people, not servers.
evolution: At 1,000 orders per day and a few warehouses, one database, a polygon check in SQL and a simple dispatch screen are enough. At 100,000 orders per day, add the cell map, a cache for catalog and availability, the outbox and a message queue, and per-warehouse dispatch. At 400,000 per day and 600 warehouses, shard by warehouse, add safety stock and cycle counts, routing with batching and capacity-aware ETAs. At several million orders per day and many countries, run a separate stack per country or region (data and law differ), share only the catalog and the machine learning models for demand forecasting and ETA, and place stock by forecast. At that point the software problem is again a planning problem: where to put stock and drivers before the orders arrive.
followups: How do you decide which warehouse serves an address? || Convert the lat and lng to an H3 or S2 cell id, and look it up in a precomputed in-memory map from cell to a ranked list of warehouses with drive minutes. The map is built offline from road-network drive times, so it follows real roads. The API then filters by open status and capacity.
How do you avoid selling a product that is out of stock? || Reserve with a conditional update in one transaction: reserved = reserved + qty where on_hand - reserved - safety >= qty. If any line fails, roll back the whole cart. Availability shown in the app is cached and approximate, and the reservation is the real check. Safety stock and cycle counts handle the gap between the database and the shelf.
Why partition by warehouse? || The inventory, orders and dispatch of one warehouse are used together, so single-shard transactions are enough, and failures or hot spots stay local. It also matches how the business works. Cross-warehouse operations are rare and handled by explicit steps.
What if two customers buy the last unit at the same moment? || Both run conditional updates. The row lock makes them serial, so the first sees available 1 and succeeds, and the second sees 0 and its transaction rolls back with a 409 for that line. No retry loop is needed, and the second customer gets a message to change the cart.
How do you keep the cached availability correct enough? || Cache a coarse state (in stock, low, out) with a 30-second TTL, and update the entry when an inventory event changes the state. Reads never decide the sale. A wrong "in stock" costs one failed reservation. A wrong "out of stock" costs a lost sale, so we refresh quickly after restocking events.
Why is the road distance not enough for ETA, and what else goes into it? || A road path has turns, one-way streets, traffic and parking time. The ETA is picking queue time plus driver wait plus drive time from the routing engine plus a margin. At peak the picking queue is often the biggest part, so the warehouse's capacity must feed into the estimate.

title: Design a dating app like Tinder
category: Web & product
level: Senior / Staff
concepts: spatialidx, cachelayer, bloom, partitioning, realtime, websocket, nosql, idempotence, funnel, recsys, fanout
summary: A dating app shows a person a stack of nearby profiles, one card at a time. He swipes right to like or left to pass. When two people have liked each other, it is a match, and they can chat. The product is simple, but the system has four hard parts. First, candidates must be found by place, age and preference among tens of millions of users, in about 200 ms, so the data must be split by geography. Second, the app must never show the same person twice, for years of swiping. Third, a match must be detected exactly once even when both people swipe at the same moment. Fourth, the order of the cards decides whether people find anyone, so a multi-stage ranking funnel with a recommender model sits at the center. We design the candidate service with a spatial index, the swipe pipeline on a key-value store, the seen filter with Bloom filters, and the ranking funnel.
requirements: Functional requirements. A user creates a profile with photos, age, gender and a short text, and sets preferences: who he wants to see (gender, age range) and how far (distance up to 160 km). The app shows a deck of cards ordered by the system. Swipe right (like), left (pass) and, with limits, "super like". A mutual like creates a match, and both users are notified at once if they are online. Matched users chat. Users can unmatch, block and report. A profile is never shown again to someone who already swiped on it, unless the user resets. Free users have a daily limit of likes. Paid users can see who liked them and can send boosts that raise their profile in the local queue for 30 minutes.
Non-functional requirements. Latency: a swipe is acknowledged in under 100 ms, and the next card is always ready (the deck is prefetched). A deck refresh takes under 300 ms at p99. Matching: no mutual like is missed and none is created twice. Availability: browsing and swiping work 99.9 percent of the time; chat can degrade to push notifications. Privacy and safety: exact locations are never exposed, blocked users never see each other, and photos are moderated. Quality: the recommender should raise matches and real conversations per active user, not only swipes.
Assumptions. 75 million monthly users and 15 million daily active users. A daily active user makes about 100 swipes per day, and 40 percent are right swipes. About 2 percent of right swipes complete a mutual like. A user opens the app 3 times a day, mostly in the evening. A big city has up to 300,000 active users within a 50 km radius.
estimates: Swipes. 15 million times 100 = 1.5 billion swipes per day, which is 1.5 billion divided by 86,400 = 17,400 per second on average. The peak (evening, 3 times) is about 52,000 per second. Right swipes are 40 percent, about 21,000 per second at peak.
Swipe storage. A swipe record is (swiper id 8 bytes, target id 8 bytes, action 1 byte, time 4 bytes), about 40 bytes with overhead. 1.5 billion times 40 bytes = 60 GB per day, 22 TB per year. Keeping 2 years and 3 replicas needs 22 times 2 times 3 = 132 TB. This is a key-value workload, with writes spread over many users.
Seen filter. For a 1 percent false-positive rate a Bloom filter needs 9.6 bits per element (the optimum is \( -\ln p / (\ln 2)^2 \) bits, with \( p = 0.01 \)). A user with 5,000 swipes needs 5,000 times 9.6 = 48,000 bits = 6 KB. A heavy user with 100,000 swipes needs 120 KB. For the 15 million daily active users at an average of 6 KB: 90 GB. This fits in a cache cluster of 6 to 8 nodes of 16 GB each, and an exact list is kept in the key-value store. An exact set of the same size, with 8 bytes per id, would need 5,000 times 8 = 40 KB per user, which is 6 times more.
Candidate funnel. A deck refresh in a big city: 300,000 active users in the radius. After hard filters (gender, age, mutual preferences, blocks) about 60,000 remain. After removing the seen ones about 55,000. A cheap model scores all of them and keeps the best 1,000. A heavier model scores these 1,000 and a final step builds a deck of 50. Deck builds: 15 million users times 3 sessions = 45 million per day = 520 per second on average, and 1,600 per second at peak. Heavy model scoring: 1,600 times 1,000 = 1.6 million scores per second. A small neural network or tree ensemble on a CPU takes about 20 microseconds per candidate, so 1.6 million times 20 microseconds = 32 CPU cores. The cheap stage over 55,000 users costs more, which is why it must use an index and precomputed scores and not a full scan: 1,600 times 55,000 = 88 million per second at 1 microsecond each is 88 cores. Both are small.
api: Deck: GET /v1/deck?limit=20 returns {cards: [{user_id, name, age, distance_km (rounded), photos[], bio, rank_token}], next_refresh_hint}. The rank_token lets the server log which candidate list and model produced a card.
Swipe: POST /v1/swipes with body {swipes: [{target_id, action: like | pass | super, client_id, ts}]}. The client sends swipes in batches of up to 10 every few seconds, or right away on a like. It returns {results: [{client_id, status: ok | limit_reached, match: {match_id, ...}?}]}. A repeat of the same client_id returns the original result.
Profile and settings: PUT /v1/me/profile, PUT /v1/me/preferences {genders, age_min, age_max, max_distance_km}, POST /v1/me/location {lat, lng} (sent on app open and after a move of more than about 1 km).
Matches and chat: GET /v1/matches?cursor=.. lists matches. WebSocket /v1/stream carries events {type: match | message | typing | unmatch}. POST /v1/matches/{match_id}/messages {client_msg_id, text} sends a message. DELETE /v1/matches/{match_id} unmatches. POST /v1/users/{id}/block and /report.
datamodel: Swipes (key-value or wide-column store, partitioned by swiper id). Table swipe: partition key swiper_id, sort key target_id, columns action, ts. Reading "has A swiped on B" is a point read. Table likes_received: partition key (target_id, bucket), sort key swiper_id, columns ts, super, used for "likes you" and to put people who liked me into my deck. The bucket (0 to 15) spreads very popular targets over several partitions.
Matches. Table match: key match_id = (min(a, b), max(a, b)) as a composite key, columns created_at, state. Because the key is derived from the two ids, creating the same match twice is a conditional insert that fails the second time. Table user_matches: partition key user_id, sort key created_at desc, with match_id and last message time, for the match list.
Profiles and the index. Profile store: key user_id, value the profile document (photos, bio, preferences, last_active). The candidate index is in memory in each geo shard: for each S2 cell of level 12 (about 5 km² each), a list of active users with a few compact fields: user_id, gender, age, last_active bucket, a precomputed desirability score and a quality flag. Shard assignment: S2 cell to shard by a table that balances the number of active users per shard (not area). Seen filter: key seen:{user_id} holds a Bloom filter (a bit array) in the cache cluster, and the exact data is in the swipe table.
design: The deck path. When the user opens the app, or when his prefetched deck falls below 10 cards, the client calls GET deck. The candidate service reads the user's location and preferences, finds the S2 cells that cover his search circle (a region coverer returns about 10 to 20 cells of mixed sizes), and asks the geo shards that own these cells. A circle near a shard border touches up to 4 shards, so the query is a scatter-gather with a short timeout. Each shard applies the hard filters on the compact fields in memory and returns up to 2,000 candidate ids with their scores. The service merges them, removes seen ids using the user's Bloom filter, removes blocked ids and ids that the user passed recently, and sends the rest to ranking. The ranker scores, adds boosts and the people who liked this user, applies diversity rules, and returns the top 50. The deck is stored in the cache, 20 cards are returned, and the profile cards are filled with a multi-get from the profile store and CDN photo URLs.
The swipe path. The app sends the swipe. The swipe service writes (swiper, target, action) to the key-value store with a quorum write, which is idempotent: the same key is set to the same value. For a left swipe it adds the target to the Bloom filter and finishes. For a right swipe it also writes to likes_received for the target and then reads the reverse swipe, (target, swiper). If the reverse is a like, it creates the match with a conditional insert, writes both user_matches rows, and puts a "match" event on the message queue. The response to the swiper contains the match, so he sees "It's a match" immediately. The other user is told through his WebSocket if he is online, or by a push notification.
Fan-out of the match event. A consumer of the match event does the small fan-out: it pushes the event to the gateway of the other user, sends a push notification through the phone vendor's service (APNs for iPhone, FCM for Android) if he is offline, and creates the empty chat. Fan-out here is only to two users, so it is on write. The deck itself is the opposite: it is built on read, when the user asks, because the set of candidates changes with location, preferences and activity, and 15 million precomputed decks would go stale in minutes. The only precomputed piece is the likes_received list, which is read at deck time to put people who already liked the user near the top, since they have a high chance to match.
Chat and real-time. A user keeps one WebSocket to a gateway after he opens the app. The gateway registers (user_id to gateway id) in a presence store with a TTL, refreshed by heartbeats. To send a message the chat service writes it to the message table, then looks up the gateway of the recipient and sends the event; if he is offline, a push notification goes out and the message waits in the table. The client keeps the last message id and fetches the gap after a reconnect.

deepdive: The simultaneous swipe race. Suppose Ana and Ben like each other in the same millisecond on two servers. If each server first reads the reverse key and then writes its own, both reads find nothing and the match is lost forever, because nobody will check again. The fix is to write first and read second: each side writes its own swipe, then reads the other's. Now at least one of the two reads must see the other write, as long as the store gives read-your-writes visibility across the two operations (quorum writes and quorum reads, or a strongly consistent read). Both reads may see each other, and both servers try to create the match, so creating must be idempotent. The match key is the sorted pair of ids, and the insert is conditional ("only if not exists"), so the second attempt fails harmlessly and returns the existing match. This is the same argument as in mutual exclusion algorithms: two flags, each side sets its own then checks the other. A repair job also scans right swipes of the last hours for pairs with no match, as a safety net.
The seen filter and the Bloom filter. A user may swipe 100,000 times in his life. Checking each of 55,000 candidates against an exact list on every deck build is expensive. A Bloom filter answers "have I seen this id?" in a few nanoseconds with memory of 9.6 bits per id at 1 percent error. A false positive means we hide a profile the user has not seen: the loss is one candidate out of 55,000, which nobody notices. A false negative cannot happen, so a swiped profile is never shown again by the filter. The filter cannot delete, so when a user resets his history, or when a filter becomes too full (the false-positive rate rises as it fills), we rebuild it from the exact swipe table. Per-user filters are sized with room to grow (for example for 2 times the expected count) and are rebuilt in the background. The exact table is the truth, and it is used for the final check of the 50 cards in the deck, to catch the rare stale or lost filter.
Geo-sharding and hot cities. Users in one city are looked up together, so the data is partitioned by location: a user lives on the shard that owns his S2 cell. A fixed grid fails because New York has 100 times more active users than rural Montana. We therefore assign cells to shards by the number of active users, with small cells in dense areas and large cells in sparse ones, and we search for the assignment that has the lowest spread of load between shards. Cells are re-balanced slowly, because moving a cell means moving its index entries. A search that crosses a border queries a few shards. Travel is a corner case: when a user changes location (he flies to a new city), his index entry moves to the new shard, and the old entry is removed after a delay. A shard is replicated 3 times for reads and failover.
The ranking funnel. A funnel has stages that trade cost for precision. Stage 1 is retrieval: spatial index plus hard filters, producing about 55,000 candidates in under 20 ms. Stage 2 is a light scorer (a linear model or small tree over a few compact features, like precomputed desirability, last active time and distance) that cuts to 1,000. Stage 3 is the heavy ranker (a neural network with user and candidate embeddings, photo quality and interaction history) that predicts the probability of a mutual like: \( P(\text{match}) = P(a \text{ likes } b) \times P(b \text{ likes } a) \). Using both sides makes the system two-sided: it does not just show people each user finds attractive, it shows people who would like him back. Stage 4 re-ranks for the whole marketplace: diversity, freshness for new users, a limit on how often a very popular profile is shown (an exposure cap), and fairness rules. Popularity concentration is the main failure of a naive recommender, since the top 1 percent of profiles would take most right swipes and almost never swipe back, and the rest would feel invisible.
Data, labels and training. The raw signal is swipes: like, pass and their context (position in the deck, time spent on the card, whether the person saw the first photo only). Labels: a right swipe is a weak positive for "a likes b", a mutual like is a stronger label, and a conversation of more than a few messages is the best label for real success, but it comes late and is rare. Pass is not always negative (the user may have swiped without looking). The training pipeline builds a daily dataset from the swipe log with features as they were at the time of the swipe (a feature store with point-in-time joins to avoid leakage), trains the model, runs offline checks, and publishes the model. The serving path loads user features from a cache, candidate features from the shard memory and runs the model in batches of 1,000.
Evaluation and A/B tests. Offline: AUC and log loss for like prediction, calibration (the predicted probability must match the observed rate), and ranking metrics such as NDCG@50 for matches. Online: matches per daily active user, conversations that pass 3 messages, retention at 7 days, and guardrails (report rate, swipe volume per user, share of matches that go to the top 1 percent). A/B tests in a marketplace have interference: if group A's model makes its users take more matches from a limited supply of people, group B's users have fewer, and the difference is wrong. We randomize by geo cluster or run switchback tests (a whole city on model A for a day, then on B) for the main decisions.
Monitoring and failure modes of the model. We monitor the input features (distribution drift), the output score distribution, the share of the top 1 percent in exposures, the match rate by city, and cold-start performance. A feedback loop exists: the model decides who is shown, so the data it learns from is data shaped by itself. We keep a small random-exploration share (for example 2 percent of cards) to measure true preferences without bias. If the heavy ranker is slow or down, the service serves the stage 2 order, which is worse but safe.
tradeoffs: Geo-sharding versus sharding by user id. Choose geo-sharding when the main query is "find people near me", because it keeps a query on 1 to 4 shards and the index in memory. Choose sharding by user id for the swipe table, the profile store and chat, where the access is by key and the load is spread evenly. Geo-sharding gives up even load (cities are hot) and makes travelers move; user-id sharding gives up locality, so a geo query would hit every shard.
Bloom filter versus an exact set. Choose a Bloom filter in the cache for the first pass, since it is 5 to 6 times smaller than an exact set and answers in nanoseconds, and a 1 percent loss of candidates is harmless. Choose an exact set (or a database lookup) when a false positive is costly, for example "has this user already been charged" or in the final 50-card check. You give up deletion and a little recall with the filter, and memory and latency with the exact set.
Strong versus eventual consistency for swipes. Choose quorum writes and quorum reads for the swipe table, because a missed match is a lost user experience that cannot be repaired by later reads. Choose eventual consistency for the seen filter, the deck cache and the match list, where a delay of seconds is harmless. Quorum operations cost about 2 times the latency and lower availability under a zone failure compared with a single-replica read, so we limit them to the match check.
failures: A geo shard fails. Its replicas serve reads; the index is rebuilt from the profile store in minutes if all replicas are lost. During the gap users in that area get decks from a cached deck or from neighboring shards with a bigger radius. Swipes continue, since they do not depend on the index.
Cache cluster loss (deck and Bloom filters). The decks are rebuilt on demand, which raises compute load by about 10 times for a few minutes; we protect with load shedding by serving smaller decks (20 cards) and with request coalescing. The Bloom filters are rebuilt from the exact swipe table at 5,000 ids per user; during the rebuild the final exact check on the 50-card deck prevents repeats.
Ranker or feature store down. The funnel falls back to stage 2 scores or even to a simple order by distance and last active time. The match rate drops a little, but the app works. A circuit breaker opens when the ranker latency passes 150 ms.
evolution: At 10,000 users in one city, a single database with a spatial query (PostGIS) and a simple SQL check for mutual likes is enough. At 1 million users, add a cache, a key-value store for swipes, and the first Bloom filters. At 15 million daily users, move to an in-memory geo index sharded by S2 cells, the multi-stage funnel, a feature store and online experiments. At 100 million and many countries, run regional stacks (data residency and latency), keep profile and swipe stores in the user's home region, route a traveler's queries to the region where he is, and invest in a learned retrieval stage (embeddings with approximate nearest neighbor search) to replace the hand-written filters and the light scorer.
followups: How do you find nearby candidates quickly? || Split the world into S2 or H3 cells, keep an in-memory index per cell of active users with compact fields, and partition cells over shards by active user count. A search circle becomes 10 to 20 covering cells, which map to 1 to 4 shards. Hard filters run in memory and return a few thousand ids in tens of milliseconds.
How do you make sure you never show the same profile twice? || Keep the exact swipe history in the key-value store and a per-user Bloom filter in the cache. The filter answers in nanoseconds, with no false negatives and about 1 percent false positives, which only hides a few unseen profiles. Rebuild the filter from the exact data when it fills up or on reset, and check the final deck against the exact store.
How do you detect a match when both users swipe at the same instant? || Write your own swipe first, then read the reverse swipe, with quorum or strongly consistent reads. At least one of the two sees the other. If both see it, the conditional insert on the match key (the sorted pair) makes the creation idempotent. A repair job scans recent right swipes as a safety net.
How does the recommender decide the order of cards? || It is a funnel: spatial retrieval and filters, a cheap scorer that cuts to 1,000, a heavy model that predicts the mutual like probability (both directions), and a marketplace re-ranker for diversity, exposure caps and new users. Labels come from swipes and matches, with a feature store giving point-in-time features.
How do you run A/B tests on a two-sided marketplace? || Users compete for the same limited attention, so treatment and control interfere. Randomize by geographic cluster or use switchback tests by city and time. Watch marketplace-level metrics such as matches per daily user, share of exposure to top profiles and retention, not only the swipe-level click rate.
