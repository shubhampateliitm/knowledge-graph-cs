@@ anomaly
value: Anomaly detection finds events that do not look like the rest: a card payment from a new country at 3 a.m., a sudden fall in daily impressions, a device that clicks 40 ads a minute, a server whose latency moves away from its usual pattern. Fraud detection is the same problem with a financial or adversarial edge, where someone is trying to look normal. The main difficulty is that you rarely have good examples of the thing you want to find. Fraud may be 0.1% of events, new attacks look different from old ones, and labels arrive weeks late through chargebacks or manual review.
For that reason the standard approach is to describe what normal looks like and to measure how far each event is from it. The description can be a statistical baseline (mean and spread by hour of week), a tree-based isolation score, a distance to the nearest cluster, a reconstruction error from an autoencoder, or features taken from a graph of shared devices and accounts. A score above a threshold creates an alert, and rules and human review confirm it. Where some labeled fraud exists, a supervised classifier is added, because labeled examples are the strongest signal there is.
The layer above uses this everywhere. Data quality and observability systems run volume and distribution monitors on pipelines, which are anomaly detectors on counts. Alerting watches metrics for unusual values. Security teams look for intrusions and account takeover. In programmatic advertising, invalid-traffic detection filters bots, click farms and spoofed apps before an advertiser pays. The layers below supply the tools. Probability and statistics (L4) define "unusual" as a small probability under a model, give tail bounds, and show how many false alerts a threshold will create. Decision trees and boosting (L2) give isolation forests and supervised fraud models. Clustering (L2) gives distance-to-cluster and mixture-model scores.
terms: Baseline — the model of normal behaviour, such as an expected value and a spread for each hour of the week.
Anomaly score — a number that grows as an event looks less normal; an alert is a score above a threshold.
Point, contextual and collective anomaly — one strange value; a value strange only in its context (high traffic at 4 a.m.); a group of events that is strange only together (a burst of small payments).
Z-score — the number of standard deviations a value is from the mean, \( z = (x - \mu)/\sigma \).
Isolation forest — an ensemble of random trees; points that are separated from the rest in few random splits are anomalies.
Reconstruction error — the difference between an input and what an autoencoder rebuilds from its compressed form; high error means the input is unlike the training data.
Base rate — the share of events that are truly anomalous; it controls how many alerts are real.
Precision at k — the share of the top k highest-scored events that are truly anomalous.
Concept drift — the slow change of what is normal, which makes an old baseline wrong.
history: The statistics of outliers is old. Chauvenet (1863) and Peirce (1852) proposed rules to reject observations that are too far from the mean, and Grubbs (1950, 1969) gave the test that is still taught. Shewhart (1920s) invented the control chart at Bell Labs: plot a process measurement over time with limits at three standard deviations, and investigate points outside. These tools assume one roughly normal quantity at a time.
Fraud detection moved to machine learning in the 1990s as banks collected card data. Neural networks for card fraud were deployed in the early 1990s, and large processors have used models since. Breunig, Kriegel, Ng and Sander's local outlier factor (2000) compared the density around a point to the density around its neighbours, which handles clusters of different density. Schölkopf and colleagues' one-class support vector machine (2001) learned a boundary around normal data. Chandola, Banerjee and Kumar's survey (2009) organized the field into statistical, nearest-neighbour, clustering and classification techniques.
Liu, Ting and Zhou's isolation forest (2008) made a conceptual change: do not model normal and measure distance, just count how easy it is to separate a point with random splits. It is fast, has few parameters and works in many dimensions, and became a default. Deep methods followed: autoencoders for reconstruction error, variational and adversarial models, and self-supervised methods (Pang and colleagues' 2021 survey covers them). For time series, Twitter's S-H-ESD method (Hochenbaum, Vallis and Kejariwal, 2017) combined seasonal decomposition with a robust test and became the pattern for metric monitoring. In fraud, the last decade added graph features, because organized fraud shares devices, addresses and payment instruments, and gradient-boosted trees on engineered features became the strong supervised baseline.
how: Every detector has three steps: build a model of normal, score each event by its distance from the model, and decide a threshold. The methods differ in step one.
Statistical baselines are the simplest. For a metric such as hourly impressions, keep a baseline per hour of week, because traffic has strong daily and weekly cycles. A robust choice uses the median and the median absolute deviation (MAD) instead of the mean and standard deviation, since the outliers you want to catch would otherwise distort the baseline. The score is \( z = (x - \text{median}) / (1.4826 \times \text{MAD}) \); the constant makes the MAD comparable to a standard deviation for normal data. Probability tells us what a z-score means. For normal data, \( P(|Z| > 3) \approx 0.0027 \). Without any assumption about the distribution, Chebyshev's inequality still gives \( P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le 1/k^2 \), so a deviation of five standard deviations has at most a 4% probability, a weak but safe bound. For time series, remove trend and seasonality first (STL decomposition or a forecast model) and score the residual.
Isolation forest uses trees (the decision-tree concept, but with random splits and no labels). Build 100 trees. Each tree is grown on a random sample of 256 points. At each node choose a random feature and a random split value between that feature's minimum and maximum in the node, and continue until each point is alone or a depth limit is reached. A point far from the others is separated after very few splits, while a point inside a dense region needs many. The anomaly score of a point uses the average path length \( E[h(x)] \) over all trees:
\[ s(x) = 2^{-E[h(x)]/c(n)}, \qquad c(n) = 2H(n-1) - \frac{2(n-1)}{n} \]
where \( H(i) \approx \ln i + 0.5772 \) is the harmonic number and \( c(n) \) is the average path length of an unsuccessful search in a binary search tree, used to normalize. Scores near 1 are anomalies, and scores below about 0.5 are normal. Building needs no labels and no distance computations, and the cost is linear in the sample size.
Clustering gives distance-based scores. Fit k-means or a Gaussian mixture model to normal data, and score an event by its distance to the nearest centre, or by the negative log-likelihood under the mixture (which accounts for the spread of each cluster). Local outlier factor goes further and compares local densities, so a point that is sparse relative to its neighbours scores high even if the whole region is sparse. Autoencoders learn to compress and rebuild normal inputs. Anything that the network cannot rebuild well, because it differs from the training data, has high reconstruction error.
When labels exist, use supervised learning on the labeled part. Gradient-boosted trees on features such as amount, time since last transaction, count of events per device in the last hour, and mismatch between billing and shipping country are the strong baseline in fraud. Class imbalance is handled by sample weights or undersampling the majority class (and then correcting the probabilities), and the model is evaluated by precision-recall curves, not accuracy. Unsupervised scores are often fed in as features of the supervised model, so the two approaches combine.
Graph features catch what single events cannot show. Compute, for each account, the number of distinct devices it used, the number of accounts that share its device, the degree and the connected-component size, and the age of the neighbours. A cluster of 40 new accounts sharing 3 devices is a ring, although each account looks normal. These features are cheap to compute in a batch job and powerful in practice.
Deployment completes the system. Scores are thresholded using the cost of each error: if reviewing an alert costs 5 minutes and a missed fraud costs 200 dollars, the threshold is the score at which the expected loss equals the review cost. A rules layer adds business knowledge, and a review queue is ordered by score. Confirmed outcomes flow back as labels, and the baseline is refreshed so that it follows drift. The pipeline stays under human control because attackers change behaviour once they learn what triggers alerts.

example: Take two checks, one statistical and one base-rate calculation.
Step 1: a metric. Daily impressions of a campaign have a baseline mean of 10.0 million and a standard deviation of 0.5 million for this weekday. Today the number is 8.2 million. The z-score is (8.2 − 10.0) / 0.5 = −3.6. For normal data the two-sided probability of a deviation this large is about 0.0003, so this is unusual. But it is not proof: a one-in-3,000 event will happen to some campaign every few days if you watch thousands of them.
Step 2: multiple checks. Suppose you evaluate each metric once per minute with an alert at |z| > 3, which has a false alarm probability of 0.0027 per check for normal data. Each metric makes 1,440 checks a day, so you expect 1,440 × 0.0027 ≈ 3.9 false alerts per metric per day. With 500 metrics that is almost 2,000 false alerts a day. This is why monitors need longer windows, persistence rules ("three minutes in a row"), or a higher threshold, and why alert fatigue is the real cost.
Step 3: base rate. A card processor sees 1,000,000 transactions, of which 0.1% (1,000) are fraud. A detector catches 90% of fraud (900 cases) and wrongly flags 1% of normal transactions (9,990 cases). The alerts total 900 + 9,990 = 10,890, so precision is 900 / 10,890 = 8.3%. Only about 1 alert in 12 is real, although the detector looks excellent (90% recall, 99% specificity). If an analyst reviews an alert in 3 minutes, the team spends 10,890 × 3 = 32,670 minutes, about 545 hours, to find 900 frauds.
Step 4: improve precision. Cut the false positive rate to 0.2% at the same 90% recall: false alerts become 1,998, alerts total 2,898, precision becomes 31%. The review time is 145 hours. A 5-times reduction in false positives is worth more than a few points of recall, which is why the metric to track is precision at the volume your team can review, say the top 500 alerts a day.
Step 5: isolation score. With a sample of n = 256, \( H(255) \approx \ln 255 + 0.5772 = 6.118 \), so \( c(256) = 2 \times 6.118 - 2 \times 255/256 \approx 10.24 \). A point in a dense region with an average path length of 10 gets \( s = 2^{-10/10.24} \approx 0.51 \). A far point isolated after an average of 4 splits gets \( s = 2^{-4/10.24} \approx 0.76 \). You then choose a threshold such as the top 0.5% of scores, not a fixed number such as 0.76, because the score scale depends on data.
intuition: An anomaly detector is a security guard who knows every regular face in the building: no list of criminals is needed, and the guard stops anyone who does not fit.
The design is natural because the unusual is defined against the usual, and the usual is plentiful and well-labelled by default. Rare events can never be fully described in advance, since attackers choose them to be new, so describing the majority and flagging the exception covers cases nobody has seen. The price is the same as for the guard: strangers who are innocent are stopped too, and an intruder who dresses like a regular is not. Everything else in the field is about reducing that price with context, with better features, and with human review.
mistakes: People often think a detector with 99% accuracy is good for fraud. In fact if 99.9% of events are normal, a model that never flags anything has 99.9% accuracy and catches nothing. With a base rate this low, even a 1% false positive rate buries real cases, as the 8.3% precision example shows. Use precision-recall measures at the review volume you can afford.
People often think an unusual event is a bad event. In fact an anomaly only means rare under the model. A marketing campaign, a holiday or a product launch produces large, legitimate changes. Models need context features (calendar, deploys, campaigns), and alerts should say how the event differs, not only that it does.
People often think more complex models are better detectors. In fact on tabular event data with some labels, gradient-boosted trees with well-chosen features usually beat deep models, and a median-and-MAD baseline per hour of week catches most operational incidents. Choose the simple method first and ask what it misses.
People often think the model is finished once it is trained. In fact fraud is adversarial: when fraudsters see which behaviour is blocked, they change it. What is normal also drifts. A static model decays, so you need monitoring of score distributions, label feedback and regular retraining.
friction: Labels are the biggest problem. They are rare, delayed, biased and sometimes wrong. A chargeback arrives 30 to 90 days after a payment, so the most recent data has no labels yet, and events that were blocked have no outcome at all, so the model never learns whether the block was right. This selection effect means that training only on reviewed cases teaches the model to reproduce past decisions.
Adversaries and drift add moving targets. Attackers probe thresholds, spread activity across many devices and use real device fingerprints. Legitimate behaviour changes with product releases, so a model must separate "the world changed" from "something is wrong". Interpretation is another problem. An alert with only a score cannot be acted upon; reviewers need the reason (which features contributed), and deep models give this poorly. Finally, the cost of false positives is not only review time. In fraud, blocking a good customer loses revenue and trust, and in advertising, wrongly filtering real traffic makes a publisher lose money.
tradeoffs: Choose a simple statistical baseline (median and MAD per hour of week, plus persistence rules) for operational metrics and pipeline monitors: it is cheap, explainable and works with no labels. Choose a learned model when behaviour depends on many features together and the baseline produces too many false alerts. You give up transparency for coverage.
Choose a supervised classifier when you have at least a few thousand confirmed positives and labels arrive within weeks; choose unsupervised or semi-supervised detection when positives are very few, the threat is new, or labels are late. In practice combine them: unsupervised scores and graph features as inputs to a supervised model, trained on recent data with time-based splits.
Choose isolation forest as the first unsupervised model on mixed numeric features with thousands to millions of rows: it scales linearly and needs little tuning. Choose local outlier factor or a mixture model when density varies by region and you have fewer than about 100,000 points. Choose an autoencoder when inputs are high-dimensional and structured (images, long sequences) and you have a lot of normal data. Each of the last two costs more tuning and is harder to explain.
Choose a threshold from costs: set it where the expected cost of review equals the expected loss prevented. A tighter threshold raises precision and lowers recall, and the right balance depends on review capacity and the loss per missed case. Choose real-time scoring (milliseconds, features from a fast store) for payments and bid requests, and batch scoring (hourly or daily) for account rings and pipeline checks, accepting that batch detection reacts later.
practice: Define the cost of each error before building anything. Ask how many alerts a day the team can review and what a missed case costs. Then choose the metric: precision at the reviewable volume, recall at that precision, and time to detect. Keep an unbiased audit sample: let a small random share of traffic through without blocking and review it, so you can estimate the true rate and measure the model on cases it did not decide.
Build features that capture velocity and context: counts and sums over 1 minute, 1 hour and 1 day per device, account and IP; the ratio to the entity's own history; the time since the previous event; and mismatches between fields. Compute the same features in the batch training job and in the real-time path from one definition, or training and serving will disagree. Split train and test by time. For metrics, use seasonal baselines by hour of week, check the baseline against holidays, and require persistence or a minimum volume to avoid alerts on tiny counts.
Close the loop. Send alerts to a queue with the reason codes, record the reviewer's decision, retrain with those labels, and watch the alert rate and score distribution for drift. Combine layers: hard rules for known bad patterns, a model for ranking, and human review for the top of the list. In ad traffic, follow industry standards for invalid traffic (the Media Rating Council guidelines separate general invalid traffic, found by lists and simple rules, from sophisticated invalid traffic, found by analysis) and keep both kinds of filtering auditable.
interview: A fraud model has 99.5% accuracy. Is it good? What do you ask? || Accuracy is meaningless at a low base rate. I would ask for the base rate, then precision and recall at the volume the review team can handle, a precision-recall curve, and the cost per false positive and per missed case. Then I would check how labels were created and whether the test set is time-split.
You have 500 metrics checked every minute with a 3-sigma rule, and the on-call team is flooded. How do you fix it? || The expected false alerts are 500 × 1,440 × 0.0027 ≈ 1,900 a day even if nothing is wrong. I would use seasonal baselines, require persistence over several minutes, aggregate related alerts, use a robust score, and set thresholds from the on-call capacity. I would also review which alerts people actually acted on.
How do you build a fraud detector when you have almost no labels? || Start with rules and an unsupervised score (isolation forest or per-entity baselines) plus graph features. Send the top-scored cases to review to create labels, and keep a random audit sample to estimate the base rate. Once there are enough labels, train a boosted-tree model and use the unsupervised scores as features.
Why can training on only reviewed or blocked cases hurt? || The label depends on what the old system chose to review, so the model learns the old system's behaviour (selection bias) and cannot find fraud the old system missed. Randomized sampling of traffic for review, and counterfactual or audit data, give an unbiased view.
Fraudsters adapt to your model within weeks. What do you change? || Monitor score distributions and the fraud rate by segment, retrain on recent data often, use features that are hard to fake (behaviour over time, network structure), keep some rules and models secret and diverse, and add layers so that evading one does not evade all. Expect an arms race, not a final fix.
quiz: What is the base rate and why does it matter for anomaly detection? || It is the share of events that are truly anomalous. When it is very low, even a small false positive rate produces many more false alerts than real detections, so precision is low.
Why does an isolation forest give anomalies short average path lengths? || Random splits separate an isolated point from the others very quickly, in a few splits, while points inside a dense region need many splits to be separated.
Why use the median and MAD instead of the mean and standard deviation for a baseline? || The mean and standard deviation are pulled by the very outliers you want to detect, which inflates the spread and hides them. The median and MAD are robust to a few extreme values.
A detector flags 1% of 2,000,000 normal events and catches 95% of 2,000 frauds. What is its precision? || False alerts are 1% of 1,998,000 ≈ 19,980. True alerts are 0.95 × 2,000 = 1,900. Precision is 1,900 / (1,900 + 19,980) ≈ 8.7%.
When would you prefer a supervised model over an unsupervised detector, and what is the risk? || When you have enough confirmed labels that are reasonably recent. The risk is that it learns only known patterns and the selection bias of past reviews, so new attacks pass it.
