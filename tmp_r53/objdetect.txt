@@ objdetect
value: Object detection answers "what is in this image, and where?". The output is a list of boxes, each with a class label and a confidence score. Segmentation goes one step further and labels pixels: semantic segmentation gives every pixel a class, instance segmentation gives each separate object its own mask, and panoptic segmentation does both. Classification, in contrast, gives one label per image and says nothing about position or count.
This matters wherever software has to act on a scene and not just describe it. A car must know where the pedestrians are, a factory camera must locate a cracked part, a phone must separate a person from the background, a medical tool must outline a tumour, and an ad or content system must find the logos and faces in a video frame. The difficulty is that the number of objects is unknown, their sizes differ by a factor of 100, and they can overlap. A fixed-size output vector cannot describe that.
Detectors solve it in a common way. A backbone network turns the image into feature maps. At many positions and scales, a small head predicts "is there an object centred here, what class, and how should the box be adjusted". This produces thousands of overlapping candidate boxes, and a post-processing step called non-maximum suppression (NMS) keeps the best box for each object. The layers below supply the pieces. Convolution (L2) gives the backbone that shares weights across positions, so that the same detector works anywhere in the picture. Linear algebra (L4) turns convolutions into large matrix multiplications that GPUs run fast, and provides the geometry of boxes. Optimization (L4) trains the network with a combined loss for class, box position and, in segmentation, mask shape. For the layer above, computer vision uses detectors and segmenters as basic building blocks for tracking, counting, autonomous driving, retail and video analysis.
terms: Bounding box — a rectangle around an object, written as (x1, y1, x2, y2) or as centre, width and height.
IoU (intersection over union) — the area where two boxes overlap divided by the area they cover together; 1 means identical, 0 means no overlap.
Anchor — a reference box of a chosen size and shape placed at every position of a feature map; the network predicts a small correction to it.
Backbone, neck, head — the backbone extracts features, the neck (for example a feature pyramid) combines scales, and the head makes the box and class predictions.
Non-maximum suppression (NMS) — a procedure that sorts boxes by score and removes any box that overlaps a better one by more than a set IoU.
Feature pyramid — feature maps at several resolutions, so that large objects are found on coarse maps and small objects on fine maps.
mAP (mean average precision) — the area under the precision-recall curve, averaged over classes, and for COCO also over IoU thresholds from 0.5 to 0.95.
Semantic, instance, panoptic segmentation — per-pixel classes; a separate mask per object; and both together.
Mask — a per-pixel yes/no map of which pixels belong to one object.
history: Before deep learning, detection used hand-made features and a sliding window. Viola and Jones (2001) made real-time face detection possible with simple Haar features and a cascade of classifiers that rejected most windows early. Dalal and Triggs (2005) used histograms of oriented gradients with a linear SVM for pedestrians, and Felzenszwalb and colleagues' deformable part model (2008) dominated the PASCAL VOC benchmark for years.
Girshick and colleagues' R-CNN (2014) changed the field. It proposed about 2,000 regions per image with an older algorithm (selective search), ran a convolutional network on each region and classified it. It was accurate but slow, because the network ran 2,000 times per image. Fast R-CNN (Girshick, 2015) ran the backbone once and cut features for each region from the shared map. Faster R-CNN (Ren, He, Girshick and Sun, 2015) replaced selective search with a learned region proposal network that uses anchors, and it made the whole detector one network trained end to end.
A second family dropped the proposal stage. YOLO (Redmon and colleagues, 2015) divided the image into a grid and predicted boxes and classes directly in one pass, reaching real-time speeds of about 45 frames per second in its first version. SSD (Liu and colleagues, 2015) predicted from several feature maps at different scales. These single-stage detectors were fast but initially less accurate. Lin and colleagues' RetinaNet (2017) explained why: the huge number of easy background boxes dominated the loss. Their focal loss reduced the weight of easy examples, and single-stage detectors caught up. The feature pyramid network (Lin and colleagues, 2017) became the standard way to handle scale.
For segmentation, Long, Shelhamer and Darrell's fully convolutional networks (2014) produced a class map by making every layer convolutional, and U-Net (Ronneberger and colleagues, 2015) became the standard for medical images. Mask R-CNN (He and colleagues, 2017) added a mask branch to Faster R-CNN. Carion and colleagues' DETR (2020) used a transformer and matched predictions to objects one to one, so that no NMS was needed. In 2023 Meta's Segment Anything Model (SAM) was trained on a dataset of over a billion masks to segment objects from prompts such as a point or a box, and SAM 2 (2024) extended it to video.
how: A modern detector has three parts. The backbone is a convolutional network (or a vision transformer) that turns an image of, say, 640 × 640 pixels into feature maps at strides 8, 16 and 32, so the maps are 80 × 80, 40 × 40 and 20 × 20. This is the convolution concept at work: filters with shared weights slide across the image, so a feature that detects a wheel works at every position, and deeper layers see larger regions. The neck, usually a feature pyramid, passes information from the coarse maps down to the fine maps so that every scale has both detail and meaning. The head is a small convolution applied at every position of every map, with outputs for class scores and box coordinates.
Each position is responsible for objects near it. In an anchor-based detector, every position has several anchors, for example 3 sizes times 3 aspect ratios, so 9 anchors per position. Faster R-CNN on a 1000 × 600 image with stride 16 has about 60 × 40 × 9 ≈ 21,600 anchors. For each anchor the head predicts a class score and four numbers that move and resize the anchor:
\[ t_x = \frac{x - x_a}{w_a}, \quad t_y = \frac{y - y_a}{h_a}, \quad t_w = \ln\frac{w}{w_a}, \quad t_h = \ln\frac{h}{h_a} \]
The offsets are relative to the anchor size, so one set of weights works for small and large boxes, and the logarithm keeps widths positive. Anchor-free detectors (FCOS, CenterNet, recent YOLO versions) skip the preset shapes and predict distances from the position to the four box sides, or the centre of the object, directly.
Training must decide which predictions are responsible for which ground-truth box. A common rule marks an anchor as positive if its IoU with a ground-truth box is above 0.5 to 0.7, negative if it is below 0.3 to 0.4, and ignores the rest. Newer methods assign by a dynamic cost that combines classification and box quality. The loss then has two parts: a classification loss for every anchor, and a box regression loss (smooth L1 or an IoU-based loss) only for positives. This is where optimization enters: the two terms have different scales, and the sum is minimized by stochastic gradient descent. Most anchors are background, often 99.9% of them, so the class loss must handle imbalance. Faster R-CNN samples a balanced batch of anchors. RetinaNet uses focal loss, \( \text{FL}(p_t) = -(1 - p_t)^{\gamma} \ln p_t \) with \( \gamma = 2 \), which multiplies the loss of a confident correct prediction (for example \( p_t = 0.9 \)) by \( 0.1^2 = 0.01 \), so the many easy negatives stop dominating.
At inference the head produces tens of thousands of scored boxes, and many of them cover the same object. Non-maximum suppression cleans this up in a loop: sort all boxes of a class by score; take the highest, keep it, delete every remaining box whose IoU with it is above a threshold (commonly 0.5 to 0.7); repeat with the next highest remaining box. It is a greedy algorithm and runs in \( O(n^2) \) in the worst case, which is acceptable after a score filter has reduced n to a few hundred.
Two-stage detectors add a second step. The proposal network suggests about 300 to 2,000 regions, and for each one the network pools a fixed-size feature (RoI pooling or the more precise RoIAlign, which uses bilinear interpolation instead of rounding coordinates) and refines the class and box. This costs more time but tends to be more accurate for small or crowded objects. For instance segmentation, Mask R-CNN adds a small convolutional branch that predicts a 28 × 28 mask per region for its class, and the mask is resized into the box. Semantic segmentation networks such as U-Net or DeepLab instead produce a class score for every pixel, through an encoder that shrinks the image and a decoder that upsamples it, with skip connections that carry fine detail across. The loss is per-pixel cross-entropy, often combined with a Dice or IoU loss to cope with small objects.
DETR changes the post-processing. A transformer decoder outputs a fixed set of, say, 100 predictions. During training, the Hungarian algorithm finds the best one-to-one match between predictions and ground-truth boxes, and only matched predictions receive box losses, while the rest must predict "no object". Because the model learns not to duplicate, NMS is not needed at inference. The price was slow convergence, which later variants (Deformable DETR, RT-DETR) reduced.

example: Four detections arrive for one class. Boxes are written (x1, y1, x2, y2).
Box A = (10, 10, 110, 110), score 0.90. Box C = (20, 20, 120, 120), score 0.85. Box B = (30, 30, 130, 130), score 0.60. Box D = (300, 300, 380, 380), score 0.70.
Step 1: compute IoU between A and C. Each box has area 100 × 100 = 10,000. The overlap is the square from (20, 20) to (110, 110), so 90 × 90 = 8,100. The union is 10,000 + 10,000 − 8,100 = 11,900. IoU = 8,100 / 11,900 = 0.68.
Step 2: compute IoU between A and B. The overlap is from (30, 30) to (110, 110), so 80 × 80 = 6,400. The union is 20,000 − 6,400 = 13,600. IoU = 6,400 / 13,600 = 0.47.
Step 3: run NMS with threshold 0.5. Sort by score: A 0.90, C 0.85, D 0.70, B 0.60. Keep A. C has IoU 0.68 with A, which is above 0.5, so delete it. D does not overlap A, so it stays. Take D next and keep it. B has IoU 0.47 with A, below 0.5, so it also survives, and B has no overlap with D. Final result: A, D and B. If the three boxes A, C and B were all the same person, we now have a duplicate, B.
Step 4: lower the threshold to 0.4. Now B is deleted because 0.47 is above 0.4. The cost appears in a crowd. If B were a different person standing right behind A with 0.47 overlap, the lower threshold would delete a real object. This is why the threshold depends on the data, and why Soft-NMS (which lowers the score of an overlapping box by a factor that grows with IoU, instead of deleting it) and DETR-style one-to-one matching exist.
Step 5: evaluate. A prediction counts as correct if its class matches and its IoU with a ground-truth box is at least 0.5. Precision is correct predictions divided by all predictions, and recall is correct predictions divided by all ground-truth boxes. Sweeping the confidence threshold gives a precision-recall curve whose area is the average precision. COCO averages this over IoU thresholds 0.50, 0.55, …, 0.95, so a detector must also place boxes tightly to score well.
intuition: A detector is a very large set of tiny guards, each watching one spot at one size, who all shout "object here" at once, followed by an editor who keeps only the loudest shout for each object.
The design is natural because an image has no fixed number of objects, but it has a fixed number of positions. Turning "find all objects" into "for every position and size, answer one local question" makes the output a fixed-size grid that a convolutional network can produce, and weight sharing makes the answer the same everywhere. The cost of this trick is duplicates, since several neighbouring positions will see the same object, and the editor (NMS, or a one-to-one training rule) removes them.
mistakes: People often think a detector looks at the image many times with a sliding window. In fact modern detectors run the backbone once and read all positions from the shared feature map. The old sliding window with a separate network call per window is what R-CNN did, and it is why R-CNN took seconds per image.
People often think a higher mAP at IoU 0.5 means the boxes are good. In fact mAP@0.5 accepts a box that overlaps only half. COCO mAP averages up to 0.95, and a detector can score well at 0.5 and poorly at 0.9. If you need accurate boxes, as for measuring or robotic grasping, look at the strict thresholds.
People often think NMS is a harmless cleaning step. In fact it is a hand-tuned rule that decides recall in crowds, and it is not differentiable, so the network is never trained for it. A threshold that is right for cars on a road is wrong for people in a stadium.
People often think segmentation is detection with more detail. In fact semantic segmentation cannot count: two touching cars have the same class label and form one connected region. Instance or panoptic segmentation is needed when you must separate individual objects.
People often think a model trained on a public dataset will work on your cameras. In fact detectors depend heavily on viewpoint, lighting, object size and image quality. A model trained on COCO photos, where objects are large and centred, typically loses a large share of its accuracy on small objects in a wide-angle surveillance or drone view.
friction: Small objects are the persistent weakness. An object of 16 × 16 pixels covers one cell of a stride-16 map, so its features are mixed with the background after several downsampling steps. Higher input resolution, finer pyramid levels and tiling help, and each costs compute that grows with the number of pixels. Occlusion and crowds break the one-box-per-object assumption, and unusual views, rare classes and the long tail of categories remain hard.
Labels are expensive. A box takes seconds and a tight polygon mask takes close to a minute per object, and labelers disagree at object edges, so the ground truth has noise. Class imbalance and annotation bias limit what the model can learn. Foundation models like SAM reduce the cost by producing masks from prompts, but they do not know which objects matter for your task, and their masks still need review.
Speed and size are a constant trade. Real-time video at 30 frames per second leaves about 33 ms per frame for the whole pipeline, including decode, resize, inference and NMS. Post-processing on the CPU can take a significant share of that, and the models that look best in papers may not fit an edge device. Finally, detectors are brittle outside their training distribution: they can be fooled by small changes, produce confident false positives on textures, and give scores that are poorly calibrated as probabilities.
tradeoffs: Choose a two-stage detector (Faster R-CNN family) when accuracy on small or crowded objects matters more than speed, you run offline or on a strong GPU, and you can afford tens to a few hundred milliseconds per image. Choose a single-stage detector (YOLO, SSD, RetinaNet) when you need real-time rates of 30 to 100 or more frames per second or an edge deployment. You give up a few points of accuracy on the hardest cases and gain a simpler, faster pipeline.
Choose a DETR-style detector when NMS is a problem, as in dense scenes, or when you want a simple end-to-end pipeline and have enough data and training budget. Choose a convolutional single-stage model when you have limited data or compute, since transformer detectors usually need more training and data to converge.
Choose semantic segmentation when you need regions, not objects (road, sky, tissue); choose instance segmentation when you must count or track individual objects; choose a promptable model (SAM) when you need masks quickly for labeling or for classes you did not train on, and fine-tune a task-specific model when you need reliable results at scale.
practice: Start with a pre-trained detector and fine-tune it on a few thousand images from your own cameras. Libraries such as Torchvision, Detectron2, MMDetection and Ultralytics provide trained models and training loops. Check the licence of a model before you use it commercially, since some popular detectors are released under copyleft licences.
Evaluate what the product needs. Report per-class average precision, a precision-recall curve at your operating threshold, and error buckets by object size and by scene type (day and night, near and far). Look at false positives and misses in pictures; the pattern, such as "misses everything under 30 pixels", points to the fix (higher resolution or tiling). Tune the confidence threshold and the NMS IoU on a validation set from your own domain, not on COCO.
For deployment, export to an optimized runtime (TensorRT, ONNX Runtime, Core ML), use 16-bit or 8-bit quantization after checking accuracy, and batch frames when latency allows. Put NMS into the exported graph or on the GPU so that it does not become the bottleneck. For video, add a tracker on top of the detector so that detections link across frames and so that you can smooth out flicker.
interview: Why do detectors produce duplicate boxes, and how do you remove them? || Every nearby position and anchor can respond to the same object, so many boxes cover it. NMS keeps the best-scored box and removes boxes with IoU above a threshold; Soft-NMS decays scores instead; DETR-style training with one-to-one matching removes the need for NMS.
Why was focal loss needed for single-stage detectors? || A single-stage detector scores about 100,000 candidate locations and nearly all are easy background. Their summed loss swamps the gradient from the few real objects. Focal loss multiplies each example's loss by \( (1-p_t)^\gamma \), so confident correct predictions contribute almost nothing and training focuses on hard ones.
Your detector has good mAP@0.5 but customers complain that boxes are loose. What do you do? || Look at mAP at 0.75 and above and at the average IoU of matched boxes. Use a loss that optimizes overlap directly (GIoU, DIoU, CIoU), better box refinement (a cascade of heads), higher resolution features, and check annotation quality, since inconsistent labels cap achievable tightness.
How would you detect small objects in 4K images at 30 frames per second? || Running a full 4K image through a large model is too costly. Use tiles or a region of interest from motion or a first coarse pass, a finer pyramid level, and a light model; batch tiles on the GPU and run NMS across tile borders. I would measure recall by object size to see which choice pays.
What is the difference between semantic, instance and panoptic segmentation, and when does it matter? || Semantic assigns a class per pixel and cannot separate two touching objects of the same class; instance gives each object a mask but ignores background regions; panoptic combines both. It matters when you need counts or per-object tracking, which need instances.
quiz: What does IoU measure, and what is the IoU of two identical boxes? || It is the area of overlap divided by the area of union of two boxes. Identical boxes have IoU 1.
Why does non-maximum suppression make crowded scenes harder? || It deletes every box that overlaps a higher-scoring box above a threshold, so a real object standing close to another one can be removed along with the duplicates.
Why do anchors predict offsets relative to the anchor size instead of absolute coordinates? || Offsets normalized by anchor width and height make the targets similar in scale for small and large objects, so shared weights work for all sizes, and the log for width and height keeps sizes positive.
A detector has a stride-16 feature map for a 640 × 640 image with 9 anchors per position. How many anchors does it have at that level? || The map is 40 × 40 = 1,600 positions, and 1,600 × 9 = 14,400 anchors.
When would you choose a single-stage detector over a two-stage detector, and what do you give up? || When you need real-time speed or must run on an edge device. You may lose some accuracy on small or crowded objects, which two-stage detectors with region refinement usually handle better.
