@@ crawling
value: A web crawler is a program that discovers and downloads web pages by following links. It starts from a list of seed URLs, fetches each page, extracts the links in it, and adds the new ones to a queue of work to do. Repeating this billions of times builds a copy of a large part of the web. Search engines crawl to build their index, Common Crawl publishes crawls as open data, price and news aggregators crawl to collect fresh facts, and the training text of most large language models comes largely from crawled pages.
The idea is easy and the engineering is not. The web has hundreds of billions of pages and changes all the time. A crawler must fetch hundreds or thousands of pages per second, yet it must never overload any single website, so it needs politeness. It must not fetch the same URL twice, which means remembering billions of URLs. It must recognize that thousands of different URLs return the same or almost the same content, it must avoid traps that generate endless pages, and it must decide which pages to fetch first and when to come back.
For the layer above, a search engine is only as good as its crawl: pages that were never fetched cannot be found, and pages that are stale give wrong answers. An LLM's pretraining corpus is shaped by what crawlers could reach and what was filtered out. The layers below do the heavy lifting. HTTP (L3) is the protocol for fetching, including conditional requests that save bandwidth, status codes, redirects and the robots.txt convention. DNS (L3) resolves host names, and at crawl scale its latency dominates unless the results are cached. Queues and priority structures (L3) form the URL frontier. Bloom filters (L2) remember which URLs were seen in a small amount of memory. Locality-sensitive hashing (L2), with MinHash or SimHash, finds near-duplicate pages without comparing every pair.
terms: Seed URL — a starting address given to the crawler by hand or from an earlier crawl.
URL frontier — the data structure that holds the URLs waiting to be fetched, with their priorities and the per-host scheduling.
Politeness — the rule that a crawler limits the load it puts on any host, for example one request at a time and a pause between requests.
robots.txt — a text file at the root of a site that tells crawlers which paths they may fetch; now specified in RFC 9309.
URL-seen test — checking whether a URL has already been discovered, so that it is queued only once.
Content-seen test — checking whether the downloaded content duplicates a page already stored.
Canonicalization — rewriting a URL into one standard form (lowercase host, removed fragment, sorted parameters) so the same page does not appear under several names.
Near-duplicate — a page whose content is almost the same as another page, such as the same article with a different advertisement.
Spider trap — a set of pages that generates an unlimited number of URLs, like a calendar that links to the next month forever.
Freshness — the share of stored pages that still match the live web; re-crawl policies try to keep it high.
history: The first crawlers appeared in 1993 and 1994, soon after the web began: Matthew Gray's World Wide Web Wanderer measured the growth of the web, and Brian Pinkerton's WebCrawler (1994) was the first to index full text. Early crawlers sometimes overloaded small servers, and in 1994 Martijn Koster proposed the Robots Exclusion Protocol, a voluntary convention that stayed informal for 28 years until it was published as RFC 9309 in 2022.
The scale grew quickly. In "The Anatomy of a Large-Scale Hypertextual Web Search Engine" (1998), Brin and Page described Google's crawler: several distributed crawlers, each keeping about 300 connections open at once, fetching on the order of 100 pages per second at peak. The same year Cho and Garcia-Molina studied which URLs to crawl first (ordering by link counts or PageRank), and Najork and Wiener (2001) found that simple breadth-first order already downloads high-quality pages early. Heydon and Najork's Mercator (1999) became the reference design: a modular crawler with a two-level URL frontier for priority and politeness, fingerprint-based duplicate tests, and a design that could be written in Java and run on a cluster.
Duplicates were the next problem. Broder and colleagues (1997) introduced shingling and MinHash to estimate how similar two documents are, and they applied it to the web crawl of AltaVista. Manku, Jain and Das Sarma (2007) showed at Google that 64-bit SimHash fingerprints, compared by Hamming distance, detect near-duplicates in billions of pages. Cho and Garcia-Molina (2000, 2003) modelled page changes as random events and derived refresh policies that maximize freshness.
Common Crawl, a non-profit, began publishing monthly web crawls in 2008, and these became the main open source of text for language models, with later crawls holding billions of pages each. The 2010s added the problem of JavaScript: many pages show their content only after scripts run, so crawlers began to use headless browsers. In the 2020s publishers began to add rules for AI crawlers to robots.txt and to block or license access, so the crawling landscape is partly a question of permission and not only of engineering.
how: A crawler is a loop around four components: a frontier of URLs to fetch, fetchers that download pages, parsers that extract links, and filters that decide what to keep. The loop starts with seed URLs in the frontier. Take a URL, fetch it, parse out links, filter them, and put the new ones back in the frontier. The design questions are all about doing this at scale and politely.
The frontier is the core of the system. A simple FIFO queue is not enough, because it gives no priority and no politeness. The Mercator design uses two layers. Front queues order URLs by priority: pages from important sites, fresh news pages, or URLs that changed often rank higher. Back queues enforce politeness: there is one queue per host, and each host queue has a "next allowed time". A min-heap (the priority-queue concept) keyed by next allowed time tells the fetcher which host may be contacted now. When a fetch for host H finishes, the crawler sets H's next allowed time to the current time plus a delay, and a common rule is about 10 times the time the last fetch took, with a minimum of one second or the `Crawl-delay` the site asks for. A host never has more than one request in flight. Many hosts are fetched in parallel, so total throughput comes from the number of hosts and not from hammering one.
Before a host's pages are fetched, the crawler reads its robots.txt (cached for hours or a day) and checks every URL against the rules for its user agent. Next it needs the IP address of the host. DNS lookups can take tens to hundreds of milliseconds, and a crawler may touch hundreds of millions of distinct hosts, so it runs its own caching resolver with many parallel queries, since a generic resolver would become the bottleneck. Then the fetcher opens an HTTP connection (reusing it for the same host), sends the request with a clear user-agent string and contact address, and uses timeouts and size limits, because some servers answer slowly or send endless data. It follows redirects up to a limit, accepts compressed responses, and handles the status codes: 200 stores the page, 301 and 302 add the target URL, 404 and 410 mark the URL dead, and 429 or 503 mean slow down.
Parsing extracts links from the HTML (and from sitemaps if the site provides them). Each link is canonicalized: resolve relative paths against the base URL, lowercase the host, remove default ports and fragments, remove known tracking parameters, and sort query parameters. Without this, one page appears under many addresses. Then comes the URL-seen test, where the Bloom filter helps. A set of 10 billion URLs stored as 8-byte fingerprints needs 80 GB, but a Bloom filter at 10 bits per URL (about 1% false positives) needs about 12 GB. A false positive only means that a new URL is wrongly skipped, which is acceptable for most crawls; a rarely-skipped URL is found again from another link. Large crawlers combine a filter in memory with an exact structure on disk. The rule for the exact check is the same fingerprint set.
The content-seen test comes after fetching. An exact duplicate is found by hashing the page body (after removing boilerplate) and looking the hash up in a set. Near-duplicates need more. MinHash works on the set of overlapping word sequences (shingles) of each page: a short signature of, say, 128 minimum hash values is stored, and the fraction of equal positions in two signatures estimates the Jaccard similarity of the shingle sets. To avoid comparing every pair, locality-sensitive hashing splits the signature into b bands of r values each; two pages become candidates if any band matches exactly. The chance that two pages with similarity s become candidates is
\[ P(\text{candidate}) = 1 - \left(1 - s^{r}\right)^{b} \]
which is an S-shaped curve with a threshold near \( (1/b)^{1/r} \). SimHash is the alternative: compute a 64-bit fingerprint in which similar documents differ in few bits, and find all fingerprints within Hamming distance 3 using bit-permuted lookup tables. Duplicate pages are dropped before indexing, or marked so that the search engine shows only the best copy. The same step removes pages that are copies in a spider trap.
Finally the crawler decides when to return. Pages change at different rates, and a news page changes hourly while a manual changes yearly. If changes happen at random with rate \( \lambda \) per day, the chance a stored copy is still current after t days is \( e^{-\lambda t} \). A policy then spends the crawl budget where it improves freshness the most, and an interesting result of Cho and Garcia-Molina is that pages that change very fast should be crawled less, since the copy would be stale almost at once. Conditional requests (`If-None-Match` with an ETag, or `If-Modified-Since`) let a server answer 304 Not Modified with no body, which saves most of the bandwidth on revisits.
At scale, the crawl is distributed. URLs are assigned to crawler machines by a hash of the host name, so each host is handled by exactly one machine, which keeps politeness local and keeps the per-host queue in one place. Links to hosts owned by other machines are sent in batches. Pages are written to a distributed file system in large compressed files, and downstream jobs build the index.
```python
import heapq, time

class Frontier:
    def __init__(self):
        self.queues = {}          # host -> list of urls
        self.ready = []           # heap of (next_allowed_time, host)
        self.scheduled = set()    # hosts in the heap or being fetched

    def add(self, host, url):
        self.queues.setdefault(host, []).append(url)
        if host not in self.scheduled:
            self.scheduled.add(host)
            heapq.heappush(self.ready, (time.time(), host))

    def next(self):
        t, host = heapq.heappop(self.ready)
        time.sleep(max(0, t - time.time()))
        return host, self.queues[host].pop(0)

    def done(self, host, fetch_secs):
        delay = max(1.0, 10 * fetch_secs)
        if self.queues[host]:
            heapq.heappush(self.ready, (time.time() + delay, host))
        else:
            self.scheduled.discard(host)
```

example: Plan a crawl of 1 billion pages in 30 days and check what it needs.
Step 1: rate. 30 days is 30 × 86,400 = 2,592,000 seconds. One billion pages in that time is 1,000,000,000 / 2,592,000 ≈ 386 pages per second.
Step 2: concurrency. Each fetch takes about 300 ms in total (DNS cache hit, connection, response). By Little's law, the number of requests in flight is rate × time = 386 × 0.3 ≈ 116. In practice you run several hundred concurrent fetches to cover slow servers and timeouts.
Step 3: bandwidth and storage. If an average HTML page is 100 KB, the download rate is 386 × 100 KB ≈ 38.6 MB/s, about 310 Mbit/s. Storage is 1 billion × 100 KB = 100 TB raw. Compressed text is usually 4 to 5 times smaller, so about 20 to 25 TB.
Step 4: politeness. Suppose one large site has 1,000,000 pages and you allow one request every 2 seconds. Fetching it needs 2,000,000 seconds, which is 23 days. If you did only this site at that speed, the job would take most of the month. This is why the crawler must work on thousands of hosts in parallel, and why the frontier orders work by host: at 386 pages per second with a 2-second delay per host, you need at least 386 × 2 = 772 hosts active at any moment to keep the fetchers busy. It also shows a limit: a crawl cannot get a huge single site faster without asking permission to go faster.
Step 5: URL-seen memory. If the crawl discovers 10 billion distinct URLs (each page has many links), a Bloom filter with 9.6 bits per URL for a 1% error needs 10 × 10⁹ × 9.6 bits = 96 × 10⁹ bits = 12 GB. Exact storage of 8-byte fingerprints would take 80 GB. At a 1% false-positive rate, about 1 in 100 new URLs is wrongly skipped, but the pages that matter are usually linked from several places and are found anyway.
Step 6: near-duplicates. Each page has a MinHash signature of 128 values, split into 16 bands of 8 values. A pair with similarity 0.9 becomes a candidate with probability \( 1 - (1 - 0.9^8)^{16} \approx 0.9999 \). A pair with similarity 0.5 becomes a candidate with probability \( 1 - (1 - 0.5^8)^{16} \approx 0.06 \). The candidates are then verified by comparing the signatures, so only the pairs that really are similar are removed.
intuition: A crawler is a polite librarian's assistant sent to copy every book in a city: always walk to the next unvisited shelf, never stand at one shelf for too long, and keep a list of what you already copied so you do not copy the same book twice.
The structure follows from three limits. The web is a graph, so you must remember where you have been (the seen-set). The sites are independent machines with finite capacity, so you must spread the work across them (per-host queues). And the web is full of copies, so you must compare cheaply (hashing). Each component maps to one data structure: a queue for what is next, a Bloom filter for what was seen, and a locality-sensitive hash for what is similar.
mistakes: People often think a crawler is a simple loop that follows links. In fact the loop is a small part of the work. Politeness, deduplication, canonicalization, trap avoidance, retries, scheduling revisits and handling billions of URLs are the engineering. A naive crawler gets blocked within minutes or fills its disks with copies.
People often think robots.txt prevents access. In fact it is a request, not a security control. Well-behaved crawlers follow it, and others do not. Anything private must be protected with authentication, not by listing it in robots.txt, which also tells anyone where to look. Also, a page blocked by robots.txt can still appear in a search index through links to it, since blocking stops fetching, not indexing.
People often think duplicate detection is just comparing hashes. In fact exact hashes only find byte-identical pages, and most web duplicates differ slightly (timestamps, ads, session ids in links). Near-duplicate detection needs similarity measures such as SimHash and MinHash, with LSH to avoid comparing all pairs.
People often think the crawled web is the whole web. In fact a large part is not reachable by following links: pages behind forms and logins, pages generated by scripts, pages blocked by robots.txt, and pages nobody links to (the deep web). The crawl also over-represents what is easy to fetch and often what is in English.
friction: Freshness and coverage compete for a fixed budget. Re-crawling old pages costs the same as discovering new ones, and the web grows and changes faster than any crawler can follow, so every crawler is a sample with a delay. News and social pages need minutes of freshness; the long tail is crawled every few weeks or months.
JavaScript-heavy pages are costly. A simple HTTP fetch returns an empty shell, and getting the content needs a headless browser that runs scripts, which can cost 10 to 100 times more CPU and time per page than a plain fetch. Crawlers use it selectively, for example when the raw HTML is nearly empty. Anti-bot systems, rate limits, CAPTCHAs and legal conditions add more constraints, and terms of service and copyright rules differ across countries and are changing, especially for AI training data.
tradeoffs: Choose breadth-first crawling with simple host priorities when you want broad coverage and have no quality signal, since it finds popular pages early. Choose a priority-based crawl (by PageRank, traffic or change rate) when your budget is smaller than the web and the use case is specific, such as news or e-commerce prices. You give up neutral coverage for focus.
Choose a Bloom filter for the URL-seen test when the set is large (billions) and a small loss of coverage is acceptable. Choose an exact set on disk or in a key-value store when you cannot afford to miss URLs, such as for a small focused crawl, and use the Bloom filter only as a first-level filter in front of it.
Choose SimHash for large-scale near-duplicate detection when each page needs a compact 64-bit fingerprint and you look for very similar pages (Hamming distance 3 or less). Choose MinHash with LSH when you want to control the similarity threshold, for example 0.8 Jaccard, and can store a signature of 100 or more values. SimHash costs less space, and MinHash gives a tunable and well understood trade-off.
practice: Respect the sites you crawl. Identify yourself with a user-agent that includes a contact URL, obey robots.txt, limit to one connection per host with a delay of at least a second (more if the site asks or responds slowly), back off on 429 and 503 responses, and cache robots.txt. Use conditional requests on revisits. Keep per-host and global rate limits as configuration that operators can change at once.
Build in safety against traps: cap the number of URLs per host, cap the URL length and depth, detect repeated path patterns, and limit the number of query parameters. Canonicalize URLs carefully and log the rules, as errors there multiply across billions of pages. Treat each downloaded page as untrusted input: limit size, set time limits, and parse with a safe parser.
For a first project, use an existing framework such as Scrapy for a focused crawl, or the Common Crawl indexes and files for large-scale text work (they let you fetch specific pages with HTTP range requests). Store raw responses in WARC files with headers, so you can re-parse later without fetching again. Measure per-host fetch rate, error rates by status code, duplicate rate, frontier size and age of the oldest pending URL; these show problems early.
interview: Design a web crawler that fetches 1 billion pages a month. What are the main components and numbers? || About 386 pages per second, so roughly 120 to a few hundred concurrent fetches, 40 MB/s of bandwidth for 100 KB pages, and 100 TB raw storage. Components are a frontier with priority and per-host queues, fetchers with a DNS cache and robots.txt cache, parsers, a URL-seen filter, a content-seen test, a store, and a recrawl scheduler, partitioned by host hash.
How do you make the crawler polite without losing throughput? || One request at a time per host, a delay (for example 10 times the last fetch time or the site's Crawl-delay), and a heap of hosts by next allowed time. Throughput comes from fetching many hosts in parallel: with a 2-second delay and 386 pages per second you need about 772 active hosts.
How do you avoid fetching the same URL twice, and what are the costs? || Canonicalize URLs and test them against a seen-set. A Bloom filter uses about 10 bits per URL at 1% error, much less than storing fingerprints, but a false positive makes the crawler skip a new URL. For stricter guarantees, combine it with an exact store on disk.
How do you detect near-duplicate pages across billions of documents? || Compute a SimHash fingerprint or a MinHash signature per page, and use locality-sensitive hashing so that only pages sharing a band or a permuted fingerprint prefix are compared. Candidates are verified by exact similarity. All-pairs comparison would be quadratic and impossible.
How would you decide when to re-crawl a page? || Estimate each page's change rate from past visits, for example with a Poisson model. The probability that a copy is stale after t is \( 1 - e^{-\lambda t} \), so allocate visits to maximize expected freshness per fetch. Conditional requests make unchanged pages cheap to check, and important or fast-changing sites get shorter intervals.
quiz: Why does a crawler keep one queue per host? || To enforce politeness. Each host queue has a next allowed time, so the crawler sends only one request at a time to a host and waits between requests, while fetching many other hosts in parallel.
Why is a Bloom filter suitable for the URL-seen test? || It stores billions of URLs in about 10 bits each and answers "seen" quickly, and its only error is a rare false positive, which skips a URL; it never forgets a URL that was added.
What is the purpose of canonicalization? || It rewrites different spellings of the same URL into one standard form, so the page is fetched and stored once and the seen-set stays accurate.
A crawl must fetch 500 million pages in 10 days. What page rate is needed? || 10 days = 864,000 seconds. 500,000,000 / 864,000 ≈ 579 pages per second.
When would you use SimHash rather than MinHash with LSH? || When you need compact 64-bit fingerprints and only want to find pages that are very similar (a small Hamming distance). MinHash with LSH is better when you need a tunable similarity threshold.
