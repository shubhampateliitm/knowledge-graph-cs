@@ gnn
value: A graph neural network (GNN) is a neural network whose input is a graph: nodes with feature vectors, and edges between them. Each layer lets every node collect information from its neighbours, transform it, and combine it with its own state. After k layers, the vector of a node (its embedding) depends on everything within k hops. These embeddings can then be used to classify nodes, to predict properties of the whole graph, or, in link prediction, to score a pair of nodes and decide whether an edge between them is missing or will appear.
Plain networks do not fit graphs. A multilayer network wants a fixed-size vector, and a convolutional or recurrent network wants a grid or a line. A graph has no fixed size, no order of nodes, and each node has a different number of neighbours. Flattening the adjacency matrix gives a different input for every relabeling of the same graph. A GNN is built so that it gives the same answer however the nodes are numbered, and so that it uses the same small set of weights for every node, in the same way a convolution shares weights across image positions.
For the layer above, GNNs turn the structure that products already store into signal. Recommenders such as Pinterest's PinSage learn from the graph of pins and boards. Social and commerce platforms predict "people you may know" or "items bought together" as link prediction. Identity resolution can score whether two device or account nodes belong to one person. AlphaFold-style models reason over a graph of residue pairs. Graph theory (L4) supplies the object and its adjacency matrix, linear algebra (L4) shows that one message-passing layer is a normalized matrix multiplication, embeddings (L2) give the idea that a node becomes a vector, and backpropagation (L2) trains everything end to end.
terms: Node feature — the input vector attached to a node, such as a user's profile or a pin's image embedding.
Message passing — one round in which each node receives vectors from its neighbours, combines them, and updates its own vector.
Aggregation — the permutation-invariant function (sum, mean, max) that merges the neighbour messages into one vector.
Receptive field — the set of nodes that can influence a node's embedding after k layers; the nodes within k hops.
Adjacency matrix — the matrix \( A \) with \( A_{ij} = 1 \) if there is an edge from i to j, and 0 otherwise.
Link prediction — predicting whether an edge exists, or will appear, between two nodes, usually by scoring the pair of their embeddings.
Negative sampling — building training examples for "no edge" by choosing random node pairs that are not connected.
Over-smoothing — with many layers the embeddings of all nodes in a connected region become nearly equal and lose their differences.
Inductive vs transductive — an inductive model can embed nodes it never saw in training; a transductive model learns a fixed embedding for each training node.
history: Early work on neural networks for graph-structured data came from Gori, Monfardini and Scarselli (2005) and Scarselli and colleagues (2009, "The Graph Neural Network Model"). Their model repeated a local update until the node states settled to a fixed point. It worked but was slow to train. In parallel, spectral graph theory offered another route: Bruna and colleagues (2014) defined convolutions through the eigenvectors of the graph Laplacian, and Defferrard, Bresson and Vandergheynst (2016) made them cheap with polynomial filters.
The simplification that made GNNs popular was Kipf and Welling's graph convolutional network (GCN), posted in 2016 and published at ICLR 2017. They reduced the spectral filter to a first-order form: each layer multiplies the node features by a degree-normalized adjacency matrix with self-loops, and then by a weight matrix. It was a few lines of code and gave strong results on citation-network benchmarks. The same authors also proposed the variational graph autoencoder (2016), which predicts links by taking dot products of node embeddings.
In 2017 the field widened quickly. Hamilton, Ying and Leskovec's GraphSAGE showed how to learn an inductive function by sampling a fixed number of neighbours, which made large graphs and unseen nodes possible. Veličković and colleagues' graph attention network (GAT) learned weights for each neighbour. Gilmer and colleagues' message passing neural network paper (2017) unified many variants for molecules. Xu and colleagues (2018, "How Powerful are Graph Neural Networks?") proved that message-passing GNNs are at most as discriminating as the Weisfeiler-Lehman graph test, and designed the GIN layer to reach that limit.
Industrial scale arrived with PinSage (Ying and colleagues, KDD 2018), trained at Pinterest on a graph with about 3 billion nodes and 18 billion edges. Zhang and Chen's SEAL (2018) showed that link prediction improves when the model looks at the small subgraph around the candidate pair. Later work studied the limits (over-smoothing, Li and colleagues 2018; over-squashing, Alon and Yahav 2020) and mixed GNNs with attention in graph transformers.
how: Start with a graph of N nodes. Each node i has a feature vector \( x_i \), and the graph has an adjacency matrix \( A \). A GNN layer does the same three steps for every node. Step 1: compute a message from each neighbour, usually by multiplying the neighbour's vector by a weight matrix. Step 2: aggregate the messages with a function that does not depend on their order. Step 3: combine the result with the node's own vector and pass it through a nonlinearity. The Kipf and Welling layer is the simplest example, and it can be written as one matrix expression:
\[ H^{(l+1)} = \sigma\!\left(\tilde D^{-1/2}\, \tilde A\, \tilde D^{-1/2}\, H^{(l)} W^{(l)}\right), \qquad \tilde A = A + I \]
Here \( H^{(l)} \) is the N × d matrix of node vectors at layer l, \( W^{(l)} \) is a learned weight matrix, \( \tilde D \) is the diagonal degree matrix of \( \tilde A \), and \( \sigma \) is a function such as ReLU. This is where linear algebra and graph theory carry the weight. Multiplying by \( A \) sums the vectors of the neighbours of each node. Adding the identity \( I \) lets a node keep its own vector. The factor \( \tilde D^{-1/2} \) on both sides divides each message by \( \sqrt{d_i d_j} \), so a node with 10,000 neighbours does not produce a vector 10,000 times larger than a node with 1 neighbour. Multiplying by \( W \) is the small neural network from the multilayer perceptron concept, shared by all nodes. Because the sum does not depend on the order of the neighbours, relabeling the nodes only permutes the rows of the output; the model is permutation equivariant.
Each layer widens the receptive field by one hop. After two layers, node i has a vector that depends on its neighbours' neighbours. This is why the number of layers is usually small (2 to 4): the receptive field grows exponentially in a graph with high degree, and a few hops can already reach a large part of the graph.
Training works like any network. For node classification the final vector goes through a softmax and a cross-entropy loss against labels. For link prediction, take the final embeddings \( z_u \) and \( z_v \) of two nodes and compute a score, most simply the dot product, turned into a probability: \( p(u,v) = \sigma(z_u \cdot z_v) \). The loss rewards high scores for real edges and low scores for sampled non-edges (negative sampling). Backpropagation sends gradients through the scoring function, through every layer, and into the shared weights. Nothing is hand-designed per node: the weights learn what is useful for predicting edges. Better decoders replace the dot product by a small neural network over \( [z_u, z_v] \), or use relation-specific scoring in a graph with several edge types. The set of positive edges used for training must be hidden from message passing when computing the loss for that edge, or the model sees the answer.
Large graphs need sampling, because the full matrix \( \tilde A \) and all intermediate \( H \) do not fit in the memory of one GPU. GraphSAGE changes the aggregation: for each target node, sample a fixed number of neighbours at each layer (the paper used 25 for the first hop and 10 for the second), then average or pool their transformed vectors and concatenate them with the node's own vector. Cost per target node is then 25 × 10 = 250 sampled nodes, not the whole graph, and it is the same for every node. PinSage adds random-walk-based neighbour sampling, so the neighbours are the ones with the most visits and not just direct edges, and it trains with minibatches on a graph stored on CPU memory. The sampling also makes the model inductive: it learns a function from neighbourhoods to vectors, so a new node can be embedded as soon as it has neighbours.
Link prediction on real systems is usually a two-stage job. The GNN produces embeddings offline or in a nightly batch. At serving time, candidate generation uses an approximate nearest neighbour index over the embeddings, and a ranker scores the shortlist with extra features. This is why a dot-product decoder is popular: it turns "find the best new links for u" into a maximum inner product search.
diagram:
  One message-passing layer for node C (neighbours A, B, D)

        A (x_A)     B (x_B)
          \          /
           v        v
            C (x_C) <------- D (x_D)

  1. message   m_j = W * x_j            for j in {A, B, D, and C itself}
  2. aggregate a_C = sum_j  m_j / sqrt(d_C * d_j)
  3. update    h_C = ReLU(a_C)

  Stack k layers: receptive field of a node = nodes within k hops

  layer 0:  C sees {C}
  layer 1:  C sees {A, B, D, C}
  layer 2:  C sees A's, B's, D's neighbours too

  Link prediction head

  h_u ----\
           (dot product) --> sigmoid --> p(edge u,v)
  h_v ----/
example: Take four nodes with edges A–B, A–C, B–C and C–D. Give them one-number features: A = 1, B = 2, C = 3, D = 4. Use the GCN rule with W = 1 and ignore the nonlinearity, so we can see the aggregation clearly.
Step 1: add self-loops and count degrees. A has neighbours B, C and itself, so its degree is 3. B is the same, degree 3. C has A, B, D and itself, so degree 4. D has C and itself, so degree 2.
Step 2: for each node compute the sum over j of \( x_j / \sqrt{d_i d_j} \). For A: from A itself, \( 1/\sqrt{3 \cdot 3} = 0.333 \); from B, \( 2/3 = 0.667 \); from C, \( 3/\sqrt{12} = 0.866 \). The total is 1.866. B gives the same total, 1.866, because its neighbours have the same degrees. For C: A gives \( 1/\sqrt{12} = 0.289 \), B gives \( 2/\sqrt{12} = 0.577 \), C itself gives \( 3/4 = 0.75 \), and D gives \( 4/\sqrt{8} = 1.414 \). The total is 3.030. For D: from C, \( 3/\sqrt{8} = 1.061 \); from itself, \( 4/2 = 2.0 \). The total is 3.061.
Step 3: after one layer, A and B have the same value (1.866) although they started with 1 and 2. Their neighbourhoods are almost identical, so they are pulled together. D started at 4 and is now 3.06, pulled toward its only neighbour C. After a second layer, D's value would depend on A and B too, which are two hops away. This is the receptive field growing, and also the first sign of over-smoothing: values move toward each other.
Step 4: link prediction. Suppose training has given 2-dimensional embeddings \( z_A = (1, 0) \), \( z_B = (0.8, 0.6) \), \( z_D = (-0.5, 0.9) \). The dot products are A·B = 0.8, A·D = −0.5 and B·D = −0.4 + 0.54 = 0.14. After the sigmoid the probabilities are 0.69, 0.38 and 0.53. The pair A–B is an existing edge, so its loss is \( -\ln 0.69 = 0.37 \). The pair A–D is a sampled negative, so its loss is \( -\ln(1 - 0.38) = 0.47 \). Gradient descent will move \( z_A \) and \( z_D \) apart and \( z_A \) and \( z_B \) together.
Step 5: the cost of sampling. With fan-outs of 25 and 10, one target node needs at most 250 second-hop samples plus 25 first-hop ones, about 275 vectors. A batch of 1,024 target nodes then needs roughly 280,000 vector reads, regardless of whether the graph has a million or 3 billion nodes. Without sampling, a node near a hub with 100,000 neighbours would pull in 100,000 vectors at the first layer alone.
intuition: A GNN is a group of people who each repeatedly ask their friends what they think, mix that with their own opinion, and ask again; after k rounds, each person's view reflects the whole group within k steps.
The design is natural because the useful signal in a graph is local. Your interests are correlated with those of your neighbours (homophily), and a missing edge is likely between two nodes that share many neighbours. A GNN learns how to combine neighbour information, instead of an engineer writing "count common neighbours" by hand. The fixed set of weights shared by all nodes is the same idea as the shared filter of a convolution: the rule for combining neighbours is the same everywhere, so one model works for a graph of any size.
mistakes: People often think a GNN needs the whole graph to be small enough for one machine. In fact the layer depends only on a k-hop neighbourhood. With neighbour sampling, as in GraphSAGE and PinSage, training cost per node is fixed, and graphs with billions of edges are trained on in minibatches.
People often think more layers always help, as in image models. In fact repeated multiplication by the normalized adjacency matrix pushes all node vectors toward the same dominant eigenvector, so after many layers nodes in a connected region look alike (over-smoothing). Most useful GNNs have 2 to 4 layers, or add residual connections.
People often think link prediction is the same as ranking by common neighbours. In fact common neighbours and similar scores are strong baselines, and a GNN beats them mainly by using node features and longer paths. On graphs where structure alone explains the edges, the simple heuristic can be as good, so always compare to it.
People often think the test edges can be left in the graph during training. In fact that is data leakage: if the edge u–v is in the graph while the model learns to predict it, message passing carries the answer between the two nodes. Split edges first, and build the message-passing graph only from the training edges.
friction: Neighbour explosion is the first limit. A k-layer GNN with average degree d touches \( d^k \) nodes per target, and real graphs have hubs with millions of neighbours. Sampling bounds the cost but adds variance and a bias toward the sampled nodes, and building the sampled subgraphs is often slower than the matrix multiplications that follow, so the data pipeline can dominate training time.
Expressiveness is the second limit. Message-passing GNNs cannot tell apart some graphs that the Weisfeiler-Lehman test cannot distinguish, for example they cannot count triangles reliably without extra structural features. Over-smoothing and over-squashing limit depth. Over-squashing means that information from many distant nodes must pass through the fixed-size vector of a bottleneck node, so long-range signals are compressed away. Graph transformers and added positional features address this at a high cost.
Practical issues come next. Real graphs change every minute, so embeddings go stale, and an inductive model still needs fresh neighbours at serving time. Graphs are often heterogeneous (users, items, devices, with different edge types), and the model must handle each type. Labels for edges are biased by what the product has shown to users: an edge that was never observed may be a non-edge or just unseen. Negative sampling choices change what the model learns, and offline metrics such as AUC can be high while the top of the ranking is poor.
tradeoffs: Choose a GNN when the structure carries information that node features do not, and relations are many-to-many (social graphs, item co-purchase graphs, device graphs). Choose a plain model on node features, or a gradient-boosted tree model with graph features such as degree, common neighbours and PageRank, when the labeled data is small or when you need a model you can explain and serve cheaply. You give up the learned multi-hop signal but gain simplicity; on many tabular problems the engineered-feature baseline is within a few points of the GNN.
Choose a full-batch GCN when the graph has fewer than about a million nodes and fits in GPU memory: it is simple, exact and fast. Choose neighbour sampling (GraphSAGE style) when the graph is larger or when you need an inductive model for new nodes. You give up exactness and a little accuracy for a bounded cost per node.
Choose a dot-product decoder when candidates must come from a nearest-neighbour index among millions of items; choose a learned pair decoder (a small network over both embeddings and pair features) for reranking a shortlist. The first is fast and slightly less accurate, the second the opposite.
Choose precomputed embeddings (nightly batch) when freshness of a day is acceptable; choose online sampling at request time only when new edges must count within seconds, and expect a latency budget of tens of milliseconds to be dominated by neighbour fetches.
practice: First build the baselines: common neighbours, Adamic-Adar, PageRank features and a gradient-boosted model. Report the GNN's gain over them, not over random. Split edges by time, not at random, if the product predicts future links, since random splits leak.
Use a library. PyTorch Geometric and the Deep Graph Library provide GCN, SAGE, GAT layers, samplers and link-prediction examples; for web-scale graphs, look at distributed samplers and graph stores. Start with two layers, hidden size 64 to 256, mean aggregation, and add residual connections before adding depth. Sample negatives that are harder than uniform (for example nodes within two hops, or popular items) after the first model works, and evaluate with ranking metrics such as recall at k and hits at k on a held-out set of edges, not accuracy.
For production, separate the embedding job from serving. Compute embeddings in batch, write them to a vector index, and handle cold start with a model on node features alone. Monitor drift: the distribution of embedding norms and nearest-neighbour overlap between today and yesterday are cheap signals that a pipeline broke. Be careful with sensitive attributes, because the graph can reveal them through neighbours even when you remove the column.
interview: Walk through exactly what one GCN layer computes, and why the normalization matters. || Each node sums its neighbours' (and its own) vectors, scaled by \( 1/\sqrt{d_i d_j} \), multiplies by a shared weight matrix and applies a nonlinearity. Without the normalization, high-degree nodes get huge vectors and low-degree nodes tiny ones, which destabilizes training; the symmetric form also keeps the operator's eigenvalues in a stable range.
Why can a GNN embed a node it never saw in training, and a classic node-embedding table cannot? || A GNN learns a function from a node's features and its neighbours' features to a vector. A lookup table such as DeepWalk learns one free vector per training node, with nothing to compute for a new one.
Your link-prediction AUC is 0.97 offline but the product impact is small. What do you check? || First leakage (test edges present in message passing, random split on temporal data), then whether negatives are too easy, then whether the ranking at top k matches the product need. AUC averages over easy negatives and says little about the top of a ranked list; I would report recall at k against candidates the product would really show.
How do you serve GNN recommendations at 10 ms latency? || Do the graph computation offline: batch-compute embeddings, put them in an approximate nearest-neighbour index, and serve by a vector lookup plus a light ranker. Online neighbour sampling needs several remote reads and rarely fits the budget, so only use it when freshness requires it.
What can message-passing GNNs not do, and what would you change? || They are bounded by the Weisfeiler-Lehman test, so they miss some structure, such as counting cycles, and they struggle with long-range dependencies through over-squashing. Options are structural or positional features, higher-order GNNs, adding virtual nodes, or a graph transformer, each at extra cost.
quiz: What does a k-layer message-passing GNN let a node's embedding depend on? || Exactly the nodes within k hops of it (its k-hop neighbourhood), because each layer brings in information from one more hop.
Why is the sum over neighbours used with normalization, and what goes wrong without it? || The sum is independent of neighbour order, which makes the layer permutation-equivariant. Without dividing by degrees, nodes with many neighbours get very large vectors and training becomes unstable.
Why does stacking many GCN layers produce over-smoothing? || Each layer multiplies by the normalized adjacency matrix, and repeated powers of that matrix converge to its dominant eigenvector, so the vectors of nodes in a connected region become almost the same.
With sampling fan-outs of 20, 10 and 5 for three layers, how many nodes does one target node need at most? || The sampled counts multiply: 20 + 20 × 10 + 20 × 10 × 5 = 20 + 200 + 1,000 = 1,220 neighbours, plus the target itself.
When would you prefer a gradient-boosted model with graph features to a GNN? || When labeled data is small, when explainability and cheap serving matter, or when structure is well captured by a few features such as degree and common-neighbour counts; the GNN's extra accuracy may not justify its pipeline cost.
