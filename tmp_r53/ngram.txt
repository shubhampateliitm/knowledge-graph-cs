value: An n-gram language model estimates how likely the next word is, given the previous n − 1 words, by counting. A bigram model (n = 2) asks: in my training text, how often did "tea" follow "like", out of all the times "like" appeared? A trigram model (n = 3) looks at the two previous words. Multiplying these conditional probabilities along a sentence gives the probability of the whole sentence. That number answers questions such as "which of these two transcriptions is more fluent?", "which word is the user probably typing?", and "does this text look like English?".
The idea is simple, and the difficulty is in the counts. Even with a billion words of training text, most possible word sequences never appear. If "tea with oat milk" was never seen, plain counting says its probability is exactly zero, and one zero makes the probability of a whole sentence zero. Smoothing is the set of methods that fix this: take a little probability away from the sequences that were seen and give it to those that were not, in a principled way. Good smoothing (Kneser-Ney) is the difference between a model that is useful and one that is not.
For the layer above, statistical NLP was built on n-gram models: speech recognition, machine translation, spelling correction and autocomplete all used them for decades, and tools such as KenLM still do, for example to filter web text by how natural it looks. An LLM is a next-token model of the same kind, and the evaluation measure, perplexity, comes straight from this era. The layers below supply the foundations. Markov chains (L4) justify the n-gram assumption that only the last n − 1 words matter. Information theory (L4) gives cross-entropy and perplexity as the measure of model quality, and Shannon's own 1948 experiments with approximations of English. Statistics and estimation (L4) explain that the counts are maximum-likelihood estimates, why they overfit, and what smoothing does as a prior or a correction.
terms: n-gram — a sequence of n consecutive words (or tokens); unigram, bigram, trigram are n = 1, 2, 3.
Markov assumption — the next word depends only on the previous n − 1 words, not on the rest of the history.
Maximum-likelihood estimate (MLE) — the probability that makes the training data most likely; for n-grams it is the count of the n-gram divided by the count of its context.
Sparsity — most possible n-grams never occur in any finite training set, so their counts are zero.
Smoothing — adjusting counts so that unseen n-grams get a small non-zero probability and seen ones get a little less.
Backoff and interpolation — using a shorter context (n − 1 words) when the longer one has too little data; backoff replaces the estimate, interpolation mixes both.
Perplexity — the exponential of the average negative log-probability per word on test text; the model is "as confused as" choosing among that many equally likely words.
Out-of-vocabulary (OOV) word — a word not in the model's vocabulary; usually mapped to a special token such as UNK.
Continuation count — the number of distinct words that precede a word; Kneser-Ney uses it in place of the word's raw frequency.
history: Andrey Markov studied dependence between consecutive letters in Pushkin's "Eugene Onegin" in 1913, counting vowels and consonants, which is the first example of what we now call a Markov chain. Claude Shannon's "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" (1948) generated English-like text from letter and word frequencies of increasing order, and showed that a longer context gives a more English-looking result. This set up the idea that language has entropy that can be estimated, and that a model can be judged by how well it predicts.
The method became practical with speech recognition. At IBM in the 1970s and 80s, Frederick Jelinek's group framed recognition as picking the word sequence with the highest product of acoustic probability and language-model probability, and used trigram models. They also met sparsity at once. Good (1953), crediting Turing, had given a formula for the probability of unseen events based on how many things were seen exactly once. Jelinek and Mercer (1980) proposed interpolating models of different orders, and Katz (1987) introduced backoff. Kneser and Ney (1995) improved the lower-order estimate with continuation counts, and Chen and Goodman's large comparison (1996, expanded 1999) showed modified Kneser-Ney was consistently the best smoothing method.
Scale then became the main driver. Google released the Web 1T 5-gram data in 2006, built from about a trillion words, and Brants and colleagues (2007) showed that a simple "stupid backoff" approached the quality of Kneser-Ney as the training data grew, and that more data kept improving translation, which supported the view that data can matter more than cleverness. Heafield's KenLM (2011) made big models fast and small with efficient data structures, and it is still the standard tool. Neural models took over the main role: Bengio and colleagues (2003) proposed a neural n-gram model with learned word embeddings, recurrent models (Mikolov 2010) removed the fixed window, and Transformers followed. N-gram models did not disappear. They stayed as fast, cheap components, and in 2024 the Infini-gram work (Liu and colleagues) showed that an n-gram model with no fixed n, using a suffix array over trillions of tokens, gives useful predictions and can be used to analyse the training data of LLMs.
how: Start with the chain rule of probability, which is exact. The probability of a sentence is the product of the probability of each word given all the words before it:
\[ P(w_1, \dots, w_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}) \]
The conditional probabilities with long histories cannot be estimated, because almost every long history is unique. The Markov assumption cuts the history to the last n − 1 words:
\[ P(w_t \mid w_1, \dots, w_{t-1}) \approx P(w_t \mid w_{t-n+1}, \dots, w_{t-1}) \]
This is exactly an (n − 1)-order Markov chain over words. For a bigram model the state is the previous word. Sentence boundaries are handled with special tokens: add `<s>` at the start (so the first word has a context) and `</s>` at the end (so the model can say that a sentence is finished).
Training is counting. The maximum-likelihood estimate is
\[ P_{\text{MLE}}(w \mid v) = \frac{c(v\,w)}{c(v)} \]
where \( c(v\,w) \) is the number of times the word w followed v. A single pass over the text with a hash map of counts gives the model. For a trigram model the key is a pair of previous words, and the table holds all trigrams observed. This is the statistics layer at work: counting is the MLE of the model, and it is the best estimate for the data seen, but it assigns probability zero to everything unseen. With a vocabulary of V = 100,000 words there are \( V^3 = 10^{15} \) possible trigrams and \( 10^{25} \) five-grams, but a corpus of 10 billion words contains at most 10 billion distinct trigram instances. Most of what a user types next will be unseen, so smoothing is essential.
The simplest smoothing is add-k: pretend every n-gram was seen k extra times, \( P(w \mid v) = (c(vw) + k)/(c(v) + kV) \). Add-one (k = 1) moves far too much probability to unseen events when V is large, because there are V possible words after each context and most are impossible. It is a poor method for language and is useful mainly to understand the idea. Better methods use the shorter context. Interpolation mixes estimates of different orders with weights that sum to one:
\[ P_{\text{interp}}(w \mid v) = \lambda\, P_{\text{MLE}}(w \mid v) + (1 - \lambda)\, P(w) \]
The weights are tuned on held-out text. Backoff in Katz's form uses the long-context estimate when its count is positive, and otherwise falls back to the shorter context, scaled so that the total probability stays 1.
Kneser-Ney is the method that works best. It combines two ideas. The first is absolute discounting: subtract a fixed amount d (about 0.75) from every observed count, and give the freed mass to the shorter context in proportion to how many different words followed the context. The second is the key insight: the shorter-context estimate should not use raw frequency but continuation counts. The word "Francisco" is frequent, but it follows almost only "San". As a backoff for a new context, "Francisco" should get low probability, because it rarely appears after new words. The probability that a word is a good continuation is estimated by the number of distinct words that precede it:
\[ P_{\text{KN}}(w \mid v) = \frac{\max(c(vw) - d,\, 0)}{c(v)} + \lambda(v)\, P_{\text{cont}}(w), \qquad P_{\text{cont}}(w) = \frac{N_{1+}(\bullet\, w)}{N_{1+}(\bullet\,\bullet)} \]
where \( \lambda(v) = d \cdot N_{1+}(v\,\bullet) / c(v) \) is the mass freed by discounting, \( N_{1+}(\bullet\, w) \) is the number of distinct words preceding w and \( N_{1+}(\bullet\,\bullet) \) is the number of distinct bigram types. The model is evaluated by perplexity on held-out text:
\[ \text{PP} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \ln P(w_t \mid \text{context})\right) = 2^{H} \]
where H is the cross-entropy in bits per word, the information theory link. A perplexity of 100 means the model is, on average, as uncertain as if it chose uniformly among 100 words at each position. Test text must be kept apart from the training text, and unknown words are mapped to `<unk>` in both.
Large models need engineering. KenLM stores the counts in a trie or in a hash table with probing and quantizes the probabilities, so that a large 5-gram model fits in the memory of one machine and answers a query in microseconds. To get a language-model score for a sentence in speech recognition or translation, the decoder queries the model for each word and multiplies the results (or adds the logs) with the other scores.
```python
from collections import Counter
uni, bi = Counter(), Counter()
for sent in corpus:                       # each sent is a list of words
    toks = ["<s>"] + sent + ["</s>"]
    for a, b in zip(toks, toks[1:]):
        uni[a] += 1
        bi[(a, b)] += 1

def p_mle(w, v):
    return bi[(v, w)] / uni[v] if uni[v] else 0.0
```

   seen in 10^10 words           <= 10^10
   => almost all possible 3-grams have count 0 => smoothing

  Backoff chain (interpolated)

   P(w | a b c)  =  discounted count part
                  + leftover mass * P(w | b c)
                                    + leftover * P(w | c)
                                                 + leftover * P(w)
example: Use a training corpus of three sentences: "i like tea", "i like coffee" and "you like tea". With boundary tokens each sentence has four events. Token counts, not counting `<s>`: c(i) = 2, c(you) = 1, c(like) = 3, c(tea) = 2, c(coffee) = 1, c(</s>) = 3, so 12 tokens in all.
Step 1: maximum-likelihood bigrams. P(i | <s>) = 2/3, P(you | <s>) = 1/3. P(like | i) = 2/2 = 1. P(like | you) = 1. P(tea | like) = 2/3 and P(coffee | like) = 1/3. P(</s> | tea) = 1 and P(</s> | coffee) = 1.
Step 2: score a sentence. "you like coffee" has probability P(you | <s>) × P(like | you) × P(coffee | like) × P(</s> | coffee) = 1/3 × 1 × 1/3 × 1 = 1/9 ≈ 0.111. Its perplexity over N = 4 events is \( 9^{1/4} \approx 1.73 \). Notice that this sentence never appeared in training, but each of its bigrams did.
Step 3: the zero problem. The test sentence "i tea" needs P(tea | i) = 0/2 = 0. Its whole probability is 0 and its perplexity is infinite, although it is only mildly odd.
Step 4: add-one smoothing. Take the six possible next words {i, like, tea, coffee, you, </s>}, so V = 6. Then P(tea | i) = (0 + 1)/(2 + 6) = 0.125, but also P(like | i) = (2 + 1)/(2 + 6) = 0.375, down from 1.0. The method gave 62.5% of the mass of this context to events that were never seen, which is far too generous.
Step 5: interpolation with the unigram, with λ = 0.8. The unigram probabilities are P(like) = 3/12 = 0.25 and P(tea) = 2/12 ≈ 0.167. Then P(tea | i) = 0.8 × 0 + 0.2 × 0.167 ≈ 0.033 and P(like | i) = 0.8 × 1 + 0.2 × 0.25 = 0.85. The seen event keeps most of its mass and the unseen one gets a small share.
Step 6: Kneser-Ney with d = 0.75. There are 8 distinct bigram types: (<s> i), (<s> you), (i like), (you like), (like tea), (like coffee), (tea </s>), (coffee </s>). Continuation probabilities: P_cont(like) = 2/8, since two different words (i, you) precede it, and P_cont(tea) = 1/8. For the context "i": c(i) = 2, one distinct follower, so λ(i) = 0.75 × 1/2 = 0.375. Then P_KN(like | i) = (2 − 0.75)/2 + 0.375 × 0.25 = 0.625 + 0.094 = 0.719, and P_KN(tea | i) = 0 + 0.375 × 0.125 ≈ 0.047. Check that the context sums to 1: the discounted part gives 0.625 and the backoff part gives 0.375 × (sum of all continuation probabilities = 1) = 0.375, so the total is 1.
intuition: An n-gram model is a very well-read autocomplete that remembers only the last few words and, for anything it has not read, guesses from shorter phrases it has read.
The design is natural because language is locally predictable: the next word depends strongly on the last two or three words and weakly on the rest. Counting is the most direct way to measure that. The only real difficulty is that language is made of rare events, and smoothing is the principled answer to what to believe about the events you have not seen. Kneser-Ney's insight adds an elegant point: when you back off, ask not "how common is this word?" but "in how many different contexts does it appear?", because a word that appears in many contexts is more likely to appear in a new one.
mistakes: People often think n-gram models are obsolete because LLMs exist. In fact they are cheap, fast, need no GPU, can be trained in minutes on a large corpus and can be updated by adding counts. They are still used in speech recognition, input methods, spelling correction, and data filtering (perplexity under a KenLM model is used to separate clean text from spam in web crawls).
People often think a higher n is always better. In fact the number of contexts grows exponentially with n and the counts per context shrink. Beyond 4 or 5 words, most contexts are unique, and the model needs backoff to shorter histories. The best n depends on the amount of data.
People often think smoothing is a minor technical fix. In fact the choice of smoothing changes perplexity by 10 percent or more, and it decides whether the model is usable. Good smoothing is a statistical model of the unseen, not an adjustment of numbers.
People often think perplexity can be compared across any two models. In fact it depends on the vocabulary and the tokenization. A model with a smaller vocabulary or one that treats rare words as `<unk>` has an easier task and a lower perplexity. Compare only on the same test text with the same tokens, or convert to bits per character.
friction: The fixed window is the basic limit. An n-gram model cannot use information further back than n − 1 words, so it cannot track agreement across a long clause, a topic, or a story. It also cannot generalize between similar words: seeing "the cat sat" tells it nothing about "the dog sat", because the two words are unrelated symbols. Neural models solve both with embeddings and long context, at much higher cost.
Smoothing choices make the models fragile in practice. Discounts and interpolation weights must be tuned on held-out text from the target domain, and a model trained on news performs poorly on chat. Probabilities are not calibrated for rare events, and the estimates for rare contexts are noisy. Finally, the models are only as good as the training text, and they reproduce its biases, duplicates and errors, since counting does not distinguish true from false.
tradeoffs: Choose an n-gram model when you need a fast, cheap, explainable score: filtering web text, rescoring candidates in speech or spelling, autocompletion on a device, or a baseline. Choose a neural language model when quality matters more than cost and you have GPUs: it captures long context and similar words. You give up a model that is trained in minutes and queried in microseconds for one that is trained in days and queried in milliseconds.
Choose modified Kneser-Ney with n = 3 to 5 when you have between 100 million and tens of billions of words. Choose stupid backoff (a fixed factor of about 0.4 for each backoff step, which gives scores, not true probabilities) when you have trillions of tokens and need simple distributed counting, since with enough data the smoothing method matters less. You give up normalized probabilities for scalability.
Choose a small n (2 or 3) when data is small (less than about 10 million words) or memory is tight; choose a larger n (5 or more) with pruning when data is large and the application benefits from exact phrases, such as transcription of names and set expressions. The deciding factor is the number of times a typical context was seen: if most contexts have fewer than about 5 examples, lower the order.
practice: Use KenLM. Build with `lmplz -o 5 < corpus.txt > model.arpa`, then convert to the binary format with `build_binary`, and query from Python or C++. Tokenize and normalize the training text exactly as you do at query time. Remove duplicates from the training text, because repeated documents inflate counts and make the model look better on them. Keep a held-out set from the target domain to tune pruning thresholds and to measure perplexity.
Match the training text to the use. For autocomplete, train on your own queries or messages and mix in a general model by interpolation, with the weight tuned on held-out data. For web-text filtering, train on a clean reference such as Wikipedia, score each document by perplexity per word, and inspect what falls at both ends: very low perplexity is often repeated boilerplate, and very high is often another language.
Evaluate sensibly. Report perplexity on held-out data with the same tokenization, but also the downstream metric (word error rate, translation quality, keystrokes saved). Lower perplexity does not always give a better downstream score. As a learning exercise, build a bigram model with add-k and with Kneser-Ney on a small corpus, and compare perplexities on held-out text; the gap between them shows what smoothing is worth.
interview: Why can't you use the maximum-likelihood estimate directly, and what does smoothing do? || MLE gives probability zero to any n-gram not in the training data, which makes the probability of a whole sentence zero and perplexity infinite. Smoothing takes some probability mass from observed n-grams and distributes it to unseen ones, usually by discounting counts and using shorter contexts.
Explain the idea behind Kneser-Ney. || When backing off to a lower-order model, the question should be how likely the word is to appear in a new context, not how frequent it is. It uses the number of distinct preceding words (continuation count) for the lower-order estimate and absolute discounting for the higher-order one. "Francisco" is frequent but follows only "San", so it gets a low continuation probability.
How is perplexity defined, and what does a value of 120 mean? || It is the exponential of the average negative log-probability per token on held-out text, which equals 2 to the cross-entropy in bits. A perplexity of 120 means the model is on average as uncertain as a uniform choice among 120 tokens at each position. It depends on the tokenization, so comparisons need the same tokens.
How would you build autocomplete for a product with 50 million past queries? || Count query n-grams in a trie, apply Kneser-Ney or simple interpolation, prune low counts to bound memory, and combine with prefix matching and popularity. Evaluate on held-out sessions by keystrokes saved. Update it by adding counts of recent queries and mixing with a general model.
Where do n-gram models sit relative to LLMs today, and what can you learn from them? || LLMs predict the next token from learned representations of long contexts. N-gram models are baselines, fast components for filtering and rescoring, and tools for analysing data. They show how much of language is predicted by short local context, and how much value the extra capacity of neural models adds beyond that.
Your language model's perplexity improved after you changed the tokenizer. Did the model get better? || Not necessarily. Perplexity per token depends on the number of tokens, so a tokenizer with longer tokens has higher per-token perplexity for the same quality of model. Compare bits per character, or the downstream metric on the same text.
quiz: What does the Markov assumption say in an n-gram model? || The next word depends only on the previous n − 1 words, and not on the earlier history.
Why does a trigram model with a 100,000-word vocabulary have a sparsity problem? || There are \( 10^{15} \) possible trigrams, far more than any corpus can contain, so most trigrams in new text were never seen in training and would get a zero count.
Why does Kneser-Ney use continuation counts instead of word frequencies for the backoff? || A word's total frequency is misleading if it occurs in only a few contexts, such as "Francisco" after "San". The number of different contexts it follows predicts better whether it will appear in a new context.
A model assigns probability 1/9 to a 4-token test sentence. What is its perplexity? || \( (1/9)^{-1/4} = 9^{1/4} \approx 1.73 \).
When would you choose stupid backoff instead of Kneser-Ney? || When the corpus has trillions of tokens and counting must be distributed simply. With that much data, careful smoothing matters less, and you accept scores that are not normalized probabilities.
